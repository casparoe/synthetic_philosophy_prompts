I'm giving a 30-minute talk plus discussion at a philosophy department colloquim in about three weeks and what I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. No reading lists or references — I need what the people in that specific room will actually say out loud.

The view, briefly. The chariot exchange in the Questions of King Milinda — Nagasena getting the king to agree that no axle, wheel, yoke or reins is the chariot, and that the chariot isn't something over and above them either, so "chariot" is a mere designation dependent on the assembled parts — gets read by analytic philosophers almost universally as an argument for something like mereological nihilism: wholes are conceptual fictions, only the parts are real, so the person is a fiction spread over the five aggregates. I argue that reading is wrong in two places. First, the argument isn't about what exists, it's about the direction of explanation: a term is a mere designation when it applies only in virtue of something that can be picked out independently of it, and the ordering only ever runs one way. Second — this is where most of my talk goes — once you take that seriously it doesn't stop at the person. The aggregates fail the very same test. Form can't be identified independently of the causal and conceptual order it sits in, and likewise for the rest, so the reduction has nowhere to terminate and there is no bottom floor. That's why I say anatta is a competitor to Parfit rather than an ancestor of him: Reductionism keeps the psychological-continuity facts as the real facts doing all the work, and my reading denies there are any terminal facts of that kind for it to keep.

Argument sketch, so you have something to aim the objections at: (1) a designation is conventional iff it applies only in virtue of an explanatory ordering that terminates in what's independently identifiable; (2) the chariot argument establishes (1) rather than a claim about parthood, because every step Nagasena runs is about how the king would pick the thing out, not about whether it's a whole; (3) the same test, applied without special pleading, demotes the aggregates; (4) therefore the view is not "only the parts exist" but "nothing is ultimate," which is a different and much less comfortable position, and the one that puts it in competition with Parfit instead of in his family tree.
