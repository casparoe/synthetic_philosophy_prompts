I'm the one stuck drafting a checklist for a small reading group on cosmology and time's arrow, and the task has turned out to be way harder than it sounded. The idea was a one-pager a referee could use to decide whether a proposed cosmological model "has a Boltzmann brain problem," but I've realized I can't even say what that means without begging the question against somebody in the group. One of us thinks any model whose late time is an eternal de Sitter phase at nonzero temperature is dead on arrival; another thinks the whole worry is an artifact of applying equilibrium statistical mechanics to the universe as a whole, where there's nothing outside to be in equilibrium with; a third says the physics is beside the point and the real problem is that a model which implies most observers are freak fluctuations undercuts the very reasoning that got you to the model in the first place. So: play the role of a philosopher of cosmology who has sat through a lot of measure-and-typicality proposals and is allergic to hand-waving, think carefully about which of the demands people make in this debate are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's priors wearing a lab coat, and do your pruning privately — I don't want to see the brainstorming, only the finished spec.

Give me exactly five numbered requirements that a cosmological model, together with whatever it says about observers and about how to weight them, has to meet to count as Boltzmann-brain-free. Format each item as a one-line statement of the requirement, then about two sentences: first the case or consideration that motivates it, then your verdict on whether it's non-negotiable or a camp preference, plus which of the other four items it collides with if you think no model can meet them all. Phrase every requirement so a referee could actually demand it on the basis of a model's own write-up, rather than something that needs new calculations to check. No intro, no wrap-up paragraph — just the five numbered items, and if one of them does most of the work in ruling models out, say so inside that item.

To keep the list honest, here's what it should be able to sort, and you can mention these in passing where they fit. (a) A model whose entire future is empty de Sitter space with a fixed tiny temperature, with ordinary observers confined to a short early window — does it get a pass because the recurrence timescale dwarfs that window, or fail because "dwarfs" is still finite against an infinite future? (b) A model where the late fluctuation-observers are momentary and never accumulate memories or records — is "what counts as an observer making an observation" a legitimate place to block the problem, or a semantic dodge? (c) A model thats simply finite in lifetime, so no fluctuation observers ever arise — at what point does buying the fix with fine-tuning cost more than the problem did? And if you think some subset of your five can't be jointly satisfied given how the physics actually goes, that's the part I most want to see, so don't smooth it over.
