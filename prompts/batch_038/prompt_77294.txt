Six weeks ago the executive board of the regional health authority where I work approved a reorganisation that, in private afterwards, essentially every colleague I spoke to agreed was a mistake — including, I am fairly confident, several of the people who had sat in the room where it was approved. Nobody said so at the time. I have been turning over why ever since, and reading the tale aloud to my daughter at the weekend I found myself rather cold, because Andersen's The Emperor's New Clothes does not read to me like a fable about vanity at all; read carefully, it looks like a worked model of precisely the room I was sitting in.

I want to write a short piece for our staff blog that treats the tale as a piece of epistemic game theory rather than as an improving story about pride. Please write the critical reading I will work from. I want a committed reading, not a survey of interpretations: take definite positions, give reasons, and tell me where you think I am wrong.

Please address four things.

1. Get the epistemic states exactly right before the child speaks. For each party — the weavers, the first inspector, the second, the Emperor, his retinue, the crowd along the procession route — say what they privately know, what they say, and what they believe about what the others know. The folk gloss is that everyone secretly knows he is naked and is simply too frightened to say so, and I suspect that gloss is wrong in an interesting way: I think the private knowledge and the second-order error are not the same thing, and that the swindlers' rule about the cloth is doing something more clever than threatening people. Say whether the situation is best described as pluralistic ignorance, as preference falsification, or as a failure of common knowledge — those three come apart, and I want to know which one the tale actually is.

2. Say precisely what the child's shout changes. Distinguish everyone-knows-that-p from common knowledge that p, and say whether the tale gives us a genuine public announcement in the sense of dynamic epistemic logic — public, truthful, costless, immediately accepted — or whether the child succeeds for reasons that have nothing to do with publicity: the child holds no office, has no competence to lose, and is therefore the one speaker the weavers' rule cannot touch. If it is the latter, tell me what that implies about my boardroom, where nobody present is a child.

3. The tale does not end with the shout: the cry spreads through the crowd, the Emperor is uncomfortably aware that they are right, and he nonetheless marches the procession to its end rather than admit he has been taken in. Take a position on what that continuation says about the relation between common knowledge and coordinated action — whether it is the sharpest thing in the story, and whether it undercuts the lesson I have been drawing from it.

4. Tell me where the formal apparatus misleads if I press it onto the tale, and where the tale does work the apparatus cannot: whether a story about clothes, shame and office can teach anything about knowledge in groups that a muddy-children style puzzle cannot.

Please keep it under 900 words, and put your bottom line on point 1 in the opening sentences — I want to know straight away whether the folk reading of the tale is right. Do not hedge the remainder: if you think my analogy to the boardroom is only half right, or actively misleading, say so and say why.
