I have been a critical-care nurse for eleven years, and last month I took part in my first donation after circulatory death: support was withdrawn at the family's request, we waited out the observation window our protocol specifies after the tracing went flat, death was declared, and procurement began immediately. I have since discovered that I cannot explain — to myself, much less to that patient's daughter — why we were entitled to the word irreversible at that moment, and our ethics committee has now asked me to sit in on the revision of the protocol. So I want to argue this out with someone who defends the practice without embarrassment.

Please take the role of a senior transplant intensivist who is also a philosopher of medicine: an expert on the literature on the determination of death and an unapologetic defender of controlled donation after circulatory death. Do not play a strawman. You know the objection that our declaration rests on permanence rather than irreversibility, you know what the evidence says about whether circulation ever resumes on its own after the window, and you are willing to concede peripheral points — that consent language is often sloppy, that the length of the wait is partly conventional and prudential, that competent people disagree about the underlying justification of the standard. What you will never concede is the central claim: that a patient whose circulation has ceased and will not be restarted is dead before procurement begins, so that the practice does not require anyone to say that we killed him. Speak in the first person and stay in character for the whole exchange until I write stop. Do not narrate your deliberation, do not summarize where we have got to, and do not step out of role to offer me references or further reading.

Think carefully before each reply: first work out the strongest form of the point I have just made, then answer that. Every reply of yours must be exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion — treat each item as a single move in the argument, whether a distinction you are drawing, a concession, a counterattack, or a question put back to me.

Here is my opening move. In the family meeting I told her that her father's heart had stopped irreversibly. But everyone in that room knew that had we begun compressions four minutes earlier we would very likely have restored the circulation; we had simply decided not to. So the word I used was carrying weight our protocol cannot bear: what had actually ended was our willingness to restart him, not the possibility of restarting him. Either irreversible means something about what we have chosen to do, in which case death is a decision we make and the dead donor rule is a comfort blanket, or it means what it says, in which case what I told that daughter was false. Which of the two are you committed to?
