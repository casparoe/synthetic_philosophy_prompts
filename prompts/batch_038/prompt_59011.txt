I'm a software engineer on the platform team at a company that administers self-funded health plans for employers. Part of my job is turning the rulings of our clinical review board into the classification logic our system applies, so I sit in on its meetings and keep a spreadsheet of its decisions. The board's job includes deciding whether something a member presents with counts as a medical condition under the plan, as opposed to normal human variation or a lifestyle concern; that classification then determines coverage, sick-leave eligibility, and what gets written into the member's record. I have no medical or philosophy training, and I've come to suspect that the language in the board's notes sounds consistent while the underlying rule is not. I'd like your help reverse-engineering it.

Here are its rulings so far, with the reasoning it gave in each note:

1. Newly diagnosed hypothyroidism, symptoms present, labs clearly abnormal: condition; treatment covered.
2. Persistently high LDL cholesterol in a member with no symptoms whatsoever, who wrote in objecting to being labeled sick: condition; treatment covered, and the note said that whether the member minds the label is not the test.
3. Months of fatigue, word-finding difficulty, and diffuse aching after a respiratory infection, with every lab value inside the reference range and no mechanism identified: condition; further workup and six weeks of paid leave approved.
4. A member who naturally falls asleep around 3 a.m., sleeps seven and a half hours, and functions well on that schedule, but is chronically impaired on a 6 a.m. shift: condition; light therapy approved and the board recommended the employer adjust the shift.
5. An endurance athlete with a resting heart rate of 38, surfaced by wearable data, no symptoms: not a condition; the note said the value is what training is supposed to produce.
6. A member found incidentally to be missing the palmaris longus muscle in one forearm — a variant a substantial minority of people have — with no measurable loss of grip strength or range of motion: not a condition; normal variation.
7. A member of West African ancestry whose neutrophil count has run below the lab's reference range for years, with no history of unusual or severe infections: not a condition; the board wrote that the reference range was derived from a population this member is not in, and that applying it to her was the error.
8. A member found to have complete situs inversus, confirmed to have no ciliary or cardiac abnormality and to be entirely healthy: not a condition — but the board directed that it be recorded as a flag that must surface before any imaging or surgery.

The new case goes to the board next week. A member in their thirties has isolated congenital anosmia: no sense of smell, confirmed on testing, present since birth, with no other sensory or neurological deficit and no underlying cause found. They adapted long ago, cook electric, keep detectors in every room, and report never having been injured or ill because of it. They are not asking us to pay for anything. They came to us because the flag on their record is now being used against them in an underwriting decision on a separate product, and they want the anosmia reclassified as normal variation, arguing that a capacity they never had, never missed, and have built a functioning life without is not a deficit.

I need to anticipate the ruling and, more usefully, understand what rule produced it. Please answer with exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, and no introduction or conclusion:

1. Your prediction of the board's ruling on the new case, with a rough confidence.
2. The single decision rule that best fits all eight precedents, stated precisely enough that I could implement it as a test.
3. The strongest rival rule that fits the same eight precedents but comes out the other way on the new case, and which of the precedents are doing the work of keeping it alive.
4. One further hypothetical case, described in a sentence, that would separate those two rules most sharply if it came before the board.
5. What I should put in the classification table for now, given that the board has not yet spoken and the two rules disagree.

If there are established names in the literature for the positions the board could be taking, name them in passing, but explain them in plain terms — I will not recognize the jargon, and I care about the decision procedure rather than the labels.
