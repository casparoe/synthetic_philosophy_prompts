I am preparing the framing section of a three-year project on bounded doxastic states. My collaborators are a formal epistemologist and an engineer who builds resource-limited reasoning systems; the plan is that students and postdocs will propose formalisms over the next two years, and I want a written specification they will be measured against before anyone proposes anything. So what I need is not a survey of the responses to logical omniscience — I have those on the shelf — but the list of requirements that any proposed framework must meet to count as *solving* the problem rather than relocating it, stated so that my engineer colleague can tell of a given formalism, on inspection, whether it clears each item.

Please give me that list. Numbered desiderata, each with (a) the case or consideration that motivates it and (b) a test: what the framework must be able to say, or represent, or derive, if it satisfies the desideratum. A label alone — "handles the preface," "respects granularity" — is worth nothing to me; the test is the point.

The following cases must appear somewhere in your list, and any others you think more probative are welcome:

1. The mathematician who accepts the axioms, accepts a lemma, and whose acceptance entails a theorem she has never entertained.
2. The agent who believes *p*, believes *p* → *q* because she found the proof convincing, and yet does not believe *q*, having never put the two together. Whether she is criticizable, and how the framework earns that verdict rather than asserting it.
3. The preface: every claim in the book is believed, and it is also believed that at least one is false.
4. The fine-grained treatments that dissolve omniscience by making contents highly structured, and the resulting worry that the same granularity forces us to read ordinary believers as harboring contradictions they would instantly disavow — the other horn.
5. Testimony: a layperson who believes everything a trusted expert says. Does she thereby believe the expert's remote consequences, and if not, why does the failure transmit?
6. Self-knowledge: an agent who is mistaken about whether she believes something that follows trivially from what she professes. Any account must explain how this ignorance is possible without making first-person authority a fiction.
7. The ten-minute case: at t₁ she does not believe *q*; at t₂, after reflection and with no new information whatsoever, she does. What, in the framework's own ontology, changed?
8. Idealization: decision theory and game theory model agents as closed under implication and as sharing common knowledge, and get away with it. A successful account should say whether it *licenses* those idealizations or merely fails to interfere with them.

Then mark each item: non-negotiable, or a settled prejudice of one camp. I suspect several of the standard demands belong in the second column — that belief must be a relation to a proposition at all, that it must be closed under conjunction, that inconsistency should be ruled out by construction rather than made criticizable, that belief reports must come out true as ordinary speakers use them — but I want your judgment, not mine, with reasons.

Most important: identify any subset of the desiderata that looks jointly unsatisfiable, and argue the incompatibility rather than waving at it. Test at least these three suspicions of mine. First, that non-omniscience, a graduated norm of rational criticism, and an account of the resources available to the agent that is fixed by the framework rather than stipulated case by case cannot coexist: if what counts as available on reflection is read off the agent's actual architecture, then every failure is a limit of capacity, and capacity limits are not the sort of thing one can be criticized for; if it is not read off the architecture, the account has simply relabeled the problem. Second, that the granularity needed to defeat omniscience, plus charity in belief attribution, plus the stability of ascriptions across short intervals of time, are mutually hostile. Third, that once closure fails, the self-referential paradoxes in the neighborhood — the knower, the surprise examination — lose their bite, and a framework that dissolves them that cheaply has bought non-omniscience at the price of an acid that eats through arguments we are sure are sound.

Tell me also which single requirement, in your view, eliminates the most candidates, and why that one does the heavy lifting.

Close with a brief assessment — a paragraph per family, no more — of how impossible-worlds semantics, awareness operators, the implicit/explicit distinction, syntactic belief bases, structured contents, and fragmented accounts fare against your list. Do not pad this section and do not cite anything; the scoring is the least interesting part of the deliverable, and I will do it in seminar.
