I am the operations manager at a mid-sized freight terminal, and fourteen months ago I signed the authorization that put an autonomous yard tractor into service on our night shift. In March, one of my stevedores was killed by it. The internal review is now closed, and this is what it found: the machine performed within its specification at every moment; the vendor's testing met the industry standard; my sign-off followed every procedure I was required to follow; and the interlock that would have stopped it had been disabled three weeks earlier by a maintenance crew who have since left the company and who never connected what they did to what followed. The investigators' conclusion, in effect, is that no one is at fault.

I have been asked to speak at his memorial service on Saturday. The room will hold his wife and two daughters, several generations of longshoremen, our general counsel three rows back, and a union representative who has been telling the press for a week that somebody has to answer for this. My difficulty is not grief — grief I can manage. It is that the honest sentence, "no one is to blame," is true and would be an obscenity in that room, while the sentence they want, naming a culprit, would be a lie. Counsel has separately asked me to say nothing that could be read as an admission of fault. I have been thinking hard about whether there is a third thing to say, and I believe there is: that accountability can outlive blame — that I can stand up and accept that this is mine to answer for and mine to repair, without confessing to a fault I did not commit and without letting the machine, the vendor, or the missing interlock absorb it in my place.

Please write that speech. It must be deliverable in about three minutes and no longer than 350 words, in plain language a room of dockworkers will respect: no jargon, no reference to philosophy or to the ethics of automation by name, because the ideas have to arrive only as things a man says about his own conduct. Give me one continuous address I can read aloud exactly as written — no headings, no stage directions, no bracketed alternatives, no notes on delivery. It has to say, in some form, that I am not going to stand there and tell them this was nobody's fault; it has to name concretely what I am taking on (the family's future, the fix, the testimony I will give, whatever you judge right); and it must leave the question of whether anyone was actually at fault visibly unsettled rather than tidily closed, because I have not closed it in my own mind and they will know if I pretend to. One further constraint: no agentless constructions — no "what happened," no "this tragedy struck." A sentence with no subject is precisely the trick I am trying not to perform, and the grammar of the speech should be the argument.
