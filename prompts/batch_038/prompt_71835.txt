I am drafting a section of a dissertation chapter on Scheffler's afterlife conjecture — the thesis, argued from the doomsday scenario in *Death and the Afterlife* (Oxford, 2013), that much of what we value depends for its value on the continued existence of humanity after our deaths. I set out to show that the reasoning behind the conjecture proves far too much. It does seem to, but the conclusion I get is that the conjecture is empty, and I am not prepared to accept that either. The argument as I now have it:

1. If, on learning that some condition will cease to hold shortly after my death, I find that things I currently value strike me as pointless, then the value of those things depends on that condition's continuing to hold.
2. Suppose I learn that I will die in my sleep tonight and that, a month later, all human life will be extinguished. Much of what I care about — the students I train, the language I write in, whether the theory I have spent twenty years on is ever confirmed, my daughter's children — strikes me as pointless in a way it does not when I imagine simply dying tonight with humanity carrying on.
3. So, by (1), the value of these things depends on humanity's continuing after my death.
4. Now substitute a grander catastrophe: I learn that I will die in my sleep tonight and that, a month later, a vacuum-decay event will alter the constants of nature so that no structure anywhere survives. My reaction is, so far as I can introspect, indistinguishable from the one described in (2).
5. So, by the same application of (1), the value of these things equally depends on the constants of nature retaining their present values after my death, and *mutatis mutandis* on the sun's continuing to burn, on gravity's continuing to hold, and on the persistence of a breathable atmosphere.
6. Every conclusion licensed by (5) is a truism: nothing anyone values survives the destruction of everything, and no one needed an argument to know it.
7. An inference form that yields only truisms when one background condition is substituted into it cannot yield a substantive discovery when a different background condition is substituted, since the premises nowhere said anything special about the first condition that they fail to say about the second.
8. Therefore (3) is a truism as well, and the doomsday scenario establishes nothing distinctive about our relation to the survival of humanity.

Two of my readers give incompatible verdicts and I cannot choose between them: one says the collapse happens at (4)–(5), because the two catastrophes are not the same kind of case; the other says (4)–(5) is perfectly sound and the argument dies at (1), which is doing quiet work in both instances. I would rather keep (1) than (7), and I would rather keep the conjecture than my objection, but I do not know what either preference costs.

So: which step or steps do you reject, and why did each look acceptable when I wrote it down? If the way out is to insist that the survival of humanity and the persistence of the constants are relevantly different sorts of background condition, state precisely what the difference consists in — I have tried contingency, susceptibility to human influence, capacity to surprise us, and the fact that future humanity consists of valuers rather than mere enabling conditions, and each of these either seems to fail outright or to generate its own version of (8). And if instead the argument is valid and the real choice is among (1), (6), and (7), tell me which is cheapest to surrender, and whether surrendering (1) takes Scheffler's original argument down with it. I am after the diagnosis and the pricing, not a recommendation about what to write in the chapter.
