I do incident investigations for a mid-size food processing company — root cause analysis, timelines, the boring forensic stuff. Two weeks ago a maintenance tech cleared a jam on a conveyor and his hand got pulled back in when it restarted. The operator at the control panel was maybe four feet away with a clear sightline and an e-stop within reach, and he did not press it. No bodily movement at all, we're pretty confident about that now. His supervisor said it in the review meeting at least six times: "he didn't do anything," and he said it like it settled the question of whether we're allowed to put the operator in the causal chain on the report. Which is awkward for me, because our template has a field that literally reads "describe the act that caused the injury," and honestly half of everything I ever write up is of the form "so-and-so failed to do Y." The operator, for what it's worth, claims he never saw the tech reach in — there's a chirping alarm in that room and a forklift running past — so there's a separate fight about whether he could have seen it.

Can you lay out for me the different precise claims "he didn't do anything" could be making? I want the whole menu, from the ones that are just trivially true (no bodily movement, fine, whatever) down to the ones doing real metaphysical work — something like: nothing happened, and if nothing happened there's no event for a causal relation to take as a relatum; or absences aren't in the world, so there's nothing there to be a cause; or the scarier one, where if not-doing counted as causing then everybody on the planet caused this injury because none of them pressed that button either. And don't skip the ones about agency and awareness rather than metaphysics — the reading where you can only fail to do something you were in a position to do, which is exactly where my operator's "I never saw him" defense lives. Number them, and for each say whether it's true, true-but-empty, defensible-but-contested, or just false. Then the bit I actually need: which of those readings, if it were right, would let my supervisor keep the operator out of the causal section of the report, and which ones he's probably reaching for without knowing it.

Two more things I keep circling. First, our own tooling seems to have picked a side already: fault trees and bowtie diagrams let me draw a box that says "operator fails to initiate stop" and hang a probability off it, and the whole swiss-cheese picture we're trained on is basically an accident caused by holes — by absences that line up. Is that just enginers being metaphysically sloppy, or is it evidence about the slogan? Second, someone in a philosophy class I audited last year said causation claims are secretly incomplete without a contrast — that "he didn't press the stop" is only a cause relative to some alternative you've got in mind, and that picking the wrong contrast is what makes omissions look like they explode into everybody's failure to act. I couldnt tell whether that rescues me or buries me, because if the contrast is what matters then maybe the question was never whether he did anything. Tell me where that lands for my report, and be blunt about which of the readings is a dead end.
