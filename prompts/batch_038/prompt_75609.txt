For the last decade or so I've defended a pretty uncompromising version of disjunctivism about perception: when you veridically see a lemon on the table and when you undergo a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination of one, there is no fundamental kind of mental episode that you have in both cases — the highest common factor picture is not merely false but unmotivated, and the arguement from illusion only gets traction by assuming what it needs to establish. I'm drafting a chapter for a volume where the editor has asked, reasonably enough, for a section on what would count against my own view, and I've discovered that I can't write it, because for everything I can think of I already have a story. Which is itself the worrying part, I realize.

So tell me straight where this position is actually exposed. Please skip the textbook argument from illusion — I know the version with the bent stick in water and the afterimages, and I know my reply that illusion cases don't generalize to causally matched hallucinations. I want the harder stuff. (i) The mixed cases: someone sees a real table and hallucinates a lemon sitting on it, or a hallucinated hue is spread over a genuinely seen shape. What does relationalism say here, and is the available answer strained enough to count as a real defeater rather than an embarrassment? (ii) The screening-off worry: if there's a proximal neural cause common to the good and the bad case that fully accounts for why they're indistinguishable to the subject, why isn't that common cause doing all the phenomonological work? And is there a principled reason to deny it, or does "same vehicle, different metaphysical kind of episode" turn into a get-out-of-jail card I could fire back at any empirical result — TMS phosphenes, the visual-cortex activity reported in Charles Bonnet hallucinations, the imaging overlap between mental imagery and perception? (iii) How much weight is my view leaning on Martin's negative epistemic account of indistinguishability — the hallucinator simply cannot tell by introspection that things aren't as they seem — and what does that cost me about self-knowledge, about whether anyone can ever know they are hallucinating, and about whether a hallucinator's reports can serve as evidence for anything? (iv) Is there a live risk that the position collapses into a rival? If a representationalist can grant me everything I say about the good case being a distinct kind of relation to the world, and I can grant them everything they say about what happens in the head, is the dispute just about labels — and would that be a defeat for me rather than a truce? (v) The payoff question: the epistemic motivation is that in the good case my warrant doesn't depend on ruling out the bad case. If that can be had on its own, without the metaphysics — by someone who keeps factive seeing but stays neutral about the nature of experience — then my metaphysical claim is doing no work, and I'd like to know whether that counts against it or is simply beside the point.

Rank all of that for me: fatal, serious but survivable, merely awkward. And be explicit about which ones a committed disjunctivist ought to concede as genuinely counting against the view, as opposed to explaining away — I don't want a defense brief, I want the worst case from someone who knows the territory. Then answer the meta-question honestly: does disjunctivism risk anything at all, or has it been qualified into immunity, and if it is insulated, is that a defect for a thesis about the fundamental kinds of perceptual episode or a perfectly legitimate feature of it? Relatedly, what does my view predict that its rivals don't, such that some conceivable finding would actually distinguish them? For what it's worth I suspect the mixed cases and the collapse worry are where I'm most vulnerable, and I'd much rather hear that from you now than in front of the seminar — about half of which are vision scientists who already think its all talk about nothing.
