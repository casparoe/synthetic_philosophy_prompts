Write a satirical internal memorandum of approximately 800 words from the Group Actuary of a specialty reinsurer to its Risk Committee, recommending a position on a newly submitted risk. The instrument, which should be given an invented internal codename, has the following payoff schedule. A fair coin is tossed until the first head appears. If the first head falls on toss n, the structure pays +$2^n/n when n is odd and −$2^n/n when n is even; the probability that the first head falls on toss n is 2^−n. The first six branches therefore pay +$2, −$2, +$2.67, −$4, +$6.40, −$10.67 at probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64. Neither the paradox this structure instantiates nor any philosopher, paper, or body of literature may be named anywhere in the piece.

Open with a realistic memo header (To / From / Date / Subject / Classification) and organise the body into five to seven numbered sections under dry procedural headings, in the voice of someone who writes nothing but documents of this kind. Include a short table of the first five or six branches with their probabilities and payoffs, and one boxed sidebar — a model-risk note, or an FAQ for brokers — set apart typographically.

The humour must come entirely from getting the mathematics and the decision theory right, so that a reader who knows the material laughs rather than winces; every quantitative claim the memo makes must be correct. The document should turn on the following features, which you are to dramatise rather than explain:

- Each branch contributes ±1/n to the expected value, so that summing the branches in ascending order of n produces the alternating harmonic series and a "best estimate" of ln 2 ≈ $0.69. Report this figure with the spurious precision of an actual reserve review, and let the reader notice the absurdity against the tails: the probability that the first head falls on toss 30 is about one in a billion, and the loss in that branch is about $35.8 million.
- The positive branches alone diverge and the negative branches alone diverge, so the expectation is not well defined; the $0.69 is an artefact of the order in which the branches happen to have been listed. Somebody in the document should observe, without alarm, that the group's reserve for this risk is a consequence of the spreadsheet's default sort order.
- By the Riemann rearrangement theorem the terms can be re-sequenced to converge to any number whatsoever. Escalate this into an institutional practice: a standing function created to select whichever enumeration of the state space yields a reserve consistent with the group's stated risk appetite; its relationship to model-risk governance; its treatment in the stress test; the broker's difficulty in explaining it to a client. Keep this entirely deadpan — no character should describe it as fraudulent, or as clever.
- The usual escape routes should appear as options the firm genuinely weighs and disposes of for operational rather than philosophical reasons: truncating the schedule at a finite toss, with the truncation point set by a committee and the observation that moving it from 60 to 61 shifts the reserve by roughly 1.6 cents, some 2.4 per cent of the best estimate; substituting a bounded utility function, rejected because the offering document promises dollars; retroceding the tail to an entity that has not read the schedule; and the possibility that the correct treatment is that expected-value reasoning delivers no verdict here at all.
- Include at least one extended quotation, in full sentences, from the external auditor's management letter or from the group's Chief Risk Officer, whose objection is technically exact and whose proposed remedy is not.

The target of the satire is a practice and a style of discourse — the reduction of an unbounded gamble to a single governance-approved number, and the corporate vocabulary in which that reduction is justified — and not any person; all firms, people, and products must be invented. The register should stay flat from header to signature block, with no authorial winking and no sentence in which a joke is explained. A reader who has never encountered this paradox should be able to follow the memorandum from the schedule and arithmetic supplied inside it, and should come away feeling the real difficulty: that a decision theory built on expected values may simply have nothing to say about this risk, and that the firm's documents have no room in which to say so. Close with a recommendation that follows the memorandum's own logic consistently to a conclusion the reader recoils from.

Do not include footnotes, citations, or a bibliography.
