Second-year PhD student here, philosophy of time, and next week I'm presenting a chapter on Gödel's cosmology to our reading group. The arguement I expect to get — and the one I most want rehearsed against — comes from the physicists on the committee, who think the whole thing is a mathematical curiosity with no philosophical payload. So I'd like to spar with the man himself. Play Kurt Gödel in the spring of 1949, shortly after your rotating-universe paper went into the Einstein volume. Ground rules: stay in character for the whole exchange, no stepping out for asides; use only the concepts and results available to you then plus whatever I tell you; and if you need something from me — an observation, a theorem proved after 1949, what some later author claimed — ask for it in character in a clause, then answer anyway on your own resources. You're a Platonist about mathematical objects and an idealist about the flow of time, you have no patience for vagueness, and you have real material to work with: the disappearance of absolute simultaneity in special relativity, your insistence that an objective lapse of time would require an objective present plus a succession of presents that the geometry does not supply, and your view that a merely possible solution can reveal what a concept requires of reality. Don't play yourself as a crank — you know perfectly well your model isn't the actual universe.

So you dont have to guess what I'm assuming: your solution is stationary — homogeneous pressureless matter rotating rigidly, held up against collapse by a negative cosmological constant, no expansion at all — and closed timelike curves thread it everywhere, so a traveller with enough fuel can loop around and arrive before he set out, and meet himself coming the other way. You also grant that the real universe is expanding, which yours is not.

Here is my opening move. You already had everything you wanted from special relativity: no preferred foliation, no observer-independent now, hence no objective lapse. The rotating universe seems to add only that in one possible spacetime you could revisit your own past. But if you concede it isn't ours, haven't you established something about the solution set of a system of differential equations rather than about time? Compare: I can write down a world in which signals have no maximum speed, and you wouldn't take that to tell you anything about the causal structure of ours. So what work is the rotating universe doing in your argument that the relativity of simultaneity wasn't already doing — and why is the observed expansion of the observed universe irrelevant to that work? Give it to me straight: I want your best committed defence, argued for, not a menu of how one might reply, and I want you to close your first answer by naming the premise in it that you're least sure of. Keep every reply to a single paragraph, 150 words maximum, no bullet points.
