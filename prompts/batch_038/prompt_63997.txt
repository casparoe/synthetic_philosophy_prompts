I'm writing the methodology chapter of a book and the whole thing hangs on a claim I can't yet defend properly: that the gaps in the record mean something, that who got written down and who didn't is itself a fact about power that I'm allowed to draw conclusions from. Every time I say this to a sceptical reader they hear "you're finding significance in missing paperwork," and some days I'm not sure they're wrong. So before I commit to a position I want to know what I should be asking of one.

Write the desiderata: what any theory of archival silence — an account of which absences license which conclusions — has to deliver to be usable in actual historical work. For each requirement give the case or the consideration behind it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are really just one camp's preferences dressed up as demands on the whole field.

Cases it has to get right, at minimum:

- a group the state documented obsessively — police files, registers, case notes — where the abundance of paperwork is part of the silencing rather than a cure for it. The theory can't just say sources are biased.
- a gap that means nothing at all: routine records destroyed on a standing retention schedule, a fire, a war, paper that didn't survive the climate. It has to be able to say no here, and say it without already knowing the answer.
- records closed to protect the people in them — privacy rules, duty-of-care policies, living individuals — so that now the vulnerable are the ones you can't see while a well-connected donor's papers sit wide open. This case breaks accounts written before it was common.
- the silence produced by description rather than by absence: the file is in the box, but it's catalogued under the institution's own vocabulary for the people in it, so nobody searching for them ever finds it.
- the same holdings, digitised or not: what's searchable isn't the same as what exists, and funding decided the difference.
- a record the absent people made later themselves — oral history, memoir, decades on. Does that fill the silence or just move it?

Also: which distinctions it mustn't collapse (never-created vs. created-and-destroyed vs. created-and-withheld vs. created-and-unfindable), what it owes to hostile readers — can two trained historians run it on the same gap and get the same answer — and where it has to stop and hand the question back to me because it's about values rather than evidence.

Flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied. I suspect the demand that a theory be able to declare some gaps meaningless and the demand that it treat silences as patterned productions of power are in real tension, and if the list turns out jointly unsatisfiable then thats the finding, so tell me. And tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking out candidates.

Then briefly — two or three sentences each, no more, I don't need a bibliography — how the three accounts I keep running into do against the list: the one that treats the archive as an effect of the power relations that made it, the one that treats the absence as license to imaginatively reconstruct what an undocumented life was probably like, and the plain evidentialist one that says a gap is only ever a gap until something independent fills it.
