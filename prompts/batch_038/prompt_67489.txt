I chair the Public Grounds and Assembly Committee of a county hospital system, which grants, conditions, or refuses permits for demonstrations on our plazas and walkways under a policy requiring that any restriction be justified by harm to patients and staff rather than by disagreement with the message. An applicant seeks a two-week, round-the-clock vigil on the covered plaza directly outside our main emergency entrance, protesting the system's practice of referring unpaid patients to collections. The organizers pledge to keep the ambulance lane clear, but they also intend to read aloud over a bullhorn the first names and street names of patients whose accounts were referred, and to display panels listing those names. Our clinical directors report two harms: stretcher and wheelchair transfer through the covered plaza is already tight at shift changes, and several identifiable patients in the collections program have been recognized and photographed entering the building. The committee is split three ways — refuse the permit outright, grant it unconditionally, or grant it with conditions (relocation to the lawn forty metres away, no amplified reading of names, no name panels visible from the clinic entrance). Draft our decision memo: commit to a single disposition and give the reasoning for it, engage the strongest consideration available to the faction whose position you reject, and explain where you would draw Mill's line in On Liberty between protected expression and conduct that harms others in a case of this shape, including whether restricting the naming of patients differs in kind from restricting the message itself.
