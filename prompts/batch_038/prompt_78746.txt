Run the WHO's smallpox eradication campaign — intensified program from 1967, last natural case in 1977, certified gone in 1980 — as a longtermist case study rather than a public health one. I don't want the epidemiology. I want the mapping: on basically any discount rate this looks like a colossal win, hundreds of thousands of deaths averted every year forever, so what did the people who funded and ran it think they were buying at the time, how much of that longtermist justification was available to them versus only visible in hindsight, and where does the view start doing work retrospectively that it couldn't have done prospectively? Then the wrinkle, which is what I actually care about. Eradication is everyone's go-to example of a one-shot permanent intervention, but the virus still sits in two official repositories, the resolution to destroy those stocks has been kicked down the road for decades, and rebuilding it from sequence isnt science fiction anymore. I've taken to splitting benefits into terminal ones, which just persist, and custodial ones, which persist only for as long as somebody keeps paying to keep them. Does longtermist EV reasoning have the resources to tell those apart, or does it quietly assume everything worth doing is terminal? Bottom line: does smallpox confirm the view, get misdescribed by it, or fall into a category it never anticipated?
