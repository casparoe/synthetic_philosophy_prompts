Our philosophy-of-mind reading group runs a public interview series each term: forty-five minutes on stage in front of an audience of graduate students and a few faculty, recorded and posted afterward as a podcast. This term's guest is a philosopher of psychology whose recent work attacks a piece of orthodoxy about the color phi phenomenon. As I understand her position, the phenomenon is this: when two dots of different colors are flashed in quick succession at nearby locations, subjects report a single dot traveling between them and changing color partway along the path, even though the information carried by the second dot cannot be available until after the midpoint of the reported trajectory would already have been experienced. The standard treatment, going back to Dennett and Kinsbourne, sorts the possible explanations into Orwellian ones, on which the wrong content reaches consciousness and is subsequently written out of the record, memory included, and Stalinesque ones, on which a mechanism stops the show before the wrong content ever reaches consciousness, so that only the settled content is experienced. It is then argued that the two are indistinguishable by anything a subject can report, and that this undercuts the idea that there is a determinate time at which an experience occurs, or a determinate place where processing becomes experience. My guest denies the indistinguishability premise. She holds that on the Orwellian account the first content really is experienced and therefore really is broadcast through the system, and that broadcasting leaves consequences — in priming, in autonomic response, in the programming of eye movements — which a revision of the experiential record need not and could not sweep away, whereas on her own view no such residue of a superseded first experience should exist. She is consequently unembarrassed about saying that experiences have determinate onset times, and that color phi is a case in which the onset of the relevant experience is later than the stimulus.

Please draft me seven or eight questions, sequenced so that the conversation tightens as it goes: start somewhere she can answer comfortably and finish on what result she would treat as counting against her. I have read only the introduction to her paper, so each question should be self-contained enough that I can put it without additional setup. For every question, add one line on the answer she is most likely to give and one line on the follow-up that answer licenses — I do not want paragraphs of stage direction. Nothing of the "what first drew you to this topic" variety, and please do not append references or suggested further reading; I will not have time to chase them and they are not what I need here. She has said in advance that she wants to be treated as making a testable claim, so at least one question should be about what the experiment would actually look like.

At a minimum, the sequence should reach the following pressure points. First, whether anything her proposed measures would detect is a trace of an experience as opposed to a trace of mere early processing of the first dot, which her opponent will say occurs on both accounts regardless. Second, the single-dot control: if the mechanism that withholds the first experience is waiting for information about what comes next, what fixes how long it waits, and what does her account say happens when nothing comes next. Third, whether her determinate onset times reinstate exactly the finish line that the regress objection against a Cartesian theater was meant to rule out, and what she says to that objection. Fourth, how her view differs from the backward-referral hypothesis, on which an experience is dated to the stimulus even though the cortical activity required for it takes several hundred milliseconds longer, and whether the reasons she has for rejecting that hypothesis cut equally against her own delayed onset. Fifth, generalization: if her account posits a uniform latency in the onset of visual experience, how she handles the fact that observers can launch an eye movement toward a newly flashed target remarkably fast — fast enough, on some estimates, to leave no room for the delay she needs — and whether the dissociation between action guidance and conscious content that she will require is independently motivated or bought to save the theory. Finally, if you think I have mis-stated her position anywhere above, say so before giving me the questions and adjust them accordingly.
