Help me get straight on something about the leveling-down objection. Parfit's point is that a monist egalitarian, valuing equality for its own sake, has to admit that a world where everyone is equally badly off is in one way better than a world with big gaps and higher levels — even though nobody's better off in the flattened one. Prioritarians say they dodge this because their value function just applies a concave weight to each person's absolute level and sums, so flattening by making the well-off worse of strictly lowers the total.

Fine. But here's what bugs me: in any situation where a transfer is actually on the table, concavity still tells the prioritarian to shift things toward the worse-off, since that raises the weighted sum, and the egalitarian can say the same for their own reasons. So do these two views ever disagree about anything anyone could bring about? If the only place they part company is worlds where the gap can't be closed, is the leveling-down objection a real objection or just a complaint about the aesthetics of a value function?

Explain (a) what would actually have to be wrong with a theory whose disagreements with its rival never touch on action, and (b) whether there's a case where a prioritarian and a monist egalitarian rank two genuinely reachable outcomes differently — and if there is, name the feature of the case doing the work. Then tell me whether the reply that leveling down is better in one respect, though not better all things considered, gets the egalitarian off the hook.
