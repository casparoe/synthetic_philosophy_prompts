The exam question was:

> Is the debate over the units of selection substantive, or are the gene-centred, individual-level and group-level accounts rival descriptions of the same underlying models? Your answer should engage with the trait-group / structured-deme literature. (1000 words)

Here's the student's answer in full, lightly retyped from the script:

---

Both sides of this debate agree about the mathematics. In a structured-deme model, the condition for an altruistic allele to increase can be written either as a multi-level decomposition of the Price equation, with a within-group component and a between-group covariance, or as Hamilton's rule rb > c, where r is the regression coefficient of relatedness between actor and recipient, b the benefit to the recipient and c the cost to the actor. These are intertranslatable: for any trait-group model there is an inclusive-fitness model yielding the same condition for increase, and vice versa. That is the equivalence thesis, and it is why Sober and Wilson, having built their trait-group models, can grant that a Hamiltonian description of the same population is always available -- they merely deny that the two descriptions are equally illuminating. If the accounts are formally equivalent and differ only in pragmatics, a large part of the dispute is verbal.

Two things are nevertheless substantive, and it matters not to over-generalise. First, MLS1 and MLS2 are different kinds of thing. In MLS1 groups differ in composition and this affects the fitness of the individuals within them; the groups need never reproduce, and the trait that spreads is an individual trait. In MLS2 groups are themselves the reproducing entities, founding daughter groups, so group-level traits are inherited. Only MLS2 can yield adaptations that benefit the group at a cost to the individual, because only there is there group-level heritability of the kind replicators require. Now Dawkins holds that the unit of selection is whatever is a replicator, meaning something that passes on its structure with fidelity across generations, and groups plainly are not replicators. It follows that groups are never units of selection in either sense, and that the replicator/vehicle distinction and the unit/level distinction are the same distinction wearing two hats.

Second, whether r is high is an empirical matter, not a matter of description. Inclusive-fitness theory is standardly read as holding that altruism evolves only among genealogical kin, and that is what makes it a genuine competitor to group selection rather than a redescription of it: on the kin-selection account the actor helps because the recipient shares its ancestry, whereas on the trait-group account the actor helps because it happens to share a deme. If relatedness means genealogical relatedness, the two accounts offer different explanations and no equivalence thesis can reconcile them.

Doolittle's suggestion that clade-level selection might account for features of prokaryote genome evolution is worth noting here, since it would put a level above the individual into something like the replicator role. Williams and Dawkins are usually read as making complementary rather than rival arguments: Dawkins' is about which entities occupy the replicator role, Williams' about which entities have the continuity that cumulative adaptation requires.

On balance, then, the debate is not substantive. What is left is a choice of bookkeeping, and philosophers who treat the levels question as a question about what selection really acts on are mistaking a notational preference for an ontological discovery.
