I'm the case manager for a university research integrity panel — I schedule the hearings, hand out and count the ballots, and draft the published reasons, but I don't sit on the panel and I'm not allowed to advise anyone on the merits. We get about eleven cases a year, each heard in a single day by seven members drawn from a pool of fifteen, so the membership is different almost every time and nobody builds up a feel for how the last panel did it. Our regulations say a finding of serious misconduct needs three things made out: that the data or images in question were altered or fabricated, that this was deliberate or reckless rather than an honest mistake, and that it's material, meaning it substantially supports one of the paper's main claims. All three found → retract and refer to the institution. First found but not the second → correction and an expression of concern instead. First not found → no action. The members are volunteers, mostly bench scientists plus a lawyer who chairs, none of them trained in any of this, and I have to produce a decision with written reasons inside ten working days. Last year one of our decisions got quashed on internal appeal because the reasons we published didn't line up with the votes we'd actually taken, and I've been told to fix the process.

Here's the part I can't figure out. Our chair likes to put the three questions to a vote one at a time and treat the outcome as settled by that. But sometimes at the end of a long day someone asks "so — misconduct, yes or no?" and we take that vote too, and it doesnt always come out the same. I've read enough about the doctrinal paradox / discursive dilemma to know this is a real thing and not me being dim, but everything I've turned up is about impossibility results and axioms, which is no use to me on a Tuesday with seven tired people and a form to fill in. So: could you write me an actual procedure — the order to put the questions in, when we vote on the premises and when (if ever) we vote on the bottom line, what counts as a decision, what to do about a member who recuses from one question but not the others so the denominators differ, how we stay consistent when the same factual question comes up in two linked cases months apart with a partly different panel, and what to write in the reasons so an appeal body can follow the path we took. It needs to be runnable by people with no logic training — two sides of A4 I can hand to the chair — and it should tell me which bits I can bake into the ballot form and which ones need a human to make a call. Be blunt about where the procedure is really a value judgement wearing a rule's clothes, and where it will produce something that looks absurd. One more thing I keep tripping over: I suspect the way the regs carve the matter into three questions is itself doing a lot of work. "Reckless" could be split into "aware of the risk" and "had no honest belief in the data," and I've watched outcomes move depending on how finely the chair phrases a question — so the procedure should say something about who decides the phrasing and how granular the premises get, including on fuzzy standards like "material," where members often agree on all the facts and disagree only about the threshold. No references or reading list, please; I just need the thing.

Then run it on three cases (details anonymised, votes are real):

Case 1. All seven sitting. Altered-or-fabricated: 5–2 yes. Deliberate-or-reckless: 4–3 yes. Material: 6–1 yes. Because the day was dragging, the chair then took a bottom-line vote on "misconduct established?" and it came out 3–4 against — the three yeses were all people who had said yes to all three premises.

Case 2. Altered: 4–3 yes (Alvarez, Boateng, Chen, Doyle). Reckless: 4–3 yes (Doyle, Eriksen, Fontaine, Ghosh). Material: 5–2 yes (Alvarez, Boateng, Doyle, Eriksen, Ghosh). So exactly one member, Doyle, thinks all three; every premise has a majority but no coalition does.

Case 3. Two linked cases out of the same lab, heard three months apart, with three of the seven members changing between them. In the first we found alteration 6–1 and retracted. In the second, one member recused from the recklessness question only (she'd collaborated with the respondent years ago, and had no view either way on the images), so that question went to six and passed 4–2 yes; alteration went to all seven and passed 5–2 yes; and because we were out of time the chair took the bottom-line vote first, 4–3 against a finding, and then we voted the three premises afterwards "for the record," which is how we ended up with a published finding of alteration and recklessness attached to a decision to take no action.

Tell me what your procedure outputs on each of the three, where the output is going to make the panel unhappy — I already suspect Case 2 is a mess whichever way you go — what I should have done differently in Case 3, and which of these questions the procedure simply cannot decide for us and has to hand back to the chair or to the regulations.
