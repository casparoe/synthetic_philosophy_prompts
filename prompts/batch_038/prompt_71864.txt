I'm a fourth-year philosophy student in a seminar on causation, and my term presentation is going to turn on a set of verdicts our seminar leader has issued, more or less in passing, over the last five weeks on cases of failure to act. He has been unusually explicit about them, which is why I think his record can be treated as data rather than as mood. I want to predict what he will say about a case he has not yet seen, and I would like help working out which principles that record actually admits.

His verdicts so far. Throughout, an affirmative verdict means he is willing to say that the failure to act caused the harm — not merely that, had the act been performed, the harm would not have occurred.

1. A caretaker under contract to water a client's houseplants during her absence never goes. The plants die. — Cause.
2. The same contract and the same neglect, except that the water supply to the house had been shut off at the mains the previous day, so the plants would have died even if she had gone. — Not a cause.
3. A night nurse does not give a dose that is charted, prepared, and sitting on the tray at the bedside. The patient dies. — Cause.
4. A cleaner working the corridor outside that ward hears the bedside call alarm, is the only person within reach, and could have fetched the nurse in time. He does not. The patient dies. — Not a cause. He stressed that the counterfactuals here are exactly as clean as in 3: but for him, the patient lives.
5. A maintenance engineer under contract skips the quarterly load test on a hospital's standby generator. The mains fail, the generator does not start, a patient on a ventilator dies. — Cause.
6. An operator on shift duty does not close the valve that would have released the fire suppressant. The fire reaches the solvent store and the wing is destroyed. — Cause. He drew attention to the shape of this one: the failure stops something that would itself have stopped the fire.
7. Any of us, himself included, could have booked a flight and watered the plants in case 1. We did not. — Not a cause; and he called cases of this shape his main reason for distrusting counterfactual treatments of omission altogether.

His general orientation, in paraphrase rather than quotation. First, he thinks such treatments are absurdly permissive, since they make large parts of the world a cause of every death. Second, he says he is uneasy about any account that makes causal claims turn on what people owe one another, because he wants causation to be a worldly relation rather than a normative one — and he concedes that he has no satisfying way of holding these two commitments together.

The new case, which I constructed for the presentation and which he has not seen. A visiting researcher from another university is working late in a building that also houses our animal facility. The facility's automatic watering line has tripped an internal fault and stopped delivering water. The fault indicator is lit in the corridor, and directly beside it is a labelled reset button requiring no key, no training, and no risk. He is the only person in the building. He has no contract with us, no role, no assignment, no prior relationship with the animals or their keepers, and, as our seminar leader would put it, no obligation of any kind, institutional or otherwise, to press anything. He notices the light and keeps walking to catch his train. The animals die of dehydration overnight. The counterfactuals are clean in the way he insists on: had he pressed the button, the line would have restarted and the animals would have survived, and nothing else in the building could have done it.

What I need from you:

(i) A prediction of his verdict, with a rough degree of confidence, and the single consideration that would most move you in either direction.

(ii) The candidate principles his record admits. I can see at least four: a rule keyed to duty, role, or undertaking; a rule keyed to whether there is a process or device already in place that the omitted act would have kept running or released; a counterfactual rule restricted by proportionality or by the specificity of the absence in question; and an eliminativist rule on which none of his affirmative verdicts is literally true but all of them track which absence is salient to an inquirer. Set these out properly, add any others you think fit, state what each implies about the new case, and say for each which of 1–7 it explains effortlessly and which it has to bend.

(iii) Which candidate you would bet on, and why. I am less interested in which principle is merely consistent with the record than in which best explains its pattern — in particular why 4 and 7 come out negative while 1, 3, 5 and 6 come out affirmative — and in how it handles the tension between his two stated commitments.

(iv) The single further case that would most sharply separate your two leading candidates. Describe it in enough detail that its causal and normative features are unambiguous, and state what each candidate predicts about it. If you can, choose a case that does not simply re-run the feature that divides them in the new one.

(v) Suppose he denies causation in the new case. What does he then owe us about the rest of it? Is he committed to saying that the visitor did nothing wrong, or to saying that the visitor's inaction plays no part in explaining the deaths? Sketch how he could deny the causal claim while keeping the normative and the explanatory ones, and say what that move costs him.

(vi) Flag any place where the record itself is unstable: any pair of verdicts that no single one of your candidates handles comfortably, and what he would have to give up in order to keep both.

Please organise the answer under those headings and keep it to roughly a thousand words. I am not after references or attributions; where a candidate corresponds to a recognised position in the literature, label the position rather than the paper.
