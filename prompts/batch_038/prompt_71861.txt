I run a weekly rationality meetup in a library back room, going on four years now — mostly LessWrong/SSC types, usually 12 to 16 people on a Thursday, 90 minutes. Very mixed room: two of them have taken real grad micro, most have seen a payoff matrix exactly once in their lives, and one guy has read every sequence and is quite sure he already knows how the evening ends.

Next session is iterated elimination of dominated strategies and I don't want to lecture it. I want them to play something and walk into the problem on their own. The specific thing I want at least one of them to blurt out unprompted is the mixed-strategy case: a strategy that survives every round of "obviously this one's bad, kill it" reasoning when you only compare it against the other pure strategies, and then turns out to be strictly dominated by a randomization between two of those pure strategies, neither of which beats it on its own. The *wait, I was supposed to be flipping a coin this whole time and my safe middle option was just strictly worse* moment. That's the entire point of the evening, so please build backwards from it.

What I need:

- The actual game or games, small enough to go on the whiteboard and to hand out on one sheet. Give me the real numbers and tell me what the numbers are doing — why that particular strategy is immune to pure-strategy dominance but not to a mixture, and why nobody should be able to see that in round one.
- A payoff scheme in points that doesn't involve real money. Points have to matter enough that people play to win instead of playing to be intresting. Right now I do "most points picks next week's topic" plus a chocolate bar for the top table, which is fine if you want to build on it.
- Round structure: how many times they play, whether pairs get reshuffled, whether each player sees the whole matrix or only their own side, how long per round. It has to be genuinely iterated — they need several separate chances to eliminate something and notice the game is shrinking.
- A move where different tables are quietly handed different elimination orders and then discover they land on the same surviving set anyway. I want them to notice order doesn't matter here. Then I want a second, very small contrast game where it does matter, because what's being knocked out is only weakly dominated. That contrast is the second thing I want them to feel and it needs to fit in the time left over.
- The debrief: which moments in play map onto which piece of the idea, the order I'd bring them up in, and where I can just shut up and let them do the work.
- What predictably goes wrong the first time you run this in a room that includes total beginners, and what to do about it. The one I'm already worried about: randomizing is noisy, and if the person who plays the mixture gets unlucky across a handful of rounds the lesson inverts and everyone concludes the mixture was the dumb play. How do I stop that without running 40 rounds?

One thing I want you to check rather than take my word for. A guy in the group claimed last month that in a two-player game "strictly dominated by some mixture" and "never a best response to anything the opponent could do" are the same claim, but that the two come apart once you get to three or more players — that with three players you can have a strategy that's never a best response and still isn't dominated by any mixture. I nodded along and I'm maybe 70% sure he's right. Tell me whether he is and where the two-player argument stops working. Then tell me honestly whether that's stageable at all with 15 people and 90 minutes, or whether it belongs in a two-minute aside at the end that I shouldn't pretend is an activity.

And be straight with me about what the room can't get from playing. My suspicion is there's some part of mixed-strategy dominance — something about how many mixtures are out there to check, or about whether they'll ever actually locate the right one rather than stumble onto it — that no amount of card-play reproduces and that I'll have to supply by hand at the whiteboard. Say what that part is and what I say about it.
