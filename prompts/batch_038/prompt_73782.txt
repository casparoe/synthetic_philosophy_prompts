drafting something short for my epistemology workshop and I want a read on the argument, not copyediting. Here's the draft:

> **Knowledge as credit.** The extra value of knowledge over mere true belief has been hunted in the wrong place — in the believer's own success rate — when it actually lives in the believer's standing as a source for others.
>
> The road-to-Larissa case in the Meno looks like it shows knowledge is worth more because it's stable: true belief gets talked out of you, knowledge doesn't. But stability, put that way, is just a probabilistic claim about persistence, and if the true believer happens not to get talked out of it, the two states deliver exactly the same arrival. Whatever stability adds is the value of a higher expected success rate — already priced in once you compare the states by outcomes.
>
> So move to the social setting. When you know, you don't merely have a belief that happens to be right; you have one you can license others to act on. You can assert it flat-out, take challenges to it, say how you know, and bear the cost if it turns out false. The reliably-true non-knower can't do these things without something like fraudulence. Knowledge on this picture isn't a more reliable token of the same type, it's a different social status — the difference between carrying cash and carrying a signature others will honor. The Meno problem then partly dissolves: why is knowledge better *for me* is the malformed question, because knowledge is better for the epistemic economy and individuals inherit that benefit by being creditworthy participants in it.
>
> Two doubts. First, this may just relocate the problem: creditworthiness still looks instrumental, and if it's valuable only insofar as it produces true belief downstream, the swamping trouble returns at group level. Second, maybe the Larissa case is an artifact of a single-agent frame — Socrates asks about a lone traveller because there was no epistemic division of labour handy to point at.
