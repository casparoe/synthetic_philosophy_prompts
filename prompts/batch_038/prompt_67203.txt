I am running a single 75-minute session of a joint graduate seminar shared between an international relations program and a philosophy department: twenty-four students, roughly half of whom have had no game theory beyond a first-year requirement, and two or three who have run professional tabletop exercises before and will be bored quickly. My aim is not to survey the deterrence literature but to have the students collide with two things in sequence. First, the structure of a threat whose credibility depends on your own control being partly surrendered, so that the probability of catastrophe is deliberately left to chance rather than ever chosen outright. Second, and arriving from inside the play rather than from me, the discovery that the very mechanism which makes major escalation unthinkable tends to make aggression below the threshold feel comparatively safe. They should leave seeing why a deterrent that its holder would never rationally carry out can nonetheless hold, and why its success may buy a more violent peace at lower levels rather than a calmer one.

Please design a runnable game for this session. It should involve two negotiating teams, with instructions for splitting twenty-four people so that nobody is idle; a disputed object worth asymmetric amounts to the two sides; a public schedule of die rolls in which the chance of an irreversible catastrophic outcome rises with each step taken toward the threshold; a cheap unilateral action available in any round that does not touch the die; and a written-message channel with a one-round delivery lag, so that threats arrive late and only as paraphrase. Handle one mechanic explicitly: a sealed card given to one team before play begins, revealing that a faction inside its own government will automatically escalate if that team has not escalated by a stated round — a card the other team can suspect but never verify, so that feigning a loss of control and actually losing it are indistinguishable from the outside.

Then supply: the materials list; point values and die probabilities, together with a short facilitator's appendix demonstrating that no strategy is dominant at those numbers, so I can answer the student who does the arithmetic; whether catastrophe forfeits points already accumulated, argued as a deliberate design choice rather than an afterthought; a minute-by-minute timeline; an opening briefing script that names none of the concepts above; a debrief that maps particular moments from the play onto each idea and only then gives them their names; the three or four things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each; a frank statement of which features of the real situation the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by argument; and an explicit note that the second discovery is a contested empirical hypothesis rather than anything the game proves.

Begin directly with the activity document — setting, materials, rules — with no preamble, no summary of my situation, and no comment on the design until you reach the debrief.
