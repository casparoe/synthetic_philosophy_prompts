I am drafting an argument against a charge I keep encountering: that the dispute between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness is idle bookkeeping, since the two theories agree about which objects fall under any vague predicate and about everything competent speakers would say, differing only in how they label the borderline. To meet the charge I need a case in which their respective semantic machinery yields materially different verdicts about something other than the label — specifically, about a decision that was made and then reversed. Please construct that case to the following specification.

For orientation, take epistemicism to hold that every vague sentence is true or false, that the boundaries are sharp, and that we cannot know them because knowledge demands a margin that borderline cases defeat. Take supervaluationism to hold that a borderline sentence is true on some admissible precisifications and false on others, hence neither supertrue nor superfalse; that excluded middle nonetheless survives; and that admissible precisifications must respect the penumbral connections among related predicates.

Scenario requirements. One paragraph, set in an ordinary institution administering a mundane vague standard — a licensing board, a customs classifier, a disability assessor, a research ethics committee, a municipal inspector. No heaps, no baldness, no colour patches, no sorites series of any kind. The case must contain: (i) an initial ruling that the item does fall under the standard; (ii) a later ruling by a second body, on the same record, under the same procedure, with no new evidence and no identifiable error in the first body's reasoning, that it does not; (iii) an irreversible practical consequence already attached to the first ruling; (iv) a second, higher-order vague term in the rule's own text — something of the form *clearly meets the standard* — so that each theory must say what it makes of second-order vagueness and not merely first-order; and (v) one further application before the same bodies on which both rules agreed and which is determinately inside the standard, to hold the institution's general competence fixed.

Demonstration requirements. Then supply:

- A list of six to eight sentences about the case — including the object-level application to the contested item, the determinately-inside comparator, the excluded-middle disjunction, the metalinguistic claim that the first ruling was correct, the higher-order claim about whether the item clearly met the standard, and at least one conjunction combining two of these — with each theory's verdict on each and the specific step of its machinery that produces it.
- An explicit identification of at least one sentence on which the theories agree and at least one on which they diverge, with the divergence stated in full. Do not state it by invoking gaps versus ignorance; those name the positions rather than the result.
- An account of which structural feature of the case generates the divergence, together with what happens if that feature is removed: delete the higher-order term, give the second body fresh evidence, make the consequence reversible, or drop the comparator.
- The strongest attack available to the theory that fares worse, and a judgment on whether that attack is available to it without simply restating the idleness charge I am trying to answer.
- A plain closing verdict on whether the case shows the dispute to have practical consequences or only metalinguistic ones. If only the latter, say so without hedging.

Prohibitions. No self-reference and no semantic paradox; no expert, instrument, or disclosed precisification that reveals where the boundary lies; the divergence must not be generated by facts about legal authority, deference, or precedent rather than by the semantics of vagueness; and no verdict may be stipulated — each must be derived by running the theory's own machinery over the case as described. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list.
