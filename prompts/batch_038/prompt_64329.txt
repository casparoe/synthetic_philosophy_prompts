Last night I argued until two in the morning with a friend of mine — she raises money for a conservation charity, I did my first degree in behavioural ecology — about whether expensive displays actually carry information or merely persuade us that they do. She holds a straightforwardly Zahavian line: cost is the whole story, and the only reason anyone trusts the peacock's tail, the potlatch or the diamond ring is that a liar would find it ruinous. I kept insisting that cost is doing far more work in her sentence than she notices, and that in most of the human cases we actually fought over, what convinces us is not how much was spent but how much worse the spending would have gone for someone who did not mean it. She cited Grafen's point that the cost has to fall unevenly on different types, which I thought was my argument; I invoked the ethologists' distinction between indices, which are physically impossible to fake, and conventional badges, which are cheap but policed, which she thought was a dodge. Neither of us budged. Rather than continue shouting across a kitchen table, I want to lay out my own verdicts and find out whether they amount to anything principled.

My working formulation, which I am entirely willing to see dismantled, is this: a display carries information about an underlying state in proportion to how differently its cost would have fallen on someone in a different state, and — the clause I added halfway through the argument — in proportion to how much of that cost falls precisely in those states of the world in which the display turns out to have been false. Here are the cases, with what I said about them after several glasses of wine; some I would defend to the death and others I would rather quietly withdraw. (1) A friend who takes unpaid leave and flies twenty hours economy to stand at my wedding: convincing. (2) A colleague who sends an identical condolence card through a service that mails such things on his behalf: unconvincing, though the money and the minutes are the same as for a note written by hand. (3) A founder drawing a nominal salary whilst paying his staff generously: convincing. The same founder drawing the same nominal salary whilst paying them badly: not merely unconvincing, but evidence of the opposite of what it advertises. (4) An heir who donates a million pounds: weaker evidence of generosity than a nurse who donates three thousand, because the loss would have been bearable for him and was not for her. (5) A couple who spend a ruinous sum on a large public wedding: I said this signals commitment. A couple who spend exactly the same ruinous sum on a long private honeymoon: I said this signals nothing whatever. I could not defend the pair to her and she would not let me drop it. (6) A man who, to convince his partner he will stay, tattoos her name on his face: strong, if grotesque, and strong precisely because it cannot be retracted. (7) A woman who turns down a once-in-a-career post abroad, tells no one, and stays for thirty years: I said this is a better signal than the tattoo, which she thought absurd, since by construction there is nobody positioned to receive it and even the recipient never learns what was forgone. (8) A person working three jobs to pay a sibling's medical bills: no signal at all, however enormous the cost, because there was no alternative open to them. (9) A promise secured by a forfeitable sum lodged with a third party: completely credible, and yet nothing about the utterance itself is costly — the cost is contingent on the promise being broken. (10) A stammer, a limp, an accent one cannot shed: evidence of the most reliable sort, but not, I want to say, a signal; and I cannot articulate what that difference comes to. (11) An anonymous donation that surfaces only after the donor's death: I said this is the clearest case of the lot, which sits badly with case 8 and worse with my friend's entire position, there being no audience left to convince.

What I want from you is the principle, or the shortlist of candidate principles, that best organises this mess. State each precisely — in whatever vocabulary you prefer, so long as it is unambiguous about how much information a display carries and to whom it carries it — and then work through it clause by clause, saying which of my cases forced each clause in and which it was merely accommodating. Test the survivors against two cases I did not raise: a public apology that costs the apologiser nothing at the time of utterance but forecloses any later denial; and a costly initiation ritual that every member of a community undergoes, so that it is expensive for all and differential for none. Then be honest about the casualties. My own suspicion is that cases 5 and 7 cannot both stand, since the first makes publicity essential to signalling and the second makes it irrelevant, and I would much rather be told which one to abandon than be handed an account with an epicycle bolted onto it to save both. If preserving every verdict requires something baroque, say so plainly and tell me what the leaner account costs me — and where two principles make the same verdicts on all eleven cases, propose one further case that would separate them. Please spare me citations and suggestions for further reading: I want the argument, not a bibliography.
