I'm writing the epistemics section of a design spec for a small decision-support tool used by a regional water utility, and I want the requirements nailed down before I commit to anything.

The situation the tool has to handle: after a contamination signal, three hydrogeologists hand you three numbers for the probability the well is bad — 0.05, 0.4, 0.8 — and none of them is being an idiot. The plan is to represent the utility's belief as the convex hull of there three opinions rather than averaging them into a single number, on the grounds that averaging destroys the one thing that matters here (nobody actually thinks 0.4, and they're wrong in different directions).

So: write the desiderata. What does any theory that represents a belief state as a set of probability functions have to deliver to count as adequate — not adequate for a philosophy journal, but good enough to build on? For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as either non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp, and say which camp.

Some things I already suspect belong on the list; tell me where I'm wrong:

- an updating rule, plus an account of whether the set actually narrows as data accumulates or can just sit there indefinitely (I think the bad version is called congestion — fix the label if that's wrong);
- a decision rule, because the tool has to output one recommendation and can't shrug when the interval straddles the shutdown threshold. There are cost settings where my three experts plus two different rules yield two different actions. Is "always picks something" a genuine desideratum, or a prejudice of the decision-theory camp?
- a principle tying the width of the set to the evidence, because otherwise the fully vacuous set — 0 to 1 on everything — is perfectly coherent and coherence stops doing any work at all;
- an answer about what the set is: are you committed to there being one true precise state that you're merely ignorant of, or is the set itself the belief state, and does the choice change any of the other requirements?

And then the case that actually worries me. Two of the three experts put contamination at almost exactly 0.4, and both say the new turbidity sensor will print high or low with odds of about half either way regardless of what's in the well. But they disagree completely about what a high reading would mean: one has turbidity tracking contamination, the other has high turbidity indicating the filter bed is doing its job and storm runoff has flushed things out. So the moment the sensor prints high, my interval for contamination goes from roughly [0.39, 0.41] to something like [0.1, 0.7] — I'm less sure after receiving a reading I was told in advance was a coin flip. Is that dilation, or have I mis-described the setup so that it doesn't generate it? Either way: does an adequate theory have to prevent this, explain it away, or just eat it? And if there's a trade on which you can dodge it only by giving up conglomerability (which I half-remember from somewhere), which side do you keep and why?

Two more things I want explicitly. First, if some subset of these requirements can't be satisfied together, say which and sketch the argument — that's the part I most want, since then the list is a result rather than a shopping list. Second, which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories? Then a brief scorecard, a few lines each: maximin, maximality, epsilon-admissability and interval dominance against your list, plus a line on whether imprecision is ever rationally required or only permitted. A table is welcome wherever it helps.
