Retired from a solid-state lab four years ago — semiconductors, thin films, nothing exotic — and I've fallen down a philosophy-of-science hole to fill the time. I keep circling back to one thing from my old world and I can't get the question into a shape I can ask properly, so maybe you can help.

The practice. For something like forty years now, high-energy theory has been run to a large degree by a criterion that isn't in any experiment. Roughly: a good theory shouldn't have to have its numbers dialed in. If a dimensionless parameter in your model comes out at 10^-30 and nothing in the mathematics explains why it's that small, that counts against the theory. There's a slightly more careful version — a small number is acceptable if setting it to exactly zero would give the theory extra symmetry — but in practice what people mean is "don't ask me to believe a coincidence."

The famous case was the Higgs. Quantum corrections want to drag its mass up toward the Planck scale, so keeping it near the value eventually measured requires cancellations good to something like thirty-odd digits unless new particles turn up around a TeV. That was treated as a scandal and as a signpost: something has to appear at the LHC. Meanwhile the cosmological constant sits somewhere between fifty and a hundred and twenty orders of magnitude away from the naive field-theory estimate, depending how you cut it off — the worst theory/experiment mismatch there is — and the community basically shrugs, and when pressed says we happen to live in one of a vast number of universes and only this kind contains observers. Then the LHC found the Higgs, found no partners for anything, and from what I read the dominant reaction was not to retire the criterion. It was to say the universe may simply be unnatural, or to push the new scale higher, or to keep the criterion on as a tie-breaker.

Here's the part I can't articulate. It looks to me like Wigner's old complaint about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics has been quietly promoted from an observation into a working rule: not just "the math keeps working, miraculously," but "the mathematically unmotivated option is the physically unlikely one, and that unlikelihood is evidence." Thats a much stronger claim and I don't think anyone ever states it as such.

So: what does this practice presuppose about the relation between mathematics and the physical world? I'd like it reconstructed as carefully as you can manage — what it has to assume about which mathematical structures are on offer, about whether the universe is the sort of thing whose parameters call for explanation, about what counts as a coincidence at all, and about who or what is doing the sampling when we call one number "fine-tuned." Which rival views does it rule out without saying so — instrumentalism about mathematics, say, or the thought that our math feels natural because our heads built it? And how much of this is genuinely presupposition versus just a sensible way to spend a very expensive decade of beam time: is there a version where the whole thing is only a search prior with no metaphysics attached, and if so does actual practice stay on that side of the line? Take the cosmological constant seriously here too — is the anthropic shrug a betrayal of the presupposition, a rescue of it, or something that only makes sense under a different one entirely?

Finally, what would have to change — in refereeing, in what counts as a result, in how a young theorist picks a problem — if you replaced the presupposition with the explicit claim that naturalness is a statement about our priors and not about the world?
