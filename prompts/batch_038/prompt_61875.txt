I am preparing a talk for a joint philosophy-of-physics and literature seminar, and my assigned text is the opening scene of Tom Stoppard's *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*. I would like a critical reading of that scene, through David Lewis's Principal Principle and the later debate about chance-credence deference. I am not interested in the usual fatalism-versus-free-will theme, and I would rather not be told that the coins symbolize mortality; I want the scene treated as a piece of epistemology.

So that you are not working blind, here are the relevant facts. Guildenstern spins coins while Rosencrantz calls them, correctly, as heads every time; the running score is announced onstage in the manner of a tennis match — seventy-six, then eighty-five, then eighty-nine — and in the published text the run reaches ninety-two. Guildenstern observes that "a weaker man might be moved to re-examine his faith, if in nothing else at least in the law of probability." He then garbles the law of large numbers into something resembling the infinite-monkey theorem, holding that six monkeys tossed in the air for long enough would land on their tails about as often as on their heads. Finally he produces an explicit list of possible explanations: that he is secretly willing it, spinning double-headed coins and betting against himself in private atonement for an unremembered past; that time has stopped dead and a single experience of one coin being spun once has simply been repeated; that there has been divine intervention, a good turn for Rosencrantz or retribution for himself; and fourth, that each individual coin spun individually is as likely to come down heads as tails, so it should occasion no surprise that each individual time it does. Beyond the scene, both men are minor characters inside *Hamlet*, being carried toward deaths the audience has already heard about.

Please read the scene under the following stipulations, since the alternatives turn it into a different play. Take the coin to be a genuine objective chance setup and take chances to be whatever a Humean best-system account makes them. Take the run of heads to be real within the world of the play, rather than stage magic or a symptom in Guildenstern. Take Lewis's principle at face value: for admissible evidence, a rational agent's credence in an outcome given that its chance is x should be x.

On that basis, I want four things.

First, a diagnosis. Is this scene a case of the Principal Principle working exactly as intended, with Guildenstern's escalating unease marking an irrationality the principle can name? Or is it an instance of the undermining problem pressed by Hall and Thau, on which conditionalizing on a history that the chance assignment itself renders wildly improbable seems to undercut that very assignment, and Lewis's later appeal to ur-chances is the price of fixing it? Or is it a puzzle about admissibility — is the observed run admissible evidence about the setup's past, or inadmissible evidence against the chance? Evaluate Guildenstern's fourth explanation head-on: is it in effect the Principal Principle's own answer, and if so is he right to dismiss it as leaving the unease untouched, or has he stumbled onto the correct response and simply failed to be consoled by it?

Second, the six-monkeys gag. Identify precisely which principles are being run together — the gambler's fallacy, the law of large numbers, the a priori improbability of a long sequence versus the probability of the next trial given the chance — and say whether Stoppard is dramatizing a confusion his character has or a confusion the play has. Does the joke land on Guildenstern, or on an audience that is expected to feel the pull of a compensation principle it knows to be false?

Third, the determinism wrinkle. Lewis thought non-trivial objective chance required indeterminism. The world of this play is a script, and its future is fixed in a way that looks like determinism from the inside. Are the characters' fatalism and Lewisian determinism the same thing? If the play's world is deterministic, can the coin have a chance of one-half at all, or is the scene really about an agent deferentially tracking a quantity that is either zero or one and that she is in principle unable to know? Relatedly, the audience holds exactly the kind of information about these characters' futures that Lewis would call inadmissible. Is the play's dramatic irony a real analogue of the admissibility condition, or merely a pun on knowing the future? If it is real, does it say anything about why admissibility has proven so hard to characterize?

Fourth, the honest part. Stoppard is writing a comedy about what it is like to be a bit player, and the probability talk may be serving tone rather than committing the play to any chance semantics. Tell me plainly which of the claims above the text supports and which I am importing, and where the reading has to be abandoned.

Close with what the play contributes that argument alone does not — in particular, whether it captures something about the experience of an agent required to align her credences with a magnitude she can estimate only through the very events whose improbability is what troubles her, and whether the technical literature, written from the outside, has any way of registering that.

Two constraints. Do not include a bibliography, a list of further reading, or citations; I know that literature and have no way to check anything you supply. Do not attempt to quote the play: I have given you the details you need, and any line you produce from memory is likely to be wrong. What I want is something I could adapt into a thirty-minute talk, organized around the four questions above, willing to reach verdicts, and willing to tell me if one of my framing questions is misconceived.
