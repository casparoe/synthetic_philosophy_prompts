I teach a two-day methods workshop for a county government's mixed cohort — roughly half data analysts, half program staff who will sit on the oversight committee for a new screening tool — and I have found that the discussion of fairness criteria stalls until people notice that their own description of the decision problem has already committed them to one. I want to build a decision tree that performs that sorting for them. The tree's inputs should be structural facts about a decision setting, not statements of values, and I want a single running example used throughout: a public housing authority that screens applicants with a model predicting whether a household will fall into arrears within eighteen months, where the arrears label is itself partly produced by the authority's prior screening decisions (households flagged high-risk are assigned to properties with thinner on-site support), and where the group attribute of interest may lawfully be used for auditing but not at decision time. Please give me ten to twelve decision nodes and eight to ten leaves, plus an answer key written out as paths (N1 → yes → N3 → …) so the exercise can be run on paper without any software.

Every node must be phrased as a question a program staffer could answer from knowledge of their own system without ever having encountered the criterion vocabulary, and none of them may ask whether the respondent cares about equal outcomes, equal treatment, or anything else of that shape. The nodes I want to see in some form include: whether the system emits a score or a decision; whether the authority controls the threshold or inherits it; whether the outcome used for validation is generated in part by the authority's own past decisions; whether the group-specific base rates are estimated from the same population under the same measurement procedure; who bears the cost of a false positive, the individual or the public; whether the group attribute is available at audit time but forbidden at decision time; and whether the authority can act on the population rather than only on the classifier. Jargon belongs exclusively at the leaves. The leaves must include calibration (sufficiency), error-rate balance (separation) at a single common threshold, separation at group-specific thresholds, predictive parity, independence or demographic parity, an escape-hatch leaf for settings where the impossibility simply does not bind (equal base rates, or near-perfect prediction), a measurement-skeptic leaf on which no criterion evaluated against an endogenous label is meaningful at all, a leaf for withdrawing the automated decision, and a leaf on which the remedy is a change to the world the classifier predicts rather than to the classifier. For each leaf give the name, one sentence stating what it is forced to give up, and which result licenses it — the Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan trade-offs, the Chouldechova incompatibility, or none, in which case say so explicitly rather than inventing a citation.

Two further things, because the workshop is meant to produce arguments rather than satisfaction. First, flag at least two pairs of leaves that my nodes cannot genuinely separate — I expect predictive parity and calibration to be one candidate, since a well-calibrated continuous score collapses part of the distinction, and I want to know where else the tree is pretending to a resolution it does not have. Second, build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of positions I gave you: after presenting it, lay out at least two combinations of answers that are perfectly coherent as paths but have no leaf attached, and diagnose each — is the cell empty because the resulting view is incoherent, because it is stable but unattractive to anyone who has considered it, or because nobody has simply staked it out yet? If one of those unoccupied cells looks to you like the position my housing-authority example actually describes, say so plainly, since that would be the most useful thing the exercise could reveal.
