I am a senior, and I sit on my school's Constitution Review Committee, which has been charged for the spring term with settling one question: who counts as a member of the student electorate. Each member must submit a written statement of position in advance, and mine is drafted below. Before I file it, I would like a rigorous consistency check — not a survey of how this question is usually discussed, but an audit of my package as a set of claims.

Here is what I currently hold:

1. The right criterion of inclusion is affectedness: anyone whose interests are affected by a decision ought to have a say in it.
2. Because affectedness cannot be assessed decision by decision — the electorate would change shape every week — the electorate should be fixed in advance by asking who is subject to the council's authority, and everyone so subject receives a standing vote.
3. To measure affectedness across time, I apply a rolling four-year horizon: a person counts as affected by a measure if it changes what they can reasonably expect their situation at this school to be at any point within the next four years.
4. The boundary itself should be settled democratically: the sitting electorate votes, by a two-thirds margin, on who the electorate is to be.
5. Ninth graders do not vote in school-wide elections; they elect a class council with delegated authority over ninth-grade matters.
6. Students in our fully online program should not vote. They are enrolled, they are bound by every rule the council passes, and they pay the same fees, but they do not share in the life of the school, and student government exists so that a community may govern itself.
7. Students on long-term medical leave should not vote either, including on the referenda that set the health-services fee, which is the fee that most directly concerns them.
8. Inclusiveness and deliberative capacity are both requirements of legitimacy, and I do not think a larger electorate necessarily deliberates worse.
9. The council should decide its own size, powers, and procedures, but not its own electorate; letting a sitting body define its voters is letting incumbents entrench themselves.

Please tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. I expect that some pairs that look contradictory are not, so do not simply flag everything you can find. Rank the real tensions by severity, most serious first. For each, state which commitment must be revised or abandoned, what is lost by abandoning it, and whether any single revision would clear more than one tension at once. I am not attached to any particular item and I have not decided in advance which I would surrender, so please do not assume I will protect the ones I listed first or last.

Finally, I want a direct answer to this: can my verdicts in 6 and 7 be reached from the principles I have actually stated in 1 through 3, or are they being carried by a commitment I have not written down? If the latter, name the missing commitment and say whether adopting it openly would force changes elsewhere in the list.
