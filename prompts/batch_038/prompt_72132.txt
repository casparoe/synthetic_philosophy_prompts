So, third novel, and I've hit a wall that I'm now pretty sure its a game theory wall, which is deeply annoying because I have never taken a day of game theory in my life and don't intend to start now.

The book's spine is a strike at a paper mill in a company town — four hundred families, one employer, one road in. The union local has taken a public strike vote and mailed the tally to every member's family. The owner has signed a ten-year supply contract with a buyer that carries brutal penalties, and has announced in the local paper that he'll close the mill permanently before he meets the wage demand. Both of them burned their bridges in front of witnesses, on purpose. There's a deadline that I won't bore you with, and structurally neither side can back down without losing everything they actually care about — which is the thing that makes it a novel and not a news story.

My problem is that I can *feel* there's a real body of thinking about this shape of situation. Schelling, commitment races, brinkmanship, the whole business about a threat only being credible once you've genuinely removed your own option not to carry it out, the idea of deliberately making yourself a bit crazy in order to be unbeatable. I've read two of his books at the level of skimming for sentences I could steal (*The Strategy of Conflict* and *Arms and Influence*, I think — correct me if I'm mangling it), and everything else I find is either equations or magazine gloss, and neither one helps me write a scene.

I think in craft. My working vocabulary is: want vs. need, stakes, POV and what the POV character is permitted to know, the point of no return, reversals, escalation as a run of beats where each one costs the character something concrete, and the reader's position relative to all of it. That's the language I actually reason in, and I want the theory in it.

So: can you translate the logic of commitment races into that vocabulary as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me a straight report on what didn't survive the trip? I care at least as much about the second half as the first. Ground rules:

- Prose. No math, no payoff matrices, no notation. If a point needs a diagram I'm the wrong audience.
- Don't smuggle my own craft intuitions back at me and present them as translation. If a concept has no honest counterpart in how novelists talk about plot, say so flatly instead of handing me a good-sounding synonym for "escalation" and calling it a match. I'd rather have an honest gap than a flattering one.
- I want the residue to run both directions. I have a suspicion that dramatic irony — where the reader knows one side's commitment is hollow and the other character doesn't — is something the theory structurally cannot say, because it assumes everyone knows what everyone knows. Tell me whether I'm right or whether there's a place for it that I haven't found.
- Also, I keep seeing "commitment race" used by people talking about two AI labs sprinting at each other. If that's the same animal as the Schelling thing, or a different one wearing the same name, sort it out somewhere in the residue section — my characters are people, and I need to know which version I'm writing.

What I'd like back, in this order:

**The machinery, in my words** — 400–500 words. Take the core moves one at a time: commitment as a deliberate act rather than a state of mind, credibility and who has to observe what, brinkmanship as the manufacturing of shared risk, and whatever else you think is load-bearing that I've failed to mention. For each, the craft counterpart.

**Where you had to bend it** — around 300 words. Which of those translations hold up clean and which only work if I squint. I especially want you to be honest about the ones that only work if you assume people know their own preferences, because my entire cast doesn't.

**What doesn't survive** — around 300 words. The residue proper. Concepts with no counterpart in my vocabulary, and concepts in my vocabulary with no counterpart in theirs.

**Whose gap is it** — around 250 words. Is the residue a hole in how novelists think about conflict, or a sign that the theory is thinner than it sounds — that "commitment race" is mostly an impressive relabeling of two stubborn people? Pick a side and argue it. Don't split the difference to be polite, I have no ego in this.

**What I do Monday morning** — around 200 words. Two or three concrete changes to the standoff as I've described it, at the level of structure rather than sentences. And please be willing to tell me the strike is the wrong engine entirely and I should be reorganizing around something else; I'd much rather hear that now than in year two of revisions.

Roughly 1,700 words total is about right. Longer if it's earning its length, shorter if you can say it tighter.
