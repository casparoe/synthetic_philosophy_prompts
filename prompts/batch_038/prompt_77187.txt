So I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a broadsheet, and every so often the paper sends me out to do schools stuff — this time it's a 45-minute slot at a sixth-form "Ideas Day" in Leeds, 30-odd sixteen-to-eighteen year-olds, roughly half of them doing art and design and half doing maths or further maths, and precisely none of them will have read a page of Gödel, Escher, Music. The session is supposed to be on one idea from it: where meaning actually lives. My handle on it, from having skimmed the thing and argued about it with a composer friend for years, is the three-way distinction between the frame (whatever fixes which reading you're doing), the inner message (what a particular decoder pulls out — the tune in the grooves), and the outer message (what survives when you swap decoders and frames around, and also the message-about-the-message, like the fact that the record destroys the player — I think that dialogue is called Contracrostipunctus but don't hold me to it). What I want from you is not an explanation of any of this. I want the teaching plan. Tell me the order the pieces have to go in and what genuinely depends on what — I suspect you can't get the outer message across before they've stopped thinking of meaning as a thing inside somebody's head, but I don't know where that fits — and then pick the one example that carries the whole 45 minutes. Be specific about what that example has to have and what it has to lack: it can't be a sentence, because then they just relapse into "so what did the author mean", and I don't think it can be a pun or a deliberately ambiguous picture either, because lexical ambiguity is a different phenomenon and they'll weld the two together. Then give me the misconception map — the ones they'll walk in with (my bet is "meaning is whatever Hofstadter intended", and its evil twin "so it's all subjective, anything goes"), the ones the sequence itself will manufacture if I'm not careful, and the exact point in the arc where I head each one off rather than answering it later.

Also: the art half will want to turn this into symbolism in paintings and the maths half will want to turn it into Gödel numbering, and there's always one kid who's watched the strange-loop videos and will try to take the room into the incompleteness theorem, so tell me how the plan absorbs that without humiliating him. I've got a whiteboard, a laptop, a projector and speakers, no instruments and no props I can't make out of paper. Give me a rough minute-by-minute split, a fallback in case the carrying example just doesn't land (how will I know within ninety seconds that it hasn't?), and one question or task at the end that would show me they've actually got the frame/inner/outer distinction rather than merely enjoyed a clever talk — something where they have to apply it to a case they've never seen, not recite it back at me. Finally, be explicit about what I should leave out at this level and say so out loud to the room: I'd rather flag the simplification I'm making and tell them it'll need unlearning than let them discover it later, so name the one thing I'm definitely going to have to fudge and give me the sentence to fudge it with. British English please, and don't recommend me any reading — they wont read it and I'm not allowed to hand out lists anyway.
