I write about science and ideas for a UK magazine and I've been stuck for weeks on a piece about the multiple realisability argument — the one that's supposed to sink the claim that mental states just are brain states. I've got the shape of it down: the opening move, that a creature whose neurology is nothing like ours could still be in pain; the follow-up that multiply realised kinds are perfectly respectable anyway, since economics and biology are full of them; then the counter-attack, roughly that if the realisers really are that different they can't all be counted as doing the same job, so either the functional description is carrying all the weight or the realisations were never so different to begin with. Plus the rejoinder from people who actually do comparative neuroscience, who say the thought experiments are too clean and that homologous structures don't line up across species the way the debate assumes. And hanging over the whole thing, the causal question: if the realiser is what does the causing, what is left for the mental kind to do?

I have six versions of this argument and the piece isn't moving. My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather then flatter: there isn't one disagreement here, there are two wearing the same coat — one about whether reduction can be true and still leave the special-science kinds autonomous, and one about whether causal work has to be booked at a single level. If that's right, the thing has stalled because each camp is answering a question the other side isn't asking, and the neuroscientific evidence can't break the tie because neither side's claim was ever empirical in the way it looks.

Can you diagnose it properly? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually does — the moves they make, the examples they reach for, what each treats as needing explanation — not in a survey of who holds what. Its a diagnosis I want, not an adjudication, and not a history. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction somebody needs to draw, a kind of case that would actually be decisive, a formal result, or a change of subject. If your answer is that nothing would move it and we should stop pretending otherwise, say that and give me the reason.

One wrinkle worth folding in. A computational neuroscientist I interviewed thinks the whole thing dissolves if you replace the question with a substitution test: two systems share a mental state just in case you can swap one into the other's causal context — the same inputs, the same downstream effects, the same place in the network — and nothing about the system's behaviour or internal dynamics registers the difference. He thinks that's a third option between the camps. Half the philosophers I put it to said it's functionalism with the serial numbers filed off; the other half said it's doing something new, because it makes sameness of state a matter of degree and of context rather than a yes/no. Where does it actually land, and does it break the deadlock or just relocate it?
