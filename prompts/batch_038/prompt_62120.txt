I want to argue that the Letter is not, at bottom, a moral argument about plays corrupting the audience, and that the two moves people usually treat as its weak points are actually its spine. First: Rousseau concedes that a play mostly reflects and intensifies the moeurs already present in the people watching it, and that a genuinely good play can't do much harm — readers usually treat that concession as a rhetorical feint to make the ban sound reasonable, but I take it seriously, and it relocates the whole target. If the theater only amplifies what's already there, then what's objectionable isn't the repertoire, it's the amplifying arrangement: the ticket, the dark room, the hired body on a platform, the fact that two hundred people are made to feel the same thing at the same time while being strictly forbidden from doing anything about it or to each other. That's a crowd that has been rendered private. Second: the long stretch on the Genevan pastors isn't a digression or a tactical flattery of the clergy — pastor and actor occupy the same slot in Rousseau's taxonomy, both being salaried professionals who sell their persons, perform sentiments that aren't theirs, and stand in a relation of dependence on an audience that cannot answer back. And the festival proposal is defined almost entirely negatively, as the subtraction of exactly those three features: no stage, no price of admission, no intermediary, spectators turned into participants. So the Letter, on my reading, is a treatise on the conditions under which an assembly stops being an aggregate of solitary feeling and becomes a public — which makes it continuous with the Social Contract's claim that sovereignty cannot be represented, same structural worry pointed at a different institution.
