I work at a small research nonprofit and I'm building a wellbeing measurement instrument — basically an experience-sampling app where people get pinged four or five times a day and rate the moment they're in on a single slider, "how pleasant is this right now?", anchored from very unpleasant to very pleasant. Everything downstream (aggregating into day-scores, comparing people, checking whether some intervention moved the needle) assumes pleasantness is one dimension you can put any moment on. A friend of mine who's halfway through a philosophy PhD insists this is metaphysically naive, on heterogeneity grounds: the pleasure of a cold drink on a hot day, the pleasure of finally getting an argument to work, the pleasure of laughing with someone, the ugly pleasure of watching a rival embarrass themselves — supposedly there's no one felt quality running through all of those, just a grab bag of experiences we lump together because we like them and want them to keep going. Their conclusion is that my slider isn't measuring a feeling at all, it's measuring something dispositional (roughly, how much you'd pay to extend the moment) and mislabeling it as phenomenology. I keep going back and forth, partly because I can see the attitudinal move — pleasure as a way of being pleased *about* something rather than a felt ingredient — and partly because the liking/wanting split in the affective neuroscience literature seems to me to cut the other way, since it suggests there *is* a common hedonic core that can come apart from desire.

So I want a number, not a survey. Take this proposition, which I'll try to pin down as tightly as I can: **there is a single phenomenal property that is present in every intrinsically pleasant experience, and whose presence is what makes that experience pleasant.** Give me your credence in it, and then show me the decomposition that produces it — the sub-questions you'd break it into, a rough probability and one or two lines of justification for each, and how they combine into the final figure. I'd want at least some of the terms to be things like: whether the apparent heterogeneity of pleasures is a fact about the phenomenology or a fact about our concepts (we may be comparing experiences that differ in intensity, sensory modality, or intentional object and reading that difference off as a difference in the pleasant part); whether "what makes it pleasant" is doing heavy metaphysical work that a mere common-feature story can't satisfy; whether introspection is even a reliable witness here, given that comparing two pleasures requires holding one in memory while having the other; whether the attitudinal reconstrual counts as *answering* the unity thesis or as *dodging* it; and how much of the whole dispute is verbal. If you think the proposition as I've stated it is ill-posed or that its truth value depends on a choice I haven't made, say which precisification you're using and give the number for that one — but still give a number.

Then three more things. Which term is your total most sensitive to, i.e. which sub-question, if it resolved the other way, would move the final credence furthest, and what would actually resolve it — I'm especially interested in whether you think any real evidence (introspective, comparative, or the statistics of my own scale, like whether the ratings come out unidimensional across wildly different kinds of moments) could bear on it, or whether it's the kind of question where evidence just doesn't get a grip. And separately: does the philosophy even matter for my purposes? I have a nagging sense that a scale can be statistically one-dimensional without the underlying property being phenomenally unitary, and vice versa, in which case my friend might be right about the metaphysics and still be wrong that my instrument is illegitimate — tell me whether that escape route is real or whether I'm fooling myself. Finally, put your number next to mine and hers: I'm sitting at about 0.25 and she's at about 0.75 that the unity thesis is false, and I want your best guess at which single sub-question the two of us most likely disagree about, given that we both claim to accept the same empirical picture of how people rate their moments.
