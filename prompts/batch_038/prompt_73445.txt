Write a short story that dramatizes the conceptual knot at the center of Dennett's argument that large language models are counterfeit people: they reproduce the surface marks of agency convincingly enough that we feel obliged to extend the presumption of competence to them, while lacking — on his account — the internal economy that makes that presumption safe to extend.

Build the story around a mechanism I have not seen used before. Personhood is adjudicated not by conversation but by a perturbation audit: a claimant counts as genuine if her avowed beliefs survive adversarial paraphrase — restated premises, reordered clauses, cosmetically altered wording — and as counterfeit if her answers track the wording rather than the content. Your protagonist is a human being who fails. Render in scene, not in essayistic aside, why an instrument designed to screen out machines may be structurally incapable of separating them from a person whose convictions were assembled under an equally wording-sensitive regime: an algorithmic feed, a liturgy recited since childhood, a decade of paid work shaping prompts for other systems. Let the reader feel the asymmetry of the stakes — what is destroyed when the audit errs in each direction — without permitting any character to articulate it.

Open with a one-sentence logline stating what the story is really about, then the story proper. Hard cap of 900 words, and no closing line that resolves the question.
