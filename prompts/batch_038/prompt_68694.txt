I'm auditing a book for my reading group and I've hit a passage I can't evaluate on my own. I work in constitutional law, so I know the modern materials cold, but my medieval Latin philosophy is essentially nil; the group has been working through a recent trade history of political assassination by a journalist-historian who writes with great confidence and, as far as I can tell, no footnotes. His chapter on the Middle Ages builds to this paragraph:

"The medieval contribution to this debate was made by Thomas Aquinas, who gave the Christian West its most systematic permission to kill a tyrant. In On Kingship, addressed to a ruling prince, he argues that a king who turns from the common good to his own advantage has destroyed the basis of his own authority, so that a community which appointed him does him no wrong in deposing him and, if deposition proves impossible, in putting him to death. The killing is not sedition, Aquinas insists, but its opposite: it is the tyrant who has torn the polity apart, and whoever removes him restores its unity. He compares the tyrant to a diseased limb that must be cut from the body so that the whole may live, and observes that those who performed this operation were honoured by posterity rather than punished. Aquinas's one real restriction is institutional rather than moral: the deed must be undertaken on the authority of the community, so that a private individual acting on his own judgement commits murder even when the tyrant deserves to die. Later Catholic thought found all this uncomfortable. When a French theologian defended the assassination of the Duke of Orléans before the Council of Constance, the Council condemned the proposition that a subject may lawfully kill a tyrant, and the natural-law tradition thereafter taught patience under unjust government. The quietism usually attributed to medieval Catholic political thought is thus not Aquinas's teaching but the suppression of it."

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me what to trust. For each assertion, I'd like to know whether a specialist in Aquinas's political thought would accept it, accept it as a fair popular simplification, or reject it outright — and in the last case, what the correction is. Three places especially worry me. First, the author treats Aquinas as granting a *permission* to kill the tyrant, indeed a fallback permission when deposition fails; my vague recollection is that Aquinas is much more cautious than that, more concerned with the evils a botched revolt unleashes than with licensing the deed, and that the confident permission belongs to other medieval and early modern authors. Second, the diseased-limb comparison and the claim about honouring tyrannicides: I suspect the author has taken an analogy Aquinas uses somewhere else, about a different subject entirely, and welded it onto the ruler, and that where Aquinas does mention men who killed tyrants he is drawing a cautionary moral rather than an admiring one. Third, the historical framing of Constance: even if the Council did condemn propositions defending the assassination of the Duke of Orléans, does it follow that this was a repudiation of Aquinas, or a reversal in the tradition, rather than a condemnation of claims considerably more permissive than anything he wrote — and is it true that the natural-law tradition became quietist afterwards?

Also tell me where you're confident and where the scholarship is genuinely divided — for instance, whether Aquinas's very early remarks on killing a tyrant and his mature treatment of sedition and of killing in the Summa amount to a change of mind, a difference of question, or a difference of genre, since the author's whole narrative depends on reading them as one doctrine. Finally, and separately from the audit: the author never raises the hard case, and I'd like to know what Aquinas's framework does with it. Suppose the ruler holds office by an unimpeachable title, governs unjustly but not catastrophically, and there is no longer any corporate body in the polity competent to depose him. What resources does Aquinas leave a private person in that situation, and is the answer different from the one the paragraph implies?
