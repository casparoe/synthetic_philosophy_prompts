For the past year a foundation has hosted a structured adversarial collaboration between two economists. A holds the Mises–Hayek position: where the means of production are publicly owned, no genuine prices for capital goods can arise, so rational economic calculation — the comparison of heterogeneous alternative uses of resources — is not merely difficult but impossible, and Hayek's later reframing of the issue as one of knowledge that is dispersed, partly tacit, and continuously changing sharpens the difficulty rather than dissolving it. B holds the Lange position: a central planning board can arrive at the same price vector a market would by announcing one, observing the resulting surpluses and shortages, and iterating; managers can be directed to minimize cost at the announced factor prices and to set output where price equals marginal cost; and whatever force the objection had in the 1930s has been overtaken by mechanism design and by computational resources its authors could not contemplate. B also accepts two concessions in advance: that the Lange scheme was never in fact implemented, and that Lerner, whose work was closely intertwined with Lange's, thought investment allocation under it would end up politicized.

They have been instructed to produce a single document that both will sign. Write it.

**1. Agreed claims.** A numbered list of propositions both parties can endorse — about the formal structure of the allocation problem, about what a price vector does and does not convey, about what a planning board could in principle compute if it were simply handed the relevant information, and about what the record of twentieth-century command economies does and does not establish, given that those economies also faced world markets. At least one claim must be one that A regards as damaging to A's own case, and at least one that B so regards. Any proposition both can sign only because it says nothing should be marked as such rather than padded into the list.

**2. Disagreements.** A table with four columns: the disagreement stated in wording A would accept as a fair description of A's view; the same disagreement stated in wording B would accept for B's view; a classification of the disagreement as verbal, conceptual, or empirical; and, for the verbal and conceptual rows, the substantive question both parties agree lies underneath.

**3. Cruxes and pre-registered predictions.** Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. At a minimum address: whether the problem is one of computation or of knowledge generation — that is, whether A's impossibility claim survives a stipulation that measurement and computation are costless; how either arrangement handles uses of resources that no one has yet articulated, and whether a product that does not yet exist can have a shadow price at all; incentives, including residual claimancy, the political capture of the board's investment function, and the honesty of local reporting; and the firm-boundary case — why administrative allocation inside a firm is not a standing counterexample to A, and what fixes its limits. For each crux, name the case, experiment, or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, specific enough to be scored later.

To make the first two cruxes concrete, construct a stipulated twenty-period episode and run both arrangements through it: a shift in consumer preferences across sectors, the loss of an imported input, and the emergence of a technically feasible product for which no expressed demand exists. For each of the three shocks, give each party's prediction about the resulting allocation in each arrangement, the lag before adjustment, and the information the center would have had to collect in order to adjust at all. State the setup in a neutral formal idiom that does not presuppose either position, then audit your own setup: list every stipulation one party would contest as question-begging and give the neutral replacement.

**4. Falsification schedule.** Each party writes down the observation, result, or argument that would cause them to abandon their position; the other party then states whether that thing is in principle available and what would count as its having occurred. A must confront the charge that "impossible" is doing unfalsifiable work, and the document should record whether A can discharge it.

**5. Verbal residue.** State which parts of the century-long exchange were disputes over words — I expect "calculation" against "computation," and "possible" against "rational," to be among them — and what substantive disagreement remains once those are removed.

No bibliography, no citations, no suggestions for further reading; the document must stand on the parties' own reasoning.
