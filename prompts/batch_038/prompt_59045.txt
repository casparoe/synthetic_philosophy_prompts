I'm a policy analyst on a city council staff, and I need help taking apart a sentence that did an enormous amount of work at a public hearing last month. The council was considering a redress program for the descendants of families whose homes were taken by eminent domain in the late 1950s to build a highway interchange: the two neighborhoods involved were cleared, the land is still municipally owned in part and partly sold off, and the program would be financed by a surcharge on property transfers, meaning the revenue would come from people buying houses here today. A council member opposed to the program closed her remarks with the claim that guilt is not inherited — that she had nothing to do with what the city did sixty-odd years ago, and neither did the residents who would be paying the surcharge. Three people in public comment then repeated some version of the same line, and as best I could tell they meant three different things by it. One meant that you cannot blame a person for what an ancestor did. One meant that the body being asked to pay is not the body that acted, or at least that its current members are not the people who acted. One meant that the wrongs do not travel down the family tree either, so the people who would receive payments were never wronged — some of them, he said, were not even born at the time, and a claim needs a victim.

I have to speak at the second hearing and I do not want to answer a version of the argument nobody made. So: what are the distinct precise claims that "guilt is not inherited" could be making in this context, from the ones that are close to tautologies through the ones that are substantive and debatable to the ones that are simply false? For each, tell me exactly what it asserts — which property is in question (culpability, blameworthiness, liability to pay, a duty to repair, the mere retention of a benefit that came from someone else's wrong, or something about the taint on a title to land), whose property it is (individuals, their descendants, or the continuing corporation that acted), and whether "inherited" is being used in the sense of genealogical descent or in the sense of institutional succession. Then tell me which of these readings would actually block this particular proposal, given that it is a claim against a city that still exists under the same charter, still holds some of the land, and proposes to collect from parties who were not present in the 1950s — and which readings would only block the accusation that current residents are wicked, leaving the proposal untouched. Where you can, say which reading each of my three speakers was probably relying on, and whether it would survive being stated out loud in its precise form.

I am not asking you to argue for or against the program, and I do not want to be sent off to read anything. Please write this to me as a letter: continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way one writes an email — but signpost each reading clearly enough in the sentences that I can find my way back to it.
