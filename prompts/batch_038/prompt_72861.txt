For the last six months I've been the philosopher bolted onto a team writing the speech and participation rules for a network of online citizen assemblies — a few thousand randomly selected people per assembly, run by a foundation, moderated day to day by staff who are not philosophers and who need a rule they can apply on a Tuesday afternoon. I have one RA and until the end of term, and my job is to hand the team a defensible line on the tolerance problem: when is a deliberative body entitled to silence or eject someone whose whole contribution is an argument against deliberation itself?

I can already recite the greatest hits, so please don't. Popper's footnote about not being obliged to tolerate people who refuse rational argument and answer it with their fists; the Rawlsian move where you only restrict the intolerant once the institutions doing the tolerating are themselves in danger; the militant-democracy tradition of deliberately asymetrical rules. What I actually need is a dependency-aware prioritisation of the conceptual questions sitting underneath all that, because six months isn't much and I want to spend it on the ones that gate the policy rather than the ones that sound interesting in a seminar.

Here's my shortlist, roughly as I've been thinking about it:

(a) procedural vs content triggers — Popper's refusal-to-argue criterion sounds procedural, but you may not be able to distinguish refusing to argue from arguing very badly without smuggling in a judgment about content, and if you can't, the criterion collapses into a list of forbidden views;
(b) whether tolerance is even the right unit of analysis — a personal virtue versus a ruleset enforced by an institution holding a monopoly on exclusion, and whether the paradox dissolves or gets worse once you move up that level;
(c) the self-application thing: is a policy of suppressing intolerance itself intolerant, and does the answer just depend on whether you treat tolerance as a disposition, a practice, or a distribution of liberties;
(d) the reciprocity/asymmetry problem — the party that plays by procedural rules only until it's strong enough not to, and whether the relevant asymmetry is about their predicted future behaviour or about the structural position they occupy right now;
(e) the identification problem under contamination — in our assemblies, once a group gets the intolerant label the label spreads and stops being evidence about them, so any rule we write has to work when its inputs are themselves produced by herding.

Give me exactly five questions to work on, ranked most-worth-my-six-months first. Draw them from my list, cut them together, or replace one if you think something upstream is missing — I suspect there's an unasked question about what counts as a threat to an institution as opposed to a threat to its individual members, and I'd like to know if that belongs at the top. Tell me plainly which of (a)–(e) to drop as a toy: I half suspect (c). Rank on the criteria that matter here — how much of the rest depends on it, whether a non-philosopher administrator could actually apply the answer, and whether it's settleable in the time I have rather than being a permanent dispute.

Format: exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion. Each item names the question, says why it sits in that slot rather than one above or below, and gives the one thing that would move it.
