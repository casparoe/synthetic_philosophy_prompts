I write about psychology and history for a general-interest magazine — the crossover stuff, obedience experiments, memory politics, that beat. Last spring I published a long piece arguing the Browning/Goldhagen fight over Reserve Police Battalion 101 has been conducted on the wrong axis, and that piece somehow got me a 20-minute invited slot plus open Q&A at a two-day symposium on ordinary cruelty at a Dutch university in November, which is a room I did not think I'd be standing in and now can't gracefully back out of. My editor is flying over "for the color," which I did not ask for.

Here's the argument as I'll put it. Both sides treat the killers' beliefs as already fixed before they act, and then argue about where the behavior came from — situation or culture. I say the order runs backwards: they killed first and acquired reasons afterwards, and the antisemitic culture mattered mainly as a menu of justifications men picked from retrospectively, once the acts were done and had to be lived with. I call it retrospective authorization, which is admittedly a fancy name for territory that self-perception and dissonance people have been walking around in for decades. My second claim is the one I'm most attached to and most exposed on: Trapp's offer at Józefów to let any man step out wasn't a mercy that reveals motives, it was itself a compliance device, because the moment opting out becomes a public act in front of your mates an order has been converted into a visible choice, and the dozen-odd out of roughly five hundred who took him up on it are what made staying the default rather than proof of either obedience or willingness. Browning and Goldhagen use that same datum to argue opposite conclusions, which I take as evidence the datum isn't about motivation at all. I should flag: I've read Ordinary Men closely, I skimmed the middle four hundred pages of Hitler's Willing Executioners, and I have basically not read the replies to it — Birn, Bauer, whoever else — so if an objection depends on something in that literature, say so and give me the one sentence I need to survive it.

The room will be about forty people: mostly social psychologists who work on conformity, obedience and moral disengagement (they've all taught the Milgram material, several have done replication work and are permanently braced for pop uses of it), two Holocaust historians — one on Order Police and the postwar interrogation files, one on German public opinion and what ordinary people knew — a criminologist who studies policing in mass atrocity, and some memory-studies grad students. Nobody there is a Goldhagen defender; if anything the historians think that debate was settled against him, and I suspect one of them believes my piece rehabilitated him by accident.

So: give me the Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Exactly five numbered entries and nothing before or after them — no intro, no summary, no good luck. Order entries one through four by how much damage they'd do to me in that specific room, not by how interesting they are. For each: the objection phrased roughly the way that person would phrase it, including their vocabulary and the particular study or file collection they'd reach for; then two sentences of reply, with the concession in the first clause and what I hold onto after it; then one sentence on where that reply is softest. Entry five is the question I'm praying nobody asks, treated the same way. Tag every entry high, medium or low for how well you think my answer actually lands, and mark, inside the list, the single point you're least sure about yourself — I'd much rather know you're guessing at what these historians will say than get a smooth sheet thats partly invented.
