I joined a mid-sized industrial software company three months ago as the person who owns onboarding and "global capability" content. We have offices in seven countries, and the first thing I inherited is a vendor-supplied e-learning module called "Working Across Our Offices," mandatory for anyone assigned to a cross-border project. Each country gets a one-page profile: a score from 1 to 10 on four dimensions (deference to hierarchy, directness in disagreement, individual initiative, tolerance of ambiguity), a short paragraph of behavioural expectations, and three bulleted cautions — the Japan page, for instance, tells you not to expect a decision in a first meeting with the Nagoya team, and the Germany page tells you to state your objection plainly rather than hedging. Part of my job is now to sit in on new-hire sessions and answer questions about this material, and last week a new hire in Dublin asked me one I could not answer.

The details matter, so let me give you some. The scores are not from any external study: they are the mean self-reports of our own employees in a 2019 climate survey. That means the "national" figure for France rests on eleven respondents, while the figure for India rests on four hundred people who are all in one city, in one function, and were largely hired through the same two campuses; the German office was built almost entirely from one university's alumni network. The pages are revised only when someone complains, so the Brazil profile still describes an acquired business we closed in 2018. Three clicks away on the same platform sit our anti-harassment policy, which states that assumptions about a person grounded in their national or ethnic origin are a form of discrimination, and our hiring standard, which requires structured interviews scored against job-relevant criteria and instructs panels that "culture fit" is not an acceptable ground for rejecting a candidate. When I raised the tension internally I was told the module is "descriptive, not evaluative" and that it is really about "systems, not people" — which is hard to reconcile with pages addressed in the second person to how individual colleagues will behave.

So: what does this practice actually presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of national character that would license handing a document like ours to a new employee — what has to be true of populations, and of the relation between a population and the persons in it, for a page of this kind to be reasonable. Say explicitly which features of the practice require a strong claim (that peoples have stable, measurable, behaviour-predicting dispositions that individuals instantiate and that a mean can summarize) and which would survive on weaker ones, for example that the scores track institutional environments — who holds authority, how decisions are recorded, how employment law shapes the cost of confrontation — or that they track conventions whose violation is expensive even if the convention itself has no deeper explanation. Tell me which rival views the practice forecloses (that such categories are internally contested and generated by the powerful; that the dimensions are artifacts of the instrument and the translation; that within-country variation swamps between-country variation) and whether it forecloses them by argument or merely by formatting — by the fact that a page exists and has numbers on it. Then deal with two specific problems I cannot see my way through: the confound between a national mean and a selection effect, given that the people who join a firm like ours in a given country are not a random draw from it, and the possibility that the dimension scores mostly measure how different offices use a rating scale. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in the content, in how it is delivered, and in what we claim for it — if we rebuilt the module on the weakest theory that is still useful to a person who has to run a meeting in Nagoya on Thursday, and whether any version of such a document could sit coherently alongside a policy that forbids national-origin reasoning about individuals.
