I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar this term and I'm also volunteering a bit with a local wildlife trust, which is how I ended up actually reading the documentation behind the IUCN Red List instead of just looking up whether pandas are doomed. Now I can't unsee a puzzle and my seminar tutor keeps telling me to "just write the essay," so I'm asking you instead.

Here's the practice, concretely. The list sorts named taxa into categories — Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern, plus Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, and Data Deficient — and the thresholds are aggressively operational. To land in the top threatened category you need something like a population reduction of 80–90% over ten years or three generations, or an extent of occurrence under 100 km², or fewer than 250 mature individuals, or a population model that puts the probability of extinction in the wild at 50% or more over ten years. Extinct means no reasonable doubt that the last individual has died, after exhaustive surveys in the right places. The units being fed into those formulas are binomial names governed by a nomenclatural code, where each name is anchored to one physical specimen in a museum drawer. And it assesses species and subspecies — it doesn't score genera or families, so a genus with a single living species and a genus with four hundred contribute exactly one line each.

The bit that broke my brain: when taxonomists split a lump, the list gets rewritten and nothing happens to the animals. African elephants were assessed as one species for years; after the split into forest and savanna elephants, the forest elephant went onto the Critically Endangered list in 2021 and the savanna elephant onto Endangered. No elephant died in that transition. Nothing changed in the forest. What changed was a taxonomic opinion — and that opinion moves legal protection, funding, and headlines. Meanwhile a taxon can also sit on the list as Data Deficient, which is a strange category: the name is on the register, but there's no verdict about the world attached to it.

So what does this whole apparatus presuppose about what a species is? A few things I'd like worked through:

- Reconstruct the implicit theory. The list seems to use two incompatible grammars at once. On one hand it talks like a species is a particular thing that has a location, a range, a birth and a death — Extinct means the last one died, which sounds like an individual organism ceasing to exist, not a set going empty. On the other hand the criteria count mature individuals and draw polygons around distributions, which sounds like a class with members and a cardinality. Is that a genuine inconsistency in the practice, or just harmless loose talk that both theories can live with?
- Separate what the practice strictly needs from what merely fits it nicely. Could someone who thinks species are conventional slices we cut through a continuum of lineages, with no mind-independent boundaries waiting to be found, run the Red List exactly as it is? Or does operating the list commit you to species being real, discoverable units? And what about someone who thinks several different species concepts are each right for different purposes — does the list force a choice, or is it just quietly picking one by default?
- The equal-weighting thing. Is the practice committed to species being the indivisible atoms of conservation value, or is it neutral because it's a risk register and not a prioritisation tool? Note that schemes built on top of the list do weight things by evolutionary distinctiveness, which seems to imply that species are arbitrary cuts on a tree and that what matters is branch length. Can the base practice absorb that, or is there a tension between the register and its own downstream users?
- Point out where it's underdetermined: which features of the spreadsheet would look exactly the same under rival accounts of species, so that no amount of institutional practice could settle the metaphysics?
- And then run the counterfactual. If you replaced the presupposed view with a lineage-based one, what would have to change? What becomes the unit of assessment, what does Extinct mean, does Data Deficient survive, does the split-driven relisting problem go away or get worse? Would the resulting thing be more or less useful for the decision it actually gets used for, which is where to send a fixed pot of money?

Finally, tell me whether the split-driven relisting is a bug or a feature on each candidate theory. On some views it looks like the list getting more accurate about what was there all along; on others it looks like a document about the state of the world changing its verdicts because we changed our bookkeeping. I want to know which theories can say the first thing and which are stuck with the second.

Please actually engage with the machinery I described rather than giving me a tour of every species concept that has ever been proposed, and don't send me a reading list — I want the reconstruction and the argument.
