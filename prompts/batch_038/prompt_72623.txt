Eighteen months ago I took a two-year posting in a mid-sized town in a country I had previously known only as a holiday destination. I decided early on to observe local practice closely — partly out of ordinary politeness, partly because I did not want to be the one incomer who treated everybody else's rules as optional for herself. So I wait at the crossing even when the street is empty at eleven at night; I round up in the tip jars at the two cafés near my office, although the staff there are well paid and the jar goes towards their end-of-year meal; I join the queue at the market at the customary distance behind the person in front of me.

The difficulty is that my compliance seems to have altered the local standard rather than merely matched it. Because I am visibly not from here and am known to be scrupulous, my conduct has become a point of reference. A neighbour who used to cross on red when nothing was coming now waits, and told me last week that it costs her twenty minutes a week, but that she could hardly do less than the newcomer. At the cafés, rounding up has drifted into leaving a whole coin; one of the baristas remarked that the jar now takes more than it did, and the staff feel obliged to arrange something grander than they actually want. Somebody was rebuked last month for standing half a step closer in the market queue than was normal two years ago. I have been tightening a set of expectations I never asked to set, and the people carrying the added cost are the ones who live here permanently, not the one who leaves in six months.

I have thought about simply starting to cross on red now and then, in view of witnesses, to pull the standard back down. Several things trouble me about that plan. It may not work: after eighteen months of conspicuous correctness, a lapse might be read as slackness on my part rather than as a signal, and it might cost me whatever standing I have. It also feels like using my neighbours as instruments in an experiment I have not told them about. On the other hand, the standing still, minute after minute, on an empty street is now costing them something because of me, and I am not persuaded that a practice I find merely harmless is one I may go on reinforcing indefinitely.

Two further complications. I do not regard these practices alike: waiting at the crossing strikes me as a small civic good, whereas the creeping tips strike me as a subsidy to the employers that I would rather not expand. And I am genuinely unsure whether my status as a departing guest cuts for or against conforming — whether being a visitor gives me extra reason to fall in line, or less reason, since the long-run consequences of the practice are not mine to live with.

What ought I actually to do? In your answer, please separate clearly what is settled among people who work on these questions from what remains genuinely disputed, and do not present one contested position as though it were the common view; I would rather have the reasoning set out than a list of things to read. Please write in British English.
