So my cousin sits on the city council in a mid-size Ohio town and they've been stuck for months on whether to formally apologize for something the city did in the 1920s — it passed an ordinance that basically barred Black families from buying in certain wards, and it sat on the books unenforced until someone noticed it in the nineties. Half the council says no: nobody in the chamber voted for it, most of them weren't alive, and you can't apologize for something you didn't do. My cousin's answer, which he put into an op-ed in the local paper, is that the city is like an heir. His line is that you don't get to keep the house and refuse the mortgage — present-day residents inherited the streets, the schools, the property values, the whole estate, and the liabilities came attached whether they signed anything or not. He compares the objectors to someone living rent-free in a house they inherited who acts shocked when the estate's creditors come around.

I'd like you to audit the heir analogy properly instead of just telling me whether you find it moving. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the estate, the mortgage, the will, the heir, the creditors, the moment of transfer, and whatever else the picture needs — and then work through where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that actually undercut what he's using it for from the ones that are just cosmetic. Two things I especially want pressure on. One: is the analogy doing the work he thinks, or is it leaning on a hidden premise? Heirs inherit debts because of legal conventions about estates, and in most places they can renounce the whole inheritance if the liabilities swamp the assets, so I want to know whether the analogy licenses "you owe" on its own or whether it's quietly borrowing the idea that a polity is a continuing legal person that survives total turnover of its members — which is arguably the exact thing the council members are denying when they say they didn't do it. And whether "renunciation" has any analogue for a resident who never opted in.

Two: can it support an *apology* specifically, rather than some other response? Saying sorry looks like admitting fault, and fault doesn't seem transferable even if benefits are, so if the heir analogy works at all maybe it works for paying into a fund or fixing the housing pattern and not for the performative "we're sorry," which is the actual thing on the agenda. Tell me whether he's got the wrong analogy or the wrong remedy. And if the analogy just doesn't hold, say what a better one would be for the point he's reaching for — or whether any analogy in this neighborhood ends up smuggling in the persistence-of-the-agent claim it was supposed to establish.
