I am writing up a response for a graduate reading group on the philosophy of money, and I have assembled an argument about Berkeley's monetary theory whose conclusion I cannot accept, though I cannot locate the break in it. The starting point is Berkeley's own position as I read it in the Querist: money is not wealth but a token, a ticket or counter whose worth consists entirely in the use made of it — reckoning, recording, and transferring claims, and above all stirring up industry — while wealth itself is the necessaries and conveniences of life that labour produces.

The argument I have built on it:

(1) On Berkeley's account, the value of money consists wholly in its use, and money is therefore not itself wealth.

(2) If the value of a thing consists wholly in a use, then anything that serves that use serves just as well, and anything that serves it more cheaply serves better.

(3) Paper notes and bank credit serve the use at a fraction of the cost of gold and silver; so a nation gains by substituting them, and loses nothing when specie leaves it in exchange for goods that set its own people to work.

(4) What the money supply is for, then, is to leave no willing hand unemployed: there should be tokens enough in motion that every branch of industry capable of being worked is worked.

(5) Yet by (2) the tokens are in themselves indifferent, and nothing in the account fixes how many of them there should be; the only requirement is that they circulate.

(6) Multiplying the tokens without multiplying the industry they are meant to stir raises prices rather than employment: a supply large enough to leave no hand idle is, on any plausible account of value, large enough that each token commands less than it did.

(7) So either (1) is false and money does have worth of its own, as the writers who called it a commodity or a pledge maintained, or (1) must be read as already containing a limit on quantity that (2) then discards — in which case the functional thesis does no independent work, and Berkeley's case against specie reduces to a claim about how much money a country ought to have.

I want to keep (1), which is the reason I came to Berkeley, and I also want to keep (6), since a government that issued tickets without limit would plainly wreck the industry the tickets exist to promote. I cannot tell whether the argument is invalid, with some step resting on a reading its neighbours do not support, or valid, with a plausible premise I ought simply to abandon.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the step you reject looked innocent at the point where it was introduced, whether the position that survives is still Berkeley's or has quietly become the position of the writers who treated money as a commodity or a pledge, and, if your repair requires reading some step in a sense the argument did not intend, what licenses that reading. I am not after quotations, references, or a survey of the literature — only the diagnosis. Please write in American English, and begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, followed by the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
