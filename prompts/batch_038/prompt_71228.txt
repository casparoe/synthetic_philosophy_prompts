my quals committee wants me to write down exactly where I've landed on biological function before they attack it, so here's the list. I want an audit, not a lecture: tell me which of these actually can't coexist and which only look like they can't, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each bad one give me the cheapest revision plus what that revision costs me downstream. I genuinely don't know where it breaks and I'm not going to signal which item I'd drop, so please don't assume I've already conceded anything.

(1) Wright's core insight is right and I don't want to give it up: a trait's function is the effect that explains why the trait is there. Etiology, not mere contribution.

(2) But I don't want to pay for an actual ancestral history. I'm attracted to something like the Bigelow-Pancer propensity move: the function of a trait is what it's disposed to do such that doing it would tend to cause the trait's retention. Etiological in spirit, no selection story about ancestors required.

(3) Malfunction has to come out as genuine normative failure in the individual right now. The fibrillating heart in front of me is failing at something it's supposed to be doing, not merely siting at a low point in a distribution. So I reject the Boorse strategy of cashing dysfunction as statistically subnormal performance.

(4) I'm also a pluralist: the Cummins causal-role notion is legitimate in its own right and it's the one I actually use when reading mechanistic papers in molecular bio. I don't think the liberalism objection — that on a causal-role account everything a thing does becomes a function — has any purchase on me, since I don't ask that notion to be normative.

(5) Function claims are triadic. What counts as a function is always relative to a containing system and one of its capacities; there's no containing-system-free fact of the matter.

(6) Naturalized normativity only. Nothing irreducibly normative exists; the ought-ness of function has to be manufactured out of the etiology. This is load-bearing for me because I want teleosemantics to work: I want the content of a representation to be its proper function, and I want misrepresentation to be a real failure rather than a manner of speaking.

(7) Functions aren't necessarily unique. Genuinely pleiotropic traits have several proper functions at once, and I refuse to say there's one weighted disjunction that is the function.

(8) Here's a case from my own bench work that I can't shake. I keep a strain carrying a plasmid-borne fluorescent protein. It lives in a chemostat, and every few days we sort by fluorescence and throw away the non-expressors. So the trait is reproducibly established, and it's retained in virtue of an effect it has. On (1)+(2), the function of the protein is to get detected by our sorter. I have the intuition that this is wrong. But I wont say the sorting regime isn't a selection environment, and I won't smuggle in designer intentions to fix it, because then artifact functions and biological functions come apart and I lose (6).

(9) Method: philosophers shouldn't legislate usage, and I'm fine being revisionary about what working biologists mean by function. But my account has to be usable by people running experiments — it should tell them something they can go and check.

So: where does this break? I especially want you to be explicit about (2) with (3), and about whether (6) survives if (8) forces a change. Also flag anything you think only looks like a problem and say why it's actually fine. And then tell me what would change your mind — which consideration, if I pushed on it, would make you reorder the severity ranking or say a different item is the one that has to go — and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
