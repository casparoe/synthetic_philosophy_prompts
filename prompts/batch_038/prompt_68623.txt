I've been going around in circles on when a contrastive historical question is even a real question, using the divergence literature as my testbed, and I want to find out whether my gut verdicts add up to a principle. I'm not asking you to explain the Industrial Revolution. I'm asking about the questions.

Here's my data. Everyone asks "why Britain and not China?" and I've developed opinions about that and about a pile of nearby questions:

1. "Why did industrialization take off in Britain rather than in the Yangzi delta?" — fine. Sharpest version I know of, because both sides are actual regions you can hold fixed at the same date.
2. "Why Britain and not China?" — badly posed as literally stated. Not unanswerable, just malformed: "China" is a civilization-scale polity standing in for one advanced corner of itself, and the corner keeps changing depending on what the asker wants.
3. "Why Britain and not Belgium?" — worse, and this is the one that bugs me. Belgium shares the most with Britain of anything on this list: same century, same machinery, next door, industrialized second. By my own criteria that should make it the best question available. Instead it feels trivial.
4. "Why England and not Holland?" — fine, actually a favorite. I think it's because Holland is a case where the alternative nearly happened and we know a lot about why it stalled.
5. "Why Britain and not the Ottoman Empire?" — worse than 4, better than 2, and I can't articulate why. Something about there being a hundred differences, none of which is obviously the one doing the work.
6. "Why did it happen in the eighteenth century and not the fourteenth?" — fine, even though the foil isn't a place and there's nothing sitting there to compare against.
7. "Why did industrialization happen at all?" — not a question. No foil.
8. "Why the Yangzi delta and not Lancashire?" — same as 1 with the polarity flipped, and I insist this is NOT the same question. Different question, different answer. Any account that treats contrasts as symmetric is dead on arrival for me.
9. "Why Britain and not Scotland?" — feels silly, but I'm honestly not sure it should, since Scotland shares basically everything.

My tentative principle, for you to break: a contrastive question of the form "why P rather than Q" is well-posed only if Q is a genuine alternative in the same possibility space as P, at the same time, sharing a background that isn't itself what you're trying to explain, and there's an identifiable difference-maker between them that could plausibly have gone the other way.

What I want:

- The repaired or replacement principle stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case from my list that motivates each clause and what each clause rules out. A clause that only earns its keep on one of my nine cases is a warning sign, not a feature — I'm most easily persuaded by clauses that do work in several places.
- Whether 3 and 4 can both be saved. My hunch is that the principle needs something like an independence condition — Belgium industrialized partly *because* Britain did, so it isn't a separate draw from the same urn, and a contrast between non-independent trials doesn't license the usual inference to a difference-maker. But I can't tell if that's a real clause or just me rationalizing a gut reaction. Tell me which.
- Which of my verdicts you'd overturn, and what you'd replace them with. I'd rather you say 6 is incoherent or 9 is better than I think than preserve all nine with a baroque machine.
- Whether 5's awkwardness is the same defect as 2's or a different one. I suspect "too many differences, none privileged" and "the foil isn't the right size" are two separate failures and my principle only names the second.
- Predictions on cases I didn't list: "why Japan and not China around 1900?", "why did mechanization start in cotton and not in iron?", "why Britain and not France?" — plus at least two more of your own that you think stress the principle hardest, including one you expect it to get wrong or to say something uncomfortable about.
- The payoff. Does anything survive of the Needham question, or was the famous framing malformed from the start, with only the region-versus-region versions salvageable? And does the standard revisionist move — dropping "China versus Europe" and comparing the Yangzi delta with England, then blaming the divergence on where the coal was and on New World land — count as *fixing* the question, or as quietly swapping in a different, more tractable question while keeping the old name? There's a real worry here that the field answered a question nobody asked.
