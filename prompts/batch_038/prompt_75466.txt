Last month I was sworn in as one of seven volunteer members of the historic preservation commission for a mid-sized river city in the Midwest. I am a network engineer by training, the only commissioner without a background in architecture or history, and I took the seat largely because my grandmother's storefront was demolished in 2019 over the objections of a group I had never heard of at the time. Two cases came before us in the same evening session, and I have not stopped thinking about the contrast. The first was a wooden-hulled ferryboat built in the 1920s that a maritime museum has maintained continuously: every plank, frame, deck beam, and fastener that has rotted has been replaced with new stock worked to the same shape and joined the same way, and the museum's own file suggests only a small minority of the original timbers survive. We voted unanimously that the vessel keeps its designation. The second was a stone house from the 1870s with essentially all of its original fabric intact, whose owner asked permission to move it two blocks uphill to save it from a road widening; our staff recommended against, on the ground that the guidance we work from treats a building removed from its original site as having lost something essential and therefore ineligible, unless it satisfies one of a handful of narrow exceptions.

The rulebook we apply is a state manual keyed to the federal integrity test, which asks us to assess a nominated property along seven headings — location, design, setting, materials, workmanship, feeling, and association — and holds that a property must retain enough of them to convey why it matters. In practice the manual and our staff treat those headings very unequally. Materials are treated as almost infinitely renewable, provided the new work matches the old in shape and texture and, where possible, in substance, and provided the commission approves the work in advance; workmanship appears to be the heading that actually carries the weight, since replacement executed in the old manner is credited to the property rather than counted against it. Location, by contrast, is treated as close to non-negotiable for buildings, and rebuilding a lost structure from scratch is disfavored outright except where the copy is exceptionally well documented and exceptionally important. So we managed, in a single evening, to rule that a vessel most of whose matter is new is the same vessel we listed, while a house most of whose matter is old would cease to be the house we listed if it were carried two hundred meters. Our chairman, when I raised this afterward, said that preservation concerns significance rather than substance, which I found unsatisfying: significance looks like a property of a story we tell about a thing, and I cannot see why the story survives relocation but not replacement.

What I want is a reconstruction of the theory of identity this practice actually presupposes, rather than a retelling of the usual puzzle about the Athenian ship. Please give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Between them they should identify the account of what makes a designated property the same property over time that our voting pattern only makes sense under — continuity of form, continuity of workmanship, a causal-historical chain, the persistence of a functional role, the persistence of whatever the seven headings jointly track, or something else; state which rival accounts the practice positively rules out as opposed to those it merely declines to use; say whether the pairing of replaceable materials with irreplaceable location is genuinely inconsistent or reconcilable on a single account; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, since I suspect several incompatible theories license the very same votes; and tell me what would have to change in our rules, and in what we would be prepared to call a designated property, if the commission adopted a strict matter-continuity view instead.
