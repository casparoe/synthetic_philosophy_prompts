I'm giving a talk at a grad work-in-progress group in a couple weeks and I want someone to stress-test my package on moral worth before I say it out loud in public. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. An action has moral worth iff it's motivated by de re concern for the features that actually make it right — not by the thought that it's the right thing to do. So Huck has it, and the person who acts because "duty says so" doesn't.
2. Moral worth comes in degrees, and the degree tracks robustness: how wide a range of counterfactual situations the agent's motive would still have got it right in. Strength of feeling is irrelevant.
3. Praise is fully accounted for by moral worth — to say an agent deserves praise for an act is just to say the act had moral worth.
4. Nobody deserves praise or blame for what's outside their control, and that includes their upbringing, their temperament, and which moral community they were socialised into.
5. An agent can have moral worth while holding systematically false moral beliefs, so long as those beliefs dont distort their responsiveness to the relevant features.
6. Rightness and motive are separate axes: motives bear on the agent, never on whether the act was right.

Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do — I'm fairly sure at least one of the tensions I worry about is a nothing-burger, but I can't tell which, and I don't want to be told they're all equally bad. Rank them by severity, say what would have to give in each case and what that costs me, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Finally: where is your own analysis most likely to go wrong, and what argument would make you rank these tensions differently than you just did?
