Two students in my philosophy-of-science seminar have been arguing for weeks about whether chemistry reduces to quantum mechanics, and they have narrowed the dispute to a single test case: the definite shapes of molecules. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Marta: a molecule's exact Hamiltonian is invariant under parity and under permutations of identical nuclei, so its energy eigenstates are symmetric or antisymmetric delocalized superpositions — for ammonia, the two combinations of the mirror-image pyramidal arrangements — and none of them describes a molecule sitting on one side of the inversion barrier. The pyramidal structure that chemists measure and invoke to explain reactivity is therefore not derivable from the fundamental theory except by an idealization (clamping the nuclei) or by appeal to something outside the molecule. Molecular structure is emergent, not reduced.

(2) Dev: that is what successful reduction looks like. Reduced theories come out true only in limits or under idealizations — the ideal gas in kinetic theory, point masses in celestial mechanics — and both the clamping approximation and environment-induced decoherence are themselves obtained by applying quantum mechanics to larger systems. Nothing foreign enters.

(3) Marta: those two defenses do not cohere. If what pins down a structure is decoherence, then one and the same intramolecular Hamiltonian is compatible with different stable structures depending on the surroundings, and the shape owes as much to the environment as to the microphysics.

(4) Dev: multiple realizability means one higher-level property with many lower-level realizers. This is the reverse: one lower-level description plus boundary conditions determining which higher-level property obtains. That is a point for reduction, not against it.

(5) Marta: reduction is supposed to explain, not merely to entail. An entailment that runs through environmental degrees of freedom leaves the molecule's own quantum state explanatorily idle, which is why chemists get along without it — and a reduction that cannot account for the autonomy of the reduced theory is just a redescription.

(6) Dev: on that criterion no reduction in the history of science has ever succeeded, which is a reason to reject the criterion rather than the reduction.

They asked me to break the tie and I could not. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six steps, say who is right at that step and why, then say who is more right overall and what both of them are missing.
