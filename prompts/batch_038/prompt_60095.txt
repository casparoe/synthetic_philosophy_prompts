I work as a data analyst at a mid-size language-learning app (a couple million users). Our growth team ships tons of small changes and we decide what to keep based on internal A/B tests. Last month I ran one on a streak reminder: we wanted to see whether pushing it at 8pm instead of 7am would raise the share of people who come back the next day. After the planned two weeks, roughly 40k users per arm, the difference went the right way but the p-value was 0.09, so under our team's rule we would of scrapped it. I told my manager, who said "let's just leave it running one more week, it looks promising." After week three it was 0.041 and we shipped. Two details worth adding: shipping a change costs maybe two days of engineering time and a dud basically costs nothing beyond that, whereas missing a real winner means leaving retention on the table forever. And we happen to have four years of results from all ~60 tests a year sitting in a spreadsheet, losers included.

Here's where it gets awkward. A new colleague who came from a research background pulled me aside afterward and said the 0.041 essentially means nothing, that what we did is worse than useless, and that if I actually want to know how likely the change is to work I have to say what I believed before the test and update on the data. My manager's reply was that she doesn't want to hear about anyone's personal beliefs, she wants an objective, defintive number, and anyway the same procedure has served the company fine for years. I'm stuck in the middle and I don't fully buy either of them. I can see that the "objective number" she wants isn't quite an answer to the question she's actually asking, but I also can't see how my colleague's number is anything other than made up: if you asked five people on the team for a prior you'd get five different ones and we'd just be arguing about who guessed better. Separately, something else has been bugging me — of the tests that clear our threshold, I'd bet most don't really do anything, because whenever someone re-runs an old winner a few months later on fresh users it usually fizzles out.

Please act as someone who has spent a lot of time on both the technical side (designing and analyzing experiments) and the philosophy of statistics, and think carefully rather than giving me the standard textbook answer. Start by brainstorming the separate problems tangled up in this story before you advise — I strongly suspect there's more than the obvious one, and that some of them aren't really about our numbers at all. Then tell me concretely: (1) what should I have concluded at week two versus week three, and is there any legitimate reading of the three-week result, given how we got there? (2) is my colleague right that a prior-based number is the honest answer, or is my manager right that it's subjective decoration — and if priors are contestable, what would make one better than another, given what we already have sitting in that spreadsheet? (3) what would you actually change about how our team runs and evaluates these tests, knowing we ship about 60 a year and that my manager will never accept a workflow where the verdict depends on who's doing the analysis? Be specific enough that I could bring it to a meeting on Monday. No references or reading lists needed, I just want the reasoning.
