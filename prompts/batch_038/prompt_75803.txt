Write a dialogue between two characters who disagree about evidential cooperation in large worlds — roughly, the idea that if the universe is big enough to contain a vast number of near-copies of you facing near-copies of your decision, then your own choice is strong evidence about what all those other agents choose, so you have reason to cooperate with beings you could never causally influence or hear back from, even when defecting is locally dominant.

The two characters:
- One actually buys this. Not as a vibe or a hedge — they've thought hard about why conditioning on your own action gives you real leverage over payoffs that are, in any ordinary sense, already fixed and out of reach.
- The other thinks it's evidential decision theory smuggling in a fake causal arrow, and that once you say plainly what the correlation between two agents' choices is supposed to consist in, it either evaporates or turns into something a causal decision theorist can acknowledge while changing nothing about what they do.

Give them a concrete case to fight over instead of abstractions. Something like: two agents, in regions of the universe that cannot exchange signals with each other, each choosing between contributing one unit to a pool that pays ten units out to some agent in the other region, or just keeping the unit. Both know there are on the order of a googol agents out there running broadly the same decision procedure on broadly the same problem, and both know a chunk of those agents have been perturbed by local noise in ways that push them toward defecting — so the correlation is imperfect and neither of them is allowed to pretend otherwise.

The dialogue has to actually engage these three things rather than skate past them:
1. Whether it even makes sense to condition on your own action when that action is the deterministic output of a procedure you didn't freely pick — should you be updating on "I chose contribute," or on "I'm the sort of thing that contributes"? One of them should press this hard, and the other should have a real answer, not a dodge.
2. Whether the correlation comes from a common cause in the shared past, in which case a causal agent can just condition on it and move on with their life, or from the two agents being the same computation instantiated twice, in which case the two characters need to say what the difference is supposed to amount to.
3. How much the imperfect-copy business matters — if only some small fraction of that googol are close enough to you that your choice genuinely predicts theirs, does the case for cooperating collapse, and if so, where does the cutoff go and who has to justify it?

Some ground rules:
- Nobody gets to be a strawman. Both are sharp, both have thought about the same cases, both are allowed to make the other one revise. Each of them should concede at least one genuine sub-point and adjust their position, rather than just restating it louder.
- Real back-and-forth: each turn should respond to what the previous turn actually said. No parallel monologues.
- Don't name any actual philosophers, papers, or stock thought experiments from the literature, and don't cite anything. Just make up names for the two of them.
- Around 800 to 1100 words. Light stage direction is fine but it should be mostly talk.
- Don't resolve it. But do end somewhere narrower and sharper than where it started, so a reader could state exactly what's left that the two of them still disagree about.
