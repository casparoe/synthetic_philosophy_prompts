For thirty-nine years I worked in a solid-state laboratory, and most of what I claimed to know concerned things I never saw: the arrangement of atoms in a crystal inferred from spots on a plate, a density of states inferred from the shape of a curve, a population of defects inferred from the way a sample eventually failed. I was never troubled by the ontology, because the instrument always answered back. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy seriously, and Benacerraf's dilemma has got under my skin in a way I did not anticipate. Both halves of it look, to a physicist's eye, like difficulties I lived with comfortably for decades. In "What Numbers Could Not Be" he argues that the natural numbers admit of more than one equally adequate reconstruction in set theory, which resembles nothing so much as the underdetermination of a theoretical entity by its observable effects; and in "Mathematical Truth" he argues that if numbers are causally inert abstract objects, no account of knowledge that works for the physical world can explain how we know anything about them, which resembles the standing challenge of explaining how we know about electrons. I have been told repeatedly — most recently by a former colleague who now writes in the philosophy of science — that the resemblance is exact and the dilemma therefore dissolves: we have always known about entities we cannot touch directly, mathematics is continuous with natural science, and Benacerraf's demand for causal contact is the residue of an empiricism nobody holds any longer.

I should like that audited rather than accepted, and audited in the specific terms I find myself reaching for at the bench. There are in fact three analogies loose in my reading, and I suspect they are being run together. The first is my colleague's: mathematical objects stand to mathematics as electrons stand to physics — unobservable, posited, confirmed only through their consequences, known holistically if at all. The second is my own, which I offer with some embarrassment: numbers leave tracks. An electron leaves a track in a cloud chamber, and we read off its charge and momentum from the track; three appears to leave tracks as well — three counts in a detector run, a threefold axis in a diffraction pattern, three millilitres delivered by a pipette — and we read the arithmetic off those in the same indirect fashion. The third comes from structural realism in the philosophy of physics: we never knew what an electron is in itself either, only the role it plays within a structure, so if the Zermelo and von Neumann reconstructions disagree about which set the number three is, that is no worse than our ignorance of the intrinsic nature of the very things we detect every day.

Please lay out each analogy explicitly — a correspondence table would be welcome — identifying what plays the role of the detector, of the track, of the intervening theory, and of the possibility that the instrument simply refuses to cooperate; then separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the conclusion my colleague wants from those that are merely decorative. Several questions interest me particularly, though I do not want you confined to them. Could any observation of mine have contradicted the arithmetic, in the way a missing spot on the plate would have contradicted my proposed structure? If the tracks of the number three are really patterns in physical systems, has the analogy quietly substituted knowledge of a concrete regularity for knowledge of an abstract object — and where does that leave the platonist's further claim, that the same structure holds of systems nobody ever built? Is the structural-realist manoeuvre a solution to the identification problem or a surrender of it, since in physics I was content to know only the structure precisely because I still wanted a claim about something that behaves, whereas in mathematics the structural role seems to be all there is to be right or wrong about? And finally, tell me plainly whether the naturalist conclusion survives: is there after all no special access problem for mathematics, or has the analogy been carrying that conclusion the entire way? If all three analogies fail, I would rather be told that a better one exists somewhere in laboratory practice, and what it is, than be assured that mathematics has no empirical analogue whatsoever — though if that is the honest answer, say so, and say which of my physicist's instincts is the one misleading me.
