I am a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive science, and I co-organize our department's cross-disciplinary reading group. This term the group is taking up von Neumann's short, unfinished comparison of automata with the nervous system — the Silliman lecture material published posthumously in 1958 — and I have been handed the ninety-minute opening session, which runs a week before anyone has read a page of it. I will have roughly twenty people in the room: a mix of computational neuroscience and CS students, three or four philosophers, and a couple of psychology undergraduates who signed up because the flyer said something about minds and machines. Every one of them already carries the crude version of the thesis — that the brain is a computer, just a wet and slow one — and none of them has encountered the much more interesting and much more two-sided argument von Neumann actually makes. I do not want to open with a lecture that hands them his conclusions, because the conclusions are the least valuable part; what is valuable is the sense of how strange the trade-offs are. So I want an activity in which they run into those trade-offs physically, as participants, and only learn afterwards what they were enacting. Please do not build anything that depends on quoting him verbatim; a faithful paraphrase of the moves he makes is all I need.

The three things I want them to have felt, without having been told, are these. First, the sheer indignity of the individual component: a neuron is a threshold device that fires all-or-none and then sits through a recovery period, and it is thousands of times slower than the artificial switching elements available to him, so that any account of what the system can do has to be built on top of components that look hopeless on their own. Second, the inversion this produces: the nervous system appears to trade precision for reliability, in the opposite direction from our machines, which demand ever higher precision from each step in order to be trustworthy at all, and it buys that reliability through width rather than depth — redundant channels, averaging, something like a restoring vote among parallel elements — so that a corrupted component costs the system a small increment of noise instead of the collapse of the whole computation. Third, the price of that purchase: wide-and-noisy architectures pay in latency, in the loss of any exactly reproducible trace of how an answer was reached, and in the fact that their outputs are graded and statistical rather than exactly right or exactly wrong. I have a rough mechanism in mind and I would like you to make it work, or to replace it with something better. Participants themselves are the components. Two teams are given the same task and the same total budget of component-people, but under different imposed designs: one team is forced into a short chain of high-precision stages, the other into a broad, redundant arrangement of cheap, noisy, slow stages that must somehow reconcile their outputs. Every component is subject to a perturbation schedule that the facilitator runs covertly — a distraction, a muffled transmission, a stage knocked offline for one round — and the teams are scored with points rather than money, on both accuracy and elapsed time measured with a phone stopwatch. The task should be designed so that the failure modes come out qualitatively asymmetric, with the precise chain degrading catastrophically and the noisy parallel arrangement degrading gracefully; that is the heart of the exercise, and it is also the part I am not sure how to arrange, since the same task has to be fair to both teams while still making their error signatures look completely different. Please solve that tension explicitly rather than leaving it to me.

What I need back is a runnable package. Give me the full rules and a materials list that assumes nothing more than paper, pens, a stopwatch, a whiteboard, and a room I can rearrange; the scoring scheme, including how you weight accuracy against latency and how you prevent the perturbation schedule from being reverse-engineered by whichever team contains the sharpest CS student; a timing table across the ninety minutes; and the deliberate reframing that keeps the words computer and brain out of the room until the end, since I want the reveal to do real work. Then give me the debrief as a mapping rather than a summary: for each phase of the activity, which feature of von Neumann's argument the participants have just inhabited, and what question to ask them at that moment to make the connection surface from their side. Tell me plainly what will go wrong the first time I run this with twenty people and how to handle it — I am already worried about the redundant team discovering majority voting before I want them to, about the precise team growing bored or resentful, about stopwatch disputes, and about the possibility that the whole thing turns into a contest of athleticism rather than an experiment in design. Tell me which of the three points above the activity cannot actually reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply verbally in the closing minutes; my suspicion is that having intelligent, self-correcting human beings play the role of unreliable components quietly smuggles in exactly the reliability the exercise is meant to show as absent, and I would like you to say whether that objection is right and what to do about it. Finally, give me a fallback: if the precise serial team wins on the first round, how do I recover the point in the second without it looking as though I rigged the outcome.
