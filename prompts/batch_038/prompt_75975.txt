So here's a position I've ended up defending in a couple of seminars and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The strong version, not the watered-down one. Three claims:

1. there's no such thing as a purely aesthetic judgement that isnt already a claim about how life should be organized — taste is never innocent of the world it sorts people into;
2. a work that insists on its own autonomy is doing propaganda too, just for the arrangement that lets it look autonomous; neutrality is a partisan position with better manners;
3. so the first question about any work is what it's recruiting the audience into, and the formal questions come second.

I take this to be roughly what Du Bois was up to when he argued in the mid-twenties that all art is propaganda and always will be, whatever the purists say — and I know his own version is subtler than mine, because he also insists the artist has to be free to choose, which sits awkwardly next to claim 2. That tension is part of what I want pressed on.

What would count against this? Not the generic "but beauty!" objection — I want specific defeaters: cases the view can't describe, results that would undercut it, rival accounts that explain the same material better. How does it fare against the Locke-ish line that propaganda is dictated from outside while art is self-expression, and against reception evidence about whether audiences actually get recruited by a work at all? What does my view predict that its rivals dont? And which of these would a committed defender concede as a real cost versus wave off — is claim 2 just a self-sealing move that redescribes every counterexample as further evidence, and if so is that fatal for a thesis of this kind or is that how it's supposed to work? Rank them fatal down to merely embarassing. What would move me most is a single work whose aesthetic achievement seems to require refusing its own message — tell me whether such a thing is even describable on my terms.
