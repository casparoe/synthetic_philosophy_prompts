You are a philosopher who works on autonomy, paternalism, and the ethics of influence. I would like you to audit a position of mine rather than survey the literature on it. I am drafting a short paper on where manipulation stops and legitimate persuasion begins, and before I commit to it in public I want to know whether my commitments can all stand together.

Here they are:

1. The wrongness of manipulation lies in the mechanism rather than the result. An influence attempt is manipulative to the degree that it is engineered to work better the less the target attends to how it is working on her, and it can therefore be objectionable even when she ends up delighted with the outcome.
2. Lying is a separate wrong with its own explanation. I do not reduce lying to manipulation, nor manipulation to lying: there are lies that are not manipulative and manipulations that involve no falsehood.
3. Default-based choice architecture — automatic pension enrolment, opt-out organ donation, the arrangement of a canteen — is legitimate even when deployed paternalistically, since the alternatives stay available and the default generally tracks what the person would want on reflection.
4. The disappointed sigh, the strategic sulk, and the tally of everything one has sacrificed for the other are genuinely manipulative and objectionable, even though nothing false is asserted and nothing is concealed.
5. Manipulation does not require a manipulator. A recommendation feed shaped by an engagement objective can manipulate its users with no one intending it, and I want to call this manipulation proper rather than a loose analogy.

Please work through this carefully. Before rendering a verdict, sketch briefly what each commitment is doing and what would motivate someone to hold it. Then identify which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do — I expect at least one of the tensions to dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely, and I would rather you said so than pad the list. Rank the real tensions by severity instead of treating them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision costs. If one commitment is doing double duty, so that two apparent problems are really a single problem, point that out. Finally, tell me which one revision would clean up the most, and whether the package that survives it is still recognizably the view I began with.
