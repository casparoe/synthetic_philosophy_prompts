I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a national paper, and this week I am working on a piece about why people stop believing that institutions are entitled to their obedience — courts, police, election administrators, public-health agencies. The spine of the column is going to be Pascal, largely because everybody from constitutional scholars to newsletter writers has been invoking him this season, and I would rather get him right than merely fashionable. My editor has cleared a passage from a well-regarded general intellectual history of political thought that sits on my desk, and I intend to quote it and then take it apart. Before I do, I want an accuracy audit, because something in the passage sits badly with me and I cannot yet tell whether my discomfort is a genuine objection or a hunch I have no standing to act on. The passage runs:

'Pascal was the first major European thinker to notice that law rests on nothing more solid than force wearing the costume of justice. Because no two peoples agree about what is right — his famous observation that truth becomes error once you cross a mountain range — there is in the end no such thing as natural justice, only the customs of a particular people, hardened by time into the appearance of principle. What we call a law is simply the arrangement that has been in place long enough that nobody remembers who imposed it, and obedience to it is a habit of the imagination rather than a duty owed to anything real. Pascal draws the consequence without flinching: since we cannot make the just strong, we make the strong just, and the sensible man submits to the powers that be not because they are legitimate but because the alternative is civil war, the worst of all evils. In saying so he dissolved natural law two centuries before legal positivism got around to it, and he did so for better reasons than the positivists ever managed.'

Some of that I am confident is right, and I expect a good deal of it is right in a way that survives scrutiny. But I would like it gone through sentence by sentence, and I want to know what I am signing my name to. Specifically:

- Which sentences would a serious Pascal scholar accept as a fair gloss, which would make them wince at a simplification, and which are simply false? Please be willing to say that a sentence is fine rather than manufacturing faults.
- Does Pascal actually deny that there is such a thing as natural justice, or does he deny only that corrupted human beings can reliably identify it and build stable law on it? Is that distinction load-bearing for my column, or is it a scholarly nicety with no consequences?
- The line about making the strong just: is it a description of what established regimes have in fact done, an instruction to princes, or something else again? The book's author clearly reads it as Machiavellian counsel, and I want to know whether that is a defensible reading or a category error about the sentence's grammar.
- Relatedly, the passage says law rests on force. I have an impression Pascal thought force could get bodies to comply but could not get minds to assent, and that this is precisely why custom and imagination have to do the work. Is that impression correct, and if so how badly does the passage's framing suffer from missing it?
- Is Pascal's recommendation of obedience prudential, normative, or theological — and does he think the authority of custom can survive being traced back to where it came from?
- "First major European thinker" — howler, or defensible shorthand? I would rather not name names in print if I do not have to, so tell me what the alternative candidates are and whether the claim collapses entirely or merely shrinks.
- Does the closing claim — that he dissolved natural law and anticipated positivism — survive once the earlier sentences are corrected? If it partly survives, sketch what an honest version of it would look like.

Two practical notes. I am not asking for fragment numbers, editions, translators, or a reading list; I have a researcher handling sourcing, and I am aware that any wording I quote will depend on the translation I choose. And for what it is worth, my own instinct, which I hold loosely, is that Pascal is a melancholic rather than a relativist, and that his conservatism is resignation dressed up as wisdom — so please tell me plainly if that instinct is what is distorting my reading of the passage rather than the passage distorting Pascal. Close with a paragraph, of a length I could print, that says the defensible thing about Pascal on justice and force without overclaiming his modernity.
