Been sitting on a draft for two months and it's gone dead in my hands, so I want to try generating a completely different version of the same engine — you don't need to know anything about my book, just write me a standalone short story, call it 2,000 to 2,500 words, out of some machinery I've been carrying around.

The borrowed part: Nietzsche's 1874 meditation on the use and disadvantage of history for life — the three kinds of history, monumental and antiquarian and critical, and specifically what each of them turns into when it gets too big. The monumental one starts lying by analogy and makes the past into a costume you can wear. The antiquarian one starts preserving everything at the same level of worth, so nothing gets judged and the present suffocates under mummified reverence. The critical one has to condemn and sever and execute, and since the judge is himself made of the same stuff he's condemning, he can't stay just about it for long. I also want the two states he pairs against all that: the capacity to be unhistorical, to forget and live inside a bounded horizon like an animal that isn't haunted, and the suprahistorical standpoint, where a person sees the whole process as already finished and consequently can't get up out of a chair. Don't have anyone say the words unhistorical or suprahistorical out loud. Show them.

The part I made up, which is what I actually want you to build on: the city runs an archive, and the archive has three orders, one per kind of history, and they hate each other. Seperate from them is an Office of Horizons that licenses how much past a person or a district is allowed to keep live — how far back the record still counts as bearing on what you may do today. They measure the license in generations of recall. Two generations is the working-class default and it makes for cheerful, obedient, oddly shallow people who can't be swindled by nostalgia but also can't be wronged in any way they'd recognize. Nine generations is what the aristocracy buys, and it's a kind of paralysis with good posture. Widening a district's license is rare, expensive, and irreversible.

Story: a junior assessor, her first case alone, has to rule on a petition from a river district that was dug up during flood works. Give the reader enough of what was found that the three orders' reports on it diverge wildly — the monumental order wants a name and a precedent, the antiquarian order wants the trench sealed and everything in it kept at equal weight, the critical order wants the finding published as an indictment with defendants attached, living ones if possible. And have one character in the story already at the suprahistorical end of the spectrum: someone who knows all of it, has had their license widened as far as it goes, and is perfectly, physically unable to act. I want that rendered bodily — appetite, hands, sleep — not as a mood or a speech.

Craft rules, and these are the ones I care about: nobody explains anything. No character delivers a paragraph of theory, no narrator glosses. The philosophy has to be legible only through procedure, paperwork, objects, and what people refuse to look at. End on the assessor's ruling, and make it cost her something real — I don't want a synthesis where all three orders turn out to have been partly right and everyone goes home wiser. Somebody loses.

Then, after the story, in five lines or less and outside of it: tell me which order you let win, and whether you think the licensing system itself is defensible. I'm genuinely curious whether you land where I do.
