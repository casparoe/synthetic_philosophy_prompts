In three weeks I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-religion workshop. The room is mostly Kierkegaard scholars, plus one Kantian ethicist who regards divine-command accounts as settled nonsense, and a junior person who works on moral testimony and deference.

My thesis is a naturalizing reading of the teleological suspension. I came to Kierkegaard through the rationalist-blog question of whether an agent can rationally act on a directive it cannot locally evaluate, and I argue that Problema I is a claim about deference to a source with an overwhelming track record whose current instruction is locally incomprehensible. On my reading the ethical is neither overridden nor bracketed for Abraham: his judgment that killing Isaac is wrong remains in force and undefeated, and what is suspended is only that judgment's authority to determine action. That is meant to explain his silence before Sarah, Eliezer, and Isaac, and his difference from Agamemnon, Jephthah, and Brutus, each of whom acts for a universal his community can comprehend and mourn.

I want the prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the thesis: the seven or eight objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way the person likeliest to raise it would phrase it — the Kantian's version will not be the Silentio specialist's version. For each, a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is soft, and a flag on which objections I should concede ground on during the talk rather than meet head-on. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and the most honest answer available to me if it comes. I'll supply the citations myself.
