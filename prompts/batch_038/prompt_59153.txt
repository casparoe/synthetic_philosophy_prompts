I run a monthly reading group for adults with no philosophical training, and our text this season is Berkeley's *Principles*, ending with his argument that we cannot conceive of an object existing unconceived — the tree in a wood with nobody standing in it. The group has fractured into three camps: some think the argument is obviously invalid, since they can conceive of an unperceived tree right now; some think it is true but trivial, and so proves nothing about trees; some think it proves something, though not what Berkeley claimed. I want a self-scoring sorting instrument that shows each member which reaction is actually theirs and what it commits them to.

Ten questions, two or three answer options each, plus a key that maps answer patterns onto positions. Two constraints. First, no question may use the words idealism, realism, phenomenalism, or abstraction, or name any philosopher: every question must be a concrete case — a table in a room locked for a century, the far side of the moon before any probe photographed it, a fossil of a creature that died before there were minds, my own childhood where no memory or witness survives, another person's pain at this moment, and the number seventeen as a control case on which an answer about trees may or may not generalize. Second, the instrument must keep two axes separate and only then cross them: what the conceivability premise establishes (nothing at all, a triviality about what conceiving consists in, or a real constraint on what exists), and what, if anything, makes a claim about the unobserved true. Lay out the grid first, then locate the recognizable views in it, and for each cell nobody occupies say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, flag every pair of positions your ten questions cannot pull apart. I expect at least one: the case where the tree in the wood is held in being by God's perception and the case where it is held in being by a standing possibility of being perceived should give the same answers to everything I have listed. For each such pair, either supply a tie-breaking eleventh question or explain why no case could distinguish them.
