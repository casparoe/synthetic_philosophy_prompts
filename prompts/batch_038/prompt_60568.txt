I'm drafting a post pushing back on a habit I see constantly in AI-risk writeups: enumerate several failure modes, declare them plausibly independent, multiply. My objection is that essentially all the risk sits in the multiplication step, but arguing that abstractly doesn't land with anyone. I want a toy case from elementary number theory that makes it concrete for an audience who knows what a prime is and can follow an infinite product, but has never taken a number theory course.

Build one to specification. The question should ask for the natural density of integers satisfying two conditions, each of which individually has a density your audience can be told in one sentence. The presumption of independence should then yield a specific number, and the true answer should be provably a different specific number — with the proof sketched in a handful of lines, not resting on an unproven conjecture or on a theorem nobody in the room has heard of. The gap must come from a genuine statistical dependence between the two conditions: not from one condition implying the other, not from their being jointly impossible, and not from anything a moment's thought would have ruled out. Write both the naive and the correct answers as convergent products over primes, so the audience can see exactly which factor went wrong and in which direction.

Then tell me what changes if you strip the dependence out — a nearby variant where the product rule is simply right — whether your failure is a shallow one that careful local analysis would have caught or a deep one that nothing available in advance would have, and what diagnostic you would actually trust for telling which you are facing.

Numbers first, derivations after. Five hundred words maximum.
