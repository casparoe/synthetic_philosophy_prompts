ok, I need help writing something and I'm kind of stuck on it, so bear with the long setup.

I sit on my county's charter review commission. Eleven of us, all volunteers, no pay, we've been meeting monthly for four months. Our one job is to recommend a voting method for county races to the board of supervisors. We've narrowed it to three: keep plurality as-is, switch to instant-runoff ranked choice, or switch to approval voting. There's also a fourth proposal on the table from Dale, another commissioner and a friend of mine, who wants us to seriously look at sortition — literally choosing the seven council seats by lottery from a pool of willing residents. He's not crazy, he's read stuff, but he's also using a bad argument and I don't want to say so in public.

The bad argument: at the September meeting somebody pulled up Arrow's impossibility theorum on their phone, read the first few paragraphs out loud, and announced that mathematics has proven voting can't work, so the commission is wasting everyone's time and we should just adopt Dale's lottery. Roughly half the room nodded. Nobody pushed back properly. That moment is still sitting in the middle of the table and we have a half-day public workshop next Saturday where we present the three options to the public, and I've been asked to give the opening remarks.

So: please write the opening remarks. About five minutes spoken, so maybe 700 words, skip the throat-clearing — I'll ad-lib the welcome and the thank-yous myself, start on something with a little weight.

Things that matter to me, roughly in order of how much I care:

- It has to take that September moment seriously instead of pretending it didn't happen, but it also can't just say "that guy was wrong, moving on." He wasn't entirely wrong. The theorem is a real theorem and it does say something genuinely uncomfortable, and I'd rather we own the uncomfortable part than paper over it, because if we paper over it Dale wins by default.
- No equations, no notation, no "consider a profile of preference orderings." Plain English, gestures at the logic at most. Two of the eleven are actually academics — an economist and a woman who does survey statistics for the state — and they will notice every sloppy claim, but the other nine are a retired high school math teacher, a firefighter, an ER nurse, two small business owners, a pastor, etc. It has to land for all eleven in the same sentences, not in separate tracks.
- I'm pretty sure the way out is in what the theorem assumes rather than in disputing the result. So say which assumption or assumptions are doing the heavy lifting, in ordinary words, and be honest that giving one of them up costs us something real. I don't want a speech that treats an assumption as a free escape hatch.
- It has to survive the objection I'm certain the statistician will raise from the floor: "we never rank anybody, we pick one winner per seat, so this theorem doesn't apply to us at all." That's a decent objection and it deserves an actual answer, including the honest version of what does and doesn't carry over to single-winner elections. If the correct answer is "you're right that Arrow isn't literally the relevant theorem here, but the relevant one is worse, not better" — then say that, just don't be cute about it.
- Give me exactly ONE illustration that the retired math teacher could repeat to her students the following Tuesday and have it still be true. Portable beats clever. I'd rather it be a small, slightly boring scenario that survives retelling than a brilliant one that falls apart when someone paraphrases it. You can use our own history if it helps: we currently elect by plurality, and the 2019 race for the at-large seat was won by 14 votes with a third candidate in the race who everyone agrees was not going to win, and people here still argue about it at the grocery store.
- It should NOT end up being a case for sortition, and it should NOT make Dale look stupid. He's on the panel with me on Saturday, sitting two chairs down, and he knows I'm writing this.
- It should close by pointing at what we're actually doing Saturday: comparing ways of failing, not hunting for a way that doesn't fail. If there's a version of that thought that isn't a bumper sticker, use it.

One more thing. I'm not a philosopher and I haven't done this stuff since one undergrad class. After the speech, add a short note — call it ten bullets max — listing the places where the natural thing for a layperson to say here would be flat wrong or would make a social choice person wince. I want to know the traps before Saturday, not be quietly protected from them. If I've already walked into one in the setup above, put that in the note too.
