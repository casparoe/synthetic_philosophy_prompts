I'm drafting the proposal for the third chapter of my dissertation and have four months of writing time. My half-formed idea: the debate over expected-value fanaticism treats it as a single problem located in the tails of a probability distribution, but I suspect two structurally different problems hide under that one label. The familiar one is a case where a very small probability is multiplied by an astronomically large value. The other is a case where both the probability and the value are thoroughly ordinary, but the act changes the likelihood of further similar acts, so that the expected contribution of a modest step diverges once the sequence is compounded — and here the fanatical conclusion arrives with no tail event anywhere in sight.

Please write the chapter proposal: a precise statement of the question (one problem or two, and what separates them); why the existing diagnoses and the standard remedies — discounting small probabilities, bounding the utility function, risk-sensitivity, imprecise credences — leave it open, given that each was built for the first kind of case; the method, week by week, including how I would build a minimal case of each kind and what would make the two comparable; what a successful result looks like and what would show the project failed, for instance if the second kind always collapses into the first under a suitable redescription; and a candid assessment of whether four months suffices. I'll supply the citations myself, so no bibliography — refer to positions and arguments by their content.
