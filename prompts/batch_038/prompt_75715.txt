I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly the zone where research crashes into politics, so election reform, citizen assemblies, AI governance, that beat. Seven years on it and there's an argument shape I keep walking into, from both directions, and I've never heard anyone name it, which makes me think I'm missing something obvious.

Here's the shape. Someone says: the reason this institution is broken is that the people running it are captured. They depend on donors, on the party machine, on the career ladder, on getting re-elected, so they can't exercise independent judgment about anything. Fine. Then you ask the obvious follow-up — okay, but the people you'd put in charge instead, who are they going to depend on? — and the answer is always some other supplier of information and procedure: a non-partisan secretariat, outside experts, professional staff, a facilitator, the agencies that write the briefings. And then the reformer says, in effect, that's fine, I'd rather be captured by them than by the first lot. Which is an honest answer! But notice what happened to the complaint. It started out as *dependence on an outside supplier destroys independence*, and by the end it's *dependence on the right supplier is no problem at all.* Nobody refuted anything, the argument just quietly became a different argument. It stopped being about escaping control and became about picking which elite controls you, while everyone kept talking as though it were still the first one.

Two that stuck with me. I interviewed a guy campaigning for a standing citizen's assembly — hundred-odd people drawn by lottery — and his whole pitch was that a career politician can't think straight about anything because everything reaches them filtered through re-election and the whip. I asked who'd be briefing the hundred. Neutral secretariat, outside experts, selected for balance. I asked what stops that secretariat from being where the real power sits, the way people say it already works in the civil service generally. He said: at least they're not trying to get elected. True, and completely beside the point he'd started from, because not-wanting-to-get-elected was never the criterion — not-being-shaped-by-your-supplier was.

Same structure in a term-limits fight I covered. Someone in a legislature for twenty years has been captured by lobbyists and by their own incumbency, so cap it. Objection: you'll gut institutional memory and hand the building to unelected staff who never leave. Answer: yes, and staff are professionals, they don't have donors. Again — maybe right! But the original complaint was about a body losing its independence to the people it depends on for knowledge and process, and staff are exactly that, so the reply is a preference between two dependencies dressed up as freedom from dependence.

And it isn't confined to my beat. Central bank independence: accountable politicians versus unelected economists, and when you ask who briefs the economists the answer is the banks. Handing questions to judges instead of ministers. The AI version, where the problem with corporate labs is that they're profit-driven, the fix is a public lab, and the people staffing it are seconded from the corporate labs.

So — is this a named thing? I know some of the neighbors and none of them feel right. It isn't motte-and-bailey: there's no defensible core being retreated to, the reformer isn't defending the orginal claim in a weaker form, they've swapped the criterion entirely and then carried on as though they'd won the same argument. It isn't hypocrisy, since most of them say the quieter half out loud and genuinely think they've been consistent throughout. It isn't quite moving the goalposts either, because they aren't relocating a standard to save a claim — they're conceding the standard was never really the point. And I know there's old sociology about organizations always ending up run by a small clique, and economists have a thing about regulators coming to serve the industries they regulate, but those are claims about how the world works and I'm asking about a move in an argument, the slide itself.

To be clear the label isn't the prize, though one would help me write about it. What I want is the carving. Is this already a thing people have picked out, and if so where are the edges — does it only count when the reformer introduces the second supplier themselves, or does it also cover a critic pointing out you'll need one? Is it a fallacy at all, or a perfectly legitimate change of mind being presented illegitimately, because sometimes the expert-briefed body really is the better option and I don't want to build a gotcha that fires on good arguments too. What separates this from simply discovering your principle was too broad, and what separates it from a straight tradeoff being acknowledged as one? Walk me through the nearest established concepts, say where each of them stops short of this, and if there's genuinely no term for the slide, coin one and tell me what falls under it and what doesn't — including the case where the reformer turns out to be right.
