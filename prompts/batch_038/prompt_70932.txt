I'm in the second year of a philosophy PhD, and for a seminar paper on the social ontology of language I've been trying to sharpen a distinction that my reading group keeps collapsing back into an older one. Let me stipulate the concept rather than borrow it, and please treat my definition as authoritative for the purposes of your answer. Call an utterance a *ratified misfire* when it fails to meet one of Austin's felicity conditions in a way that renders the intended conventional act void, and yet the relevant audience nevertheless converges on treating the world as having been changed — so that the act is null in the constitutive register while remaining fully operative in the descriptive one. The cases I have in mind are not merely ones where the hearers are mistaken about what happened, since error is cheap; they are cases where the participants go on to organize their expectations, entitlements, and reactive attitudes around the void act, and where that organization is stable, mutually known, and reproduced in later interactions rather than corrected at the first opportunity.

My worry is that the notion may be empty, or may collapse into something already well named. I'd like you to test it by construction rather than by argument. Give me two examples of a ratified misfire that are not drawn from the standard textbook stock: please no unauthorized wedding ceremonies, no christening of the wrong ship, no defective bequests, no forged letters of appointment, and nothing involving a gavel. The two examples should differ in which condition fails, and ideally one should involve a person purporting to act in an institutional role they do not occupy, while the other should involve the right person following the right procedure under circumstances the procedure was never meant to cover. For each, lay out (a) the concrete situation in enough detail that the failure is visible on its own terms, (b) precisely which felicity condition goes unmet and why the intended act is therefore void rather than merely hollow or abused, (c) what the ratification consists in — who does what afterward, and why that behavior is best described as uptake rather than as mistake — and (d) what the example implies about whether an act's force is fixed at the moment of utterance or is partly settled by what happens to it afterwards.

Finally, since I need to know whether the concept earns its keep: state the most damaging thing a careful reader of Austin or Searle could say against it. I want the strongest version of the charge that a ratified misfire is either a perlocutionary effect dressed up in illocutionary vocabulary, or simply a case in which a different act than the intended one succeeded on the first try, with the null act doing no explanatory work at all. Tell me which of my two examples is more vulnerable to that charge and why, rather than reassuring me that the distinction is coherent.
