I hold the philosophy seat on my university's Library and Special Collections Committee, and I have been asked to circulate a paper ahead of our meeting on the twelfth of November. The business concerns a bequest. The executors of a private collector, a former member of this university, have offered us a sealed strongbox together with a notarised schedule of its purported contents: the sole surviving run of a correspondence between the collector and the author of *The Salt Meadow*, the novel published in 1927 under the pseudonym Ivor Combe, whose authorship our English faculty has disputed for thirty years between two candidates, both of whose papers we hold. The deed of gift is unusual in two respects. It prohibits the box from being opened by anyone at any time, and it requires us to destroy it unread on 31 December 2125. The collector's covering letter, which the executors have permitted us to see, closes with the observation that the answer to the Combe question is inside the box and that no one will ever know what it is. The University Solicitor's Office advises that a covenant to destroy chattels is probably void as against public policy, but will not certify the point; and the executors have told us that if we decline the gift it passes to a residuary charity with no interest in preserving it. We must answer them by the end of Michaelmas term.

The complication is Clause 7 of the Collections Charter, which reads: "Nothing shall be entered in the catalogue as a matter of fact unless it is such that some person could, in principle, come to know it." The clause was inserted twenty-odd years ago, at the instigation of a philosopher who then sat where I sit, after an episode in which a provenance resting on family legend was recorded as fact and had to be publicly withdrawn. Our Head of Special Collections, who is not a philosopher but has a good nose for trouble, observes that if we accept the deposit we shall have to describe it, and that any description saying the box contains the Combe correspondence asserts something which, on the face of the deed, nobody will ever be in a position to know. She therefore recommends refusal. The Professor of English, who sits ex officio, recommends acceptance and, if need be, opening the box in defiance of the deed. Between them they have left the rest of the committee — the Bursar, a solicitor and a retired county archivist — unsure what Clause 7 is for. My own view, which the memo must test rather than assume, is that the Head of Special Collections has half-identified a genuine difficulty: read as a rule of cataloguing discipline, Clause 7 is worth keeping; read as a general thesis about truth, it is not survivable. For if we really held it in that stronger sense, we should have to say that there is no fact of the matter about who wrote *The Salt Meadow* — in which case the box contains nothing worth having, and the covenant to destroy it costs the university nothing. I am not confident that is right, and I am less confident still that the committee could live with it if it is.

Please draft the memo I shall circulate: not more than 1,300 words, addressed to lay members who will not tolerate formal notation and who have no patience with the word "epistemic" used decoratively. It should give a clear recommendation on the bequest — accept, refuse, or accept on terms we should specify to the executors — together with the timetable for answering them, and a clear recommendation on Clause 7, including, if it is to be amended, replacement wording drafted and ready to be moved as a resolution. In the course of that, it should do four things. First, set out in ordinary prose why Clause 7 cannot be maintained as a general claim about truth, making the reasoning explicit enough that the Bursar could repeat it to someone else afterwards, and saying what a committee which did maintain it would thereby be committed to. Second, state which sense of "could in principle come to know" the clause is to be taken to employ — what is logically possible, what is physically possible, or what is compatible with undertakings we have ourselves given — and explain why the choice matters, since a reading loose enough to admit the strongbox may be too loose to do the work the clause was inserted to do against family legend. Third, tell us whether the familiar strategy of restricting a knowability principle so that it does not apply to statements bearing on their own knowability has any purchase here, where the contents of the box are a plain empirical truth about a novelist rather than a statement about anybody's knowledge. Fourth, give the case for refusing the gift its full weight rather than dispensing with it: in particular, that a covenant to destroy makes the archive the instrument by which evidence is annihilated; that a catalogue containing certified descriptions of material nobody may ever inspect is a standing invitation to speculation; and that the first such deed will be followed by others. I do not want a reading list or a table of authorities; the committee will read neither, and any recommendation that depends on its members having read philosophy will fail.
