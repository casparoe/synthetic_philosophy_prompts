ok so I keep coming back to the Luring Lottery — Hofstadter's actual, really-run version from the June 1983 Scientific American column that got reprinted in Metamagical Themas, not the Platonia thought experiment it was leading up to. I know the setup and I know the prisoner's dilemma derivation, so skip both. What I want is a case analysis of the episode as a test of superrationality, running in both directions.

Facts as I have them, correct me if I'm off: you mail in a postcard bearing a number, and that number is how many entries you're submitting. The pot is a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, and one entry gets drawn at random. So your expected take is a million times your share of entries times the shrinking pot — which makes the individul maximizing move to submit exactly as many entries as everyone else submitted combined, which is obviously not a fixed point anyone can sit on. Hofstadter's suggested superrational play was to randomize instead: estimate how many readers are likely to respond, then submit a single entry with probability 1/that number. What actually came back in the mail was people entering googolplexes and postcards covered in expressions engineered to denote the largest number that fits in the space, so the prize dissolved into a fraction of a cent and nobody could work out who'd won.

So, do the mapping carefully first. Who are the players here, what's the real strategy space (it isn't "pick a number", it's "pick a number plus a notation system plus a guess at everyone else's notation system"), and what does the symmetric-reasoning assumption actually license in a game where the payoff rewards being the outlier rather than matching? Then the interesting part: say whether the googolplex responses falsify superrationality, or only falsify the claim that Scientific American's readership are your symmetric peers, or whether superrationality was never a theory about winning this game at all and the episode just reveals what it's actually load-bearing for. Be explicit about where the theory goes silent instead of making it talk. And compare against Platonia — does the fact that the thought experiment has a clean 1-in-20 answer while the real lottery has no equilibrium whatsoever tell you something about which of the two was doing the work in Hofstadter's argument?
