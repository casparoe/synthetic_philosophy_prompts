I'm not a just war person — my field is philosophy of mind — but a colleague in our political science department is sending an op-ed to a magazine and asked me to sanity-check the argument before he submits it. He's smart and he's obviously read something, but the paragraph below is the load-bearing part of the piece and I don't trust my instincts about it. Here it is, verbatim from his draft:

> Since Michael Walzer's *Just and Unjust Wars*, the mainstream just war tradition has accepted what he calls the moral equality of combatants: the rules about what you may do in a war apply identically to both sides, whether or not your cause is just. Walzer's argument is that individual soldiers don't pick their wars — they're conscripted or enlisted young, fed a story by their own state, and in no position to work out which side is actually right — so it would be unfair to treat them as liable for what their government decided. That's why a Wehrmacht conscript who shot at Soviet soldiers in 1943 did nothing morally wrong, and why we prosecute war criminals for how they fought rather than for fighting at all. I'd call this the referee principle: whatever you believe about who started the match, the official applies one rulebook to both corners, and a morality that wants to be usable in the middle of a fight has to do the same. The cost is that the tradition has quietly stopped asking whether a war is just and replaced that question with a code of conduct for participants. The revisionists who object — McMahan and company — say liability tracks moral responsibility for an objectively unjust threat, so soldiers on the unjust side are liable to be killed and their opponents aren't, which makes anyone fighting for an unjust cause fair game. Grant them that the moral symmetry fails: they still haven't shown you can actually run a war on asymmetrical rules. If you want a morality of war that can condemn the aggressor's soldiers, you have to give up the equal application of the rules, and there's no version of just war theory that gets to keep both.
