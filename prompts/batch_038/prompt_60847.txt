I sit on the board of a member-owned credit union in a small town, and in three weeks we vote on whether to open a small-business lending line that would charge interest. One of our directors, a retired classics teacher, says we shouldn't, and he grounds it in Aristotle rather then in scripture. I wrote his case out carefully for the board packet so people could actually engage it, and now I think it proves way too much — but I can't say where.

(1) Money exists by convention, not by nature; it was invented as a stand-in for need, so that unlike things could be exchanged in proportion.
(2) Its only natural use is to be handed over in exchange. Taking gain from money itself, rather than from what money is for, uses it contrary to its purpose — that's the whole force of the complaint that interest is money breeding money, a way of getting wealth with nothing natural behind it.
(3) So charging for the use of money is charging for a use money doesn't have.
(4) But the same structure applies to any asset whose productive power is itself conventional: a dividend on a share (a share is just an agreed-upon claim), rent on a trademark, a licensing fee for a software key. None of these produce anything by nature.
(5) If (3) condemns interest, it condemns those too.
(6) Obviously it shouldn't — the dividend on a share in a working factory is a return on something genuinely productive, and nobody thinks renting out a house is unjust.
(7) So either money does have a natural productive power, in which case interest is fine, or the natural/conventional distinction can't carry the weight Aristotle puts on it, in which case his objection to interest has no foundation.
(8) Either way we can vote yes.

Honestly I'd like to keep (2) — the idea that money shouldn't be treated as a self-multiplying thing seems worth defending, and I'd rather pay something than give it up. Tell me which single step you'd cut and why it looked innocent going in; then say what the Aristotelian owes by way of explaining why a share in a factory differs from a loan; then price keeping (2). Start with the one-line verdict on the step, then the reasoning. Under 450 words.
