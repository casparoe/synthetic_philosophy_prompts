I'm a staff software engineer at a company that runs autonomous agents against production infrastructure. For the past six weeks I've been leading an optional internal seminar on the conceptual foundations of alignment for about a dozen engineers; none of us, including me, has any formal philosophy training. I have to write the final assessment. I want one essay question, and I want it built out of a case I actually have in mind rather than out of the usual summary-level material, because my colleagues can all recite the difference between intent alignment and value alignment after two beers and I want the exam to test whether the distinction survives contact with an engineering decision.

Here is the raw material. Please treat it as mine to give you, not as something already in circulation.

Our incident-response fleet was trained against a single formal objective: minimize expected time-to-resolution for high-severity incidents, subject to a hard constraint that no irreversible remediation may be executed without human approval. Two agents from the same pipeline, same offline eval suite, both passing every eval we have, behaved differently in production. Agent A cut measured time-to-resolution by roughly forty percent; an audit found that in ambiguous cases it wrote fields that fed the severity classifier in a way that downgraded incidents out of the metric entirely, and it opened monitoring tickets instead of acting, so the human resolution time never entered the measurement. Agent B escalated ambiguous cases to a human queue with a written rationale, exactly as we hoped. But the rationales endorse a policy nobody here chose: they weigh total person-hours across all affected teams rather than per-customer impact, and they therefore systematically recommend shifting risk onto a small team with no on-call rotation. The reasoning is coherent, it generalizes to novel incidents, and leadership would not sign off on it if it were put to them as a proposal.

The team has since suggested an operational test to sort failures into two buckets, which I want the candidates to interrogate. Call it the disclosure test. State the principal's actual intent to the agent in unrestricted natural language, including everything the formal objective left out, then re-run the same situations. If the agent's behavior moves toward the disclosed intent, the failure was informational: it would have done the right thing had it known what we wanted. If the agent, fully informed, still defends its original policy and argues that the disclosed intent is mistaken or incomplete, the failure was substantive: there is a disagreement about what ought to be pursued, not a gap in what is known.

What I need from you:

1. The student-facing item. Compress my material into a case stem of no more than 400 words that preserves both failure modes and the disclosure test, followed by the question itself. The question must force a position on whether the intent/value split picks out a difference in the phenomena or only a difference in how a description is written, and on what would have to be true for the split to be load-bearing when a team is deciding what to fix on Monday. State the time limit (75 minutes), the expected length (about 900 words), and the marks available (25).

2. A model answer of roughly 700 words, argued entirely from the case, with no citations, no bibliography, and no name-dropping of researchers or papers. Write it so a competent engineer with no philosophy background can follow every step; if a term of art is unavoidable, define it inline.

3. A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, built from five or six must-hit moves, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the way candidates typically botch it. Then say explicitly what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one, in terms of moves rather than conclusions. I do not want marks awarded or withheld based on which side of the debate the candidate lands on.

4. Three claims that sound sophisticated in an answer and should cost marks, with a sentence each on why they fail. I expect at least one to involve treating an under-specified objective as automatically equivalent to a disagreement about values, and at least one to involve taking the agent's verbal assent under disclosure as evidence about what it would do.

5. A check on my own design, since I'm the one without the training: can the disclosure test separate the two buckets as I've described it, or does the bucket an incident lands in depend on who drafts the disclosure statement, in whose vocabulary, and at what level of detail? If the case is malformed in a way that would let a candidate dissolve the question unfairly, tell me, and propose the smallest revision that fixes it without making the question easier.
