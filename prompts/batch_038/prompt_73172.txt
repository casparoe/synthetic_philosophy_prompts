I am a secondary-school student writing a research essay on the foundations of probability, and I have gotten stuck on Bertrand's chord problem in a way that has made me suspicious of the whole classical project. Rather than read my way through the proposed repairs, I want first to know what I ought to be demanding of one.

The purpose I have in mind, so that adequacy is not left vague: I want to know what it would take for a claim of the form "the probability is one half" to be true of a situation in which no generating mechanism has been specified, and defensible as a fact about that situation rather than as a report of my own ignorance. That is a high bar, and it may not be reachable; part of what I want is to be told whether it is.

Set out the specification an account of probability under ignorance must meet to count as solving the paradox rather than dissolving it by fiat. I want the list, not the history:

- The phenomena it has to explain, including why the three textbook procedures over a circle and an inscribed equilateral triangle really do give one third (two endpoints chosen on the circumference), one half (a point on a radius, with the chord drawn perpendicular there), and one quarter (a midpoint chosen uniformly in the disk), and why the same move that feels obviously correct for a fair die goes wrong here.
- The cases it must handle, including the ones where a physical mechanism is genuinely given, and the factory-style variants where indifference over edge length, over volume, and over face area of the same product yield different numbers.
- The distinctions it may not collapse. Among those I already suspect matter: a problem being ill-posed versus having no determinate answer; having no reason to prefer one outcome to another versus having equal reason for each; refusing to commit to a number versus committing to a set of numbers; and symmetry serving as a test of well-posedness versus symmetry serving as a rule for picking the answer.
- The mathematical results it must not contradict. One I have in mind: no countably additive, translation-invariant probability distribution exists over the whole real line, which seems to constrain how far a general invariance requirement can be pushed.

For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it either non-negotiable or a commitment belonging to a particular camp. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates, and whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that demanding invariance under every re-description, demanding a unique number, and demanding that the rule be usable in total ignorance cannot all be had at once; if that is right, the list is itself the result, and I would like it stated plainly.

One further thing, which is the part I most want judged against the list. I invented a fourth procedure: choose a point uniformly in the disk, choose a direction uniformly, and draw the chord through that point in that direction. I coded it, and it settles near 0.61. It looks to me as though it survives both the size and the location tests that the symmetry-based repair insists on, and yet it does not return one half. If that is so, say which requirement it offends and why. If I have misdiagnosed it, explain what really separates throwing straws at a circle from throwing point-direction pairs at it, and whether the fact that my procedure effectively weights each chord by its length, longer chords containing more interior points, is a defect or a legitimate difference in the question asked.

Close with a sentence or two each on how the main repairs on offer fare against your list: stipulating the mechanism, the symmetry approach, maximum-entropy-style reasoning, accounts grounded in long-run frequencies, and the move that treats the credence as a set rather than a number. Keep that part short; the weight should be on the requirements and their motivating cases.
