I'm TAing a first-year social-science methods seminar and I've been asked to make a handout that sorts students into positions on the individualism-versus-holism debate, and every version of that handout I've ever seen just asks whether society is more than the sum of its parts, which everyone answers yes to and which tells you nothing. I want to build it out of the logical space instead of out of a list of isms, and I want you to tell me whether my space is actually the right one.

Here's the framing I've landed on. I think people who call themselves methodological individualists are really answering three different questions and conflating them:

(i) what exists — are there social entities (a firm, a class, the state, a norm against queue-jumping) over and above individuals and their doings?
(ii) what can figure in the explanans — can a good explanation of a social outcome have a macro-level fact as one of its premises?
(iii) what counts as finished — does an explanation that stops at the macro level fail outright, such that you owe a sketch of the chain running through individual agency before you've explained anything (roughly the three arrows in Coleman's boat diagram: macro-to-micro, micro-to-micro, micro-to-macro)?

Build me the tree on those axes. Rules: each node is one axis, phrased as a verdict on a concrete case, and the words ontology, supervene, mechanism, holism, individualism and reduction are banned at the nodes — a student who's never heard the labels has to be able to answer them. Draw the cases from this list: the bank run, where the best account of why I joined the queue cites the fact that everyone else was already in it; the price of wheat in England in a given year, which nobody intended and nobody could have predicted; a queueing norm that persists in a city where nobody sanctions violators and nobody can articulate why they comply; a revolution that broke out after three bad harvests rather than at the point of worst grievance; and the fact that English borrowed its word for a certain kind of gloating from German rather than the other way around.

Then give me all eight answer-combinations. Say which are occupied and by what, and name the position sitting at each leaf. At minimum I want these somewhere on the tree: the hard-liner for whom social-entity talk is just a façon de parler, the Hayekian spontaneous-order view, the Durkheimian social-facts view, the emergentist who thinks macro properties are real and causally efficacious but still wants to be a straight-up naturalist about method, and whatever the interpretivist option is where the question dissolves because the social world is made of rules and meanings rather than causes. For each empty combination, say why it's empty: incoherent, merely unattractive, or nobody's thought to go there.

Two more asks. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate rather than pretending it sorts everything — I have a suspicion the Hayekian and the Durkheimian end up adjacent on my axes even though they're supposed to be opposites, and I'd like to know if that's a flaw in the axes or a genuine finding. Second, and this is the part I most want checked: my hunch is that (iii) is the only axis doing real work, and that (i) and (ii) collapse into it, so the tree really has two leaves and six cells of bookkeeping. If you think that's wrong, argue it — hand me a recognisable position that comes apart from another one on (i) and (ii) but agrees on (iii), or concede the point. Relatedly, if you think any of my three axes isn't actually binary, say so and tell me how to split it, but keep the total number of leaves somewhere a first-year could still hold it in their head.

Oh and correct me if I've got the direction of Coleman's arrows wrong, I wrote that bit from memory.
