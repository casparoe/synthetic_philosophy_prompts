I want to work out how much epistemic deference I owe my future self, using van Fraassen's Reflection principle as the benchmark: roughly, my credence now in a proposition, conditional on my credence in it at some later time being r, should itself be r. Below are my verdicts on a batch of cases, and I want to know what principle they add up to.

Tomorrow night I will have been drinking and will be 0.9 that an old relationship is still salvageable: conditional on that, my credence now stays low. Tomorrow I will also watch a fair coin land, and conditional on my future self being 0.95 that it came up heads, I should now be 0.95. And tomorrow I will read a tabloid reporting that it came up heads and, being tired, go to 0.9 on the strength of that: conditional on that credence, I stay near 0.5. Yesterday, before the result was posted, I was 0.9 that I had failed an exam; that fact about my past self moves me not at all, even though it looks like the mirror image of the coin case. In a year I will have finished a statistics degree and will be 0.8 that a certain widely reported effect is real: I should now be 0.8, though nothing I will learn bears directly on that study. Next month I will have been persuaded by a colleague I rate well above myself on a technical question I cannot currently evaluate: here I genuinely waffle. In three months I will have quietly restricted myself to sources that flatter a diet I already committed to, and will be 0.9 it works: don't defer. Finally, a case I don't know what to do with: tomorrow I will be asked to record today's credence of mine in some claim, I know I will answer honestly, and my future credence is about a present state to which I have privileged access — Reflection then seems to require me to defer about the one thing I cannot be mistaken about.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the statistics degree and the colleague. In both, the change is driven by something other than new evidence about the hypothesis, yet I defer in one and hesitate in the other; my instinct is that the degree-holder is a better reasoner while the colleague's influence is social, but I suspect that distinction is smuggling in the answer.

Please state one principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically rather than by feel. For each clause, name the case above that forces it in. Then give its verdicts on two cases I have not raised — my future self judging a job offer after a night with no sleep, and my future self who will have discovered that a source I currently trust fabricated data — and say flatly which of my listed verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the honest result is that a conditionalization-plus-retention principle handles everything except the statistics degree, tell me whether that case is a real counterexample or an intuition I should abandon, and what the loop case does to the whole enterprise.
