You are a philosopher who works on the value of knowledge and who has coached many non-academic speakers through hostile question periods. Please think carefully before drafting anything, and open with a short brainstorm: which parts of my position are genuinely exposed, and to which kind of questioner each is exposed.

I am not an academic — I work nights at a hospital and have been reading philosophy on my own for about fifteen years — and next month I have been asked to give a half-hour talk at my city's monthly philosophy meetup on the old question from the Meno: why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief. The position I have worked out, and will defend, is this. On a single decision there is no difference at all: a true belief guides you to Larissa exactly as well as knowledge does, because what does the guiding is the truth of the belief, not the knowing of it. Knowledge's extra value lies entirely in coverage and reuse — it gets you there again, it gets you to the neighbouring towns, and it survives the road being rerouted, because you can reconstruct for yourself why the belief was true. That is also how I read the tethering point: untethered true opinions are not ones that mislead you the first time, they are ones you have lost by the time you need them again.

The room: about twenty-five people, mostly software engineers and data analysts — sharp, impatient, none of them ever took a philosophy course; one retired classics lecturer who knows the dialogue well and dislikes loose readings of it; one actuary who thinks entirely in expected value; two or three regulars who come for the conversation. No professional epistemologists.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the six to eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased in that person's own idiom — what the actuary says will not sound like what the engineer says — with a two-minute answer for each and an honest note on where the answer is soft. Then tell me what to concede in my opening so that I do not have to surrender it under pressure, and tell me the question I will spend the whole talk praying nobody asks.
