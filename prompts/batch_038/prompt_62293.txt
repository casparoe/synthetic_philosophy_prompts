I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm doing a piece on the endless wave of "what Rome teaches America" books. A classicist friend forwarded me a clip from a public lecture she went to, where the speaker compressed the whole thing like this:

"The Republic didn't fall because of one man or one battle. It fell because its politicians stopped keeping the unwritten rules that were doing the actual work. When Tiberius Gracchus pushed his land reform in 133 BC, a fellow tribune vetoed it, so he had the man voted out of office — and the Senate replied with its final decree, authorizing the consuls to do whatever was necessary to save the state. That's the first time Romans killed political opponents rather than out-arguing them. From there it escalated: two factions with rival platforms, the optimates and the populares; Sulla, the first general to march his legions on the city, who then made himself dictator for life; the free grain Caesar handed out to buy the urban mob; and finally the Rubicon. Notice that the institutions never changed — the Senate was still the Senate, the consulship was still the consulship — only the norms that made them work disappeared, and once they did the whole structure went down with barely any resistance. That's the lesson for us: it isn't the constitution that saves you, it's the habits."

She says it's broadly right but sloppy, and didn't have time to mark it up. Can you go through it claim by claim and sort it into what a Roman historian would nod at, what they'd wince at as a fair simplification, and what's just wrong? I mostly want to know which specifics would get me pilloried if I repeated them — the dates, who actually did what, that decree business, the grain, the dictatorships. Then tell me whether the moral at the end survives the corrections: if the stuff the speaker waved off (the armies and who paid them, citizenship, running an empire through a city-state's machinery) was doing real work, say so, because that changes my lede entirely.
