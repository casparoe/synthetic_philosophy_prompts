I am a junior at a high school where a student-run ethics group meets weekly, and last Thursday's topic was whether governments in countries with below-replacement birth rates ought to be encouraging people to have more children. I argued on the pronatalist side: that a world containing more people whose lives are worth living is, other things equal, a better world, and that this is a genuine reason to favor such policies. A senior on the other side cut in with, "you can't make someone better off by creating them — there's nobody you'd be doing it for." Several people nodded, the moderator moved on, and my argument never recovered.

I have the sense that the line was doing more work than it can bear, but I can't say what it was actually asserting. Could you lay out the different precise theses that sentence might stand for? I can imagine several families: a claim about what comparisons are even available when the alternative to existing is never having existed at all; a claim about who is eligible to be a beneficiary; a claim that value has to be good-for-someone in order to count as value; a claim that nobody can be owed a coming-into-existence; and possibly a stronger claim that entering existence can be neither a benefit nor a harm. I also suspect this is tangled up with something I have heard called the non-identity problem, though I am not sure whether that is the same issue or a different one that my opponent was smuggling in.

For each reading you identify, please tell me: how to state it so that it is clearly distinguishable from the others, and where two apparent readings really collapse into one; whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false or question-begging; and whether it genuinely blocks my claim that a greater number of worthwhile lives makes for a better world, or whether it only blocks a different claim, such as that having a child is good for that child. Then tell me which reading you think my opponent most likely meant, given how the line is usually deployed, and whether any of the readings would leave my original position intact if I restated it more carefully.

Please give this to me as a numbered menu I can bring to next week's meeting rather than as a general essay on population ethics, and do not bother with sources or a bibliography — I want the distinctions sorted out, not a list of who has said what.
