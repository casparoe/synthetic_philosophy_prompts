Some background first so this is answerable: my reading group did a session on the idea of a "safe Pareto improvement" — roughly, the thought that a deal which makes both sides better off on paper can still be a bad idea to adopt on your own, because if the other side isn't actually playing conditionally on you, you can end up worse off than if you'd never proposed anything. Afterward Nadia and Owen got into it about a live-ish case and I mostly shut up and took notes. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Nadia: two open source projects in our city overlap a lot. If A says "we'll drop our duplicate plugin work and focus on docs if you do the same," both projects strictly gain, so it's a Pareto improvement and A should just go ahead and do it.
2. Owen: it isn't an improvement A can count on. If B ignores the offer and keeps building the plugin anyway, A has burned months of work and is worse off than the status quo. Safety is precisely the property this deal lacks.
3. Nadia: so don't play it unconditionally, play the conditional version. A specializes only if B does, otherwise A keeps doing what it was already doing. If your move is genuinely conditional on theirs, the worst case is just the default, so you can't do worse than not proposing at all.
4. Owen: thats only true if B's move is also conditional on yours, and A has no way of knowing that. B might be three volunteers who never read the proposal. And if A somehow did know that B is the kind of agent whose strategy responds to A's strategy, A wouldn't need the deal — cooperation would already be happening. So the safety condition either fails or makes the whole apparatus redundant.
5. Nadia: you don't need certainty, just a probability. Put p on B being conditional and adopt the plan once p clears some threshold.
6. Owen: then "safe" has definately evaporated. You've turned it into an ordinary expected-value bet on the other side's dispositions, which is what any negotiator is doing anyway. The word isn't buying anything.
7. Nadia: it buys something — the point was never to get cooperation started, it's that there are lots of ways to cooperate and you need a principled way to pick which one to converge on. (Owen's answer was that this is just equilibrium selection, which proves safety was never the load-bearing part of the concept.)

Separately, at one point they argued about the baseline. Nadia said "better off than the default" is ambiguous because you have to choose a default — the non-cooperative equilibrium, the worst case A could guarantee itself, or the thing that would really have happened. Owen said it isn't ambiguous, the baseline is just whatever would actually have happened. Nadia shot back that "whatever would actually have happened" is a guess, so calling a deal a Pareto improvement is a claim about a deal plus a prediction, not a property of the deal itself.
