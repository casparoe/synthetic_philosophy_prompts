Okay so I spent basically all of last night arguing with my friend D about deplatforming and social sanction and we got nowhere, again — fourth time, and every time it ends with us saying the same things louder. She's a public defender, I do trust and safety at a mid-size platform, so you'd think we'd land near each other. We don't. Her view: getting fired, blacklisted, and stripped of your accounts over something you said *is* punishment, and what makes it grotesque is that it's punishment without any of the machinery punishment is supposed to come with — no defined offense, no proportionality, no chance to answer, no end date, nobody with authority to commute it. She waved at the philosophy-of-law checklist (Hart's criteria, she said) and then said the checklist was beside the point anyway, because what matters is the function: an organized infliction of costs on someone for a norm violation, intended to express condemnation and to deter. So for her the question in any given case is procedural — who decided, under what standard, with what chance to respond, for how long. My view: that's smuggling the conclusion in. Punishment is what an authority with standing does to you under rules you're subject to; a thousand strangers separately deciding not to hire you, not to platform you, not to date you is just each of them exercising freedoms they already had, and the aggregate isn't a new kind of act. Call it punishment and suddenly you get to demand due process, and the demand for due process was the point the whole time. She said I was being precious about a word; I said she was being precious about a function; and when I asked whether the aggregate could matter even if no single act does, she said obviously, and we were back at square one.

Then we tried a case and it broke both of us, which is why I'm writing this down. A regional manager at a logistics firm, barely any online presence, someone digs up a post from 2011, it circulates, he's fired within about 36 hours, and then he can't get work in his field for years and his certification body quietly declines to renew him. Nothing illegal happened, no employer policy covered it, no platform rule was invoked, and five years on nobody remembers his name. On her account it's clearly punishment and wildly disproportionate and it never terminates — except her framework has no way to say when the sentence should end, because there was no sentence. On mine every individual act in the chain is legitimate private choice, so the outcome is unobjectionable, which I don't believe for a second. Neither of us could say anything about the case that didn't sound evasive.

So here's what I actually want: a diagnosis, not another survey of the literature and not a both-sides summary, I've read all of that. What kind of disagreement is this, really? The options as I can see them — (a) it's verbal, we're running different extensions of "punishment" and everything downstream follows mechanically; (b) it's substantive but hidden, i.e. the real question is empirical, something like whether informal sanction tracks desert at all or is dominated by pile-on dynamics and status competition, and we're talking past each other because neither of us is looking at that; (c) we're answering three different questions under one heading — is it punishment (classification), is it legitimate (justification), does it work (consequences) — and each of us retreats to whichever one we're currently winning on; (d) the concept of punishment simply can't bear this weight outside a state and the whole framing is broken. My own guess, offered as a guess and probably self-serving, is (c) plus a bit of (a): I suspect the word isn't doing classificatory work at all, its doing intensifying work. We each already know whether we like the sanction, and then reach for a term that imports a whole normative package — authority, desert, procedure, limits — that we'd have to argue for piece by piece if we didn't have the word. If that's right, is the package breakable, and does breaking it apart actually resolve anything or just generate four smaller arguments we'd stall on too? And whatever you diagnose: what would count as progress here? A distinction I could hand her at dinner? Some piece of social science that genuinely bears on it — and be honest about whether that data exists or is junk? A reframing of the question? Or is this one of those disputes where the right answer is that it isn't going to move and we should stop trying? I'd want you to argue from what each side actually says, since I've given you both positions pretty specifically, rather than from what people generically say about cancel culture.
