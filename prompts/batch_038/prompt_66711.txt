I sat in on a colloquium last week that our philosophy department co-hosted with the computer science group, in which the speaker — a complexity theorist who has lately turned to metaphysics — argued that a proof of P ≠ NP would not merely be a technical result but would settle, or at least severely constrain, the standing dispute between realism and anti-realism about mathematical truth. His claim was that intractability, unlike undecidability, delivers particular propositions that are true or false and yet beyond the reach of every possible knower, which is exactly what an identification of truth with knowability cannot tolerate. I am writing a response paper for a seminar and have reconstructed his argument as carefully as I can, but I cannot tell whether it is valid, unsound, or resting on a suppressed assumption I have failed to notice. Here is the reconstruction: (1) For a proposition to be a candidate for knowledge at all, there must be some procedure by which a knower could come to know it. (2) A knower can carry out only procedures that terminate within a time she could wait out, using resources she could gather; a procedure whose cost outruns any physically realizable machine is not a procedure she can carry out at all. (3) So a proposition is knowable only if the question it answers can be decided efficiently — on the standard idealization, in time polynomial in the size of the data. (4) Assume P ≠ NP, and assume the related and widely believed conjecture NP ≠ coNP. The first assumption supplies a concrete decision problem, SATISFIABILITY, with no polynomial-time algorithm; the second, together with the Cook–Reckhow characterization of propositional proof systems, supplies something stronger — for every proof system whatsoever, there are tautologies with no proof of polynomial size in it. (5) Therefore there are particular, fully determinate, decidable propositions — for instance, that this specific million-clause formula, just generated by a procedure the speaker sketched on the board and which no one has read or ever will read in full, is unsatisfiable — that no human being could come to know, and that no successor species of knower could come to know either. (6) But a proposition whose truth value is fixed and forever out of reach for every possible knower is not a truth; truth must be at least in principle epistemically accessible, and that is the intuition the anti-realist begins from and the realist must either accept or explain away. (7) So if P ≠ NP and NP ≠ coNP, that intuition is false, and mathematical realism is thereby vindicated: there are mind-independent facts about arithmetical and combinatorial questions that transcend any possible verification.

The speaker treated (2) as the least controversial premise in the chain, remarking that the whole content of complexity theory is that some computations cannot be done, and he gestured at the halting problem in a way I could not follow — I think he meant that intractability is somehow more damaging than undecidability, but he never said why, and I suspect it matters. What I want is a diagnosis rather than a summary of the debate. Which step fails, and which steps are valid only on a reading the premises do not license? For each step you judge vulnerable, say what made it look innocent and what a repair would require of the argument as a whole. Then price the alternatives: if I reject (2), do I lose the philosophically interesting content of complexity theory, as the speaker claimed, or only an over-strong idealization of it? If I reject (6) instead, what is left of anti-realism — is there a resource-bounded version of the view, one on which assertibility conditions rather than truth conditions govern meaning, that survives (5) intact? And does (7) genuinely follow from (5) and (6), or has the speaker helped himself to something along the way? One thing that struck me in the room was that a proposition about the unsatisfiability of a large formula might be knowable on grounds that are not proofs at all — probabilistic, structural, or testimonial — and that he simply assumed throughout that knowing a tautology requires proving it; if that assumption is load-bearing, tell me where it belongs in the chain and whether its rejection rescues (6) or merely relocates the problem. For what it is worth, the claims I would most like to keep are (1) and (2); it is (7) I find unacceptable, since I cannot see how a conjecture about the growth rates of algorithms could ever settle a metaphysical question about the reach of mind — but I need to be able to say precisely why, and I need to know what I am giving up when I say it.
