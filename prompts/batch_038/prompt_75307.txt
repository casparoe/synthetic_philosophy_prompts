I work on the bioinformatics side of an environmental monitoring consortium. Under the terms of a permitting arrangement, several of our member agencies have to file an annual account of which species were detected at a set of survey sites and which have ceased to be detected, and increasingly that account is being built not from collected specimens or field observations but from environmental sequencing: bulk water and soil samples, assembled into genome fragments, many of them from organisms nobody has ever cultured, seen, or named, plus a fair number that are visibly partial, contaminated with sequence from a neighbor, or recoverable at very uneven depth from one survey round to the next. The organisms involved run the gamut from free-living bacteria and archaea, where genes move sideways between lineages as a matter of routine, to microbial eukaryotes that appear to reproduce clonally, to a handful of multicellular eukaryotes in the same samples that hybridize freely where their ranges meet. Our engineering group's proposal is simply to fix a sequence-similarity cutoff and emit a label for every bin that clears it; our taxonomic advisers insist that species is a theoretical notion that cannot be reduced to a threshold at all, and that any operational rule we adopt will silently build in a metaphysics nobody voted for. Before we settle the argument by fiat, I want the specification that the argument should have been about.

So: write the desiderata. What must any account of what a species is — and, separately, any procedure for assigning organisms to species — deliver in order to count as adequate for a purpose like ours, where the same scheme has to cover clonal microbes, horizontally exchanging prokaryotes, and hybridizing animals, has to be applied by different laboratories to the same samples and come out with the same labels, and has to be legible to non-specialist auditors who will use it to decide whether something has been lost? For each requirement, give the requirement itself, the case or consideration that motivates it, and your judgment about whether it is non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp. I have already run into a jumble of candidate demands and I cannot tell which are genuine: that the account handle ring-like geographic variation without arbitrary cuts; that it say what persists through time, including cases where a lineage splits and where it merges back; that it handle lineages that are too young for their gene trees to have sorted; that it not define species by reference to the very evolutionary processes it is supposed to explain; that it be usable when the only evidence is a partial genome from an unknown organism; that membership be determinate rather than vague; that it draw no distinction between what marks a species and what follows from being one. Please sort these, add the ones I have missed, and in particular flag the distinctions an adequate account must not collapse — between a species as an individual and a species as a class, between a criterion of identity and a diagnostic procedure for detecting it, between vagueness in the world and mere ignorance on our part, and between the job of fixing a stable name for a sample and the job of tracking a real historical lineage. Note also any requirement that conflicts with what we know about gene flow, drift, and incomplete lineage sorting, since a scheme that contradicts population genetics is dead on arrival regardless of how elegant it is.

Then do the two things I most want: identify any subset of the requirements that looks jointly unsatisfiable — and if one is, say plainly which requirement has to be weakened or dropped, whether the conflict is specific to a purpose like ours or general, and which single requirement turns out to be doing the most work in excluding candidate accounts. Finally, and briefly, tell me how the biological, the phylogenetic, and the ecological conceptions of species, along with a pluralist or general-lineage view that treats the others as different operationalizations of one underlying notion, fare against your list; a compact assessment is enough, since the reasoning I actually need is in the requirements. Assume I have not read the specialist literature, so define technical terms as you go, and please write the whole thing in American English.
