I have no academic background and never went to university, but for eleven years I worked the front desk of an outpatient pain clinic, and I have been thinking about pain on my own for most of that time, reading as widely as one can manage without an institution behind one. What follows is a thesis of my own, with the reasoning that got me to it. I would like you to argue for it as well as it can be argued, rather than to weigh it against alternatives.

You may know the condition called pain asymbolia. It is rare and usually follows brain injury, commonly in or near the insular cortex. The patients say that they still feel the pain; they can tell you where it is and how intense it is, sometimes on the usual zero-to-ten scale; but they are not bothered by it. They do not guard the injured part, they do not shrink from the stimulus, and some will handle or examine a wound with what looks like idle curiosity. A great deal has been made of these cases. The common move is this: here is a pain whose sensory properties are all intact and whose badness has been switched off, so badness cannot be a matter of how pain feels — it must be something else, a demand the experience makes on you, an evaluation carried in the experience, or a disposition to be moved by it. I have also come across the suggestion that such patients have lost not a reaction to pain but a more general hold on their own bodily integrity, and the contrary suggestion that the clinical reports have been misread and these patients do not really feel pain at all. I do not want to settle that dispute. I want to use the cases.

Here is my own distinction, whose labels are mine and which I have not seen stated this way elsewhere. When people talk about how bad a pain is, they slide between three different things.

- The sting: how much it hurts at the moment, the felt character, what the zero-to-ten number is trying to capture.
- The verdict: whether the person is set against it, resents it, fights it, wishes it away, or has made peace with it and lets it be.
- The footprint: how much of the person's practical field the pain occupies, meaning how many of their plans, hours, routes, and options have to be shaped around it as a standing constraint.

These come apart in every direction. A person can have a large sting and a calm verdict; a large sting and a small footprint; a settled verdict laid over a footprint that has quietly eaten twenty years.

My thesis is that what is bad about pain is the footprint. Sting is a sensation and verdict is an attitude, and neither is where badness lives; badness lives in what the pain does to a person's capacity to shape what comes next. That is also what makes asymbolia interesting on my view, though not for the reason usually given: those patients feel a pain and nothing in their planning bends around it, which is precisely why they get hurt. Their footprint is near zero because they hold no verdict against the pain, and yet the damage is real. So footprint cannot simply be what the person has managed to arrange; it has to be something a person can fail to register. That is the knot I have not been able to untie myself.

Please argue for the thesis. In particular:

1. Build the argument itself, with numbered premises and defended steps, from the ground up. Do not assume I already grant that badness must be located in one of the three rather than, say, in the injury or in the person's flourishing.
2. Show that the footprint view handles the asymbolia cases better than the alternatives, the felt-quality view and the demand-or-evaluation view, and say what it must claim about footprint being a relation between a pain and a person's plans rather than a property of the experience, since that is where it parts company with views already in circulation.
3. Resolve the knot explicitly and by choice. Does a small footprint mean a small harm, because the pain has been absorbed into a life, or does the absorbed life stand for a large harm already paid and now invisible? Pick one, defend it, and explain what the other answer would have to say differently about the asymbolic patients.
4. Answer the three objections I expect. First, that footprint tracks resources: the same pain has a smaller footprint for a person with a flexible job, a lift in the house, and money, so the view makes their pain less bad. Second, that footprint collapses into sting, since nothing occupies a life without hurting enough to. Third, that the view cannot explain why anyone should relieve a chronic pain that has been perfectly accommodated, because it seems to say there is nothing left to relieve.
5. Defend the consequence that makes me most uncomfortable: that a person who has rebuilt their life around a standing pain for a decade is, on this account, less badly off than a person with the same sting whose pain arrived four months ago and has taken everything over, even though the two of them say the same number to me at my desk. Argue this as substantive rather than verbal, and without turning it into a reproach to either of them.
6. Close by telling me what I would have had to observe at that desk in order to give the thesis up.

Two conditions. Do not give me references, a reading list, or the names of philosophers and books; I cannot look any of it up and it would only be decoration. Use my three terms as I have defined them, and argue the position throughout: I am not asking for a balanced survey, and I would rather have a strong case I can attack than a fair summary I already half know.
