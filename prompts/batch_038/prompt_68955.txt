I have been asked to contribute a short methodological credo to a collection on the epistemics of long-range forecasting, and before I submit it I would like a consistency audit. Malthus is my running example, but the commitments below are about forecasting practice generally rather than about him in particular. Here they are, in no order of importance:

1. A prediction must carry explicit temporal bounds. A claim about what will happen in the long run is not a prediction at all and therefore cannot fail; scoring it is a category error.
2. I nevertheless hold a broadly Popperian view of content: a theory that is in principle immune to refutation says nothing about the world, and I will not treat its mere survival as evidence in its favour.
3. I think the principle of population was refuted by the historical record — two centuries of sustained real-wage growth, and, once industrialisation was under way, fertility that fell as incomes rose rather than rising.
4. I regard the addition of moral restraint to the catalogue of checks in the later editions of the Essay as a rescue: an auxiliary introduced to absorb an anomaly.
5. I take Boserup's point that population pressure can itself induce the agricultural innovation Malthus held fixed, and I count that as a substantive empirical discovery rather than a redescription.
6. I accept ceteris paribus clauses in the social sciences, but only where the interfering conditions can be specified independently of the theory they are protecting.
7. I score my students' forecasts with strictly proper scoring rules, and I apply what I call a goalpost penalty: a forecaster who revises a call after seeing the outcome, in a way not licensed by new evidence, is scored as though the revised call had been the original one.
8. I think a research programme should be appraised across a run of time rather than at a moment, and that an auxiliary revision is legitimate when it issues in novel predictions that come out right.
9. I think right-but-early is a genuine category of predictive success. A theory whose mechanism is correct but whose triggering conditions have not yet arrived deserves credit rather than blame, and I want my scoring practice to reflect that.
10. I also accept that Malthus was broadly right about the pre-industrial world: real wages across most of recorded history did hover near subsistence, and the escape is recent.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than reporting them all as equally troubling, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision is, including what that revision costs me elsewhere on the list. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I would also like to know which commitment is doing the most concealed work — the one I have stated as a modest methodological preference but which is in fact carrying a substantive thesis.

Where a verdict depends on a question of Malthus interpretation — whether the principle states a tendency or a prediction, and what the later editions actually altered — flag the dependency and state which reading you are assuming rather than silently choosing one.
