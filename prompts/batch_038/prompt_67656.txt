I'm an epidemiologist, American, moved to KwaZulu-Natal about nine months ago to work on a TB-adherence study, and I've somehow ended up as the numbers person on a hospital research ethics committee plus two community advisory boards, which is not at all what I trained for. In that time I've lost count of how many times one sentence has been used to close down an argument: "a person is a person through other persons" — the ubuntu maxim, which I've also heard in isiZulu, though my isiZulu is basicaly nothing. It gets said with real finality and then everyone moves on, and I've started to suspect the different people saying it aren't saying the same thing.

Four uses I've actually sat through. (1) A community advisory board chair invoked it to argue our trial can't run on individual consent alone — that we need agreement from households, ideally from the ward, before we approach anyone. (2) In the same meeting another member invoked the identical sentence the other way, saying ubuntu doesn't swallow the individual, that each person still has to sign for themselves and that's precisely what the maxim means. (3) A provincial policy document I was reading for background uses it to ground human dignity, in a way that reads like dignity belongs to everyone unconditionally, no matter how they behave or who they're related to. (4) Then at a workshop someone used it about a man who'd abandoned his children, and clearly meant he'd lost some of his standing as a person — that personhood is a thing you can have more or less of, and can forfeit. When I pointed out that (3) and (4) don't look like they can both be true, I got a very polite non-answer. Oh, and at a bereavement visit someone said it while talking about the ancestors, which made me realize I don't even know whether "other persons" in that sentence is supposed to include the dead and the not-yet-born.

So here's what I want: lay out the different precise claims that one sentence could be making. A menu, not a summary — for each reading, state it exactly enough that I could tell which one a given person is using, and say whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or maybe not even coherent. I'm especially interested in how much work the little word "through" is doing: is it saying other people cause you to become a person, or that they constitute you as one, or only that you're obliged to treat people as persons and they're obliged to do the same back? And whether "person" in there names a status everyone has, a capacity, or an achievement you accumulate. Then map my four uses onto the readings: which reading does each one need to actually get its conclusion, and are any two of them in flat contradiction rather than just tension? Finally, be honest about whether the English gloss is carrying extra weight — if the sentence only sounds profound in translation, or if some of these uses are people borrowing authority for a conclusion they already had, I'd rather know that now then find out in a committee meeting.
