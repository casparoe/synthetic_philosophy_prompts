I serve on the governing board of a research consortium, and we are redrafting the amendment article of our charter. One member — a corporate lawyer by trade — has proposed that the consortium's open-data obligation be entrenched against amendment and, crucially, that the entrenching clause itself be unamendable and unrepealable. When several of us objected that a provision which freezes the very article that authorizes change either contradicts itself or is mere decoration, he replied that we were overthinking the matter: entrenchment is simply Ulysses lashed to the mast. The founders know a siren song is coming — a future board facing a funding crisis will want to restrict or license the data — so they have themselves tied down while still in their right minds, and the ropes protect them from the selves they are about to become. On his account the alleged paradox dissolves once one sees that the binding is done by a single agent upon itself at an earlier time.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than the clause. Specifically:

1. Give an explicit correspondence table: what plays Ulysses, the mast, the ropes, the crew, the sirens, and the moment of lucid foresight — and for each entry, state whether its counterpart in a charter is a causal constraint or a normative one. The distinction should be doing work later in your answer, not merely recorded.

2. Sort the disanalogies into those that actually defeat his use of the story and those that are local color. Please make sure to handle these three: the ropes cannot retie or untie themselves, whereas the clause in dispute is amendable only by a procedure the clause forbids; the crew acted on a standing order from Ulysses and were not themselves exposed to the song, whereas whoever refuses to release the future board — a chair, a general counsel, a court — holds that authority under the same document; and Ulysses is one continuous person, while a board across fifteen years is not obviously the same agent in the sense the analogy requires.

3. Identify what the analogy smuggles in. I suspect at least three things: that the earlier self is the authentic one and the later self's contrary preference is pathology rather than revised judgment; that binding operates on capacity rather than on validity; and that the live question is whether precommitment can be made effective rather than whether an amending power can consume its own source. Tell me which of these his argument actually depends on.

4. Say whether his conclusion survives, and please separate it into two claims: that self-entrenchment is conceptually coherent and unparadoxical, and that it is legitimate for this board to bind its successors. State which, if either, the Ulysses story supports, and whether securing one of them costs him the other.

5. If the analogy is load-bearing but broken, propose a replacement that respects the self-referential structure — something that captures a rule whose operation is to alter the rule conferring it, of the kind real drafters reach for with unamendable provisions and consent requirements of the sort that bar depriving a state of equal suffrage in the Senate without its consent. Say what your replacement is good for, and exactly where it, too, stops carrying weight.

I am after the reasoning about what the analogy licenses and what it does not, not a survey of positions on legislative omnipotence; argue the points through, and flag anything in my colleague's version of the story that you think I have mischaracterized in his favor.
