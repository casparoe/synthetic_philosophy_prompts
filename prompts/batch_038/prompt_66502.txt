I work in the evidence-communications unit of a public-health agency, and for the past year our team has treated a line usually credited to Otto Neurath — 'words divide, pictures unite' — as a kind of procedural rule. Last week I proposed adding two sentences beneath a chart on hospital waiting times: one identifying the reference population the figures are drawn from, one flagging that those figures are estimates with wide error bars. A senior colleague deleted both, on the grounds that the chart should stand on its own and that qualifying text would only start arguments. He offered the slogan as his warrant, and the meeting accepted it. I am fairly confident something has gone wrong here, but I want to be precise about what.

Could you lay out the distinct claims that sentence might be making, as a menu I can take into our next review? I would like each reading stated as a proposition sharp enough to be argued with, and I would like the axes of ambiguity separated rather than blurred together: the comparative form versus the absolute one; what an audience remembers versus what it understands; the capacity to cross language barriers versus the capacity to produce agreement, where agreement may be genuine convergence or merely the suppression of visible disagreement; and comprehension versus solidarity, since 'unite' may be doing political work rather than epistemic work in Neurath's own museum context. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false — and then, separately, whether it would actually license deleting my two sentences.

Two further points I would like addressed. First, the slogan is itself a sentence, and a fairly abstract one at that; say what follows from the fact that no picture could carry its own content. Second, I want the disambiguation tested against what the Isotype programme actually did, since that is where the line comes from: quantities were shown by repeating an identical symbol rather than by enlarging one; continuous curves were avoided on the ground that the data are discrete points; pie charts and scaled histograms were rejected; symbols were stripped of perspective and shading and drawn from a standardized set; and a designated human role, the 'transformer', sat between the collected numbers and the finished picture deciding what to keep and what to drop. Neurath called the result a helping language, to be used alongside text rather than instead of it. Does that apparatus count as evidence for the slogan, or as a standing admission that honest pictures require exactly as much discipline, and are exactly as capable of dividing a room, as honest sentences do?

Finally, tell me which reading people who repeat the line most likely intend, and give me the strongest honest version of their point — one I could concede without surrendering my captions.
