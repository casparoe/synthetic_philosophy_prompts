Last month I was appointed to the governing council of a professional association — about four hundred voting members spread over three regional chapters — and at Thursday's meeting we are supposed to decide whether to keep electing our officers by secret ballot. One of my colleagues has circulated a memo arguing that we should not. I trained as a structural engineer and have no background in political theory, so I cannot tell how much of the memo is sound and how much is rhetorical momentum. Here is the relevant part of it, quoted in full:

"Downs proved in 1957 that voting is irrational. The arithmetic is trivial: your ballot only matters if it breaks a tie, the chance of that in any large election is essentially zero, and zero multiplied by any payoff is zero. So the expected benefit of voting is zero while the costs — the time, the travel, the effort of becoming informed — are plainly positive. A rational actor abstains. This is what economists call the paradox of voting: not that some people vote, but that anyone votes at all when it cannot possibly matter.

Riker and Ordeshook's famous repair was to bolt a 'duty' term onto the equation — the warm glow of doing your civic bit — which balances the books only by defining rationality as whatever people happen to do. It explains any turnout figure you feed it, and therefore explains nothing.

The better reply, I will grant, is that the probability is not literally zero: one chance in a hundred million times a stake of a billion dollars is still ten dollars, which beats an afternoon's cost. But this merely relocates the difficulty, since it makes the entire case rest on magnitudes nobody can estimate.

The real point is that voters do not vote as isolated individuals. If everyone who thinks as I do reasoned as I do, the result would differ; so my reason for voting is not my own causal power but the fact that my reasoning is shared with others. That is why the empirical observation that turnout runs slightly higher in close races is beside the point, and why this council should stop pretending the officer election is a decision procedure. I propose we rotate the office by lot and settle substantive questions by facilitated consensus."

I am inclined to think this is too glib, and I will admit I would like the election preserved. Please audit the memo rather than reassure me.

1. Go through it claim by claim and sort each into what a political scientist working on participation would accept, what is a defensible simplification of a more careful position, and what is simply wrong. Be specific about which sentence each verdict attaches to.
2. Is "Downs proved that voting is irrational" a fair description of what Downs actually argued, and does the memo characterize the paradox itself correctly — that is, what is the surprising fact that made it a paradox in the first place?
3. Does the memo's dismissal of the duty term hold up? Separate the version of that objection that has real force from the version the memo states, and say what the model still predicts even after the term is added.
4. Is the memo internally consistent about whether the decisive probability is zero or merely very small? Trace whether that slippage does any work in its argument.
5. The contest in question is among roughly four hundred members, not a national electorate. Does any of the memo's arithmetic transfer to that setting? Walk me through, roughly and without pretending to precision, what order of magnitude one should expect for the chance that a single ballot is decisive in a body that size, and whether that changes the conclusion.
6. Is the move from "an individual ballot rarely decides an outcome" to "we should replace the election with sortition and consensus" a non sequitur, or is there an argument available there that the memo has not actually spelled out?
7. Separately: in the reading group I belong to, "the paradox of voting" means the phenomenon where majority preferences cycle and no option beats all others. Is the memo using the term the way the participation literature does, and if the two senses are being traded on, does that affect how the memo reads?
8. Finally, tell me where the memo is right. I would rather concede its correct points at the meeting than be accused of strawmanning it, and I want a short, accurate statement of what I can say on Thursday that does not overstate my own case.
