I'm a novelist at work on a book whose viewpoint characters include two nonhuman minds, and I've been using Daniel Dennett's four kinds of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — as a design grid, mostly to keep myself honest about what a given character can and cannot do. A friend who teaches philosophy of mind read my notes and sent back this summary, along with the observation that I have been misapplying the scheme:

"Dennett sorts minds into four kinds, and every species belongs to exactly one of them. A Darwinian creature is one whose behavior is fixed by natural selection alone: nothing it does can be revised within its own lifetime, so plants and microbes are the pure cases. A Skinnerian creature adds trial-and-error learning, adjusting what it does according to reward and punishment; that covers essentially every animal with a nervous system. A Popperian creature is the first kind that can think about its own thinking — it has beliefs about its beliefs, which is why it can settle what to attend to before settling what to do, and why only Popperian and higher creatures can properly be said to reason. A Gregorian creature is one that makes and uses tools, so otters that crack shellfish with stones and chimpanzees that fish for termites are Gregorian along with us. The four are a ladder in the strict sense: each rung adds a capacity, a higher rung means a more intelligent animal, and nothing reaches a rung without having passed through the ones below. Your characters therefore face two hard constraints. An alien can be Gregorian only if its species keeps records outside the head, since a tool has to be external and lasting to count; and any Gregorian character you write has to out-think every merely Popperian one."

I would rather not rebuild my characters on a bad gloss, and for what it is worth my instinct is that she is being stricter than the underlying scheme requires — but I can't say which of her claims is doing that work. Please go through the paragraph claim by claim and tell me what a Dennett scholar would accept without hesitation, what is a tolerable popular simplification, and what is simply wrong, including whether her four definitions capture the distinctions Dennett actually draws or quietly substitute different ones. Then tell me whether the two design constraints survive once the summary is corrected, or whether the corrected picture leaves me freer, or differently constrained, than she suggests. Where the point is genuinely disputed among people who know this literature — for instance, whether the boundary between the second and third rungs does any real work, or whether any nonhuman animal qualifies for the top one — say so plainly, and don't dress a contested position up as settled exegesis. Please write in American English.
