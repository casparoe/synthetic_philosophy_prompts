I work in the ethics-and-policy stream of a palliative-technology programme, and our seminar this term is required to produce something other than another essay: a piece of fiction that makes a live philosophical problem feel like a problem to a reader who has never taken a course in it. My own assignment concerns hedonism, and specifically the difficulty of saying what all pleasant experiences have in common. The familiar set-up is this: the warmth of a bath, the relief of setting down a heavy bag, the pleasure of a joke landing, of an argument finally coming out right, of cold water when you are thirsty, of sex, of a piece of music you would call beautiful but would not want to feel cheerful about. Sensory hedonists insist a single hedonic tone runs through all of these, faint but findable if you attend closely enough; their opponents say that when you actually look, you find nothing shared except that each is, in its own way, welcomed — that pleasantness names a relation between a person and an experience rather than an ingredient inside it. Both sides have their counterexamples: the person who keeps going, and keeps wanting to keep going, without any enjoyment; and the person plainly enjoying something they would refuse to prolong.

Please write a short story of roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words that stages this dispute instead of explaining it, and that you may adjust freely in its details. The scaffolding I would like: a near-future public body certifies experiences for a welfare metric, and its instrument prints two separate channels for every subject — one tracking how strongly an experience is sought and held onto, the other supposed to track felt pleasantness itself. The second channel was calibrated decades ago against a fixed list of paradigm cases, and the narrator is a junior technician who, while re-running an old audit, discovers that the calibration never isolated a common signal: the channel is a bundle of unrelated responses wearing a single label. Give the narrator one anomalous subject — I would suggest either someone who reports that the taste of a peach and the satisfaction of a finished argument feel exactly alike and cannot be trained to tell them apart, or a hospice patient in whom the two channels come apart in a way no existing policy can handle — and one senior colleague who defends the instrument not as a fool or a cynic but as someone with a real argument: that a disjunctive property is still a property, that a public body can only act on what is interpersonally checkable, that the rival view hands the metric over to self-report and its worse failures. Let the two disagree about a third person who is present in the story, on whom their respective tests deliver opposite verdicts, and let the story end on a decision that costs something either way rather than on a resolution of the metaphysics.

Two constraints on the writing. First, no named philosophers, no quotation from any text, and no passage in which a character lectures the reader about qualia, attitudes, or the nature of value: the conceptual content has to be carried entirely by what the characters do, what the instrument prints, and how people argue under time pressure and in the language of procedures, forms, and shift handovers. Second, keep the prose concrete and unshowy, with the institution's paperwork and habits rendered realistically rather than as futuristic set dressing. Begin your response with the story itself — no title, no preface, no note on the choices you made, and nothing after the final line.
