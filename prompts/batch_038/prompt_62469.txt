I am drafting a rebuttal to a reviewer report on a submitted paper, and one sentence in that report is doing far more work than I know how to answer cleanly. Our paper reports that a language model fine-tuned on a synthetic task with an explicit compositional structure generalizes systematically to constructions that are absent from its training distribution, and we describe this, cautiously, as evidence of compositional structure in the model's internal representations. The reviewer concedes the results and then writes: "Ultimately, however, the model is just predicting the next token, and the authors' language about understanding is therefore unwarranted."

My suspicion is that two words in that sentence — "just" and "ultimately" — carry an argument the sentence never states, but I cannot say what they could be carrying, or whether any version of the claim is sound.

Could you set out, as a menu rather than a single favoured interpretation, the distinct precise theses that "it is just predicting the next token" might express? I would like roughly six to eight readings, ordered from the least to the most contentious. For each, please give: the claim stated so that it is capable of being true or false; a verdict as to whether it is a truism about the architecture and training objective, a substantive empirical hypothesis about the model's internals, or false; and an explicit statement of whether that reading, taken on its own, would license the inference from our behavioural results to the absence of understanding. Where a reading is empirically substantive, say what would count for or against it.

Two further requests. First, treat the descriptive content of "next-token prediction" separately from the dismissive work performed by "just" and "ultimately": the latter two appear to assert something about levels of explanation — that the lowest-level description of what the system was optimized for somehow cancels or exhausts higher-level descriptions — and I would like to know what that something could be, and whether any version of it is defensible. Second, some of my colleagues hear an implicit comparison in this way of talking, to a trained bird that reproduces a phrase it has heard many times; if that constitutes a further reading, include it, and say whether the comparison survives contact with what such birds are actually able to do.

Close by identifying which reading is load-bearing in the reviewer's sentence — the one the argument genuinely requires — and telling me what would and would not settle it, so that I can say so plainly in the rebuttal rather than trading slogans back.
