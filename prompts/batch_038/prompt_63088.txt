I'm on a second-year French political thought module and we've just finished Weil's *Note sur la suppression générale des partis politiques* — the one she wrote in London in 1943, a few weeks before she died, and which only got published in La Table ronde seven years later. My essay is due in nine days and I want to do a deliberately anachronistic section: run her argument against party politics as it actually exists now, rather than against the Third and Fourth Republic mess she was reacting to. My tutor said that kind of speculation is fine as long as I'm explicit about which of her own criteria I'm using, and as long as I don't turn her into a generic grump about partisanship (apparently every student on the module does that). The passage I keep coming back to is the one in the Simon Leys translation where she carves out an exception for us: "In the Anglo-Saxon world, political parties have an element of game, of sport, which is only conceivable in an institution of aristocratic origin, whereas in institutions that were plebeian from the start, everything must always be serious." That feels like a concession she'd want to renegotiate if she'd seen the last fifteen years, but I can't work out in which direction.

So — could you predict what Weil would make of the following, none of which existed when she died in August 1943?

1. **Citizens' assemblies picked by lot.** The Irish one in 2016–18, the French climate convention of 150 randomly drawn citizens in 2019–20, and the permanent citizens' council in Belgium's German-speaking community, where a rotating sortitioned group sets the agenda for later assemblies. Would she read these as the first instalment of the abolition of parties, or as a fig leaf that parties keep folded in their back pocket and deploy whenever it suits them?

2. **Algorithmic feeds.** Political content ranked by predicted engagement — i.e. a machine optimising for exactly the collective passion she thought parties manufactured, except it's run by firms that aren't parties, don't field candidates and don't want to govern. Does her analysis of the party as an engine for producing passion transfer onto something that has no programme and no members, or does it break?

3. **Mass-membership parties with one-member-one-vote leadership elections.** Labour going from under 200,000 members to over half a million in the back half of the 2010s, joining done on a phone in about ninety seconds. My flatmate thinks giving every individual member a real vote makes a party less of a collective and more of a heap of private consciences. I think it just makes the pressure on each conscience cheaper to apply. Which of us is closer to what Weil would say?

4. **"Proposal C"**, the thing our seminar group drafted in week five as a mock constitutional convention: parties banned from fielding candidates; everybody stands as an individual and has to publish a full written statement of their positions before nominations close; a recall triggered by signatures from 8% of registered voters in the constituency; and a second chamber of 200 sortitioned citizens who are legally barred from taking money, briefings or seconded staff from any organisation that employs people. Weil did want candidates to publish their views instead of hiding behind a label, but I've no idea how far she'd go on recall, and I suspect she'd be pretty hostile to the idea that you can legislate collective passion away with a lobbying ban.

Could you lay the answer out in these sections, in this order: **Bottom line** — one sentence giving her overall verdict on Proposal C. **The test I'm applying** (~120 words) — which criteria from the pamphlet you're actually using, and why those rather than others. **Verdicts** — one short paragraph (~80 words) on each of items 1–4, each ending with her most likely position, a confidence score out of 10 that you've got Weil rather than a 2020s liberal in a Weil costume, and a single line saying what would move that score. **The game exception** (~150 words) — does the Anglo-Saxon carve-out quoted above survive contact with the present, or does she have to withdraw it? **Strike-out list** (~100 words) — the bits of Proposal C she'd delete, and what she'd put in their place if anything. **Where I'm probably talking nonsense** (~100 words) — the ways this whole exercise risks being anachronistic or just straight wrong about her. Please don't give me a reading list or references, I only have the pamphlet and I'm meant to be arguing from it.
