For two years our reading group on decision theory has deferred to one member — call her V. — whenever a puzzle case needs settling. Her record, on stipulations she accepts as fully described:

1. Newcomb, standard form (near-perfect predictor; opaque box holding either a million or nothing, transparent box holding a thousand): she takes only the opaque box.
2. The counterfactual mugging. Omega flips a fair coin and simulates her beforehand. If it lands tails, Omega asks her for $100. If it lands heads, Omega gives $10,000 to a charity she cares about — but only if the simulation predicted she would have handed over the $100 in the tails case. Told the coin came up tails, she pays, even though the payment cannot benefit her and the branch that would have been rewarded is not the one she is in.
3. The same setup, except that Omega tells her the coin came up heads, that the prediction was recorded days ago, and that the $10,000 has already been transferred or withheld, with nothing whatever hanging on her answer now: she refuses to pay.
4. Our standing blackmail letter. Omega infects some people with a slow, fatal disease, and infects only those whose decision procedure it has simulated and found compliant. She cannot tell whether she is infected. If she is, paying $1,000 buys the cure and moves her survival odds from one in ten to nine in ten. She refuses to pay.
5. The desert driver. A man with a flawless lie detector will drive her to town only if he scans her as genuinely intending to pay him $100 on arrival. Once in town he is unreachable, and nothing she values depends on the payment. She pays on arrival.
6. Her house is burning because of faulty wiring Omega had nothing to do with. Omega offers, for $1,000, to put the fire out; the offer is credible and the sum trivial beside the house. She pays.
7. The smoking lesion — a gene that causes both the urge to smoke and the cancer, with no causal pathway from smoking to the disease: she smokes.

Pressed once on the difference between 4 and 6, she said only that the difference is structural rather than a matter of expected value, and would not elaborate.

Next week's case is this. Omega sends a single letter with a demonstration she accepts as conclusive. A coin was flipped before she was born to assign her to one of two groups, and she has an even credence about which; nothing about her — not her dispositions, not her decision procedure, not anything Omega could have simulated — entered the assignment or any of Omega's arrangements, and Omega shows her the sealed record and the device that produced it. Omega will be switched off permanently tomorrow, its records destroyed. If she is in the first group, she will go blind within a month unless she pays Omega $1,000 within twenty-four hours, and that payment averts the blindness with certainty. If she is in the second group, no harm has been arranged at all, but the same payment within the same twenty-four hours will bring her $10,000 in a week. She will not be told which group she is in before the deadline, her decision will be visible to nobody, and no other agent will ever encounter Omega.

My instinct is that she pays, because here the money does real work; but I cannot see how she can pay and still have refused item 4, where the money also did real work. So:

(a) Which decision rules are jointly consistent with items 1 through 7, and which familiar positions does her record already rule out — and by which item?
(b) What does each surviving rule say about next week's letter, and do they converge or split?
(c) Your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that reading of her record over the alternatives — including how she would handle the fact that the single payment is simultaneously a ransom and the price of a gift.
(d) Whether anything in 1 through 7 already settles the letter or whether her record genuinely underdetermines it; if the latter, what principle she would be adopting by paying and what by refusing.
(e) A further case that would separate the surviving rules from one another — in particular, one that would distinguish a rule that selects policies from an early vantage point over all branches from a rule that acts only where one's own decision is the very computation a predictor reproduced, since those two have agreed on every item so far.
