I am completing a chapter on the legality objections to the postwar trials, and my adviser — I will call her V. — has annotated the draft with her verdicts on a series of episodes. I need to predict her verdict on a new proposal before a panel discussion, and I want that prediction to be driven by the rule she is actually running rather than by her general sympathies about the trials.

Her verdicts so far, with the reasons she gave in the margins:

1. The International Military Tribunal's rejection of the nullum crimen objection to the crimes-against-peace charge — its reliance on the 1928 Kellogg-Briand Pact, and its treatment of the maxim as a principle of justice rather than a limit on sovereignty — she calls correct. Her reason: an obligation that binds a state before the conduct can bind the individuals who act for it, and international law has never required that a court already exist for a duty to be enforceable against the officeholders who breached it.

2. The Dönitz case is for her the best hour of the entire enterprise. The Tribunal found against him on the aggression and war-crime counts but declined to rest his sentence on the submarine-warfare charge after weighing Admiral Nimitz's affidavit and the fact that Allied naval practice in the Pacific had been materially the same. She insists this is not leniency toward a defeated officer but the standard being applied to the prosecuting powers as well, and she adds that a rule which runs in only one direction is not a rule.

3. The Tribunal's narrowing of crimes against humanity by requiring a connection to the war — which left the pre-1939 persecution of German Jews largely outside its reach — she treats as its most credible moment, evidence that the four powers would shrink their own jurisdiction rather than stretch the Charter.

4. The acquittals of Schacht, von Papen, and Fritzsche she takes as proof that the bench was deciding rather than ratifying; her note says that a tribunal which acquits no one has not tried anyone.

5. On Tokyo she agrees with the majority that the ex post facto objection to crimes against peace fails, but she treats the general conspiracy count — the theory of a single continuing agreement running back to 1928 — as a genuine overreach, because it imputed a shared design to officials who had entered government at different times and for different purposes.

6. Pal's dissent she refuses to dismiss: she writes that his conclusion on retroactivity is legally serious, and that his remedy of treating the whole Tokyo proceeding as a nullity is disproportionate only because the alternative was no adjudication of the Nanking atrocities at all.

7. The separate opinions of the French and Dutch judges, directed at the composition of the bench and at the decision not to prosecute the head of state, she calls institutionally well taken but beside the point. She rejects the framing that the trials failed because they were victors' trials.

8. The judges' case brought under Control Council Law No. 10 in 1947 she calls the decisive precedent, since the standard applied to German jurists for perverting the judicial process was one that any Allied judge could have been held to, and since a number of the accused were acquitted.

9. On the Yamashita proceedings her objection is not the outcome but the reviewing court's refusal to examine the sufficiency of the record; she calls the dissenting point — that the duty charged had never been communicated, and that the commission sat after hostilities had ended — the strongest thing said about notice in the entire postwar period.

The new case, on which she has been asked for a written opinion:

A coalition of forty-one states, none acting under Security Council authorization, proposes to establish by treaty in 2027 a special tribunal for the crime of aggression. State X launched a cross-border invasion in 2025. State X has ratified neither the Rome Statute nor its aggression amendments, and the Court's aggression jurisdiction does not reach its nationals, which is the coalition's stated reason for bypassing it. Aggression was defined in those amendments, in force since 2018, and, on the coalition's account, in customary international law at the time of the invasion. As at Nuremberg, the charter will be promulgated after the conduct it punishes. Jurisdiction runs only against the political and military leadership of State X. Three coalition members conducted cross-border bombing campaigns within the past two decades; all three have refused any clause extending jurisdiction to their own nationals, and one will not permit its serving officers to appear as witnesses. The first two indictments are (i) a career naval officer who signed the orders establishing a blockade of a neighboring state's ports and who was never present at the planning meetings, and (ii) a finance minister who joined the cabinet eleven months after the invasion and who had signed a private memorandum opposing it beforehand.

What I need from you:

- Predict V.'s verdict on the tribunal as a whole, and then separately on each of the two indictments, in that order.
- State the rule you think she is running. At least three candidates are consistent with everything above — a pre-existing-obligation rule, a reciprocity rule keyed to whether the prosecuting powers accept the standard applied to themselves, and an individual-notice rule keyed to what the particular defendant could reasonably have foreseen — and there may be a fourth or fifth, including one about what the alternative to the tribunal would have been. Explain what each predicts for this case and where they come apart.
- Say which you would bet on and why, distinguishing which of her nine verdicts are load-bearing for the rule and which are incidental to it.
- Identify the closest analog to each of the two indictments, whether from her list or from the historical record.
- Describe one further case, specified concretely enough that it could be put to her next week, that would best discriminate between the two rules you take most seriously.
- If her verdicts genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say what principle the coalition would be adopting under each of the competing readings, and which of those principles you would advise her not to endorse.

Argue from the episodes as I have set them out; I am not looking for references or a reading list.
