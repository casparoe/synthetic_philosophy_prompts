I am a third-year undergraduate reading political philosophy, and my course has just moved into a seminar unit on the legal regulation of sex work. For the final session our tutor has set an unusual exercise: rather than an essay, each of us must reconstruct the document that an honest adversarial collaboration between the two main camps would actually produce. I should like you to draft that document for me.

The two parties are as follows. The first is a committed defender of the Nordic or neo-abolitionist model — the asymmetric regime introduced in Sweden in 1999, which criminalises the purchase of sexual services while declining to penalise the seller, and which frames prostitution as an instance of men's violence against women rather than as a form of labour. The second is a committed defender of full decriminalisation on the New Zealand model (the Prostitution Reform Act 2003), which removes criminal liability from selling, from buying, and — subject to occupational health and safety regulation — from third-party management, and which frames prostitution as work. Note carefully that neither party defends the legalisation regimes of the Netherlands (brothel ban lifted in 2000) or Germany (in force from 2002): both criticise those regimes, and I want the document to record that shared criticism among the agreed claims while making explicit that the grounds on which the two sides reject them are opposed.

The document should contain three parts:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can genuinely sign — for instance concerning trafficking, the involvement of minors, debt bondage and third-party exploitation, the distinction between decriminalisation and legalisation, and the difficulty of assembling evidence about a partly hidden population. Do not pad this section: if a candidate claim is true only at a level of generality at which it commits nobody to anything, flag it as such rather than counting it.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the Nordic-model defender would accept as a fair statement of her own position, and once in wording the decriminalisation advocate would accept as a fair statement of his — with a further column marking whether the disagreement is conceptual, empirical, or partly verbal.

3. Three to five cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. For each, give a concrete case, experiment or body of evidence both parties would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, in their own words.

My own hunches about where the cruxes lie, which you may accept, refine or reject with reasons: whether consent to sell sex can be valid under conditions of severe economic necessity, and whether the Nordic answer, generalised, would also undermine consent to other hazardous or stigmatised paid labour; whether criminalising the buyer can coherently be claimed not to harm the seller, given that the transaction cannot occur without him; which harm baseline each side is using — the seller's counterfactual life with no sex trade available, or her situation within an existing trade; whether the state may adopt a policy that predictably leaves current sellers worse off because of what the law expresses about the status of women; and whether the term sex work picks out one population or several (indoor and managed, street-based, migrant and undocumented), such that each side may be right about a different segment.

Please also say which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and identify the load-bearing terms — voluntary, exploitation, work, victim, choice — where the two sides are not using the same word in the same sense. Offer no verdict on who is right, and let neither party be a strawman. Write in British English, aim for roughly 1,400 words, and give no bibliography or reference list: I will add citations myself, so refer to positions, arguments and regimes by name only.
