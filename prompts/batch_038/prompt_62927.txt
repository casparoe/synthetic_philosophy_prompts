ok so I'm drafting the position section of a paper on Keynes's epistemology and I want a consistency check before I show it to anybody, because I have a feeling I'm pulling in two directions and I can't tell where. Here's what I actually hold, more or less in the order it came to me:

1. Probability is an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion. Not a frequency, not anybody's degree of confidence — a relation between propositions, and when you follow an argument closely enough you can just see it.

2. Not every pair of propositions stands in such a relation, and where they do, it often isn't measurable by a number at all, let alone uniquely. So plenty of probability comparisons simply fail rather than coming out even. Rational belief is partially ordered, not one real-valued function.

3. Weight of argument is a genuinely separate axis from probability. Two conclusions can sit at the same probability while one rests on a pile of relevant evidence and the other on almost nothing, and which of the two you're holding matters for whether you should act on it. New evidence can add weight without budging the probability.

4. Base rates are fine. Actuarial tables, observed relative frequencies over a stable reference class — I use them without embarrassment and I don't think they need to be reduced to anything else.

5. But I also think Ramsey won the technical argument, or at least that the tradition coming out of him did: degrees of belief get measured by the odds you'd accept, the coherence constraints fall out of that, and this is why probability functions and expected utility are the right way to model choice. Keynes's refusal to let belief be numerical looks like a dead end to me now, however symapthetic I am to the motivation for it.

6. On the macro side I accept the later story: for the decisions that actually move aggregate investment there's no evidential basis for a calculation at all, so people fall back on convention — treat the existing state of affairs as right unless something specific turns up to change it — plus spontaneous urge, and what the market is really doing is guessing what average opinion expects average opinion to be rather than valuing anything.

7. And I want to say that conventional state of long-term expectation is criticizable, not merely different from a calculating one. Its a spurious stand-in for real calculation, and that's what makes the whole arrangement liable to collapse without warning. Which is also why I think there's a decent case for the state steering more of the investment: it can hold the longer, steadier view that speculators structurally can't.

I'm pretty sure some of them only look like they clash and are fine once you spell them out — tell me which those are and why, and don't just flag everything as equally suspect.
