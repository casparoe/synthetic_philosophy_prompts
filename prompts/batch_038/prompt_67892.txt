I was at a workshop dinner last week and two people at my table got into a pretty heated arguement about the "alignment tax," and I mostly just sat there eating bread because I'm not in AI at all (health econ, mostly cost-effectiveness stuff). Neither of them would drop it and I still dont know which one was on firmer ground. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dev opened by saying the alignment tax basically isn't a tax. His claim: interpretability, evals, and control work are capability work wearing a disguise. A model you can actually steer and audit tends to be a model that's more reliable on hard tasks, so money spent on safety compounds instead of getting burned. Which means the competitiveness constraint — the fear that if you slow down, a rival passes you — is mostly a self-serving story labs tell to license corner-cutting. He challenged her to name one safety technique that made a frontier model worse at anything anyone cared about.

(2) Mara said that's exactly backwards, and if it were true it should worry him more, not less. If safety research is capability-enhancing, it doesn't slow the race down at all; it just makes the race faster with better seat belts. And she said the cost is real for a different reason than he thinks: safety results leak, because you publish them to get credit and goodwill, so what you pay for, your rival gets free.

(3) Dev said leakage runs both ways — rivals publish too, so it's a commons everybody fishes in, not a tax on whoever pays in. And he said her "relative timing" framing proves far too much: on that logic every expenditure is a competitive handicap, compute, hiring, lawyers, benefits, so it collapses into "spend on nothing but the frontier," which no lab does and no lab could.

(4) Mara said there's a clean line he's skipping: compute and hires are rival goods you keep, safety knowledge is non-rival and you're actively rewarded for handing it over. Then she said the metaphor itself is the problem. A tax is borne by the payer. But the costs of moving fast land downstream, on users and on whoever is standing nearby when a deployed system fails. So it isn't a tax, it's an externality, and externalities don't respond to Dev's argument at all, because the lab never feels the bill it's running up.

(5) Dev's last move was an analogy I didn't really follow. He said what she's describing is the Porter hypothesis in new clothes: strict environmental regulation was supposed to wreck competitiveness and in plenty of cases improved it, because the constraint forced innovations the firms would never have made otherwise. So maybe a real tax, paid under real competitive pressure, is precisely what produces techniques worth more than they cost. Mara said the analogy dies on contact: in the pollution case the innovator could hold onto the gains through lead time or patents, and there's nothing like that here, because the moment an interpretability result is public everyone has it and a competitor just folds it into their own training run.
