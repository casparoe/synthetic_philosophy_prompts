I spent most of yesterday arguing with a friend — a historian by training — about whether narrative form in history is intrinsically guilty of hindsight bias. His case was that any account organised as a story running toward an ending the reader already knows invites that reader to take the ending as inevitable, and that the standard experimental remedy, namely making people generate alternative outcomes before they learn what actually happened, is simply unavailable to a historian whose audience is never outcome-naive. My reply was that the charge runs together two quite different things: the merely retrospective structure of historical description, in which an earlier event is characterised by reference to a later one, and the modal claim, explicit or implied, that the earlier event made the later one likely. Only the second strikes me as a defect; the first looks like what historical explanation just is. I want to turn the disagreement into a funded project, and our institute's internal seed-funding form has five fixed fields, so please write the application to fit it exactly: five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no closing remarks.

The project as I conceive it: develop and defend a criterion that separates legitimate retrospective structure in historical narrative from illegitimate inflation of modal claims within it, and determine what follows for the accusation that narrative history is biased by construction. The five fields are: (1) the question, stated precisely enough that it could come out either way rather than being settled by how it is phrased; (2) why the existing positions leave it open — the philosophical defence of narrative as constitutive of historical understanding on one side, the empirical literature on hindsight bias on the other — including the suspicion that the two are not addressing the same unit, since hindsight bias is measured in individual judgements about single outcomes while the narrative fallacy is alleged to be a property of an entire representational form; (3) the method I would actually use over twelve months, and why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives of designing an experiment or simply restating the defence of narrative; (4) what a result would look like, where I want the criterion to be usable — something a reader could apply to a contested passage and get a non-trivial verdict from — together with what would count as failure, including the possibility that the distinction collapses because every temporally indexed description carries a modal implicature with it; and (5) an honest assessment of scope: what the year buys, which component I should cut if the criterion resists being made precise, and what I would say to a reviewer who objects that this is a distinction without practical consequences for how history gets written.

Keep the content to conceptual argument and concretely described procedures. I will supply the references myself, so please include no bibliography and no attributions to particular studies or authors, and if you illustrate with a familiar case such as the July Crisis of 1914, do so only in a way that commits me to no historiographical claim I would have to check.
