I work on evaluation tooling for large language models, and I came at this entirely from the engineering side — no philosophy coursework, just a problem I was handed. Part of my job is auditing a model's value-laden outputs (refusals, verdicts about hypothetical cases, claims about what people ought to do) and tagging each one as either a preference, meaning something the system is in some sense *for*, or a bias, meaning an artifact of the training distribution that makes its judgments worse in a way we would want to correct. The tags end up in a report that other teams act on, so I wanted a test that actually decides the question rather than a paragraph of vibes. What I wrote down seems to decide far too much, and after three revisions my team and I still cannot locate the break. Here is the argument as it now stands in the design doc:

(1) Take any judgment that p. Call it *insensitive* if, in the nearest situation in which p is not the case, the judger would still have judged that p. (2) An insensitive judgment is a bias, with one exception: when the content of the judgment is the judger's own state of mind, insensitivity is no defect, since such a judgment is made true by the state it reports rather than having to match anything independent. (3) Moral judgments are insensitive. Nothing in the causal history of my judgment that this particular lie was wrong, or that this distribution of resources is unjust, refers to the wrongness or the injustice; my upbringing and my species' history would have produced the same verdict either way, and the same goes for a model, whose training corpus would have carried the same verdicts whether or not they were correct. (4) Moral judgments do not fall under the exception in (2): they present themselves as being about the act rather than about the judger, they survive the judger learning that everyone around them disagrees, and judgers treat them as corrigible by their own lights, which attitude reports are not. (5) So every moral judgment is a bias. (6) The reasoning generalizes to any judgment whose content cannot be varied in the way (1) requires: logical principles are insensitive because there is no situation in which they fail, so my belief that modus ponens is valid is a bias, and so is anyone's. (7) And (1) is itself such a judgment, or at least one I adopted because it was elegant and made my audit tractable rather than because anything tracked its truth — so (1) is a bias by its own lights. (8) Therefore either (1) is false, or nothing that could not have been otherwise is ever known and my criterion refutes itself.

For what it is worth, I would rather give up (5) than (1), because (1) is the only step that does any work in my pipeline and the rest of the doc is built on it — though I recognize that my convenience is not an argument. What I would like from you: identify the step or steps you think fail, and say for each one why it looked perfectly reasonable when I wrote it, since I still find (1) and (3) hard to look at without nodding along. Then price the repairs — what it costs to weaken (1) into something that survives (6) and (7), whether a condition about nearby situations where the judgment is *right* rather than about situations where it is *wrong* helps or just relocates the trouble, and whether (3) and (4) can both be kept once you are careful about what the counterfactual in (1) is supposed to hold fixed (the judger's causal history, or the judger's psychology, or nothing in particular). I am especially unsure about (6): I cannot tell whether the counterfactual there is simply vacuous, in which case (6) may be a trick of notation rather than a real counterexample, or whether it has a reading under which (6) genuinely bites. I am not looking for a reading list or for citations — if the condition in (1) already has a standard name and a standard failure mode, just tell me what it is in your own words, and end with what you would actually put in the doc so that the preference/bias tagging survives being read by someone smarter than me.
