Unicode is basically the phone book of written characters — every one gets a number, and the number is supposed to be *the character*, not any particular drawing of it. So a Times 'a', a Helvetica 'a' and my awful handwriting 'a' all share a codepoint, and the single-storey g people write by hand and the double-storey g you see in books are officially the same character even tho they barely look related. Same logic for East Asia: the standard deliberately handed one codepoint to Han characters whose stroke shapes differ from country to country and pushed those differences down into the fonts, which Japanese typographers were pretty furious about at the time.

Then flip it around. In most fonts the Latin A, the Cyrillic А and the Greek Α are pixel-for-pixel identical, and they get three different codepoints, becuase they belong to three different scripts. That mismatch is the entire basis of the spoofed-domain-name phishing trick, and it's why the consortium ended up publishing a big list of "confusables." ASCII went the other way again and gave the apostrophe and the opening single quote one shared codepoint despite meaning different things. Meanwhile ß is a full character with its own capital form, but the ﬁ ligature is officially nothing more than f followed by i, and the Ohm sign and the micro sign are declared to really be Greek omega and Greek mu. And a flag emoji is two letters, a family emoji is several codepoints glued together with a zero-width joiner, so what a user counts as one symbol is often three.

So what theory of what-makes-a-letter-that-letter is this practice actually committed to? Is there a single one — identity fixed by role in a writing system, say, with shape treated as mere costume — or is this a pile of engineering compromises and legacy round-tripping that only *looks* like a theory when you write it up after the fact? Which rival accounts does it rule out: the view that a letterform just is its shape and style, so that a Helvetica 'a' and a handwritten one are different letters of a kind? Or a view on which being an 'a' is a graded, boundary-less matter of sliding similarity across everything anyone has ever written that counts as one? Be concrete about where the practice is genuinely inconsistent with itself versus where the pieces can be reconciled under something principled, and be honest about which parts are philosophically underdetermined — several theories licensing the same numbering. Finally: if you swapped in one of those rival views, what would have to change — search, spelling checkers, domain names, passwords, font licensing — and is there any version of the practice that could survive a shape-first or a fuzzy-category theory at all?
