For about two years now I've been texting hypotheticals to my friend R. and making her commit to a verdict in writing. She's the right person to do this with — she works on allocation decisions at a foundation that funds very weird longshot projects, and unlike most people she'll actually pick a side and then explain it, and she doesn't get defensive when you tell her she's contradicting herself. I'm building an exercise for a summer school workshop (about 20 students, roughly half of them already sold on some version of "the far future is what matters," which will make the room fun) and I want to send her one more case before I finalize the handout, partly because I've got $50 riding with a mutual friend on what she'll say.

The log so far — verdict first, then whatever she said about it:

1. St. Petersburg ticket at $25: buy. I re-asked at $25 million: buy. "Infinite is infinite — I don't get to act like the number became less real because it started frightening me."
2. A 1-in-10,000 chance of averting an extinction that kills 8 billion, versus a certain program that saves 200,000 lives: take the gamble (800,000 expected lives against 200,000). Her gloss was that nothing about the arithmetic changes when the numbers get big, and if I think otherwise I should point at the step in the multiplication I'm objecting to.
3. A button. Costs a dollar, zero downside, and — stipulated as exact, machine-verified quantum randomness, no testimony involved — with probability 10^-12 it brings about a future containing 10^24 flourishing people spread over the next billion years. Don't press it and the status quo just continues; nobody dies, everything is fine. She said don't press. Her words: "There's a number of people past which I stop believing the sentence means anything. Ask me about a billion good lives and I'll say yes without blinking. Ask me about a septillion and I'm not disagreeing with you, I just don't think you've described an outcome."
4. Mugger, $5, says he's torturing 10^40 people in a simulation unless you pay; the case instructs you to grant his claim 10^-30 credence. Refuse — but her objection was aimed at the setup rather than the stakes. She wouldn't grant the stipulation, and she added that a person who happens to name figures that beat your arithmetic is telling you something about himself.
5. A foundation going all-in on a bet with enormous expected value and a 1-in-500 chance of destroying an existing program that reliably saves 200 lives a year forever. Refuse. "Expected value is an average over branches and I only ever live through one of them."
6. A ticket whose payoffs alternate in sign and grow fast enough that the series can be rearranged to sum to whatever you like. She wouldn't rank it against anything at all — no expected value exists, so "take the bigger one" isn't advice, it's a misuse of a tool; ask her instead about the order the payoffs arrive in and how long you're willing to hold the ticket.

Here's the case I want to send her. A made-up nonprofit offers you a contract for $10. The lottery is stipulated exact again, run by a university physics lab, and the nonprofit gains nothing from whether you sign — they're not recruiting you, they've said they'd be fine if you walked away, theres no pitch and no testimony. With probability exactly 1 in 100 million, a therapy already sitting in that lab's freezers reaches every person currently alive with a certain disease, about two billion of them, and each gets eighty additional healthy years. Otherwise nothing happens at all. No risk to any program you currently fund. I'm deliberately going to describe the beneficiaries as people — cities, grandparents, the texture of the extra decades — instead of as a utility figure, and I'm going to point out that the whole gamble's expected value comes to about 1,600 person-years, which is a completely ordinary number she was fine with in case 2. The comparison option is spending the same money on a program that certainly delivers 800 person-years.

So: what does she say? Give me the verdict and a rough confidence. More importantly, walk me through which rules are still live after all six rulings and which the log has already killed — I can see for myself that case 3 rules out plain maximize-expected-value, I want the finer cuts. Some of her refusals look like they're about the size of the outcome, some about the size of the probability, and at least one seems to really be about who's doing the offering, so tell me for each surviving rule which prior verdicts it explains cleanly and which it has to strain over. Include whether bounded utility would show up here as a refusal or just as a shrunken expected value that lets the certain 800 person-years win outright, since those are different claims about her. Then name the single next case I should send after this one that would split the survivors — what one-factor-at-a-time change makes two of those rules disagree — and if you think no single rule covers all six and she's just inconsistent somewhere, say which verdict has to go and which one she'd most likely sacrifice.
