I am in Year 13 and writing my Extended Project on how historians use the word "collapse". I have been reading Joseph Tainter's account, on which collapse is a rapid and substantial loss of socio-political complexity, together with the more popular treatments descended from Jared Diamond and a good deal of criticism saying that these cases are better described as transformation or reorganisation. Trying to get clear on that criticism, I wrote out the strongest version I could manage, and I now find that I cannot accept its conclusion and cannot see where the reasoning goes wrong.

(1) For a society to collapse, something must cease to exist; a collapse is not a change in a thing but the disappearance of a thing.
(2) The candidates for the thing that disappears are only the people, their culture, or their political system.
(3) The people never disappear. In the standard cases — the burning of the Mycenaean palaces, the abandonment of the cities of the southern Maya lowlands, the end of moai-carving on Rapa Nui — the inhabitants stayed, moved, or were absorbed, and their descendants are identifiable today.
(4) The culture does not disappear either. Linear B vanished from the record for centuries, but Greek went on being spoken; Maya languages and Maya identity persist in Guatemala and southern Mexico; Rapa Nui's language and genealogies were carried down to the people the first Europeans met in 1722. The familiar "ecocide" account of the island, on which the population destroyed itself before contact, is in any case now widely disputed.
(5) That leaves the political system, and political systems come and go in every society without our saying that the society collapsed. Nobody thinks France collapsed in 1789, or that the end of the Soviet Union in 1991 destroyed a society: we say that a state ended and that its people reorganised themselves.
(6) So in the paradigm cases nothing disappeared. There was a reorganisation that happened to be quick and archaeologically conspicuous — conspicuous because the palaces, the monuments and the inscriptions stop.
(7) Therefore "collapse" names not a kind of event but a verdict on how dramatic and how regrettable a reorganisation looks from where we are standing. It has no descriptive content, and historians who use the word are smuggling an evaluation into what they present as evidence.

What I cannot accept is (7). If it is true, a whole literature is empty, and I am also committed to saying that nothing went wrong for the people of the twelfth-century Aegean beyond the fact that they stopped building palaces, which I find impossible to believe. At the same time I feel a pull towards (1), because I want "collapse" to be a claim about the world rather than a mood. (3) and (4) look broadly right to me as matters of fact, so I suspect the trouble lies elsewhere.

Please tell me which single step you would strike out first, and precisely why it looked unobjectionable before you struck it. Then say what the remainder of the argument costs you once that step is gone — in particular, what you would have to say a society is, such that it can be lost while its people survive — and, for each other step you regard as a serious candidate, what it would cost to strike that one out instead. Finish by telling me which rejection is cheapest, and by giving me a formulation of "collapse" that survives this argument, one I could state in my opening paragraph without begging the question. I am not asking for sources or further reading, only for your diagnosis and your reasoning. Please write your answer in British English.
