Last week our reading group took up the objection that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the separateness of persons. A defender of total utilitarianism waved the objection away by reaching for the old maxim that everybody is to count for one and nobody for more than one: on his account, adding up welfare across persons simply is the practice of according each of them equal standing, so the objection amounts to charging utilitarianism with violating the very principle on which it is built. I left unpersuaded but unable to say why, and I now suspect the maxim is being asked to carry a weight it cannot bear — or else that it can bear that weight only on a reading the objector has independent reason to reject. I would like to sort this out before we meet again.

Please set out the distinct precise theses this maxim could be expressing, as a menu rather than as a single favoured interpretation. Axes worth separating include: whether it concerns which interests are admitted into the calculation at all, or the weight each person's interests carry once admitted; whether it is a claim about the deliberative standpoint of the agent or about the structure of the right-making facts; whether it demands equal treatment or merely treatment as an equal; whether the unit that counts is the person or the episode of welfare attributable to a person; and whether it is a moral claim at all rather than a political one about counting voters, which may be its original home and whose subject matter is countable in a way welfare is not.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude no one disputes, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false — and whether total utilitarianism actually satisfies it. Then address the question I care most about: which of these readings, if any, licenses my opponent's dismissal, and is there a reading on which the same maxim supports the separateness objection rather than answering it? If the only readings that support the dismissal are ones the objector can reject without begging the question, say so explicitly, since that would explain why the exchange felt like a stalemate.

Finally, one loose end. If counting for one means that each person counts once rather than in proportion to the welfare they contain, what follows about summing a single person's gains and losses across different times — does the maxim, on that reading, condemn intrapersonal aggregation as well, and does utilitarianism fare any better there than it does across persons? Please lay the readings out as a numbered list I can bring to the meeting.
