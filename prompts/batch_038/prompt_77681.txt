Here's the half-baked idea I want turned into a proposal.

Every critique of futarchy I've run into treats the manipulation problem as one more item in a list of practical objections — thin markets, the choice of welfare proxy (some net-national-spending measure or whatever), the "you're abdicating collective self-rule" complaint, the "real policies aren't discrete securities" complaint. I think the list format is hiding something structural. In an ordinary prediction market, buying up the "yes" contract doesn't help make the event happen; the price sits downstream of the world. Under futarchy the price IS the decision variable, so pushing up the price on the conditional "policy adopted" security is pushing up the very quantity that triggers adoption. The market isn't forecasting a fixed state of affairs, it's participating in selecting which state of affairs there will be. And the stock reply to manipulation — that it's self-defeating, because the manipulator still eats the loss when the price reverts to the true value, so only manipulations that are actually welfare-improving pay — seems to quietly assume two things: that there's a settled true value for the price to revert to, and that the other branch of the conditional is genuinely traded and arbitraged. Neither holds. The not-adopted branch basically never resolves, so the comparison isn't between a moved price and a disciplined price, it's between a moved price and a price with no information in it at all.

Second, less formed half of the idea: maybe the honest upshot isn't cleverer market design but changing what the market is being asked to do — markets as advisory inputs feeding a pre-committed implementation schedule, rather than markets as the rule — which gives up exactly the feature that made futarchy attractive. I want the proposal to confront that trade-off instead of waving at it.
