I have a ninety-minute whiteboard session with about a dozen first-year computer science PhD students on Maxwell's demon. They know Shannon entropy cold — source coding, mutual information, some Kolmogorov complexity — and essentially no thermodynamics: no partition functions, and to them entropy means bits. My goal is narrow: by the end they should be able to say where the second law actually gets saved and why the familiar one-line answer is not it. Plan the session for me; don't explain the physics to me.

I need:

1. The order the pieces must go in, given that they will try to cash out thermodynamic entropy in bits within the first five minutes — what has to be nailed down before the demon is even introduced, and what depends on what.
2. A verdict on Szilard's one-molecule engine as the load-bearing case. I suspect its isothermal expansion step smuggles in machinery they don't have; if so, what should carry the argument instead, and what must that case have and lack to do the job.
3. The misconceptions I should expect and the exact point in the sequence where each must be headed off. I'm counting on information as a kind of fuel you spend, measurement as the expensive step, Landauer's principle as an extra law of nature, and the demon losing because it forgets.
4. Where the circularity worry belongs, if it belongs at all: the charge that the erasure cost is itself derived by assuming the second law it is invoked to save. I want them able to state that objection and see what does and doesn't answer it — tell me if ninety minutes with this audience is too early.
5. One question for the last five minutes that separates having followed the argument from having memorized the slogan.
6. What I should openly refuse to cover, and say so aloud.

Mark clearly which parts of this are settled and which remain genuinely disputed among people who work on it; don't hand me one camp's position as the consensus.
