ok so — I'm about two thirds through a novel and I have been ducking chapter 19 for weeks, which usually means it's the important one.

Two of my characters are philosophers in their fifties who've known each other since grad school and have had this exact argument maybe forty times. They're in a hospital recovery area at six in the morning waiting for a mutual friend to come round from a general anaesthetic, and the waiting has gone on long enough that the arguing has started again. Write me the scene: straight dialogue, with only the minimum of gesture and movement you need to keep it from floating. No weather, no symbolic vending machine. Call it 1200 to 1500 words.

Here's what it needs to be doing under the hood, since this is the bit I can't get right on my own. One of them is a bottom-up panpsychist of the Russellian-monist stripe: physics only ever describes structure and dispositions, so the intrinsic nature of the physical has to be experiential, which means the microparticles are already feeling something, and a mind like ours is what you get when a lot of those micro-experiences are bonded the right way. The other is a priority cosmopsychist: the only fundamental subject is the cosmos, and people are modifications or aspects of that one field, so there is never any many-to-one fusion to explain at all.

The panpsychist's whole position owes an answer to the combination problem — James's old point that a crowd of feelings each sealed inside its own privacy doesn't somehow pool into one further feeler, plus the palette worry that a small stock of basic qualities can't generate the unbounded richness of actual experience. Her settled answer is a bonding relation she openly admits is brute; she insists it's no worse than any other fundamental relation, and it earns its keep because without it a person is literally just a heap of tiny subjects that happen to be adjacent. The cosmopsychist thinks that's a confession dressed as a theory. But he has his own version of the same trouble, the decombination one: how does one subject's experience throw off genuinely separate subjects? His best move is to point at what any single field of consciousness already does — the earworm, the itch you can't locate, the mood with no object, the dream-content that surfaces half formed — and say look, you live inside a subject that contains many quasi-independent contents every night, so pluralising a field is the operation we actually have evidence for, whereas fusing two subjects is the one nobody can even describe. Her counter should be sharp and right: those contents are not subjects, and describing one field with many contents is a description of unity, not plurality. To get two subjects out of one you need hard edges, and whatever does the hardening is going to look a hell of a lot like the bonding relation he was so smug about.

Nobody wins. That's the only rule I really care about. Both of them are the best people I could have put in that room — neither is the smart one, neither makes a howler the other politely lets slide, and nobody converts anybody. What I want is the feeling of a very old argument hitting a place it hasn't hit before because of where they're sitting. The friend under the anaesthetic makes the abstraction unbearable, so keep the scene sliding between the technical moves and the thing they're actually next to. Somebody has to say something about what it means that for two hours a person apparently wasn't there while the atoms kept feeling: on her view the subject is a standing achievement of binding that can lapse; on his view its a modification that dims. Let each of them press the other on whether their account can say what makes the person who wakes up the same one who went under — because if the binding relation is brute, so are its identity conditions, and if modifications are all you have, what makes this modification the same modification twice.

Craft notes. Let them interrupt, use shorthand, refer to a seminar in 1998 and to the paper one of them wrote that the other thinks is their best work and also wrong. Let one of them get tired enough to be honest for about four sentences before recovering. Each should concede one real thing at a moment where it costs them — she can't say what the bonding relation is, only that it does the work; he hasn't solved decombination, he's moved it, and his modifications may be doing the same silent labour. Don't signpost the concessions, I want to feel them rather than be told about them.

And no footnotes, no bibliography, no suggested further reading tacked on the end, and please don't have either of them explain the combination problem from scratch like the other hasn't heard of it. If they need to gesture at James they can, briefly, the way two people who both know the passage gesture at it.
