Write an essay of roughly 1,800–2,200 words on the following question: can a theory that has never made contact with observation be evidentially confirmed at all, or does what goes by the name of non-empirical confirmation amount to something else entirely — a warrant for continuing to work on a theory, a judgement about its mathematical fertility, or a claim about a research community rather than about the world?

Begin from a stipulated case. Treat it as fixed and reason about it directly; do not go looking for its real-world counterpart, and do not substitute one you know.

A framework, which I will call K-theory, has been developed for forty years by roughly six hundred researchers. It is mathematically consistent, and its internal constraints are tight enough that practitioners repeatedly report being unable to alter any of its central postulates without the whole structure ceasing to be consistent. Over those forty years no empirically inequivalent rival with comparable internal coherence has been constructed; the three serious attempts all failed on consistency grounds. K-theory has also been found to entail two structural features of a neighbouring theory that was independently confirmed, features its developers were not trying to explain. Finally, two narrower precursor theories in the same lineage were eventually confirmed empirically, decades after their proposal.

Suppose an advocate argues that these facts — the absence of alternatives, the unexpected explanatory interlock with a confirmed neighbouring theory, and the lineage's track record — jointly raise the rational credence that K-theory is true, notwithstanding that no observation bears on it directly. An opponent argues that at most they raise confidence that K-theory is a well-chosen object of study, and that any slide from the second claim to the first is a fallacy dressed as an inference.

Adjudicate between them. Your essay must do all four of the following.

First, state what is doing the evidential work in the absence-of-alternatives premise. In particular, say what the advocate must assume about the likelihood that a consistent rival both exists and would have been found by now if it did, in order for that premise to move credence appreciably — and whether such an assumption can be grounded when the relevant base rates are estimated from a handful of historical cases drawn from one narrow region of science.

Second, confront the difficulty I regard as the most serious one available, which you should engage rather than wave away: the absence-of-alternatives datum is generated by the search behaviour of the very community that reports it. A discipline that trains nearly all its members in a single lineage, and that treats rival constructions as unworthy of effort, will report finding no alternatives whether or not alternatives exist. On this reading the criterion rewards insularity — a discipline can raise its own apparent degree of non-empirical confirmation simply by shrinking and by discouraging outsiders from trying. Say whether this shows the criterion is not confirmation at all, or only that it requires external audit; and if the latter, say what such an audit could consist in that is not itself a further appeal to the same community's judgement.

Third, ask whether the three premises are evidentially independent. If the tightness of K-theory's constraints is what produces both the repeated failure to construct rivals and the neat fit with the neighbouring confirmed theory, say what follows for how much confirmation the premises supply jointly rather than severally.

Fourth, distinguish the claim that a theory is probably true from the claim that it merits the continued investment of scarce intellectual resources, and assess how much of the controversy is a conflation of these two. If a good part of it is, identify what is left over that is genuinely in dispute.

Then commit to a verdict. A conditional verdict is acceptable and probably preferable: specify the conditions under which non-empirical confirmation would be legitimate, and say whether K-theory satisfies them. Do not close by restating the balance of considerations and declining to say which way they fall. End by stating what development, if any, would lead you to abandon your verdict, described specifically enough that a reader could check whether it has occurred.

Write without citations, footnotes, bibliography, or attribution of positions to named philosophers. Argue from the stipulated case and from general reasoning about evidence and inference, not from recollection of who has said what about string theory or the multiverse.
