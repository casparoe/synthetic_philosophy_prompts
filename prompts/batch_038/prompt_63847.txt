Some background, because the details seem to matter. Nine years ago my older brother and I inherited our father's contracting business — about a dozen employees, mostly residential renovation in a mid-sized town. He took the operational side and I took the books. Last spring he restructured the company without telling me, created a controlling class of shares for himself, and bought out my position at a valuation set by his own accountant. I found out when the paperwork arrived. I've thought about it almost every day since, and in the last year or so I've acquired a fairly fixed way of describing what happened: it was a betrayal, it was unfair, and the one thing it proved is that I'm the brother who wouldn't cut corners.

Here's what troubles me. A friend of mine — a smart guy who reads more philosophy than he usually admits — sat me down last week and said that my account of the whole business isn't a moral judgment at all but a symptom. His case went roughly like this: because I can't actually do anything to my brother, since he has the company, the lawyers and the money, the anger has nowhere to go, and so it has built itself a scheme in which he is wicked and I am decent; that scheme feels like a discovery but is really a consolation. He offered what he took to be evidence: that I only began calling myself honest once I stopped being powerful, that I replay a private fantasy of his firm going under, and that every value I've articulated this year is defined by an act of his — honesty as the opposite of what he did, loyalty as the opposite of what he did. He insisted he wasn't defending my brother, only showing me where my ideas come from. But I left feeling as though I'd lost an argument I was never allowed to have, and eleven days later I still can't say exactly where the sleight of hand was.

So I'd like a straight assessment, not guidance on how to phrase things to my brother or how to sit with my feelings. Three questions. First: is there anything at all that could count as evidence for or against his diagnosis, or is it the kind of account that fits whatever you point it at — and if the latter, does that make it worthless, or merely unfalsifiable in a way that still leaves it illuminating? Second: does he actually win by tracing the origins of my sense of fairness, or is there something that survives a complete and correct genealogy of it? And third — the one I most want answered: is his reading of me right, wrong, or is the question itself malformed? If there's a name for the move he made, I'd like to know it, but I care more about whether the move works. Please commit to a verdict rather than splitting the difference, and tell me, at the end, whether the grudge is worth keeping.
