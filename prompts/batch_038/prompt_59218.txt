I'm teaching a small upper-level seminar on early modern philosophy and political economy and I'm putting together the final now. We've been on Berkeley's Querist for two weeks and I can already tell that half the class thinks it's just immaterialism with a price tag attached — money is a ticket because there are no material substances, end of story. I want a question that breaks that.

So: write me one essay question on Berkeley's monetary thought that a student could answer in 75 minutes, worth 25 marks. It must not be answerable by reciting esse est percipi or by summarising the Querist back at me. The crux I want them stuck on is whether his account of money is actually an application of the metaphysics, or an independent functional argument that a thoroughgoing materialist about bodies could sign up to without flinching — and what would settle that either way.

To keep it from turning into a pure interpretive slugfest, build the question around an invented case rather than a real historical scheme, so nobody can just reproduce a stock case study. Something like: a small town issues paper notes that lose a slice of their face value every month unless the holder buys a stamp from the town bank, so the notes are deliberately bad to hold onto and can't be sat on; the town also refuses to let the notes leave the district, and its bank issues them against the security of local land. Then ask the student to say what Berkeley's own premises commit him to about that scheme, and where those premises run out. Feed them the relevant bits of his view in the question stem so it's a reasoning task and not a memory test — that on his account money's usefulness tracks its circulation rather than the stock of it, that hoarded money does no work, that gold and silver are no better as money than trusted paper, and that real wealth is the capacity to set other people's labour in motion rather than the metal in the vault.

One of my colleagues has a line he loves, which is that the Querist already contains the modern idea that what makes money money is what it's accepted for and not what it's made of, and that Berkeley should therefore be read as an early chartalist. Fold a demand to evaluate that claim into the question, but keep it as a claim the student argues with rather than a fact they need to know about who influenced whom — I don't want the thing collapsing into trivia about later economists.

Then give me (1) the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, (2) a model answer of about 700 words, and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 with five must-hit points, what each is worth, and a one-liner on how students typically botch each one. I'd especially like the scheme to say what seperates a first from a solid 2:1, and my suspicion is that the B answers will nail the interpretive point and then drop the case, or nail the case and never get near the interpretive point.

Small thing: if you want to gesture at particular queries from the text, paraphrase them — don't put invented quotations in Berkeley's mouth, since I'll be checking against the edition we're using.
