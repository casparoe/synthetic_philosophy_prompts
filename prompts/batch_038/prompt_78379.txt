Last autumn I sat through the closing session of a two-day public forum on how journalists ought to write about age and cohort, and the final panel dissolved into a direct argument between two of the participants. I was in the second row, took notes, and have tried to reconstruct the exchange faithfully below. One participant was a polling executive who has run demographic trackers for twenty years; the other was a professor of sociology who works on classification in the social sciences. Neither was a fool, and I think each landed real blows. I left unable to say who won, which is why I am asking.

The announced topic was whether generational labels — Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z — pick out anything real.

(1) **Pollster.** In every survey we field, the spread of attitudes *within* a generation dwarfs the difference *between* generation averages. Age does a great deal of explanatory work; the birth-year bracket adds almost none. And when a gap does appear, we cannot tell whether it belongs to the cohort, to the historical period everyone was living through, or simply to being young — those three are arithmetically locked together, since a respondent's cohort is just the survey year minus their age. No dataset separates them without an assumption imported from outside the data. So a generation is not a quantity we measure. It is a narrative we lay over quantities we did measure.

(2) **Sociologist.** Your first point is true and does not do what you need it to do. The same is true of the middle class, the working class, and the Global South: enormous internal variance, no essence, and yet perfectly real, because each has a causal history and consequences. Your second point cuts the other way. Precisely because age, period, and cohort are collinear, a generation cannot be a natural experimental kind at all. There is an old distinction in my discipline between being *located* in a generation and actually *constituting* one: sharing a birth range puts you in a position, and only when the people in that position respond to it in common does a generation come into being. Being born in 1997 or later makes you nothing. Being formed by a particular stretch of history, and then acting on that formation, makes you something.

(3) **Pollster.** There is the move I was waiting for — and notice what it costs you. If a generation exists only once its members take themselves to be one, then the label's function is not to describe but to recruit. Pollsters, marketers, journalists, and human-resources departments have spent thirty years telling people which cohort they belong to and what it is like. You then measure the result and call it confirmation. The label is cashing its own cheque. That is exactly the structure of astrology: if everyone read their horoscope and organized their life around it, astrologers could generate confirming data indefinitely without ever having been right about anything.

(4) **Sociologist.** The analogy fails for want of a mechanism. Astrology posits an influence that is not there. Here the channels are ordinary and well documented: people of the same age sit in the same schools, enter the same labour market at the same moment, meet the same technology at the same stage of life, and absorb the same shocks at the same developmental point. Those are genuine causes. A feedback loop that amplifies an existing tendency is not a loop that manufactures one from nothing. Consider money. Money exists because we treat it as existing, and that does not make it a fiction; it has a mechanism, a history, and consequences. Social kinds are real in that sense and not in the sense of quarks.

(5) **Pollster.** Then tell me where it begins and where it ends. Nobody can give a non-arbitrary boundary. There is no difference worth naming between a person born on 31 December 1996 and a person born on 1 January 1997, yet the one closes out the Millennials and the other opens Gen Z. The cutoff our industry uses was fixed to yield a bracket roughly the length of the one before it and to suit clients who wanted a clean name; the justification supplied afterwards is that everyone in the bracket lived through the same events. If the edges of a category are set by convenience and nothing inside them has an essence, then the category is a ruler we made and not a joint we found.

(6) **Sociologist.** Conventional boundaries are routine in serious work and do not make the categories fake. The line between adolescence and adulthood is conventional; so is the line between a lake and a pond, and between a slowdown and a recession. What matters is whether the line is drawn across a gradient that is really there — and you have already conceded the gradient, in the form of shared formative conditions. The boundary is imprecise because the underlying thing is a matter of degree, not because we invented it.

(7) **Pollster.** A matter of degree in *which property*? You cannot state it. Asked what distinguishes the youngest cohort, the honest answer is a list of tendencies that leak in every direction — more anxious, more online, drinking less, driving later, more accepting of difference — each of which is either a period effect that will fade, an age effect that will reverse, or true of a bare plurality. A category that can be rendered only as a cluster of leaky tendencies, none of them pinned to a cause, is not a kind. It is a mood with a birth range attached.

(8) **Sociologist.** Hold that standard to any category in the human sciences. Depression. The service economy. A recession. A profession. All clusters, all contested, all admitting exceptions — and your standard would abolish the lot of them. If a kind requires sharp boundaries and an essence, then almost nothing in social life is a kind, and your scepticism cannot be wrong about anything, which is not the virtue you seem to think it is. My position is specific and could fail: cohorts matter when they have been jointly formed and then jointly labelled, and matter much less when they have not. That is a claim with a chance of being false.

(9) **Pollster.** And it is very nearly the claim I opened with, which is my real objection. Generation is doing rhetorical work that age, period, class, and parental education could do more precisely and more honestly. When a headline announces that young people no longer want to buy houses, what is happening is that mortgage credit has tightened for first-time buyers and those who can still buy are disproportionately the children of people who already own. Write that instead, and the generation evaporates from the sentence.

(10) **Sociologist.** Sometimes it evaporates. Sometimes what survives your translation is precisely the thing your vocabulary cannot express. People who entered the labour market in a given bad year carry it for life; the effect does not wash out as they age and is still visible in them at sixty. The young are not a stable set but a slot that different people pass through. A cohort is the set that stays the same while it ages — which is why it can be scarred, and why your paraphrase loses the scar.

(11) **Pollster.** Then you have defined a cohort as everyone who was the same age in year X. That is a bookkeeping convention. Congratulations: birth years are real.

(12) **Sociologist.** A bookkeeping category becomes a kind when it carries a causal history that outlives the properties that first defined it. The class that graduated in 2009 is bookkeeping too, and it explains things.

At that point the moderator called time.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than in aggregate. For each of the twelve numbered moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and identify what it actually establishes as distinct from what it assumes or overstates. Then address these questions specifically:

1. Does the collinearity point in (1) tell against the reality of generations, or only against one way of estimating their effects? Is there an assumption that could break the tie, and what would adopting it cost?

2. The analogies in (3) and (4) carry more weight than anything else said that evening. Do they succeed? What would have to be true for astrology to be the apt comparison, what would have to be true for money to be the apt comparison, and is there a disanalogy either side failed to press?

3. Does the boundary argument across (5) and (6) settle anything about kindhood? It strikes me that something odd is going on here: the extension is perfectly exact — birth dates are a matter of record — while the property attributed to that extension is vague. I cannot tell whether that combination helps or hurts the sociologist, since precise-extension-but-empty-property categories are easy to construct, and so are vague-property categories that nobody doubts.

4. Each side in effect accuses the other of holding an unfalsifiable position, in (3) and (8). Is either charge fair? Is the reply in (8) — that the sceptic's standard abolishes nearly every category in the human sciences — a genuine reductio or a tu quoque?

5. Move (9) proposes what looks like a test of kindhood: a category earns its keep only if it survives translation into age, period, class, and education. Move (12) proposes a different test: a category earns its keep if it carries causal history that persists after the properties defining it have gone. Which test is better, and is there a case that passes one and fails the other?

6. What observation or analysis would have forced one of them to concede? Please propose something concrete that neither offered.

7. What did both leave out? I suspect at least one significant consideration never surfaced on either side, and that the audience was left with a false choice between two positions that share an unexamined premise.

Finally, say who is right about what, and who is more right overall. I recognize the second question may not admit a clean answer, but please give one anyway, with your reasons.
