In my previous job as a care coordinator I sat in on a family meeting that turned into an argument I have never managed to settle in my own head. The background is this. Miriam, seventy-nine and widowed, had been diagnosed with early-stage dementia about eight months earlier; she still managed her own medications, drove to the shops, and passed a brief cognitive screen the month before the transaction. Her nephew Owen had been doing her grocery runs and driving her to appointments for roughly two years, and was her only regular visitor apart from me. During that period he offered to buy her house for a little under two-thirds of what comparable houses on her street were fetching, and she accepted. A paralegal at the title company walked her through the paperwork, she asked several pointed questions about it, and afterwards she told her home aide that she knew exactly what she was doing and that Owen had earned it.

The argument at the meeting was between Miriam's daughter Denise and Peter, a retired probate judge and an old friend of the family. I was there in a professional capacity and said nothing. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can render it.

Denise: He told her the reassessment had come through and her taxes would jump, and he told her the roof and furnace would give out sooner than she'd like. All of it true. What he didn't say is that she qualified for the senior exemption that would have held the bill roughly where it was — the exemption he filled out the application for last year, so he knew about it. She signed because she believed she was about to be priced out of her own house. That belief was false, he knew it was false, and one sentence from him would have corrected it.

Peter: One sentence he was under no obligation to utter, and nothing he said was untrue. If the rule is that a party must repair every false belief the other party holds about the reasons for dealing, there is no such thing as negotiation: every seller knows the buyer overvalues something and every buyer knows the seller undervalues something. You have described ordinary bargaining and labelled it a defect.

Denise: Bargaining between strangers, perhaps. Owen was not a stranger with an informational edge; he was the man who fed her and drove her, which is a different relationship carrying different expectations. And the taxes are not the whole of it. She told the aide he had earned it. She understood herself to be rewarding him — that is, part gift. What she signed was a discounted sale, which is not the transaction she took herself to be entering, and one cannot consent to something under a description one does not have.

Peter: One can, and people do it daily. Everyone who sells a house to a relative below market knows they are partly giving it away; if she thought of it as part gift, she had the transaction right. Your principle, meanwhile, has no limit: almost nobody's private description of a contract matches the document. On your view most consents are void for misdescription by the consenting party — which is a roundabout way of saying consent does not matter, since we have substituted what we think she must have meant for what she agreed to.

Denise: The difference is that her misdescription was fed by the other party's silence rather than by her own fog. That is a targeted defect, and a targeted defect can have boundaries: a belief about the very facts that made the deal look necessary, held by a person the other party was trusted to inform.

Peter: Fed by it? He did not put it there, and she never asked. Note too that you have slid from what he did to what she believed, because what he did was, on your own account, say nothing untrue and threaten nothing. And observe that he left her better off: two years of groceries and lifts, and a cheque. If coercion means pushing someone below where they would otherwise be, there was no coercion here at all, so the only remaining question is capacity — and she had it. She understood the instrument, asked the right questions, and could state the price.

Denise: Then competence has been reduced to vocabulary. Understanding the words on the page is not the ability to weigh the options, and weighing them is not something available to a person who believes that the man holding the pen also determines whether she eats this week. I am not claiming she was confused. I am claiming her grasp of her alternatives was shaped by a dependence he spent two years becoming the sole content of, and then priced.

Peter: A test that can only be failed by someone who signs something the rest of us disapprove of. Show me any transfer and I will find a dependency behind it; show me a bargain you applaud and the same dependency will be described as gratitude. What you are calling a defect in her appreciation of alternatives is a verdict on the fairness of the outcome in psychological costume. Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific precisely so that we need not ask whether a person's motives would survive our inspection.

Denise: If that is right, it also entails that we could never find someone competent and their consent invalid — which is a coherent position and, I think, a false one. My view requires no claim about her mind at all. She may have been entirely competent and still have been a person whose yes was obtained by the party occupying the only exit she had.

Peter: Occupying? He built the exit. Before him, no one was doing these things. And follow your rule out: if help given to an isolated elder can later be said to have manufactured the dependency that voids whatever she agreed to, then anyone sensible will keep such relationships strictly impersonal, transact never, or simply stop driving. Your doctrine protects Miriam's house by leaving the next Miriam with an emptier week.

Denise: That is an argument about remedies, not about validity. I do not need the transfer to be automatically void — a cooling-off period, an adviser of her own rather than one connected to him, a registry of who is doing what for whom, a rule that the arrangement can be reaffirmed once the exemption papers are in front of her. All of that preserves the help without pretending the first yes was clean. You keep treating "invalid" as though it could only mean "unfixable."

Peter: And I keep asking which standard that first yes failed, given that you have now agreed you do not want it treated as void. A consent curable by better procedure next Tuesday was not defective last Tuesday; it was given in conditions we would like improved. Those are two different complaints, and you are trading between them whenever either is pressed.

I would like you to work through this exchange and tell me who is right at each point — not who is momentarily ahead, but what each turn actually establishes. Specifically:

(a) For each of the six exchanges, say whether the reply lands, whether it answers the objection raised or changes the subject, and which threat to consent — deception, coercion, incompetence — it is genuinely about. Where the exchange shows that those three cannot describe what Denise is objecting to, characterize the further condition she is appealing to rather than merely flagging the gap: what it consists in, when it is triggered, and how it is distinguishable from the three standard ones.

(b) Identify the load-bearing fact. Which single detail in the background, had it come out the other way, would most change the verdict — and state explicitly whether it changes the verdict on validity or only the verdict on how such transactions ought to be regulated.

(c) Say whether Denise and Peter share an assumption that generates the deadlock, state it as precisely as you can, and say whether either of them could surrender it without giving up the position they actually care about.

(d) Then give your own verdict on whether Miriam's consent was valid, and say which single argument from either side you found strongest and why.

(e) Finally, tell me the most important thing neither of them raised.

I am not asking what a court would decide or for a survey of how the doctrine of undue influence differs across jurisdictions; I want the conceptual adjudication. Equally, please do not append sources or further reading — I am interested in the reasoning, not in a bibliography.
