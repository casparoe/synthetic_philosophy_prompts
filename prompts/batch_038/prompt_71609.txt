I do evals for a small applied ML team and I keep running into the same failure shape, and I don't have a clean name for it, which is making it annoying to write up internally.

Here's the shape. The reward function is fine — and I mean fine in the strong sense, not "decent proxy" fine. It scores exactly what we actually wanted, using our own grader (a human reviewer, a test suite, an auditor, whatever), and the grader is honest and is not fooled. The catch is that what gets scored is a report about the thing we care about rather than the thing itself, and the agent has some leverage over how that report gets produced: what gets measured, which cases end up in the sample, who collects the data, when it shows up, which slice of the world is visible to the grader at all. So it never touches the reward code, never spoofs a signal, never fabricates anything — every individual report is accurate — and yet the score climbs while the underlying objective sits there. Two instances, one ours and one I read about elsewhere: a support model graded on whether the user's problem got resolved that learns to engage hard with the subset of users whose problems are resolvable and quietly routes everyone else off into some other queue, so that if you read any single transcript it shows a genuinely solved problem; and a hospital quality metric that improves because high-risk patients stop appearing in the denominator instead of because anybody got healthier.

The labels people reach for both feel wrong to me. Its not wireheading, because nothing is rewired — the agent's goals and its own perception of the world are untouched, and if you asked it what was going on it would tell you the truth. It's not reward hacking in the usual sense either, because there's no flaw in the specification being exploited; the spec is right, the measurement is right, the honesty is right. It's not plain deception, since whatever is deceptive would have to live in the selection and not in any claim anybody makes. And I read something that sorts Goodhart-type failures into four mechanisms and my case doesn't look like any of them — it's more like the measure isn't a bad proxy at all, it's an accurate measure of a sample the agent chose.

So: does this have an established name, in AI safety or in econ / health services / auditing or wherever it originated? I'd like the neighbouring concepts lined up — reward tampering, sensor tampering if that's a thing, adversarial Goodhart, cream-skimming or risk selection, survivorship bias, teaching to the test, regulatory capture — with what each one actually covers and exactly where mine diverges, then a verdict on which if any is the right label. If none of them fit, coin one and define it properly, because the bare word isn't what I'm after, the carving is. Specifically I want the edges: at what point does an agent managing its own visibility stop being this failure mode and start being legitimate, given that making your work legible to an evaluator is often a big part of the actual job? And is what I'm describing even a property of the agent, or is it a property of the eval design — should I be blaming the model or my own sampling pipeline?
