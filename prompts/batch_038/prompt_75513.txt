Write a short story of roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words built around a fictional lottery whose payout rule is stated once, plainly, in the opening paragraph and never explained again: a single coin is tossed until it comes up tails; the holder of the winning ticket receives one unit of the national currency if tails arrives on the first toss, two units if on the second, four if on the third, doubling with every additional head.

The institution is the Directorate, the lottery office of a small, poor, meticulously administered state, which has sold this ticket at a price fixed by statute for something like sixty years and has never once had to pay past the twentieth toss. Its reserve is sized to that history, and no payout has ever come out of current receipts: every winner in living memory has been paid from the interest the reserve earns. The story opens in the week when a ticket runs to the thirty-fourth toss.

The structural requirement is the heart of the commission. Divide the story into sections headed Round 1, Round 2, and so on through Round 34, and allocate prose so that the early rounds — where nearly all of the probability sits, and where all of the human beings are — receive the overwhelming majority of the length, while the late rounds, the ones that on paper carry almost all of the value, are dispatched in a sentence or two each. Do not comment on this allocation anywhere. Let the reader come to feel, without being told, that this is the correct way to spend attention, and let the shock of Round 34 arise precisely from how little room is left in which to receive it.

Three characters must carry the entire disagreement about what such a ticket is worth, and they must carry it through procedure, paperwork and dialogue — no exposition, no formulas written on glass, no one delivering a speech:

- the claimant, an old man or woman who has bought a ticket every week for decades and whose household has treated the ticket as an asset, a dowry, a kind of standing promise;
- the officer who keeps the reserve and whose competence consists in having been right about the tail for thirty years;
- the junior clerk who must actually sign the payment order and who is the only one of the three with no reputation to protect.

Further constraints:

- The word paradox and the phrase expected value must not appear. No mathematician, living or dead, may be named. No character may state any general principle about utility, risk, discounting or the limits of a treasury as a principle; each such consideration may surface only as a stubborn fact about this office — a line in a ledger, a clause in a loan agreement, a refusal from a bank, a form that will not fit the number.
- At least two independent reasons why the ticket commands so low a price must be dramatized and left unreconciled: one having to do with what the Directorate can physically raise in coin, one having to do with what a holder can actually do with the promise while still alive.
- Somewhere in the middle, the apparatus changes: beyond a certain round the coin is withdrawn and replaced by a sealed device or a drawn lot, because nobody will trust a coin tossed that many times in public. The substitution must alter nothing arithmetically and everything about credibility, and no character may say so.
- Let the payment order do real work in the closing pages: it requires three signatures and is printed on a form laid out in a year when twenty rounds seemed generous, with a fixed row of boxes for the amount.
- A framed copy of the founding statute hangs in the lobby, and the reserve it authorizes the Directorate to hold is a round number chosen for reasons that have nothing to do with the game. A reader should be able to notice this. No character should point it out.

The ending must not settle the question. The Directorate either pays something or does not, and the claimant's reaction must not be the one the reader has been trained to expect — it should become legible only on reflection. Tone throughout: restrained, documentary, faintly bureaucratic; no magical realism, no dream sequences, no narrator who addresses the reader. Give the story a title, and supply nothing after the last line — no afterword, no author's note, no gloss on what it means.
