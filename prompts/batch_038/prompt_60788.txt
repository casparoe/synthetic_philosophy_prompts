I'm in the third year of a dissertation in the philosophy of mathematics, and the chapter I'm stuck on defends a version of the indispensability argument that my committee thinks is either trivially true or not really an indispensability argument at all. Let me state it as precisely as I can. I do not want to say that ontological commitment follows from mathematics being indispensable to the *truth* of our best theories, because that seems to beg the question against the fictionalist, who simply denies that scientific theories are true when read literally and treats the mathematics as a productive fiction. Nor do I want to lean on mathematics being indispensable to *explanation*, since that version appears to rest on a small handful of contested examples, and the whole weight of the argument ends up hanging on whether a prime-numbered life cycle or a hexagonal comb cross-section is really explained by a mathematical fact rather than by a biological or geometric one. My claim is narrower and, I think, harder to dodge: commitment follows from indispensability to *derivation*. A mathematical posit earns its place in our ontology if there is no derivation of a theory's empirically testable consequences from nominalistically acceptable premises that a finite agent could actually carry through without quantifying over it — even if, as everyone grants, some derivation exists in principle. The corollary I most want pressure-tested is that this makes platonism *local*: it commits me to something like the continuum, or to real-valued fields over spacetime regions, because that is where the derivations of classical field theory and quantum theory appear to bottleneck, while leaving me free to be a fictionalist about the reaches of set theory that no physical derivation ever touches.

So: what would count against it? I want a map of its exposure rather than a survey of the debate, with the defeaters ranked from genuinely fatal to merely awkward. Which nominalist programs, if carried through, would actually refute the position rather than merely exist as a formal possibility — and what standard of feasibility decides the matter, given that the canonical nominalistic reconstruction of Newtonian gravitational theory is described even by its author as far too unwieldy for anyone to work in? Would a physics that went discrete, or a quantum-gravity program that recovered the standard predictions from a finitary substrate, be decisive against me, or could I absorb it by insisting that the derivations running from that substrate to anything measurable still require real-valued parameters? Does the position collapse into a claim about the cognitive and computational limits of finite agents, and if so is that a fatal admission, since a fact about us cannot settle a question about what exists? Does it leave the epistemological access problem completely untouched, so that I have bought an ontology without any account of how we come to know anything about it? And does the Quinean framework I am borrowing from threaten the determinacy of the commitment itself — if reference is inscrutable, have I shown that the continuum exists, or only that our theories are satisfied by something or other playing the continuum role? I would also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and, crucially, which of these objections a committed defender of my position would concede as counting against it rather than wave away, since I suspect the honest concessions are more damaging than the standard criticisms.

For calibration: I would be moved most by a concrete case in which the same empirical prediction has been derived both ways, with and without quantifying over the continuum, and both derivations short enough for a person to check, since that is exactly the situation my account says cannot obtain. I would be moved somewhat less by an argument that "feasibly indispensable" and "in principle dispensable" are not two notions but two descriptions of one, so that the distinction does no work at all. At our work-in-progress seminar the pushback I actually got was that this is instrumentalism with an ontology bolted on, and that locality is a defect rather than a refinement because it makes my platonism hostage to whatever physics happens to turn out to be; neither struck me as the real threat, and I would like your judgment on whether you agree. Please also tell me plainly if the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — the standing reply that any successful nominalization, however ingenious, simply has not been *used* looks suspiciously like a way of never losing — and whether that is a genuine problem for an argument of this kind or an acceptable feature of one whose conclusion is about our best total theory of the world. Assume familiarity with the standard positions; I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or a history of the controversy, only the defeaters, their relative force, and your view of which of them I should be losing sleep over before the chapter goes to my committee.
