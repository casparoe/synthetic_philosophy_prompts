I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science working on inference in the historical sciences, and I have been auditing an archaeological methods seminar in which frequency seriation is presented essentially as a finished instrument. My difficulty is that the manuals describe it as a technique while the procedure seems to me to be doing a great deal of theoretical work that nobody states. I would like help reconstructing the implicit commitments, and separating the ones the method cannot do without from the ones it merely sits comfortably beside.

The practice as I have reconstructed it, with the founding case being Petrie's ordering of Predynastic Egyptian graves by pottery frequencies. A set of assemblages from one region — grave groups, midden fills, floor deposits — is sorted into stylistic types (rim forms, decorative motifs, fabrics); the proportion of each type in each assemblage is computed; and the assemblages are then arranged along a single axis so that as many types as possible trace a frequency that rises from zero to a peak and falls back to zero, the so-called battleship curve. The arrangement that maximizes this unimodality is taken to be the chronological order, and it is then used to date assemblages that have no independent dates, including at other sites, on the assumption that an instance of a type found far from home sits somewhere on that type's curve. Where the resulting order agrees with stratigraphy at one or two well-excavated sites, that agreement is offered as validation of the ordering as a whole.

My questions:

1. What picture of cultural change does the ordering rule presuppose? Unimodality appears to require that a style originates once, spreads, peaks, and is never revived, and that a zero in the frequency matrix is a genuine absence rather than a depositional or sampling artifact — a style not yet invented, or already extinct, rather than one that was present and unrecovered. How much of this is a substantive empirical claim about transmission, and how much is built into the search criterion, such that the criterion is guaranteed to find it?

2. Is the circularity vicious? Unimodality is both the rule for generating the order and the evidence that the order is temporal. I can see two readings: that the method begs the question, or that it is a constraint-satisfaction whose guiding assumption is independently tested against stratigraphy and radiocarbon. Which reading is correct, and does it matter that the testing typically happens at only one or two sites in the whole sequence?

3. Please distinguish what the method strictly needs from what merely flatters it. For example, must seriation assume that styles change because of preference, emulation, or fashion, or would a blind random-copying model of transmission license exactly the same curves? If the latter, what claim about the motives of past agents has the method in fact given up — and is that loss visible in how the results get written up?

4. There is a tension I cannot dissolve. Elsewhere the same discipline insists that distributions of artifact types do not map onto social groups, that types are constructions of the classifier rather than categories the potters themselves had, and that a style may be shared across communities with no shared identity. Yet seriation treats those same type frequencies as a clock. How can style serve as an index of time while being no index of anything social? What would have to be true of copying — of who copies whom, over what distance, and at what rate — for both commitments to hold simultaneously?

5. Finally, what would have to change in practice under a rival presupposition: one on which styles recur cyclically, or on which assemblages are palimpsests accumulated over generations rather than contemporaneous snapshots? I am less interested in which rival is true than in identifying the particular assumption whose removal would render existing orderings unwarranted rather than merely noisy.
