**Exam question (20 marks):** Samuelson's revealed preference programme is standardly described as an attempt to make utility dispensable in the theory of consumer demand. Explain in what precise sense utility was supposed to be dispensable, what the programme had to show in order to succeed, and whether the subsequent debate — Houthakker's reformulation, Little's critique, Sen's objections — shows that it did or did not succeed.

**Student's answer:** Samuelson's complaint was that utility is an unobservable mental magnitude which cannot be measured, so a demand theory built on utility maximisation is not genuinely testable; the theory should instead be constructed entirely from observable data, namely prices, income and the bundles actually chosen. On this account a consumer directly reveals a preference for bundle x over bundle y whenever she chooses x at prices at which y was also affordable, and the weak axiom of revealed preference rules out her choosing x when y is affordable and then choosing y when x is affordable.

The programme's real achievement was that it recovered everything ordinal utility theory had. Houthakker showed that the weak axiom suffices to integrate observed choices into a complete and transitive ordering, and hence into an indifference map, so the ordinalist's utility function turns out to be nothing over and above a compact numerical description of choice behaviour. Utility is dispensable in the sense of being reducible: it survives only as a book-keeping device, not as a theoretical posit doing explanatory work.

Two criticisms followed. Little argued that revealed preference theory is behaviourally equivalent to ordinal utility theory, and so buys no methodological advantage: if the two frameworks generate the same predictions, the claim to have purged psychology from economics is empty. Sen pressed a related objection, namely that choice does not reveal preference, because agents act from sympathy and from commitment, and in cases of commitment an agent's chosen act may conflict with her own ranking of outcomes. Since both criticisms amount to showing that revealed preference theory cannot be tested against any body of data that ordinal utility theory could not equally accommodate, the programme failed on its own terms and is not an empirical theory at all. The dispute was in the end merely verbal: economists had simply relabelled preference as choice.
