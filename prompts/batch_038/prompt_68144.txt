Our formal epistemology reading group is working through Lewis on the probabilities of conditionals, and we have this little ritual where everyone writes down a number for the week's question before the discussion, so we can see how much anyone actually moved. I'm presenting Thursday and I've written my number down publicly twice already this term and been embarrassed twice, so I want an outside estimate first. The setup as I understand it: Adams's thesis says the probability of an indicative conditional "if A, C" just is the conditional probability P(C|A). Lewis's proofs show that if that identity holds for every pair of sentences, and holds not just for your prior P but for every conditionalization of P, then your probability function has to be trivial — the algebra collapses so the conditional comes out truth-functional and you're left with only a handful of probability-relevant possibilities. And the later paper is supposed to show that shrinking things doesn't rescue you: you still get triviality if you keep the thesis for a decent-sized class of pairs and a class of priors that stays closed under conditioning.

Here's the move I can't stop thinking about, and what I really want is a sense of how much weight it deserves. Every derivation I can actually reconstruct by hand seems to need two things: (a) that "A→C" is itself an element of the algebra, so that you're allowed to condition on it and form things like P((A→C)∧A); and (b) that P(A→C) is a genuine unconditional probability of a proposition, so the ratio analysis is available. So refuse both. Let the conditional denote something non-Boolean — a supposition-pair, or an element of an enlarged conditional-event algebra — and let its probability be given by a primitive two-place function instead of a ratio. Then the identity is an axiom of the framework rather than a discovery, and the steps that need conditioning on the conditional itself have nothing to grip. My worry, which is exactly where my own credence goes wobbly, is whether the proofs really need (a). Maybe you can get all the way to triviality using only conditionalizations on Boolean compounds like A∧C and A∧¬C, which live in the algebra whether or not the conditional does. If that's right my escape is dead on arrival and the suppositionalist conclusion is forced.

So the proposition I want a credence for, call it T: there is a coherent account of the indicative conditional on which Adams's thesis holds with full generality — every pair of sentences, every prior in a class closed under conditioning by the Boolean compounds of those sentences — while the probability structure stays non-trivial, in the sense that conditionals don't collapse into the material conditional and you don't get squeezed down to finitely many probability-relevant possibilities. Give me one number for T, but decomposed, not bare.
