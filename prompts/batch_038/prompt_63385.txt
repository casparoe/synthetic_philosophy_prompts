I am in my third year of a PhD in ancient history, working on the historiography of the Late Bronze Age Aegean, and I have become preoccupied with an argumentative move for which I cannot find a term. It recurs in all three of the cases my cohort argues about — Homer, Arthur, the Exodus — and always in the same shape. A narrative reaches us centuries after the events it purports to describe, with no near-contemporary witness, and the scholarly consensus, quite properly, treats it as legend. Then archaeology or an independent document corroborates some incidental detail: excavation at Hisarlik reveals a substantial Bronze Age settlement with destruction horizons; fragmentary Hittite records from Anatolia contain a western place name that most philologists connect with the poets' name for Troy, alongside a people whose name resembles their word for the Achaeans; a ninth-century Latin compilation, our earliest substantial witness, lists twelve battles for Arthur and styles him not a king but a war-leader; an Egyptian victory stele from the late thirteenth century BCE names Israel with the determinative for a people rather than a place; the Exodus narrative preserves personal names that are authentically Egyptian and that a much later Judean scribe would have had difficulty inventing. At that point the register of the discussion shifts. The sceptics of the previous paragraph begin saying that the story has a historical kernel, that it is not mere invention, that something must lie behind it — and what lies behind it is never the toponym but the event: a coalition besieging a city for a decade, a real commander winning a dozen real battles, a mass departure under a named leader.

What troubles me is the implicit theory of testimony. The move seems to treat trustworthiness as a single scalar property of a source, so that verifying one item licenses confidence in the unverified remainder, as though the tradition were a witness whose character had been established on the stand. But the verified item and the upgraded claim are not drawn from the same class of content, and that is the whole difficulty. Place names, ruling-house names, general political geography, and the like are precisely the material that oral transmission carries faithfully and that a storyteller has no incentive to fabricate: inventing them buys nothing, since they are not the point of the story and no audience reaction turns on them. The siege, the hero, the deliverance are the point; they are the elements with the largest payoff for elaboration and the least resistance from an audience that already wants them. So the corroboration establishes the fidelity of the channel for one class of content and is silent about the class that matters. It presents itself as evidence of the tradition's reliability when it is really evidence of reliability in the one respect where reliability was never seriously in question.

Does this have a name, and more importantly an analysis? I have canvassed the obvious neighbourhood and found only near misses. The criterion of embarrassment points the other way; the criterion of coherence in work on the historical Jesus is structurally the closest thing I have located, but it operates on material internal to a tradition rather than on agreement between a tradition and independent evidence, and its defenders generally rank it as the weakest of the criteria. The halo effect is a psychological claim about impressions of persons, not a methodological claim about sources. Bootstrapping problems concern an agent using a source's own outputs to vouch for the source. Legendary accretion and the telephone-game objection concern how traditions degrade, not how a single local success gets over-generalised. What I want is the thing itself: a statement of the assumption the move makes, an account of why it fails in the cases above, and — this matters to me most — the boundary conditions, since I suspect a weaker and legitimate inference is available somewhere nearby, something along the lines of: certain kinds of peripheral accuracy show that the channel really does reach back to the period in question, which constrains how much of the core could have been invented later. If the field has no term for this, propose one and give it a definition sharp enough to have genuine boundary cases. A bare label will not help me; I need the carving.
