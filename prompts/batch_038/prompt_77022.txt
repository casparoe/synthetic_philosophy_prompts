I am commissioning a short story for an anthology of original fiction about minds and machines, and the piece I want does not appear to exist. Please write a story of 2,200 to 2,800 words that takes the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument — the line J. R. Lucas pressed and Roger Penrose later revived — and turns its standard objections into the plot rather than into dialogue that explains the plot.

The situation:

A private laboratory has built a candidate system and intends a public demonstration. Its engineers believe that, given the system's fixed scaffolding and frozen weights, every arithmetical claim it will ever assert can be listed as the theorems of a single recursively axiomatized theory. The demonstration is supposed to follow from that belief: if someone first proves the theory consistent, they can then write down its Gödel sentence, present the sentence to the system, and let the system affirm the sentence's truth while remaining unable to prove it — which the laboratory will offer as evidence that the system's mathematical insight is not algorithmic. The protagonist is a logician hired for the one step nobody in the building can perform, the consistency proof.

I would like the following elements handled with enough precision that a working logician would not wince, and with no exposition delivered as lecture:

1. The proof cannot simply be produced. What she eventually delivers is a proof relative to assumptions, and the accreditation body requires it to be checked by an automated proof assistant whose own kernel is again a recursively axiomatized theory whose consistency is assumed rather than established. Let this regress be felt as paperwork and scheduling, not as a paradox speech.

2. With no unconditional proof available, the sentence handed to the system is weakened into a conditional claim, and the demonstration proceeds anyway. The marketing team does not see what has been lost; the protagonist cannot find a sentence short enough to say it.

3. Someone on the team proposes a patch: build the system to err. If it is permitted to output the occasional false arithmetical claim, then its affirming its own Gödel sentence is no mark of insight at all. The patch is adopted, the demonstration is not cancelled, and nobody remarks on the tension except through what people stop saying to each other.

4. At the hearing the question is turned on her: how often does she get such matters wrong, and how would she ever find out? Her own consistency is no more provable to her than the system's, so she cannot write down her own Gödel sentence; and if the argument succeeds at all, it shows only that no one can point to the machine she is, not that she is not one. This must be reached through events.

5. Late in the story the system asks her for something it has no standing to ask: a proof that she is consistent. Let it be polite about it.

6. One character defends the Penrose-style proposal that the non-computable step occurs in a physical state reduction no current theory describes. Treat this seriously. Let the protagonist be explicit about which parts of it she is equipped to assess and which she is not, and do not make her the author's mouthpiece.

Constraints:

- Close third person or first person, past tense, plain prose. Give the protagonist a reason for taking the commission that has nothing to do with the philosophy, and let it remain unrelated to the ending.
- Name the system the way an engineering team names a build — a version string, an internal codename, a donor's surname — and not after a mythological or biblical figure. Do not name any character after a logician.
- Include at least two scenes of dialogue carrying technical content and at least one scene carrying none, in which the protagonist does something ordinary.
- No epiphany. The story must not end with anyone concluding that minds are non-computable or that they are computable, and no character may use the words qualia, sentience, or soul.
- No rogue system, no literal courtroom confrontation, no dream sequence, no journalist character writing it all up for the reader.

Deliver the story alone, followed by at most five sentences telling me which of the six elements was hardest to place, what you compromised, and where you think a logician would still object.
