I run a small interdisciplinary seminar on counterfactual history — a few historians, two people who do complexity and agent-based modelling, and me (philosophy of science background). We've spent the better part of two years on the same argument and nobody has moved an inch, which is starting to feel like the actual subject.

One camp says the big transitions were contingent, and their evidence always has the same shape: a courier held up by a washed-out bridge, an invasion fleet scattered by a storm, a king dying without a surviving son, one message not arriving in time. Their claim is that the sequence that actually happened depended on things that could easily have gone otherwise, so the outcome was not inevitable in any interesting sense.

The other camp says that's all surface noise. Their evidence is slow variables — demography, energy per head, distance and transport costs, how expensive it was to move information, the accumulated stock of techniques, state capacity. On their view, if the courier hadn't been delayed something else would have done the same work a decade later, and the outcome-class would have arrived roughly when it did, just with different names attached to it. They accuse the first camp of picking out one salient small cause after the fact and never counting the huge number of alternative small causes that would have landed in the same place.

The modellers keep interrupting to say both sides are using "sensitive to small causes" and "could easily have turned out differently" as though they meant the same thing, and they don't: a process can be wildly sensitive at the level of the trajectory and extremely robust at the level of its long-run statistics, and whether it counts as chaotic at all depends on the model and the coarseness of the description you've picked, not on the world by itself. Nobody in the room has ever said what measure over counterfactual perturbations theyre appealing to. My suspicion is that this is the whole problem — the contingentists evaluate counterfactuals that hold everything fixed except one small cause, which in a sensitive system describes a state that isnt nearby at all, so the counterfactual is quietly doing work nobody has licensed.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really? My hypothesis, which I want knocked down if it's wrong: the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, one about the identity of the trajectory and one about the likelihood of an outcome-class, and each is denying that the other's question is the question. But maybe it's instead a methodological fight about which counterfactuals are admissible, or a fight about what counts as an explanation in history at all, or just a hard question that everyone is under-specifying. Tell me which, with reasons drawn from what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction we could adopt, a modelling result somebody could go compute, a change of question, or a reason to expect no progress ever and why. I'm explicitly not asking you to adjudicate the history or say who's right about the past, and please don't survey the counterfactual literature or hand me a reading list.
