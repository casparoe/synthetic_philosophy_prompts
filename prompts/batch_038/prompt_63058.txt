I am a novelist, and I have reached the point in a new book where a character's intellectual work has to be real rather than decorative. She is a cognitive scientist of religion: her position is that religious belief and practice are natural phenomena with a natural history, and that this history can be reconstructed rather than merely gestured at. Her foil, who carries much of the second half, is an anthropologist who thinks her project is not simply wrong but malformed — that it has been aimed at a target it invented for itself. I can produce the surfaces of their quarrel from reading; what I cannot fake is its spine, and the spine is not a dispute about findings but a dispute about what would count as a finding. So I want the specification before I let either of them speak.

Write the desiderata for a naturalistic theory of religion: what such a theory would have to deliver in order to count as adequate, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. I want to know which phenomena it is obliged to explain and which it is permitted to set aside; which distinctions it must not collapse, since I suspect a great deal of the field's confusion lives in collapsed distinctions — origin as against persistence, say, or the belief people avow as against the belief their behavior implies, or the near-uniformity of the underlying cognitive equipment as against the extravagant local variety of what gets believed; and which established results, ethnographic and historical and otherwise, it is not allowed to contradict, including the awkward ones: that most religious people are perfectly competent about ordinary matters, that traditions with no creator god and no interest in salvation have been stable for millennia, that people abandon religions readily and without trauma, and that the record contains as much low-cost, perfunctory, half-believed observance as it contains costly devotion.

I also want the requirements sorted. Tell me which are genuinely non-negotiable for any theory in this area and which are the local preferences of one camp dressed up as standards — the demand that a mechanism be identified rather than a function, the demand that explanations be symmetric between adaptation and byproduct, the insistence on laboratory-testable predictions, the requirement of epistemic neutrality toward the truth of religious claims. On that last one I want your judgment rather than a summary: a naturalistic theory seems caught between the charge that it debunks by stealth and the charge that it is toothless if it disclaims all debunking force, and I need to know whether a requirement can be stated that avoids both, or whether this is one place where the list itself becomes a result. Relatedly, I want to know whether the theory is required to apply to its own author without special pleading, and what that costs.

Then the part I most want: whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that a theory asked to account for the cross-cultural recurrence of religious cognition, and simultaneously for the wild divergence of religious content, and simultaneously to issue predictions that could have come out otherwise, and simultaneously to remain neutral on whether any of it is true, is over-constrained in a way its practitioners rarely admit. If you think that is so, say which requirement has to give and why; if you think the constraint is only apparent, show me the shape of a theory that satisfies all of them at once. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates — my guess is the demand to explain why costly practice persists across generations in the absence of any payoff the practitioner can observe, rather than the demand to explain why the ideas arise in the first place, but I may be wrong and I would rather be corrected.

I have picked up various proposals in the course of my reading: a hypersensitive detector for agents that fires in the absence of agents; concepts that are minimally counterintuitive and therefore memorable and transmissible; ritual costliness as a signal of coalition commitment; the cultural selection of watchful moralizing gods as an enabling condition of large-scale cooperation among strangers; religion as a parasitic replicator exploiting capacities built for something else. Part of what I need from you is a sorting of these: which are answers offered to genuine requirements, which are answers to requirements nobody should have imposed, and which have quietly been promoted into requirements themselves, so that a theory is now judged by whether it accommodates them. That promotion, if it has happened, is exactly the move my anthropologist would attack, and I want to be able to write the attack precisely. If I have misdescribed any of them, correct me in passing rather than adopting my phrasing.

You may end with a brief assessment of how the main contenders fare against the list, but keep it short — a paragraph at most, and no names are needed if the positions can be characterized without them. The criteria are what I am after. I do not want a bibliography, a list of references, or citations of any kind; I want the argument, not the apparatus, and I will find sources on my own if I need them.

One last constraint, and please observe it strictly: write to me in continuous prose, as one writes a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered items, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Let the order in which the requirements appear do the work that headings would otherwise do, so that the one you take to be most fundamental is recognizably first, and let each requirement be followed immediately by the case that motivates it. I would rather have eight requirements argued at length than twenty catalogued.
