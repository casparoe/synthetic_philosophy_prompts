Ok, some background before the actual ask, because I think the context is the point.

I'm the programs lead at a small regional food bank, and our finance director — I'll call her D — has a phrase she reaches for whenever anyone tries to keep something alive past the point where the numbers stopped working. The phrase is "bygones are bygones." Six weeks ago it killed our mobile pantry in a county forty minutes out: we'd bought a refrigerated trailer for it two years back, it's lost money every quarter since, and the ruling was basically "the trailer is a sunk cost, bygones are bygones, decide on what's in front of you."

Here's where I'm stuck. I think she may well have been right about the trailer and I still cant tell whether she was, because that same sentence seems to be doing at least three different jobs around here. Two weeks later she used it on me about something unrelated — we're six months into migrating to a new donor database and halfway through I asked whether we should bail — and there it felt like it was carrying real weight, but I couldn't say what. Meanwhile our ED, who is not a finance person, says "if you start something you finish it" and "quitters never win" about the exact same migration, and nobody in the room is bothered that those two mottos are pulling opposite ways.

So: can you lay out the distinct claims "bygones are bygones" could be making in a decision context? I want them sorted from the ones that are true just by definition of "sunk" to the ones that are genuinely substantive to the ones that are simply false — and for each, say what kind of thing it's a claim about, since I suspect that's where the slippage is happening: the causal structure of the options, the psychology of the person deciding, what counts as a reason at all, or the strategic situation the other players are in.

Then two more specific things, because these are what I'll actually get asked about:

1. I had two counter-arguments about the trailer. First, that scrapping the program would wreck our credibility with the county health dept who co-funded it, so the real cost is downstream and still in play. Second, that the volunteers who've given three years to that route don't just get redeployed — some of what we'd lose is about who we are as an organization and what we're the kind of outfit that does. Which of those does D's slogan actually reach, and which one does it slide right past without touching? I want to know if either of them is just the sunk cost fallacy wearing a nicer coat.

2. Is there any reading of "bygones are bygones" and any reading of "if you start something you finish it" under which D and the ED are both right about the database migration — i.e. a way they're answering different questions rather than contradicting each other? If yes, say what each one is actually answering.

Give me a list I can bring to the staff meeting, not an essay.
