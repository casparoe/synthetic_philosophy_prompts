I've been covering science-and-ideas stuff for a while and I'm putting together a piece marking roughly the twentieth anniversary of the whole DARPA terror-futures blowup, and I want somebody to actually think through it with me instead of handing back the press-clipping version everybody recites.

Here are the facts as I have them, so you don't have to go looking anything up. The Policy Analysis Market was a proposed futures exchange developed out of DARPA's Information Awareness Office, first funded around 2001 and pitched by a small San Diego firm called Net Exchange, with the University of Iowa's political futures market and Robin Hanson's work as the models. Traders would have bought and sold contracts keyed to economic and political indicators in a handful of Middle Eastern countries — GDP, unemployment, whether a given regime was still standing — on the theory that prices aggregate dispersed information better than expert forecasts do. In late July 2003 two senators, Byron Dorgan and Ron Wyden, held a press conference saying the thing would let people bet on assassinations and terrorist attacks, pointing at sample screens tucked into the background graphics of the project website that included things like the assassination of Yasser Arafat, a North Korean missile attack, and the overthrow of the king of Jordan. The Pentagon cancelled it within about a day. John Poindexter, who ran the IAO, was gone within a couple of weeks — although by Hanson's own account he had very little to do with PAM specifically.

What I want is an analysis of that episode as a test case for the prediction-market/futarchy program, run in both directions: the theory explaining the case, and the case putting pressure on the theory.

So, first, the mapping, done honestly. Who are the traders the theory imagines here, and who would actually have been trading a small classified-adjacent government market in 2003 — how many people, with what information, and what happens to the aggregation story if a lot of the best-informed potential traders are exactly the ones who shouldn't be trading? What's the payoff structure on the assassination and attack contracts, and does the theory have a real answer to the manipulation objection, or does the standard reply — moving a price costs more than it pays, and betting on an attack is mostly just a noisy way of reporting it — do work that it hasn't earned? Say how big you'd need the market to be before that reply is even testable.

Second, was PAM a futarchy case at all? Futarchy proper is about governance: Hanson's slogan about voting on values while betting on beliefs, where a policy gets adopted because conditional prices say it raises a democratically chosen welfare metric. PAM was decision support. No policy was going to be triggered by any price. So does 2003 tell us anything about futarchy, or only about the politics of prediction markets? I want you to push on whether that distinction is a real one or a convenient retreat to higher ground — and if it is a retreat, what the episode still licenses.

Third, where is the theory simply silent? I'm thinking of legitimacy, of expressive harm, of the fact that a market's existence is itself a political fact that changes the thing it's measuring, of the moral-repugnance argument (is the yuck factor tracking something, or is it just a PR problem that better framing would have solved?), and of the fact that nothing died slower because the market had no constituency, no host institution with a stake, and nobody in Congress whose career depended on it. Does futarchy have a slot for any of that, or does it treat all of it as friction to design around? And how much does Tyler Cowen's worry — that you can't cleanly separate values from beliefs, so the whole architecture wobbles — have to do with how fast this collapsed?

Fourth, land somewhere. Give me a defended reading rather than a menu: either the theory was basically sound and got killed by a two-day news cycle, which makes this a story about political stupidity; or the episode exposed something structural, namely that prediction markets need legitimacy as an input and can't manufacture it as an output; or a third reading you think does better. Tell me which one you'd bet on and what would have to be true for the others to win.

One request on the epistemics, because this is going in front of readers who aren't specialists: mark clearly what's settled among people who actually work on this versus what's genuinely contested. My sense is that markets beating polls in some elections is close to settled and that the manipulation question is wide open, but I can't tell where the line sits on liquidity requirements, on how far the Iowa results generalize to non-electoral forecasting, or on Cowen's objection. Don't let the pro-market line sit in the consensus column just because it's the more fun one. And if you think my framing above is loaded, or any of the facts I gave you are off, say so up front — I'd rather hear it from you than from an editor.
