I'm slowly working through On Liberty (1859) and got stuck in chapter V, "Applications," on the free trade paragraph. Here's the part I mean:

"Again, trade is a social act. Whoever undertakes to sell any description of goods to the public, does what affects the interest of other persons, and of society in general; and thus his conduct, in principle, comes within the jurisdiction of society ... This is the so-called doctrine of Free Trade, which rests on grounds different from, though equally solid with, the principle of individual liberty asserted in this Essay. Restrictions on trade, or on production for purposes of trade, are indeed restraints; and all restraint, quâ restraint, is an evil: but the restraints in question affect only that part of conduct which society is competent to restrain, and are wrong solely because they do not really produce the results which it is desired to produce by them."

Then a bit later, about things like the Maine Law and restrictions on selling poison, he says: "These interferences are objectionable, not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer."

Can you walk me through what he's actually doing here? Three things I can't get straight:

1. If selling goods is by defintion a social act already inside society's jurisdiction, what work is the harm principle still doing against price-fixing or licensing? It feels like he just handed away the very case everyone thinks the principle is for.

2. Why does the objection to prohibition land on the buyer's liberty instead of the seller's? My gut says the seller is the one whose hands are tied, so it seems backwards, and I can't figure out what turns on the difference.

3. Is he conceding that laissez-faire is only an effectiveness argument, not a liberty argument? And how is that supposed to sit alongside the line about restraint being an evil insofar as its restraint — is that a second, weaker objection, or just a throwaway?

Basically I want to know whether I'm misreading this or whether Mill really is willing to say his doctrine doesn't cover commercial regulation at all.
