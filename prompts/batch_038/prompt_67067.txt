Twelve years into an appellate practice, I still have never taken a course in cognitive science. You are a philosopher of mind who also reads appellate briefs fluently; I want you to treat what follows as an exercise in careful translation rather than popularization, and I want the translation to be honest — do not import conclusions from the source framework and hand them back to me as if they were counterparts in mine.

I recently read a chapter describing a research project called Letter Spirit, whose question is what makes every instance of a letter — every 'a' in every typeface, every 'a' in every hand — recognizably that letter. The chapter argues, roughly, the following five things:

1. No list of necessary and sufficient features picks out the a's. Every feature proposed (a closed bowl, a stem on the right, a descender) is either present in some non-a's or missing from some a's, and the project treats this as a permanent fact about the category rather than a failure of description so far.
2. The essence of a letter is not a stored template but something worked out afresh for each new style and situation. The same flexible process both recognizes an 'a' and produces one, so there is no separate generative rule standing behind a separate test of membership.
3. Style is not decoration laid over a stable core. You cannot subtract style and find a pure 'a' underneath; a letter exists only as realized in some style, and a style is itself a coherent pattern of choices rather than an inventory.
4. Membership is graded, and the boundary is a slope rather than a line. Some forms are more fully a's than others, and a marginal form can be pulled toward a neighboring letter by the forms around it — the whole alphabet bears on what any single letter can be, because a letterform is partly constituted by what it must not be mistaken for.
5. Deciding a marginal case is not the application of a rule to a fact. It is a matter of competing pressures settling into a stable configuration, and a competent judge of letterforms need not be able to say why she judged as she did.

Restate these five claims, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually work in: holding and dicta, ratio decidendi, distinguishing and overruling, the weight of a precedent, reasoning by analogy along a line of cases, core instances and penumbra, rules against standards, and the requirement that a decider publish articulable reasons. Where a claim maps, give me the counterpart and say how tight the fit is. Where you must bend my vocabulary to make it fit, state precisely what the bending consists in and what is lost in the process. Where there is no counterpart at all, say so plainly instead of reaching for a loose analogy. If any of the five misstates the project's position as you understand it, flag it in one line and translate the corrected version.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of the five has no expression in a system where a category's content is fixed case by case by an authority who must give reasons? And what should I make of the residue — is my vocabulary missing something real, some notion the researchers are working with that common-law method genuinely cannot express, or does the residue suggest their claims are less determinate than they sound? I am especially interested in whether claims 1 and 5 are theses about the category itself or only reports about how hard it has proven to state a rule.

My own instinct, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that this is reasoning from precedent with pictures substituted for cases: a chain of comparisons rather than a definition, which is what lawyers have always said about categories like due process or unreasonable search. If that instinct is right, say so without hedging. If it works only by smuggling an assumption of my framework into the translation, show me where.

Work through the mapping carefully before you write, but do not show me the working — I want the translation and the residue report only. Write in American English and keep the entire response under 900 words. Close with a brief statement of how confident you are in your verdicts on the five claims, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, saying why.
