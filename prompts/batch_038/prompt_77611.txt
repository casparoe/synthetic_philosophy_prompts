I am one of three trustees of a small private foundation created by Dr. Helena Rourke, a physician-epidemiologist who died in 2011. Her will leaves the residuary estate to the foundation for public-health work but, unusually, names no programs. Instead she deposited with the trust thirty-seven one-page typed position papers written between 1971 and 2009, together with a covering note directing her trustees to reason from them as a body rather than from any single one, and telling us not to feel bound by the particular conclusions she reached: we are to ask, she writes, what she would endorse if she were sitting where we sit and had read what we have read.

The request before us is $1.9 million over four years for a conditional cash transfer program run by a consortium that has operated school-based health services in the region for a decade. Payments would be made to adolescents, held in a locked account, and released at twenty-one provided the recipient has not had a first pregnancy before that age; leaving the program early forfeits whatever has accrued. The applicant's proposal includes three randomized evaluations suggesting that payments of this size do move the relevant outcomes; our program officer, whom I trust, thinks two of the three are sound and one is not.

Four of the papers bear on this, and they do not sit easily together. Assume the other thirty-three do not.

1. A 1989 paper rejects incentive schemes aimed at fertility outright, for two stated reasons: modest payments cannot move such decisions, and such payments ask the poorest people to attach a price to something that cannot be priced. In the margin, in pencil, she added that if the first claim is ever shown false we should disregard the rest of the page.
2. A 1996 paper endorses paying community health workers per completed vaccination course, on the stated ground that a payment is legitimate when it compensates a cost the recipient actually bears, and illegitimate when it purchases a surrender.
3. A 1974 paper defends paying blood donors. In 2003 she wrote a single paragraph withdrawing it, saying only that she had been wrong and giving no reason. We disagree about whether to treat the 1974 argument or the 2003 withdrawal as her considered position.
4. A 2007 paper objects to programs evaluated on the behavior of participants who cannot withdraw from them, calling unexitability the specific way benevolent programs become coercive.

My colleagues have landed in three places. One says the covering note licenses extrapolation: the papers are evidence of her values, not commands, and our job is to reconstruct the principle that best fits her endorsements and her rejections and then apply it, which she thinks condemns the forfeiture feature but not the payments. Another says decline: our duty is to her expressed intent, and "what she would want if she knew more" has no content apart from what we happen to want, so every time we prefer the principle to the conclusion we are spending her money on our own view wearing her name — and she points to the unexplained 2003 withdrawal as proof that even Rourke's own revisions were not trackable. The third thinks the instruction is simply indeterminate and that we should break the tie on expected benefit and approve. My own view is that the second trustee is right about the method and wrong about the conclusion, but I cannot yet say what the method is.

I should tell you why I want this written carefully rather than quickly. Since my own diagnosis in the spring I have been drafting letters for my children about decisions I will not be present for, and I keep writing sentences intended to bind them and then crossing them out. I now know both how badly one wants to be extrapolated and how little purchase one keeps. I do not want advocacy for either impulse. I want reasoning my colleagues can argue with.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our meeting next month. It should:

- Recommend a decision on this request — approve, decline, or approve with conditions — and, if with conditions, name them specifically enough that the applicant could tell whether it can meet them. Say whether the reasoning is a rule we should apply to the next three years of requests or a one-off, and if a rule, state it in one sentence.
- Say which interpretive method you are using and why that one rather than the others available, in terms a trustee with no legal or philosophical training can follow.
- Rule on whether the penciled margin note in the 1989 paper does what the applicant says it does.
- State the strongest case for declining in a form the trustee who holds it would recognize as her own, and say what would have to be true for it to prevail.
- Mark explicitly every point at which the extrapolation is doing work the papers cannot support — where we would be supplying our judgment and calling it hers.

No citations, bibliography, or reading list, and please do not invent legal doctrine: our counsel has confirmed we have discretion and that the governing standard is the donor's intent as best we can ascertain it. Aim for a document of roughly fifteen hundred words that three people could actually vote on.
