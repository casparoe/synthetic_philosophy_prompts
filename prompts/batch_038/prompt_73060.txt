I convene an interdisciplinary graduate seminar — philosophers of social science, sociologists, economists and two evolutionary psychologists in the same room — and for the third week I want to hand out a diagnostic instrument rather than a reading. Please write a decision tree that carries a student from the bare empirical finding to a named position on what, if anything, follows from it.

The finding, as I shall stipulate it for the purposes of the exercise, and without asking you to vouch for its robustness: in the countries that score highest on the composite indices normally used to measure gender equality, the share of women among graduates in physics, engineering and computing is comparatively low, whilst women are heavily over-represented in nursing, early-years education and comparable occupations; the contrast class is countries scoring much lower on the same indices, where the shares sit closer to parity. Treat that as given. The tree is not about whether it is true; it is about what a person who accepts it, or doubts it, or accepts a variant of it, is thereby committed to.

Build the tree from the logical space first and from the literature afterwards. Six to eight question nodes, nine to twelve leaves. Every node must be phrased as a question a student could answer on reflection without knowing which position the branch leads to: no node may mention a school of thought, a discipline, an author, or the word "paradox". Nor may any node be answerable only by someone who has already settled the matter — "do you think the index is valid?" is admissible only if it is unpacked into what validity would consist in here.

The distinctions I most want represented, though you should feel free to add to them and to order them as the logic requires:

- whether the explanandum is a distribution of occupational outcomes or a distribution of reported interests, tastes and self-assessed strengths — these come apart, and a stance on one does not fix a stance on the other;
- whether the index in question measures opportunity, outcome, or attitudes towards the sexes, and whether a position can survive being asked to say which;
- whether the pattern is a feature of the world or an artefact of comparing unlike countries and aggregating heterogeneous fields;
- whether "free choice" and "shaped choice" are genuine opposites, or whether preference formation is itself a social process, so that a choice can be uncoerced and formed all the same;
- whether a gendered distribution of occupations is a standing injustice in itself, or an injustice only when traceable to constraint;
- whether the relevant question concerns the ratio between groups or the internal composition of each group.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position where one is current, and a descriptive name where none is; for each leaf add one sentence naming the commitment its holder finds most awkward. I want at least the following to appear as distinct leaves rather than being merged: the view that the finding dissolves once the index is interrogated; the view that revealed preference settles the matter and that intervention would be paternalism; the view that affluence permits choices expressive of identity rather than of security; the view that dispositions differ and that this is the whole explanation; the view that preferences are endogenous to the social order, so that consent is not exculpatory; the view that parity is required as a matter of justice irrespective of what people say they want; and the view that the question is malformed.

Then run the instrument on three cases, which I stipulate rather than assert, and report for each which leaves can accommodate it, which cannot, and which must be revised in order to do so: (i) an occupation that was majority-female and became overwhelmingly male within about two decades through administrative decisions about recruitment, training, promotion and pay, in a society whose general level of equality did not change; (ii) three professions — veterinary medicine, pharmacy and law — that each passed from majority-male to majority-female within one generation at the top of the equality indices, again with no change in formal equality; (iii) two cohorts of identical measured aptitude, in which the more equal country shows a wider split by sex in the occupations the members actually enter.

Two further requirements. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate: I suspect that a strong socialisation view and an adaptive-preference view will come out extensionally equivalent on every case I can construct, and if that is right I want it said plainly, together with a description of what evidence or argument would drive them apart. Second, list three or four combinations of answers that the nodes permit but that no one appears to occupy — for instance, "the finding is robust; it reflects dispositions; and it is nonetheless an injustice demanding correction" — and for each empty cell, diagnose whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is politically costly, or because nobody has got round to it. Close with a note on what the tree assigns to a student who answers "not separable" or "unknown" at every node: whether that is a legitimate resting place or a failure to have engaged.

Write the whole thing, notes included, in American English. Please do not append a reference list, a bibliography, or named papers, and do not cite sources anywhere in the instrument; I want a self-contained handout that can be marked purely on whether the nodes discriminate.
