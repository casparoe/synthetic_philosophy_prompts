Hospitals, airlines, and increasingly software organizations review serious failures — a wrong-route medication that killed a patient, a controlled flight into terrain, an outage that took down a payments system — under a framework usually called a "just culture" or a blameless postmortem. The stated method is roughly uniform. Reviewers are instructed to ask how the system permitted the error rather than who committed it; participants are assured that what they say in the review will not be used in disciplinary proceedings; and the reviewer then sorts the conduct into categories — an honest slip or mistake, made without any recognition of risk; "at-risk" conduct, where a shortcut was taken because the danger was discounted or because the shortcut had been informally rewarded; and reckless conduct, understood as the conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk. Coaching and retraining follow the first two; discipline, up to dismissal, is reserved for the third. A standard decision procedure asks the reviewer to consider whether a comparably trained and experienced person, placed in the same situation, would have made the same error; if the answer is yes, the finding is recorded as a system problem rather than an individual one. Meanwhile the same hospital that runs the review may also refer the clinician to a state licensing board, and cannot prevent criminal prosecution: in 2022 a former Tennessee nurse was convicted of criminally negligent homicide and sentenced to probation for a 2017 medication error in which she bypassed a dispensing cabinet's safeguards and administered a paralytic instead of a sedative, a case that many safety advocates argued would deter exactly the candid reporting the review process depends on.

I want an account of the theory of moral responsibility this apparatus presupposes — and specifically whether it presupposes one at all. Take Galen Strawson's Basic Argument as the foil: you do what you do because of how you are; to be truly responsible for your actions you would have to be responsible for how you are in the relevant respects; but nothing can be the ultimate, self-originating cause of itself; so no one is truly responsible in the sense that grounds desert. Does the reckless-conduct carve-out require that unavailable notion, or can conscious disregard of a known risk be cashed out without it? What does the substitution test commit the reviewer to about the boundary between person and situation, given that on the Basic Argument the agent's "how they are" is itself the sediment of a causal history that includes the organization's incentives? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with — its own published rationale is empirical and forward-looking, about suppressing reporting, not about metaphysics — and say where it is underdetermined, if several rival accounts of responsibility license the identical algorithm. Is a blameless review a localized, revocable shift into what P. F. Strawson called the objective attitude, and does the reckless-conduct category exist precisely to keep the reactive attitudes available at the margin? Finally: if an institution adopted the Basic Argument as its official rationale instead of the consequentialist one, what in the procedure would actually have to change, and what would have to change in the licensing referral and prosecutorial practices that run alongside it?

Tell me which reading you think is right and how firmly you hold it, and mark clearly the places where you are reconstructing a philosophical commitment that the practitioners themselves would disown rather than reporting a rationale they give. And if you think I have mischaracterized the practice, or that the metaphysical question is imported by my framing rather than latent in the procedure, say so plainly and explain why, instead of answering the question as posed out of politeness.
