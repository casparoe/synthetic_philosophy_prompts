I am midway through a novel set in the Warring States period whose protagonist trails after a wandering follower of Zhuangzi, and a scholar of early Chinese thought is reading the manuscript for accuracy. Her verdicts so far on every scene that touches death:

Approved — the widower drumming on a tub and singing, but only with the beat that precedes it, in which he concedes that at the moment of the death he could not keep from grieving, and his explanation to his visitor of why he stopped. Cut that first beat and she rejects the scene outright. Approved — the two friends composing songs beside the corpse, smiling, countenances unchanged, and the visiting envoy's remark that they wander outside the square while he wanders inside it. Approved — the disciple who shoos the wailing wife away from the dying man's couch, on the grounds that she is disturbing what is passing through its change. Approved — the man who, finding a growth on his own arm, resents nothing, since he and his companion had been watching the transformations and now the transformation has come to him. Approved as a dream, rejected as an oration — the skull's boast that the dead are more at ease than a king on his throne; she objects whenever a living character advances it to mourners as a thesis. Approved — the roadside skull and the traveller who points at it and says that only he and it know that it never lived and never died. Rejected — the man who performs three years of mourning flawlessly and in private, believing nothing, which she called worse than the tears it spared him.

The scene I must now write: a young man keeps the full three years of mourning for his brother, weeping freely and performing every rite. At the end a stranger asks whether he understands that this was only a transformation. He answers that he understood it before his brother died; that he would accept his own death today without a word; that his brother is nowhere and is not waiting to be joined; and that he would go on weeping until the mourning was spent, and would do it all again.

Predict her verdict. At least two of the following fit her record equally well — grief as an error of feeling; a constraint on duration and display rather than on feeling; understanding rather than emotion as the whole criterion; a standard binding only the agent's own transformations; the denial that there is any subject who dies; the claim that death is a better estate than life — or something else entirely. Say what each predicts about this scene, which I should write the scene against and why, and what further scene would best tell the leading candidates apart. I don't want references; I want the reasoning.
