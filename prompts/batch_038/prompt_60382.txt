so in about six weeks I'm giving a departmental colloquium on a chapter of my book and I want a real Q&A prep sheet — not a defense of the view, I already have that, I want to know what's about to hit me.

The view, roughly: environmental historians keep getting hit with the determinism charge because they write about eruptions, cold snaps and harvests as causes of human events. My claim is the charge mostly misfires, because it assumes the only thing a non-human event can do is cause. I split the roles in two. An event can cause an outcome, or it can reprice the menu of options open to agents — I call the second a horizon effect. A bad season doesn't make anybody do anything; it changes which projects are still affordable and which are already dead, and then people, behaving strategically in the ordinary way inside a reshaped possibility space, produce the history. My evidence is parish poor-relief accounts, a grain price series, and marriage and migration registers from one river valley across 1815–1820, after Tambora, and I argue the marriage pattern there is only explicable on the horizon reading. I also claim this gives a principled guardrail: horizon talk is only licensed where you can show agents were actually tracking the changed menu, which is an empirical burden and not a vibe.

The room: a history department colloquium, mostly social and economic historians — two labor people, one who works on state formation and fiscal capacity, one early modernist who thinks climate data for my period is a reconstruction with error bars far too fat to carry any explanatory weight at all. Nobody works on my region and nobody works on volcanoes.
