I run a lunchtime philosophy-of-science club at my high school, and our faculty sponsor has asked me to prepare a diagnostic handout for the first meeting of our spring unit on evidence and theory choice. I would like you to build that handout as a decision tree that takes a student from a single shared starting point — "here are two accounts of the world that agree about every observation anyone could make" — to a named position in the debate over underdetermination. The audience is sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds who have had one term of physics and no philosophy background.

The problem I am trying to solve is that every member of the club says "science is underdetermined" and means something entirely different by it. Some mean it as a skeptical argument against scientific knowledge; some mean it as a technical point about the auxiliary assumptions needed to get predictions out of a theory; some mean it as a license for deliberately keeping rival views alive. Our discussions therefore go in circles, because nobody has noticed that we are making different claims. The handout should sort us by what we actually say about cases, not by which label we prefer.

Please include the following, in this order.

1. A preamble of five or six sentences stating the axes your tree is built on. I have three in mind, though you may revise or add: whether a tie between rivals is temporary and will be broken by evidence or ingenuity not yet available, or permanent even in principle; whether the tie arises between whole world-pictures or locally, between one theory and the background assumptions required to test it; and whether anything beyond observational fit may break the tie — simplicity, explanatory scope, coherence, the priors an individual scientist brings, the norms a community has adopted — and if so, whether that resource is permissive enough to leave several rivals equally warranted.

2. The tree itself, in indented plain text, with seven or eight branch nodes and ten to twelve leaves. Every node must be a question about a case or a claim whose content is spelled out inside the question, so a student can answer it without knowing which leaf the answer leads toward. Please do not use nodes of the form "are you a realist?" or "do you believe in unobservable entities?" Draw the nodes instead from these four situations, which I will assign as reading beforehand: the fact that, before stellar parallax was first measured, the Copernican and the Tychonic arrangements of the planets agreed about every observation available; the choice between describing space as curved and describing it as flat while positing a universal force that shrinks rulers, bodies, and light paths alike; the existence of several formulations of quantum theory that reproduce exactly the same outcomes for every measurement but disagree about what is happening; and the choice between explaining galactic rotation curves by a new kind of matter and by a modification of gravity at large scales.

3. Leaves that include at least: conventionalism about the tied choice; the deflationary view that every apparent tie is temporary and that the thesis therefore does no philosophical work; constructive empiricism; a realism on which explanatory virtues settle the tie; a more cautious realism that commits only to those parts of a theory that have kept working through successive replacements; a pluralism that treats maintaining rivals as a methodological duty rather than a failure; a radical position on which underdetermination shows that no empirical theory is ever justified; and a quietist or dissolutionist position on which the question about tied rivals is not a factual question at all. You may add others. Each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it), one sentence stating its core commitment, which of the four cases it handles most comfortably, and which it handles worst, with a sentence on why.

4. A section on unoccupied cells. Because the tree is built from the axes rather than from a list of views, some combinations of answers will have nothing in them. For each, say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unstable under pressure, or because it is merely unfashionable or unexplored; and for those you judge merely unexplored, sketch in a sentence what a defender would have to maintain. I am particularly interested in the cell combining permanent, whole-world-picture underdetermination with the claim that only observational fit counts as a tie-breaker, plus realism about the entities the tied rivals disagree about: is anyone there, and if not, why not?

5. A section flagging the pairs of leaves your tree cannot really separate. I expect at least one, since some of these positions differ not over any case but over whether scientists can even conceive of the full space of rivals, which none of my four situations reaches. Name the pair, explain where the tree fails, and state what question would separate them, even if that question is not case-based.

6. Finally, run three of my club members through the tree and report where each lands, using only the statements I give you here.

- Priya: "When two geometries agree about every possible measurement, there is no further fact about the shape of space for one of them to get right. You choose whichever keeps the physics simple, and calling that one true adds nothing."
- Daniel: "Every time two theories have looked tied, somebody built a better instrument or thought of a new consideration and the tie broke. There is no reason to suppose the pattern stops."
- Wen: "I am a realist — electrons are as real as tables. But I also think the alternatives we can think of today are not all the alternatives there are, so I commit myself only to the parts of a theory that have kept working through several replacements."

Wen's answers should function as a test of your tree. If he lands on a leaf that is indistinguishable from someone else's, say so rather than forcing him into a box.

Two constraints. Keep everything self-contained: I do not want a reading list, a bibliography, or citations, since my members cannot look anything up, and I would rather have the positions described in plain terms than identified by their sources. And keep the whole thing printable on two pages — the tree is the main event, and the prose around it should be economical.
