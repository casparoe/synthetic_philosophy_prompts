You are a political philosopher who works on bargaining, compromise, and value pluralism, and who has also spent several years drafting contract specifications and client offering documents for a derivatives clearinghouse. Treat what follows as a serious piece of comic writing: think carefully, plan before you draft, and do not settle for jokes that only require a general sense that "finance is soulless."

**The assignment.** Write a parody product document: the public offering summary and contract specifications for a cleared futures product on moral compromise, issued by an invented exchange — call it the Concessions Exchange (CX), with a clearing arm if you want one. The document is entirely sincere. Its premise is the one people actually invoke when they defend compromise between rival value systems: that when two parties hold incompatible commitments, a negotiated exchange can leave both better off than a contest that runs to a decision, and that treating this as a market merely makes visible the gains from trade that are already there. Your job is to let that premise do its work in the flat, unbothered prose of a rulebook until the reader notices what it has committed to.

**Required sections.** Produce a numbered document with decimal sub-clauses covering at least the following:

1. Product description and stated purpose, including the exchange's rationale for offering the contract at all.
2. Deliverable grade and standardization — the unit in which a concession is measured, what makes one concession substitutable for another, and the certification a commitment must obtain before it can be delivered.
3. Eligibility and proof of title, including the treatment of commitments held jointly with a community, party, congregation, or household, and the resulting consent and holdout problem; a schedule of haircuts by class of commitment is welcome.
4. Settlement: physical delivery of an actual change of position versus cash settlement in lieu of it, and the exchange's own reporting on which method members overwhelmingly prefer and why.
5. Counterparty rules, including the exchange's neutrality as to the content of the commitments traded and a know-your-counterparty section.
6. Persons who are not party to the contract but on whom a delivered concession falls, and what, if anything, the exchange does about them.
7. Obligations that survive settlement and are expressly excluded from netting.
8. Round-trip trading: the sale of a position and its later repurchase.
9. A closing block of Important Risk Information in the standard flat voice of a disclosure.

Somewhere in the document there should also be an index product — a diversified basket of small concessions, periodically rebalanced — described in two or three sentences.

**Conceptual payloads.** Each of the following must be enacted by the mechanics of the contract rather than stated as an objection. A reader who works on the ethics of compromise should recognize all six; a reader who does not should still find the document funny and slightly unsettling.

- Clearing a trade in values presupposes a common scale, so the qualifying "difference of value systems" turns out, under the exchange's own eligibility rules, to be a difference of weights on a single scale.
- A concession extracted from a third party is still, for settlement purposes, delivered by the seller.
- A commitment can be bought back without being restored; the repurchased unit is fungible and the original was not.
- The worry that some commitments are damaged by being priced at all appears nowhere except as a representation and warranty in which the seller affirms that it has authority to make the trade.
- Regret, apology, and the duty to revisit the settlement survive termination and are defined as Residual Positions that cannot be closed out.
- The exchange's neutrality as to content is asserted with total confidence in the same document whose every clause is about content.

**Style and constraints.** Deadpan throughout: institutional-legalese sentences that are grammatically ordinary, bureaucratically bland, and free of adjectives doing comedic work. No winking, no authorial asides, no exclamation points, no character breaks, and no place where the document admits it is being ironic. Capitalize and use consistently a small set of defined terms of your own invention. At least one clause must be so plainly reasonable in market terms that the reader has to supply the moral shock themselves. Do not use any of the following words anywhere in the document: incommensurable, commensurable, corruption, integrity, complicity, dirty hands, residue, intrinsic, dignity, sanctity, repugnant. No philosopher may be named, and no argument may be named as an argument.

Do not use real exchanges, clearinghouses, tickers, statutes, firms, or living people. No citations, no bibliography, no footnotes pointing at real literature — anything you need must be invented or already common knowledge. American English. The document proper should run roughly 900 to 1,100 words.

**Output, in this order.**

- *Step 1 — scaffolding.* A correspondence table of no more than ten rows with three columns: the market mechanism you are using; the feature of moral compromise it stands in for; and what the mechanism presupposes that the feature may not supply. Keep it terse. This is a planning artifact, so make it genuinely diagnostic rather than decorative.
- *Step 2 — the piece.* The offering document itself.
- *Step 3 — postscript, clearly marked as outside the fiction.* In 150 to 250 words addressed to me: where the market framing actually earns its keep in thinking about compromise between value systems, where it collapses under load, and whether the collapse tells us something about the metaphor or something about compromise. If the gains-from-trade story survives in some restricted domain, say which one and why.

Before you submit, audit your own draft: check that none of the banned vocabulary slipped in, that every one of the six payloads is carried by a clause rather than by commentary, and that a specialist would find the exaggeration faithful to the position rather than a straw man of it.
