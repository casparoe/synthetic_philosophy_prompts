**Question 3 (25 marks).** Nagel distinguishes constitutive, circumstantial and resultant moral luck. Consider two drivers.

*Case A.* D1 and D2 both leave the same party having had exactly the same amount to drink, take the same route home, and make the same series of decisions at the wheel. In D1's case a child chases a ball into the road and is struck and killed. In D2's case the road is empty and nothing happens.

*Case B.* Everything is as in case A, except that in both worlds a child runs into the road in front of D1's car. In D1's world a second car in the next lane is travelling at the same speed half a second behind, and would have struck the child just as fatally had D1 never been on that road at all. The child dies either way, at the same moment, to the same degree, and nothing about D1's driving changes whether, when or how badly the child is harmed.

(a) In case B, is there any resultant moral luck between D1 and D2? (b) Does your answer to (a) depend on whether resultant luck is read as a matter of the outcome that actually occurs, or of the difference the agent's conduct makes to the outcome? (c) What does case B, if anything, show about the control principle?

and here is the student's answer, more or less verbatim:

> Nagel's basic claim is that moral luck arises when a significant part of what we assess a person for depends on factors outside their control, and he sorts it into constitutive luck, about the person you happen to be, meaning the temperaments and capacities you were born with; circumstantial luck, about the situations you happen to end up in; and resultant luck, about the way your actions turn out.
>
> Case A is the standard illustration, and case B is mostly circumstantial too, since whether a child is on that road is not up to either driver. What the modification seems to do is remove the resultant luck. Resultant luck is about outcomes, and the outcome here — the child's death — is not one D1's driving made any difference to, because the second car would have killed the child half a second later regardless. So there is nothing for resultant luck to get a grip on: D1 and D2 are in exactly the position they would have been in had the road been empty, and neither comes out worse than the other.
>
> That gives a fairly clean answer to (b). Resultant luck just is a matter of the difference the agent's conduct makes, not of the outcome that happens to occur, because if the outcome was coming anyway, calling it his outcome is only a way of describing where his car happened to be. On this reading resultant luck isn't really a separate category from circumstantial luck — it is circumstantial luck where the circumstances include the consequences of what you did.
>
> For (c), the case supports the control principle rather than putting pressure on it. We shouldn't assess D1 more harshly for something that wasn't up to him, and in case B it wasn't even up to him whether his driving contributed to the harm, so the intuition that D1 and D2 are moral equals has nothing to fight against. This is the direction Nagel pushes us in anyway: the more you peel back the layers of what wasn't up to the agent, the less there is left to assess.
>
> None of this gets D1 off entirely. He chose to drive drunk, and that choice at least is his, so he is blameworthy for the same thing D2 is. And Williams would add that a driver who kills a child has a response available to him that a bystander doesn't, agent-regret, which Williams thinks appropriate even where the driver is blameless. But agent-regret tracks having caused something, and in case B D1 caused nothing: he can be upset that his was the car in that lane, but there is no harm of his making to feel it about. It's a bit like Williams's Gauguin in reverse — for Williams, whether Gauguin's choice is justified depends on whether the project comes off, and the same shape holds here, since whether D1 is morally on the hook depends on whether his driving actually produced the death, and it didn't.
