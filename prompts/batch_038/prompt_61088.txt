I'm a doctoral student working on the foundations of probability, and I've come to accept the standard view that Dutch book arguments settle the matter: an agent whose credences violate the probability axioms is exposed to a package of bets she would each accept and that jointly guarantee her a net loss, and that is what makes incoherence irrational. A colleague in economics has raised a worry I can't answer. The argument seems to require that the agent's utility be linear in money — otherwise a bet at her own fair odds needn't be worth taking — and once background risk is admitted, Rabin's calibration result suggests that ordinary small-stakes risk aversion is incompatible with expected utility unless the utility function is close to linear. So the bookable agents may be fictions with linear utility, while real ones are bookable for reasons that have nothing to do with their credences. What would count against my holding the standard view? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed Dutch booker would herself concede (logical omniscience, sequential rather than packaged acceptance of the bets, state-dependent utility, unbounded payoffs) and which she would dismiss as beside the point, and give me an honest verdict on whether retreating to the claim that credences are betting quotients in utility rather than in money rescues the argument or quietly insulates it into vacuity.
