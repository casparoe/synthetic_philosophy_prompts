I have spent the last four months in Leipzig on an exchange in public policy, and in October I received my first payslip with a church-tax line item on it: eight or nine percent of my income tax, withheld by the state on behalf of a religious community I have never belonged to, avoidable only by filing a formal declaration of exit at a court office. I raised this in a seminar with three other students, and within twenty minutes the phrase "separation of church and state" had been used to settle three incompatible things. An American classmate invoked it to insist that the German arrangement would be flatly unconstitutional at home and that anyone defending it simply does not understand what separation means. A German law student invoked the very same phrase to argue that Germany has genuine separation — no state church, after all — and that a state-collected levy handed straight to the churches is entirely consistent with it, describing the local arrangement as a kind of limping separation. A French classmate invoked it to argue that both of us are living with a fudge, and that only the French model, which refuses to recognize or subsidize any cult, actually achieves what the phrase describes. Nobody ever said what they thought the words meant; each of them just reached for the slogan and treated the conclusion as delivered.

What I want is a menu, not an essay: lay out the distinct precise theses that one sentence could be expressing, ordered from the most anodyne to the most radical. For each, tell me what kind of claim it is — a claim about constitutional text, about judicial doctrine, about public funding, about symbolic establishment and ceremonial entanglement, about the permissible religious grounding of legislation, about religion's confinement to private life, or about a normative ideal that no existing state meets — and whether it is descriptive or normative. Then give a verdict on each: which are platitudes no one could dispute, which are substantive but defensible, which are simply false, and which are true in one of the four countries under discussion (the United States, Germany, France, England) and false in another. I should note that the phrase appears nowhere in the American Constitution and is usually traced back to a letter by an early American president, and that American justices keep returning to a wall metaphor; tell me whether any reading of the slogan depends on that provenance, or whether the metaphor has quietly become the whole content of the claim for American speakers.

Three further things I would like handled explicitly. First, the referent: does "church" in the slogan mean one privileged denomination, all organized religion, or any comprehensive worldview including secular ones, and how many of the disagreements in my seminar dissolve or sharpen once that is fixed? Second, the direction of the duty: is separation an obligation binding only the state, leaving religious bodies free to shape politics, or a symmetric constraint on both — and which reading does each of my three classmates need in order for their inference to go through? Third, two readings I have been toying with naming myself, which I would like you to assess rather than adopt: separation as mutual independence in the sense of a checks-and-balances arrangement, where interference is permitted but control is not, versus separation as functional differentiation of social spheres, where each operates by its own internal logic and the question is only whether the codes get confused. Please say whether either earns a place on the list, and whether the church tax is better described as a breach of separation under one of them and as routine administration under the other. Finally, for each reading on your list, say what sort of fact would settle whether it holds in a given country, so that I can check the claims myself rather than trade slogans; and end by pointing out precisely where each of my three speakers equivocated between readings. An itemized list with short verdicts is what I want to walk into the next session with.
