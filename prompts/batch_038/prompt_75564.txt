I teach a graduate seminar each spring on anarchy, and for six years running I've assigned the same three articles and watched the same glaze come over the room. This year I want to hand them something else. So: write me a short story, somewhere between 2,500 and 4,000 words, that dramatizes structural realism. Not realism in the sense of tough men making hard choices in wood-paneled rooms — the Waltzian structural thing. Anarchy as an ordering principle. Units that are functionally alike and differ only in capability. Distribution of capability as the one variable that actually moves over time. Self-help. And a security dilemma that runs on uncertainty about intentions rather than about strength, which is a distinction I want the story to feel rather than state.

Setting: not Earth, and not states with flags. Here's the seed — adjust the furniture, keep the shape. An inland sea, five or six port-cities, all of whose wealth rests on one fish run and on channels that silt shut and reopen on nobody's schedule. And one of the units is not a city at all: an Assay Office, a neutral body of clerks and refiners that certifies weights, coin fineness, salvage claims and grain grades for everybody, staffed by people who belong to no city and protected by nothing except the fact that everyone needs the assay to keep working. I want that unit in there precisely because it's an embarrassment to the theory. Structural realism wants anarchic systems made of like units differing only in capability, and here is one that differs in kind, owns no hulls, and survives on custom. Show whether the system can absorb it, or what it costs once it can't.

Requirements — please treat these as hard constraints, not suggestions:

- Nobody in the story says any of the above. No speeches, no ambassador explaining the balance of power to a slow nephew. A reader should be able to reconstruct the causal chain afterwards; no character should be able to.
- Every city must sincerely and defensibly believe its own moves are defensive. I want to be able to argue each one's case from the inside and win.
- The offense–defense balance has to flip partway through, and the flip has to come from ecology or institutions — silt, the fish moving, a hard winter, a change in who can lend, a harbor-master's guild getting captured — not from a better weapon. If somebody invents a superior cannon or a cleverer ship, I'll know, and it'll read like every other version of this story.
- The number of serious units goes from five-ish to two, and I want the re-pricing that follows: alliances getting cheaper or dearer, the middling cities discovering they no longer matter, the Assay Office finding out what neutrality is worth once only two buyers of it are left.
- No villains. If a reader can locate the disaster in one person's character or one bad decision, I'll count the piece as a failure.
- It ends badly but not apocalyptically. I want recurrence — the sense that this happens again, with different furniture.

Then an afterword of 300–400 words. No bibliography, no reading list, just your own account of where the story cheats. Which mechanisms did you actually earn structurally, and where did you quietly do the causal work with unit-level material — a magistrate's fear, a bad harvest, domestic politics, misperception — because a narrative needs particular people and structural realism famously refuses to talk about them?

And while you're in there, argue with me about the assignment itself. My worry is that a structural theory is supposed to explain recurrence and constraint — why similar patterns keep turning up across systems, what the structure rules out — and not particular events at all. If that's right, then asking for a story about one specific crisis is asking structural realism to do exactly the job it says it can't do, and what I've actually commissioned is a unit-level tragedy wearing realism's vocabulary. If you think so, say it plainly in the afterword and tell me what version of this exercise would be honest, or whether it can't be done honestly. I'd much rather have a good case against my own assignment than a polite story that fakes the structure. And if you think my worry is itself confused, say that too — I'm not attached to it.

One seperate thing: keep the prose plain. I dont want lyrical fog, I want the machinery visible through it.
