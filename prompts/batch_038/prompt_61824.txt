Schrödinger's epilogue to What Is Life? says "Consciousness is never experienced in the plural, only in the singular," and his diagnosis of why we think otherwise runs: "Consciousness finds itself intimately connected with, and dependent on, the physical state of a limited region of matter, the body... Now, there is a great plurality of similar bodies. Hence the pluralization of consciousnesses or minds seems a very suggestive hypothesis."

I want you to argue for a reading of this that I think gets missed: that its not Vedantic idealism or one-cosmic-mind metaphysics at all, but a claim about the grammar of 'mind' -- that 'consciousness' functions like a mass term for which no counting principle is available from inside experience, so the plural is always smuggled in from the body count. If that's right, the usual objections (split-brain, dissociative identity, the complaint that your subject doesn't have my subject's memories) all beg the question, since each one supplies a counting principle from the physical side, which is exactly the move under dispute.

Make that case as hard as you can, but don't strawman the opposition -- I also want to know where the grammatical reading is genuinely vulnerable. My guess is the one-to-one pairing between the single subject and individual bodies, since explaining it looks like it has to sneak the count back in. Convince me I'm wrong if I am.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
