I write about science and ideas for a national magazine — mostly the zone where a lab result or a leaked document turns into a policy fight. Every couple of years a university drags me in to teach something, and this term I've inherited the back half of an upper-level seminar on commitment: promises, contracts, testimony, that whole neighborhood. Eight students, all of them either pre-law or wanna-be reporters, which means they all arrive with a very confident opinion about whether it's ever okay to break a pledge you made to someone who trusted you. I have to set the take-home final. 48 hours, one question, 1,600 words maximum, 25 marks. By now they've had the standard tour — Hume's idea that promising isn't natural at all but an artificial virtue resting on a human convention, Rawls's point that a practice like promising has constitutive rules so the "why should I keep it?" question gets asked at the level of the practice rather than the individual act, Scanlon's account where you bind yourself by deliberately getting someone to expect that you'll do something and it's a distinctive kind of wrong to exploit the expectation you manufactured, and then the more recent stuff about obligations being bipolar — owed *to* somebody, not just a free-floating duty you happen to have. They can all name these and they can all summarise them, which is exactly the problem I need you to solve for me.

Here's the case I want the question built on, and it comes off my own beat. A reporter gives a source an absolute, unqualified promise of confidentiality: no name, no description, nothing that could identify them, ever. The source hands over material documenting serious wrongdoing by someone who is now a candidate for high office. Then the source dies, suddenly, before anything runs. The material is only usable if it's attributed — a lawyer friend of mine confirms there's a real case a few years back where a reporter went to jail to protect a source, and then the source personally released her and she testified anyway and got roasted from both directions, but that's the thing, in my case there's nobody left to release anybody. The dead source's family doesn't know the promise was made. No editor, no court, no professional code settles it. So the question is purely whether she's still bound, and if so, bound to whom, and what it would take to get out of it. And I want a second beat in there too: suppose she could break the promise in a way no living person ever finds out about. Does each view change its answer? The ones that do and the ones that don't should tell the student something real about where each theory thinks the obligation lives.

So the deliverables: the question as it would actually appear on the sheet — a paragraph of setup in my voice-ish, then the ask, then whatever sub-parts you think earn their keep. Then a model answer of about 700 words that I could hand to a student so they can see what full marks looks like. Then a marking scheme out of 25 that isn't just points distributed by paragraph. I want it to name the specific moves that seperate an A from a B. In my experience a B answer here will march competently through all four views, assign each one a verdict on whether she should publish, and stop — it treats the theories like a menu and never notices that they disagree about what *kind of thing* the obligation is, not just about the outcome. Tell me what the A has to add: I'd guess something about who (if anyone) could have standing to release her and what that implies, and something about the undetectable-breach wrinkle, but you know this literature far better than I do, so overrule me if you have a better crux. Also flag the two or three mistakes that should cost serious marks — sliding from "the practice of source-confidentiality is socially valuable" to "so she owes this particular dead person something," say, or treating the bipolar-obligation stuff as though it were just Scanlon with a new label. One hard constraint: no reading list, no citations, no page numbers, and please don't build the question so it only works if the student has a particular book open. Half of them won't. Name the positions where naming helps, but the argument has to be followable from the argument itself.
