I am preparing a seminar session that brings together the classical biography of the Greco-Roman world and the saints' lives of the Latin Middle Ages, and in reading them side by side I have become convinced that both genres are defeated by the same structural problem. I do not have a name for it, and I would like to know whether one exists.

The problem is this. The biographer's aim is imitation: he wants the reader to become like the subject. To serve that aim he selects for the edifying and away from the rest — the failures that were never resolved, the motives that were mixed, the long stretches of ordinary days in which nothing exemplary happened at all. But the selection works against the aim. What emerges is a figure who can be admired without cost, because the life presents no seam at which the reader's own life could join it. Admiration turns out not to be a stage on the way to imitation but a complete and self-sufficient response, and it is completed inside the act of reading. The better the life is written, the more reliably this substitution occurs; a clumsy, indiscriminate record leaves the reader unsettled and therefore still under some obligation, while a finely shaped one discharges the obligation for him.

Three observations that seem to me to belong to the same thing. First, the biographer's own stated method contradicts his practice: the preface to one of Plutarch's most famous pairs insists that the grandest achievements are not the most revealing indicators of character, and that a remark, a small incident, or a jest discloses more than a battle with enormous casualties — and yet the life that follows is assembled almost entirely out of such battles. Second, the defects return only under comparison. Plutarch's pairs close with a formal weighing of the two men, and it is striking how much of what a single life had smoothed over reappears the moment a second life stands beside it, as though the genre could only carry the truth in duplicate. Third, the same shape occurs outside religious and classical biography altogether: the modern profile of someone who has overcome great hardship reliably produces in its reader a warm feeling that is, so far as the reader can tell, indistinguishable from the feeling of having done something, and the profile is judged successful in proportion to the intensity of that feeling.

I know the neighborhood. There is the use of hagiography as a term of abuse, but that concerns accuracy, and my problem arises in lives that are perfectly accurate about what they include. There is the complaint, in the nineteenth-century German discussion of how the past should be used, that grand historical models make the present feel derivative and small; that is a claim about the reader's sense of scale, not about the substitution of one response for another. There is the contemporary view that admiration is a moral emotion which tracks exemplars and can itself ground a normative theory; that is a positive account of the mechanism my phenomenon describes as failing. And there is the more modest worry that a reader cannot tell which features of an admired life are load-bearing and which are incidental; but that is a problem about the reader's judgment, whereas mine arises even in readers of excellent judgment.

So: does this configuration have an established name, in the literature on exemplarity, on biography as a genre, on the moral psychology of admiration, or anywhere else? If it does, I want the name distinguished carefully from each of the neighbors above. If it does not, I want you to coin one and defend it — a definition, the mechanism by which it operates, the conditions under which it fails to arise, and two or three boundary cases that test its edges (for instance, a life that is honestly told but non-transferable because the circumstances were unique; admiration that does produce imitation, but of the wrong feature; a text that induces the feeling without any exemplar at all). A bare label will not serve my purpose, since I intend to put the term to work comparing two genres that otherwise have little vocabulary in common. Please argue from the concepts themselves rather than from a list of sources.
