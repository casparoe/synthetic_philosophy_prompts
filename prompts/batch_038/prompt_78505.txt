I am a junior, and last Thursday our school's math society hosted a graduate student who gave a talk on the P versus NP problem. Afterwards two members of the society — Mira, who intends to study pure mathematics, and Jonah, who does competitive programming — got into an argument in the hallway that nobody standing there could settle. I am reporting it as neutrally as I can: I did not take a side and I do not have one. Here is the exchange as I remember it, with their claims numbered in the order they were made. I may have garbled some of the technical vocabulary, so please tell me wherever a claim is wrong simply as I have stated it.

1. Mira: The reason P versus NP is philosophically deep rather than merely technical is that it asks whether finding and checking are the same activity. If P = NP, then in principle every problem whose solution can be quickly verified can also be quickly solved, so the difference between recognizing a proof and producing one is a difference of effort, not of kind. That is a claim about the nature of mathematical creativity.

2. Jonah: Then it is an empty claim, because P = NP is an existence statement about polynomial-time algorithms, and the polynomial could be n to the ten-thousandth power. A theorem like that would be true and would change nothing about what mathematicians can actually do. A philosophy immune to every observable consequence is not philosophy, it is decoration.

3. Mira: Not immune at all — if P = NP then public-key cryptography collapses, which is an observable consequence of enormous size. And setting that aside, you have conceded my point in order to attack it: whether the exponent is large is a fact about our machines, while whether the exponent is finite is a fact about what is possible. Complexity theory is the one field that turned "possible in principle" into a precise mathematical notion, and you are complaining that it did.

4. Jonah: Fine, then the deep question is not P versus NP but the barrier results. There is a theorem of Razborov and Rudich, the natural proofs result, which says roughly that if one-way functions exist then no natural argument can prove that NP problems require super-polynomial circuits, where natural means constructive and applying to many functions at once. So the possibility of secure encryption would imply a limit on how we could ever come to know a limit on computation. That is genuinely strange: the world's cryptographic structure constrains what we can prove about the world's computational structure.

5. Mira: It is not strange, it is an ordinary technical obstacle, and dressing it up as profundity is exactly the mistake. Every hard open problem has proof techniques that fail on it. You have also overstated it: the theorem is conditional on a conjecture, and natural is a defined technical property, so it does not show that P ≠ NP is unprovable, only that one family of approaches is blocked.

6. Jonah: I overstated it slightly, but your reply proves my case. What makes this barrier unlike an ordinary obstacle is that it is conditional on an empirical hypothesis about the world rather than on a mathematical guess. Most statements of the form "you cannot prove X" depend only on mathematics; this one depends on whether factoring-like problems really are hard, which is a question about nature.

7. Mira: That is a category confusion. Whether one-way functions exist is a mathematical question, not a question about nature; physics has nothing to say about it. You cannot get an empirical premise into a mathematical conclusion, so whatever is philosophically interesting about the natural proofs result must be mathematical, and then it is an ordinary obstacle after all.

8. Jonah: Then explain why nobody has found a natural problem that is neither in P nor NP-complete. Ladner's theorem says such problems must exist if P ≠ NP, yet the examples are all built deliberately and look artificial, while the problems we actually run into keep turning out to be either easy or complete. That is a pattern in the world, not in our axioms.

Neither of them would give ground. Please work through the exchange move by move: for each of the eight numbered claims, rate it as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and justify the rating in no more than two sentences; then say which of the two is more right overall and why; and finally say what, if anything, both of them are missing that a better version of this argument would have included. Keep the whole response under 700 words, and please do not spend any of it explaining what P and NP are.
