I record a fortnightly podcast on political philosophy, and next week's guest is a political theorist who defends what she calls the disclosure condition on dirty hands: an act of political wrongdoing — torture, assassination, the deceiving of one's own electorate — is justified only if the agent could announce it in full, with reasons, both to the people it wrongs and to the public who must live with it. Covert cases are therefore not tragic necessities but simply unjustified, and the familiar talk of moral residue and agent-regret is, on her account, nothing more than the agent's own inarticulate recognition that the act will not survive announcement.

Write me eight to ten questions that get past the slogan. I want at least one each on: the stock example of withholding a warning from a city in order to protect the fact that an enemy cipher has been broken, where the announcement would itself cause the harm the act was meant to prevent; the charge that she has merely reinstated clean-hands absolutism under a new name, and so cannot explain why the constraint is felt as tragic rather than as ordinary prohibition; and whether a condition on announcing particular decisions has been confused with the quite different requirement that a society's principles be publicly acknowledged. For each, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — she is very fluent at retreating to disclosure before a properly constituted oversight committee — and close with a question about what would count as evidence against her view.
