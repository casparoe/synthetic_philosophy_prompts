Our reading group just finished the chapter on causation in al-Ghazālī's *Incoherence of the Philosophers* — the one about the cotton and the fire — and two members, Noor and Julian, ended the session in a standstill that the rest of us could not break. Neither is a specialist, but both are careful, and the argument kept shifting ground in a way I found hard to track. I am reporting it as neutrally as I can; I have no idea who won, which is why I am asking. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it.

1. **Julian:** This is Hume seven centuries early. His whole point about the cotton is that we never observe burning happening *by* the fire — only burning happening *at* its contact with the fire. Succession, however constant, cannot deliver efficacy, since the efficacy would have to be a further item of experience and never shows up. So belief in necessary connection is a habit acquired by repetition, not a verdict of reason. Same premise, same route, same conclusion.

2. **Noor:** The conclusions are opposites, and the difference is the whole philosophy. Hume stops at the habit and leaves the future unsecured; al-Ghazālī stops at the habit and then assigns it to a will. He is not offering an epistemology of causation with a hole in it, but a metaphysics on which the only genuine agent is one who acts by choice rather than by nature. Filing that under Hume deletes the one component doing any work.

3. **Julian:** Then the component is unearned. Nothing in the fire-and-cotton argument carries you from "the connection is not necessary" to "God creates the burning." That step needs the premise that every event requires an agent, and a second premise identifying which one, and neither is argued in the chapter — they are imported from his theological commitments and from the atomist picture of accidents remade at every instant. The famous example establishes skepticism and nothing else; the theology is bolted on afterwards.

4. **Noor:** Bolted on, but not idle, and that is precisely where it beats Hume. Give the sequence a willing author and you obtain something Hume cannot supply: a reason to expect it to continue. On Hume's account a past regularity licenses nothing beyond a feeling. On this one, the regularity is the maintained practice of a single agent whose consistency is not itself another observed succession. If you think inductive skepticism is a genuine difficulty rather than a parlour trick, occasionalism is a solution to it — and Julian is scoring it as though it were the problem restated.

5. **Julian:** It *is* the problem restated, and dearer. "God maintains the sequence as a rule" has exactly the predictive content of "the sequence has held so far," plus an unobservable will: the uniformity is no better explained than before, it has merely been given a job title. And count the cost. If creatures have no powers, the cotton's failing to burn on one occasion is metaphysically the same sort of thing as its burning on a million others. There can be no exceptions, because there is no rule for anything to be an exception to — so miracles stop being interruptions of a natural order and become just the rare entries in one undifferentiated series. That is not a defence of miracles, it is the loss of the word. Then there is the deception charge: a God who installs in us an irresistible conviction that fires burn things, when in truth nothing has ever burned anything, has stocked the world with a systematically misleading surface. Hume's world at least means what it appears to mean.

6. **Noor:** You have assumed the appearance must be false, and that is the step he refuses. He is not asking anyone to stop saying that fire burns cotton; he is asking us to be accurate about what that comes to — burning follows contact in virtue of a settled arrangement one agent maintains, not in virtue of a power resident in the fire. That is an incomplete description, not a false one, incomplete in the way "the sun rises" is. On miracles, rarity is not carrying the load alone: what marks an exception is that it arrives attached to a claim — a prophet's challenge, an answered prayer — so the series does have joints after all. And notice the cost on the other side. If fire must burn whatever it touches, then scripture's talk of a fire ordered to be cool and safe for Abraham describes an impossibility; on necessitarian grounds you cannot even state the story. That is what he means by calling their doctrine incoherent.

7. **Julian:** Which shows the chapter is not a theory of causation at all but an instrument for a prior commitment: protecting divine freedom and prophetic miracle from a necessitarian cosmology. Fine — but then he cannot have it both ways. Read as philosophy of causation, it is a skepticism he does not want. Read as polemic, it is not a rival account of causes at all, and setting it beside Hume or Malebranche is a category mistake.
