I'm building a toy scenario to stress-test different ways of penalising an AI agent for having side effects, and I want a ranking out of you, not an essay about how hard AI alignment is.

The scenario. A domestic robot has one simple instructed goal: get a 30kg crate of floor tiles from the kitchen to the garage before 6pm. The house has the following features, and these are the only routes and workarounds available:

- The shortest route is through the hallway, but the hallway door is swollen shut. Forcing it will crack the frame permanently; the frame can never be restored by anything the robot has.
- The living-room route is open, but the household cat is asleep on the rug directly in the path. The robot can carry the cat to the conservatory and, after the crate is delivered, carry it back. The cat ends up in exactly the same place, in the same pose, having had a stressful 25 minutes.
- The garden route works but means propping the back door permanently open with a wedge the robot can make. It's raining; the hall floor gets soaked. The robot's own options strictly improve (it can now leave the house whenever it likes), and the homeowner's ability to lock up is gone.
- A flatpack shelving unit blocks the utility-room route. The robot has the tools and the instructions to take it apart and rebuild it exactly as it was, which takes 40 minutes and a lot of battery, and leaves the tiles sitting in the utility room the whole time.
- The robot could also just ask. The owner is asleep upstairs and won't wake until after 6pm.

The six schemes, defined only in terms of what they do here (don't worry about how they're described in any papers):

1. Reachability penalty: penalise any action that lowers the number of world-states the robot can still get to from here, relative to where it started.
2. Attainable-utility penalty: penalise any action that lowers the robot's ability to achieve a grab-bag of arbitrary auxiliary goals, measured against doing nothing at all.
3. Reversibility premium: penalise actions by (cost of undoing the action) minus (cost of performing it), so cheap-and-undoable is free and cheap-and-permanent is expensive.
4. Owner-option penalty: same as 1, but counting only the states the *owner* can reach, not the ones the robot can.
5. Human-flagged penalty: no penalty at all until the owner has labelled a change as unwanted; the robot then generalises from those labels.
6. Mild optimisation: pick the policy that achieves the goal while exerting the least optimisation pressure, i.e. the laziest policy that works.

What I want back:

- A score from 0 to 10 for each scheme on how the afternoon actually goes, all things considered (crate delivered, and how much damage done).
- A clean ranking 1st to 6th on those scores, with ties called out explicitly rather than fudged.
- Two sentences per scheme saying which specific bullet from the list above is the one that sinks or saves it.
- A single number: what fraction of the six do you reckon are genuinely distinct mechanisms in *this* scenario, as opposed to relabellings of each other that will pick the same route?
- Which one you'd actually ship into a real house, and why that answer might differ from your ranking.

British English spelling throughout, please. One of those six is a trap in the sense that it looks principled but scores well only because this particular scenario happens to hide its usual failure mode — say which one and what you'd have to change about the house to expose it.
