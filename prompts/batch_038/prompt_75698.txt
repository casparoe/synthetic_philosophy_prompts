I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — an economist by training, now doing survey-based welfare measurement for a regional health authority — about whether an interpersonal comparison of utility is even the sort of thing that can be true or false, and what could possibly settle it. He holds what I would call a quietist line, defended with a broadly Wittgensteinian flavour: our practice of comparing one another's pleasures and pains is not a set of hypotheses awaiting verification but a rule-governed form of life, resting on primitive pre-linguistic reactions — the flinch, the wince, the reaching-out — that we share and train into children. On his view, demanding independent empirical warrant for those comparisons is a category mistake, and the only thing that "fixes the cross-person scale" is that we are creatures who respond to each other in this way rather than that. I found the position both compelling and slippery, and the deeper problem the argument exposed is that neither of us had ever written down what one should require of an account of interpersonal comparison in the first place; we traded cases and intuitions for three hours with no specification against which to test them, so neither of us could be shown to have lost.

So, before I read anything further in this area, I want the desiderata. Fix the purpose as follows: not a theory good enough to win a seminar argument, but one good enough to bear weight in real decisions — in particular, the case of an authority that must rank the competing claims of a patient whose lifelong depression has recalibrated everything she reports about her own condition against a patient in acute pain, using instruments built largely out of self-report. Write the list of requirements any adequate account of interpersonal utility comparison must meet, with the case or consideration behind each one. That should include, at minimum: the phenomena it has to explain (both our ordinary confidence in claims like "her pain is worse than his" and our equally ordinary sense that some such claims are not merely unknown but indeterminate); the distinctions it must not collapse (level comparability versus comparability of differences; comparability versus measurability; the question whether such comparisons are true versus the question how anyone could know them; comparison of one person across time versus comparison of two people at a time; a scale being fixed by our practices versus a scale being discovered in the world); the formal constraints it must respect (uniqueness of each person's utility scale only up to positive affine transformation, and hence exactly what further structure genuine comparability demands beyond within-person cardinality, including whether a common origin and a common unit are separable requirements); the results it must stay consistent with (the impossibility theorems whose force depends on an ordinal, interpersonally non-comparable framework, and the possibility results that become available once comparability is granted — say what additional conditions those results need, not merely that they exist); and the empirical facts it cannot contradict or hand-wave (hedonic adaptation and response shift; the disability paradox, where people report a high quality of life against all clinical expectation; and the anchoring-vignette and related rescaling techniques used to correct self-report for the fact that different groups use the same response categories differently — on which last point I want an explicit verdict: do those techniques presuppose the comparability they are meant to establish, produce it, or something else?). For every requirement, mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, and then tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate accounts.

I also have a suspicion that three of the demands I have just listed cannot be jointly satisfied: that comparability be settled entirely by facts about individuals that carry no ethical evaluation; that the account be determinate enough to rank actual allocations; and that it explain why first-person report and third-person observation come apart without one of them being simply mistaken. If that trilemma is real — or if you can state a sharper version of it — say so, and say which leg should be dropped; I would rather have that result than a harmonious list, and if it is not real, show me the account that satisfies all three. I would also like my friend's quietist position run against the list explicitly: which requirements it meets, which it dissolves rather than meets, and which it simply cannot handle. Two further requests about how you present this. First, be scrupulous about separating settled ground from live controversy among people who work on this topic: for each requirement, say whether it is near-universal or contested, and where it is contested, sketch the best argument on the other side rather than assuming it away — I do not want a contested view, including my friend's, smuggled in as the default. Second, no bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus: use proper names only as labels for positions, and flag any attribution you are not fully confident of, since I have no way to check it tonight. If you want to close with a paragraph on how the two or three main families of account fare against the specification, keep it to one clearly separated paragraph — the thinking I am after is in the criteria.
