I teach one 75-minute session in an upper-division cognitive science seminar, and my assignment is Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, followed by the question of what global workspace theory is actually offering an account of. The students can reproduce the labels. What they cannot do is see why the two notions might come apart: asked last week, nearly all said that a broadcast state is by that fact a conscious one, and that the distinction is only a dressed-up contrast between the reportable and the unreportable. Half the class is simultaneously on a neuroethics rotation and will drag everything toward detecting covert command-following in unresponsive patients; I would rather spend that appetite than suppress it, but they currently take a positive command-following result to settle whether there is anything it is like to be that patient.

Plan the session for me rather than delivering it. I want the order in which the pieces must go in and what each depends on, including the point at which the broadcast-equals-consciousness conflation has to be broken before the distinction can even be stated; one carrying case, with an account of what it must contain and what it must lack, and why the usual candidates — an iconic-memory partial-report display, an unfelt pain, a population organized to realize a mind — do not do the work in this room; the misconceptions the students will arrive with or generate as the session proceeds, each tagged to the exact moment I should intercept it; a single closing question that separates those who have grasped the dissociation from those who have memorized terminology; and what I should decline to take up at this level, and announce that I am declining it.
