I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology to a mixed cohort of philosophy and law students, and I have decided to build one session around Sidney Lumet's 1957 film 12 Angry Men, read against Aumann's agreement theorem. The film is cited constantly in law schools as a parable about reasoning under uncertainty, but almost always in a loose, inspirational register. My claim is that it is better read as a study of the conditions under which disagreement survives shared evidence and the conditions under which it does not — which is what the theorem is about, and also where the film refuses to cooperate. I want a critical reading that takes both the film and the mathematics seriously. Assume your reader knows the plot and the jurors: do not summarize the film, and do not reproduce dialogue.

First, get the theorem straight. State its assumptions — the common prior, common knowledge of posteriors, Bayesian coherence — what it asserts, and what it conspicuously does not: it is a claim about whether a certain static configuration of beliefs is possible, not a claim that communication drives convergence, and it says nothing about how quickly agreement arrives or what produces it. Identify where the usual gloss, that rational people cannot agree to disagree, overreaches. Then flag the gap between agreement in belief and agreement in decision, which matters here because a juror's task is to return a verdict under a reasonable-doubt standard, not to publish a posterior.

Second, sort the film's vote changes. Some are driven by evidence generated inside the room: the demonstration that the supposedly distinctive switchblade is in fact common, the timed reconstruction of whether the older downstairs witness could have reached his door when he said he did, the inference from the impressions on Juror 4's nose to the woman across the street being a habitual glasses-wearer. Others are not. Say what Aumann's theorem can and cannot say about each class. My suspicion — which I want tested rather than assumed — is that the first class falls outside the theorem's scope altogether, because the deliberation is evidence-producing rather than evidence-sharing, so convergence there is just what ordinary conditioning predicts and the theorem adds nothing. If that is right, say so plainly, and then tell me what work is left for Aumann to do.

Third, take up what is left: the holdouts. Test the common-prior assumption against Juror 5's upbringing, Juror 9's age, and Juror 10's bigotry, asking of each whether it is a difference in priors or a difference in private information — and whether that distinction stays stable once it is placed inside the film, or whether the film suggests that a prior is often a lifetime of evidence compressed into a standing disposition to discount further evidence. Give Juror 7 separate treatment, since Juror 11 rebukes him for changing his vote for reasons having nothing to do with belief; and Juror 3's final capitulation arrives with a torn photograph and tears rather than an argument. Does either man end up holding the posterior the others hold, and if not, in what sense has this jury agreed at all?

Fourth, treat the unanimity requirement as an institutional device for forcing common knowledge — a rule under which nobody may leave the room while publicly known to dissent. Ask whether the film licenses the inference from a unanimous verdict to agreement in belief, what would have to be true for that inference to hold, and whether anything in the room gives us reason to think it does hold here.

Stay honest in both directions throughout: do not convert the film into a counterexample to a theorem it never engages, and do not inflate the theorem into a theory of deliberation it was never meant to be. Close by saying what the film shows about disagreement that the theorem and its proof cannot show, and, if you think some piece of the epistemology in the film is simply wrong, say which piece and why.

Prose rather than outline form, roughly fifteen hundred words. No reading list, no citations — the argument should stand on its own.
