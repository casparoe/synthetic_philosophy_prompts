So I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine — the sort of column a reader finishes on the train and feels slightly unsettled by — and I've got about 1,200 words due Thursday on the extended mind thesis, which really means on Clark and Chalmers' old thought experiment about Otto and his notebook. The trouble is I've been walking around for a fortnight making snap judgements about my own gadgets and my own household, and the pattern I've come up with looks nothing like the tidy "if it does the same job, it counts as cognition" line I'd assumed I'd be building the piece around. I'd rather find out what I actually think than summarise the debate back at people, so here are my verdicts, off the top of my head, and what I want to know is what principle — if there is one — sits underneath them.

The cases. A blind man's stick: yes, the stick is part of his perceiving, not something he consults. Otto's notebook, in the original version of the thought experiment: yes, he really does believe the museum is on 53rd Street. My own notes app, which I havent opened in eight months: no, that's a gym membership, not a memory. The whiteboard in my office that I rewrite every week and glance at without deciding to: yes, and honestly the clearest case on the list. A calculator: no — when I work out a long division with pen and paper I feel like I'm thinking, but the moment I punch it in to the calculator I feel like I've outsourced the thinking to something that hands me an answer. Same input, same output, different verdict, and I can't say why. My wife, who keeps track of every birthday, every boiler service date and which friends we've already had round this year, and who I ask without thinking: no, she is not a part of my mind, she's a person — but functionally her job is the notebook's job, so that can't be the whole story. A cochlear implant: yes, obviously. Otto's notebook after somebody has quietly rewritten half the entries while he slept: no — though interestingly there I want to say he still has beliefs, just false ones, which feels like a different kind of answer from the others. A chatbot I use daily and trust more than my own recall on some topics: no — but when I reread something I wrote two years ago and think "past me was an idiot", I still count that as my own mind arguing with itself, so "it talks back" doesn't cleanly separate the two. And my phone's satnav while I'm driving: yes, it's part of my navigating — and yet I'm convinced I've got worse at navigating because of it, which is a strange thing to be able to say about a part of your own mind.

What I want back: the principle, or the small handful of candidate principles, that delivers as many of those verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could tell whether some new case falls under it. Not "trust and integration" — I want the actual clauses, and which of my cases is doing the work for each clause. Then be straight with me about the casualties: I expect at least one verdict has to go, so tell me which one you'd overturn and what it costs me to hang on to it anyway (the pen-and-paper-versus-calculator pair and the wife-versus-notebook pair are the ones I suspect). Run whatever you land on against two cases I haven't listed — you pick them, but make them ones an ordinary reader would recognise — and tell me whether it lands in the "cognitive bloat" territory where my whole bookshelf and half my family end up as parts of my mind. Two house keeping things: no bibliography, please, I'm not writing a literature review and I'll check any names myself before they go in print; and write the answer in British English, spellings and all, because my editor marks up Americanisms in red pen.
