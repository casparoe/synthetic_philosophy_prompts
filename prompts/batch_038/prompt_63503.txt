Building a case for a paper on imprecise credences — the view that your doxastic state can be a whole set of probability functions rather than one, so your credence in a claim is an interval like [0.2, 0.6] instead of a point.

Construct me a thought experiment to these specs. Realistic setting — a hospital, a farm, a regulator, whatever — one paragraph of setup, and no urns, no coloured balls, no coins. The agent's utility is linear in money, so all the imprecision lives in the probabilities and none of it in the utilities. She has a small finite set of priors that I can work through by hand. There's an observation on offer, free, and one she knew in advance she'd be offered, which is uncorrelated with the payoffs of every act open to her — all it does is dilate her credence in some hypothesis (dilation being the thing where learning a payoff-irrelevant fact widens the interval instead of narrowing it). Under maximin over her credal set she strictly prefers to stay ignorant at the earlier time; but asked at the later time, having observed, she strictly prefers that she did observe — so a reversal across time. Meanwhile a precise Bayesian whose single prior is any member of her set is indifferent to the observation at both times, so the preference for ignorance has to come from the imprecision itself and not from the numbers I fed you.

Then show your working: the credal set, the acts, the likelihoods for the observation, the posterior sets at both times, and the maximin value of each act before and after. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work — my guess is the likelihoods failing some relevance condition, but say which one and why. Does the reversal survive if she uses a rule that mixes maximin with expected utility under one distinguished 'core' prior, or maximality, instead of plain maximin? And how would someone defending imprecise credences attack the case — is preferring free information actually irrational, or is this just a planning failure that doesn't indict the view?

The case is for testing whether "you can't always learn for free" is a reductio or just an artefact of one decision rule, so derive the preferences from the rule and the credal set rather than stipulating them. If you have to fudge the numbers until even a precise agent would want to stay ignorant, the whole thing fails and I'd rather you said so. British English please.
