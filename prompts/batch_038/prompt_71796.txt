I write a weekly ideas column, mostly science but increasingly the philosophy sitting underneath it, and next month I'm doing a long taped Q&A with a philosopher whose new book argues that the axioms of expected utility theory aren't a school of thought you can politely opt out of but a theorem about what consistency costs. Her whole case is the exploitability stuff: if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability calculus, a sharp bookie can package bets you'd each accept separately and take you to the cleaners overall, and if your prefernces violate transitivity or independence, a trader can walk you around a cycle of individually attractive trades and leave you worse off holding the thing you started with. She's smart and genuinely good on tape, but she has one move she uses on every objection, some version of "exploitability isn't a heuristic for irrationality, it's a definition of it," and then she just waits for you to run out of steam. I interviewed one of her colleagues a couple years back and got dinged by my editor for letting him talk straight past me for fifteen minutes, so I have about 45 minutes here and I don't want to spend twenty of them hearing that sentence four times.

So give me eight to ten questions, in the order I'd actually want to ask them, that make that line progressively harder to fall back on. Every question needs a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — not "can you say more about that," an actual second move. I know roughly where the soft spots are but not which of them are load-bearing, so pick and rank for me. At minimum: one on the fact that the pump only bites if the trader can costlessly offer the whole sequence, where she'll say that's an idealization and I want the follow-up that establishes whether the idealization is doing harmless work or is secretly the entire engine. One on foresight — if a sophisticated agent sees the cycle coming she just refuses the first trade, so nobody ever actually gets pumped, which seems to leave her either proving something much weaker about planning policies or smuggling in the dynamic assumption she's supposed to be deriving. One on whether "you can be pumped" is a fact about the agent or a fact about the offers the world happens to make her, and what her answer implies about an agent nobody ever trades with. One on the places the criterion bites in directions I suspect she doesn't want it to: lexicographic types who rank avoiding one catastrophic loss above any sum of money and therefore have no exchange rate for a trader to exploit, and the textbook-Bayesian-who-knows-she'll-forget-part-of-her-evidence cases where even a perfect updater gets booked diachronically and doesn't seem to have done anything wrong. And one on whether her claim is normative (you ought to satisfy the axioms) or constitutive (satisfying them is just what it is to have beliefs and desires), because if it's the second I want to press on what work the pumping argument is doing beyond restating a representation theorem in more dramatic language.

Ground rules: assume she's read everything and will smell a strawman from across the table, so no question whose only job is to make me look clever in print. No softballs, no asking what drew her to decision theory, no "can you summarize the book for people who haven't read it" — my editor cuts all of that anyway. End on one question that isn't a gotcha: what would actually convince her she's wrong here, and what would she give up first. I'll be transcribing this myself, so keep each question short enough to say out loud in one breath; if it needs setup, give me the setup as a sentence or two of context beforehand and tell me where I can trim it if she's moving fast.
