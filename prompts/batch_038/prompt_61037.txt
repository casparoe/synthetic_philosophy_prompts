I'm writing the internal style guide for our AI docs and I have to draw a line on when we're allowed to say a system "believes" something, and I've realized I don't have a line, I just have a pile of gut calls. Here they are.

A strong chess engine that sacs a knight and is still winning twelve moves later: fine to write that it believes it's up material, or that it thinks the position favors it. No hesitation at all.

The thermostat in our hallway: not fine. I will not write "the thermostat believes the room is too cold," even though I can predict what its going to do next with basically 100% accuracy and it's doing the same sort of thing the engine is.

Search autocomplete: also not fine. Never "it believes you want running shoes."

The spam filter: "the filter believes this message is not spam" reads fine to me, but "the filter believes its sender is a deposed prince" reads as obviously wrong. Same mechanism, same evidence, opposite verdict. I can't explain that one.

The perception stack in a self-driving car: I'll write "the system believes there's a pedestrian in the crosswalk," and I'll also say that when there isn't one the system is *wrong*, in a sense I'd never apply to the thermostat.

A robot vacuum that has never once driven down the stairs: I'll say it knows there's a drop there. I won't say it wants to go on existing.

A chatbot that tells you, sincerely and consistently, that it has no beliefs: I want to say it's either confused or being evasive, not simply correct.

The little reward model that scores candidate outputs: "it prefers polite answers" is fine, "it believes polite answers are true" is not. But for the base model underneath I'll happily write belief sentences all day. Where's the asymmetry coming from? I don't know.

A large model that tells one user free will is real and another that it's an illusion, with nothing relevant different about the prompts: I withdraw the belief attribution completely. Except my uncle holds flatly contradictory political beliefs and I still say what he believes without blinking. That pair is the one I can't fix.

And the most belief-shaped text our systems produce — the scratchpad line "I think the user is testing me" — is the one I trust least. I'd strike it from a doc immediately.

I know the standard move is Dennett's: the stance is a predictive strategy, so anything you can predict this way is a legitimate believer. But that makes the thermostat and autocomplete come out fine, which is backwards from my gut, and it doesn't tell an editor what to type.

The case I keep flip-flopping on: marketing wants to write "our model knows when a customer is lying." The evals genuinely show it tracks whether the customer is lying across thousands of trials, in a stable way. But ask it directly and it says it has no beliefs about anyone. Which way does your principle call it?

So — what principle, if any, generates all of this? State it precisely, precisely enough that a tired editor could apply it on a Friday, and say which case motivates each clause of it. Then say what it delivers on three I haven't listed: a translation model, a structure-prediction model that "believes" a protein folds a certain way, and an ensemble of five models that vote. And be honest about the cost. If no simple principle saves everything, tell me which of my verdicts has to go and why that one rather than some other — I'd rather have a clean principle that overturns two of these than a nine-clause list with a patch for each case, but you'll have to convince me.
