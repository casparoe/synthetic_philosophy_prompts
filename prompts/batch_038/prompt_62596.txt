I've been writing down my gut reactions to cases about when two people are allowed to keep disagreeing, and I want to know whether they add up to a principle or whether some of them have to go. Rough background on where my head is: the standard Bayesian picture where everyone has a prior and updates on evidence, with the assumption that there's one common prior sitting behind all of us doing a lot of unearned work. I know the old result that if two Bayesians share a prior and their posteriors are common knowledge, those posteriors have to match. What I can't figure out is whether I believe the assumption, because my verdicts pull both ways.

The cases.

1. Two friends, same total evidence about whether a drug works, both careful, they land on opposite conclusions. I think that's not okay. Somebody has gone wrong even though I cant say who, and I don't get to assume it's the other guy.

2. Same setup, except the two grew up in different countries and I'd describe them as starting from different priors rather than reading the same evidence differently. Now I think it's fine. Neither is irrational. And I don't think you can be irrational about your prior at all — it's not the kind of thing you choose. I realize 1 and 2 have the exact same shape from the outside. The difference is supposed to be where the divergence came from, and I don't know how I'd ever tell, since all I can see is two people with the same evidence and different beliefs.

3. My colleague and I both know we're both trying to be good Bayesians, we've swapped everything we have, and she announces she's at 0.8 on some claim while I'm at 0.2. I think we both have to move, and I think her announcement is itself evidence for me. But if all I learn is that she disagrees — no numbers, no idea what she's seen — I think I should move barely at all.

4. If I found out my prior had been assigned by a literal lottery before I was born, I don't think my beliefs would be any less justified than they are now. Which suggests priors aren't doing justificatory work at all. But 3 suggests they are, because otherwise why would her number budge me.

5. Two surgeons, identical training, identical test results on the same patient, one is temperamentally conservative and one is aggressive, so they differ about whether to operate. I want to say neither is irrational and that they ought to give the same recommendation anyway. I can't hold both.

6. Twelve jurors hear exactly the same evidence and split 8-4 on guilt. I think that's completely fine, nobody is failing at anything, and I'd let the verdict stand. But if juror 7 learned the tally was 8-4 I think she should get less confident, and if she learned it was 4-8 I think she should also get less confident. So the group is fine in a way that no member of it is fine.

7. People tell me the fix is that priors carry indexical information — where you are, who you are, what you've personally seen — so the "same evidence" cases never really have the same evidence. I think that's relabeling the problem. In case 1 they're in the same city reading the same trials; the indexical stuff matches too.

8. Two people with the same evidence, one has finished a long calculation and one hasn't, so they disagree about a consequence of what they both already have. Neither is irrational — you can't blame the one who hasn't gotten there yet. But they will converge, and nothing about their evidence changes when they do. So there's a third thing besides priors and evidence driving convergence and my framework has no slot for it.

9. Two people arguing about whether there was a recession while using "recession" differently. Not a real disagreement, no principle needed. But after they settle the definition they still disagree about the numbers, and now I think it's real and I think it's the case 2 kind, so it's fine. Except it feels worse than case 2 did and I don't know why.

The principle I keep trying to write down is: rational disagreement is permitted only when the two people differ in evidence. That gets 1 right and 2 wrong. Change it to "differ in evidence or in priors" and it gets 2 right, makes 3 inexplicable, and lets 5 off the hook way too easily. Neither version touches 6 or 8.

The case I actually want settled: my coauthor and I have the same data, the same model, we've run each other's code, we agree on every definition, and we still disagree about whether our main result is robust — I'm at maybe 0.75, she's at 0.4, and neither of us can name a thing the other hasn't seen. What do we do? Split to 0.575? Does splitting even mean anything here, or is it fake conciliation that lets us both stop thinking? And if we both know we're going to split, shouldn't we skip ahead to the split now, and then split again about the split?

So: give me the principle, or the two or three candidates that come closest, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without checking back with you. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it's there to save. Then tell me what it says about a couple of cases I didn't list — pick ones that put pressure on it, not ones it wins easily. And tell me straight which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I'd rather lose three of them than end up with a nine-clause principle, but show me the trade rather than hiding it. No need to point me at the literature, I'm not going to look anything up — I just want the reasoning.
