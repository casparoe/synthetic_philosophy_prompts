You are an expert in classical Indian epistemology — in particular the pramāṇa debates between Nyāya and the Buddhist epistemologists of Dignāga's and Dharmakīrti's line — and you are also experienced in running adversarial collaborations between entrenched opponents. Draft the joint statement that a committed Naiyāyika, who accepts four knowledge sources (perception, *pratyakṣa*; inference, *anumāna*; comparison, *upamāna*; testimony, *śabda*), and a committed Buddhist epistemologist, who accepts only perception and inference, could both put their names to after a serious good-faith attempt.

Before drafting, spend a short paragraph thinking aloud about what each side can genuinely concede without betraying its own commitments, and about which of their disputes is substantive and which is about how to count. Then produce:

1. **Agreed claims**, numbered. Candidate starting points, but keep only those both can actually sign: that knowledge is individuated by its source or pedigree rather than by internal features of the belief; that memory is not itself a source, since its standing depends on the original cognition; that veridical cognition is the default and error the deviation requiring explanation; that a candidate source earns its place by yielding cognitions that support unhesitating, successful action; that inference requires a pervasion established by co-presence and co-absence (smoke and fire, in the kitchen and on the hill).

2. **The residual disagreements**, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording of its own view. I want two captured exactly: whether pramāṇas are individuated by their objects (the uniquely particular versus the general characteristic, hence exactly two) or by their distinctive causal routes to a cognition; and whether the case of learning a name from an informant — a forester tells me the cow-like animal is called a *gavaya*, and months later, on seeing one, I know this is what I was told about — is a distinct kind of knowledge-event or a blend of perception, recollection, and inference from similarity. Handle testimony the same way: is sentence meaning grasped as a whole rather than assembled from words, and does a reliable speaker generate knowledge or merely supply premises for an inference?

3. **Cruxes**: the sub-questions where settling the matter would actually move at least one side, with, for each, the case or argument both accept as bearing on it and each side's verdict recorded in advance. Address the asymmetry that the Naiyāyika cheerfully reduces postulation (*arthāpatti*) to inference while refusing to reduce comparison or testimony — make explicit what principle is supposed to license one reduction and forbid the others.

4. **A short closing section**: which part of the dispute is merely about counting, and what would count as evidence against each side's own view.
