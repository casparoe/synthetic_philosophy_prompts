**Generations: stipulated extensions, accreted intensions**

Social ontology tends to pose the question about generations as a choice. Either cohorts are real kinds, carved out by shared exposure to a stretch of history, or they are convenient fictions — marketing labels pasted onto an arbitrary slice of the birth registry. I want to argue that both answers are too crude, because both assume that a kind term is either discovered whole or invented whole. Generational terms are hybrids. Their extension is stipulated and their intension is accreted, and most of the confusion in this literature comes from the fact that a publicly stipulated extension lends unearned authority to an intension nobody ever verified.

Consider how the stipulation actually happens. In early 2019 the Pew Research Center announced that it had settled on 1996 as the last Millennial birth year: anyone born between 1981 and 1996 counts as a Millennial, anyone born from 1997 onward belongs to the next generation. Note the direction of fit. Pew does not report a boundary it located in the data; it fixes one so that a decade of prior analysis remains, in its own phrase, analytically meaningful, and because the oldest members of the cohort have by now moved well into adulthood. The institution is admirably candid about the epistemic status of the exercise: "Generational cutoff points aren't an exact science." And on the naming question, which is where a realist would expect evidence to appear, it says something stronger still: "While there is no scientific process for deciding when a name has stuck, the momentum is clearly behind Gen Z." That is a confession that the term's content is settled by uptake rather than by measurement.

There is one exception, and I think it proves the rule. Pew notes that the Baby Boom is "the only generation officially designated by the U.S. Census Bureau," and that it was designated on the basis of a surge in births after the war and a marked decline after 1964. So exactly one cohort has boundaries read off a genuine discontinuity — but the discontinuity is in fertility. The one generation carved at a joint is carved at a joint in demography, not in character, and yet it is precisely the generation most confidently assigned a psychology. The demographic kind was silently promoted to a psychological one, and every subsequent label has inherited the prestige of that promotion without inheriting the demography.

What accretes onto the stipulated extension afterwards comes from journalism, from marketing taxonomies, and — less innocently — from the survey instruments themselves, which increasingly ask respondents to accept the label as a premise. This makes generations what I want to call compulsory looping kinds. Like psychiatric or sexual kinds, they partly produce what they classify: people come to describe themselves in the category's vocabulary, and their self-descriptions then show up in the data that was supposed to test the category. But unlike most of the standard examples, membership is assigned whether or not the subject recognises herself in the description. Nobody opts into being a Boomer. That combination — self-fulfilling, non-voluntary, and stipulated rather than discovered — is rare among social kinds, and I think it explains both the remarkable durability of these labels and the remarkable poverty of their content.

The resources for limiting the damage are already in the tradition people cite least carefully. Mannheim's 1928 essay, available in English since 1952, distinguishes a "generational location" — mere contemporaneity of birth — from a "generation in actuality," a shared consciousness formed by a common response to events, and within the latter, competing "generational units." He insists that shared birth dates are not sufficient, that whether a location ever becomes an actuality depends on the tempo of social change, and that actual generations remain internally stratified by class, region and culture. Popular usage collapses all three levels and reports on locations as though they were actualities. So the anti-realist's stock evidence — that most named cohorts display no distinctive consciousness at all — is not an objection to Mannheim but a vindication of him. What his framework licenses is a conditional realism: a generation is real when it is real, we can usually only tell after the fact, and often the answer is no.

There is also a reason why we cannot tell beforehand, and this is the part of the argument I'm least sure of. In any dataset, age, period and cohort are linearly dependent: cohort is period minus age. A linear cohort trend is therefore indistinguishable from some combination of linear age and period trends, and separating them requires assumptions imported from outside the data. I don't want to overstate this, since demographers have principled workarounds for particular nonlinear structures, and since some cohort-shaped effects are genuine and nonlinear — the earnings scarring borne by people who entered the labour market during a deep downturn is the standard example. But the burden of proof matters. The decomposition a polling report uses is not the only admissible one, which means the "generational effect" it displays is partly a property of the decomposition and only partly a property of the world.

The upshot is that generations are real in something like the way a unit of measurement is real: arbitrary at the boundary, non-arbitrary in its consequences, and properly citable only with its standard attached. The reform I want to propose is small and deliberately dull. Require that any generation label travel with its stipulation — Millennial (Pew, 1981–1996, designated 2019) — the way a laboratory result travels with its units. My prediction is that most of the claims people want to make with these terms would then turn out to be trivially true, or to be claims made under a rival stipulation, or to be claims about nothing in particular. A small residue would survive. That residue is what generations are.
