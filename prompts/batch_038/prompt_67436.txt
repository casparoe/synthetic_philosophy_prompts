I am a novelist in the fourth year of a book I have come to think of as a triptych about work, and I have a figure of speech problem that I would like audited rather than praised.

The three strands: a Nonconformist mill owner's daughter in the 1840s who cannot determine whether her exhaustion is a token of election or a symptom of her father's avarice; a clerk in a French regional health insurance office in the months before the thirty-five-hour week took effect, whose drama consists almost entirely of paperwork and of arguing over what a person is owed; and a contemporary Tokyo strand narrated by an occupational-health physician who keeps a spreadsheet of the dead and who wears one of those watches that report a single readiness figure each morning, as though she were a device with a charge level.

Everyone around this manuscript reaches for the same analogy, and I now suspect it has done more damage to my thinking than any of my characters has. The analogy is this: work discharges a battery, leisure recharges it. My research consultant — a man who advises Japanese firms in overwork litigation — deploys it to make a claim I want to resist. His argument, roughly, is that the death at the centre of my third strand is not a moral event but a technical one: a cell run flat because the charging schedule was wrong. On that account the French strand is doing the same thing at the scale of statute, since the thirty-five-hour week proposes no new idea about what work is for and merely tunes the charger; and the nineteenth-century strand is a superstition that happened to produce the same effect as a bad charging regime, a Calvinist who counts idleness as sin being a person who has forbidden himself to plug himself in. He says this is precisely why the figure is useful to me: it collapses three centuries into one engineering problem, and one engineering problem is a plot.

First, then, please give me the correspondence explicitly and without flattery. What plays the battery, what plays the charge, what is the circuit, what is depth of discharge and what is cycle life, and who is the manufacturer that sets the warranty? Where a term has no counterpart in the target — where I would be inventing the mapping to keep the figure tidy — say so plainly.

Second, tell me where the mapping fails, and, more importantly, sort those failures into two piles: the ones that are load-bearing for my consultant's conclusion, and the ones that are merely decorative inconvenience. I have my own list of suspected imports, and I should like it corrected, extended, or dismissed. Does the battery picture make rest intelligible only as the enabling condition of further work, so that leisure can never be its own justification? Does it assume that a person can be restored to a previous value rather than merely continued from a damaged one — that there is a charge level to return to? Does it treat depletion as a single measurable quantity when it may be several incommensurable ones: attention, appetite, the willingness to be bored, the capacity to be spoken to by someone one loves? Is energy a store at all, or is it closer to a flow, a skill, a relation, or a standing offer that lapses if unaccepted? And does the figure carry an ideological direction that survives its adoption by people who despise overwork — is the battery already a Protestant picture with the theology drained out and the discipline left standing, complete with a secular sin in the form of an unproductive hour?

One further wrinkle, from my own notes, which I think the analogy cannot absorb. I have been distinguishing between what I call recharge scenes — sleep, a weekend, a convalescence, anything whose function within the chapter is to restore capacity — and, badly, dead-air scenes: a character watching a canal, waiting in a queue, mending a thing for no reason, sitting through a service in which she no longer believes. Nothing in the second category is restorative on anyone's account, and yet the second category is where most of my affection for these people lives. A figure that has no place for dead air, or that must classify it as a leak, has already told me something about what it is for. Please tell me whether that is right, and whether it is a fatal objection or only an awkwardness.

Finally, if the consultant's conclusion does not survive the audit, what should I build on instead? I do not want a rival metaphor chosen for beauty, and I do not want the counsel of vagueness — that work is complex, that persons are not machines. I need a figure capable of carrying a chapter, and one that can simultaneously make sense of three things: a death that every character in the book agrees happened at a desk; a statute whose own drafters defended it partly on grounds of productivity, so that the reform borrowed the language of the thing it was reforming; and a woman in 1846 who experiences her fatigue as evidence of her virtue rather than as a cost of it. Offer me two or three candidates — a debt, a muscle, a tide, a leasehold, a garden gone to seed, a language half-forgotten, whatever you think earns its place — and for each say what it makes visible, what it conceals, whether it can accommodate dead air, and which of the three strands it would distort. Then commit: tell me which one you would use for the Tokyo strand and why, and what the consultant's argument would become if he adopted it instead.

Please write the whole response in British English. I want argument and analysis only: no sources, no references, no suggested further reading, and no tour of the existing literature on work and leisure, all of which I have read and none of which has settled this. What I need is your reasoning about the figure itself and about its replacements.
