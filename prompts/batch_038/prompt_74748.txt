Just spent a couple hours arguing with a friend about insurance pricing and neither of us could pin down the actual point, so I'm bringing it to you.

The practice: in the EU, since the end of 2012, an insurer can't use your sex to set your premium or your payout. But the same insurer is free to price you on your age, your postcode, your occupation, your claims record. Those seperate people into groups at least as sharply as sex ever did — postcode probably sharper, and it tracks race and income pretty closely.

My friend says the ban is incoherent. If the worry is that you're being treated as a member of a statistical class rather than as yourself, you'd have to outlaw all of it, because nobody is ever charged as an individual — only as a member of whichever class the actuary happened to pick. I said there's a principled line and then couldn't say where it was.

So what does banning one rating factor and not the rest presuppose? Reconstruct the theory of fairness — or of what counts as "you" — that makes it coherent instead of arbitrary. Is the assumed wrong located in the classifying, in where the money ends up, or in the trait being unchosen? If unchosen is the test, why does age survive it? And does the practice need any particular view of what a probability attached to one person even means, given that they sit in a hundred overlapping reference classes at once?

Also tell me what's strictly required by the practice versus what just fits it comfortably — I suspect several theories license it, including one my friend would happily accept. And: if postcode and occupation soak up most of what sex used to explain, does the ban change anything at all? What would have to change for it to do real work?
