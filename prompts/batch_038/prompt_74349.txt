I am co-teaching an undergraduate seminar this spring with a colleague in classics, on the ethics of disagreement and compromise, and we have put Sophocles' Antigone in week four. My own work is on moral trade: the proposal that agents who hold different moral views can transact without either of them revising those views, each doing something the other's view counts in favor of in exchange for a forbearance or a payment that their own view counts in favor of, so that both come out ahead as measured in their own moral currency rather than in some common one. The stock illustration is a utilitarian and a deontologist who discover that a restraint or a contribution is worth much more to one of them than to the other, and trade accordingly. The pressure points that interest me are whether there is any exchange rate at all between genuinely incommensurable values, whether a duty that can be bought off has been honored or corrupted by the transaction, and whether opening such a marketplace invites extortion from anyone willing to threaten what the other side finds abhorrent. I want to run the play through this lens, and I want you to be as concrete as possible about where in the plot the lens actually bites.

Here is what I have in mind. Creon's decree and Antigone's burial are usually staged as a collision of irreconcilable commitments, yet the play is full of what look like offers, counter-offers, and refusals to bargain. Ismene declines to share the risk on the ground that success is impossible, and then offers to share the punishment once the act is done — an offer Antigone rejects in terms that read like a refusal to let someone claim a share in a good she did nothing to produce. Haemon urges his father to yield and reports that the city privately sides with Antigone, which comes across less as an appeal to principle than as an accounting of Creon's collapsing political capital. When Teiresias warns him, Creon's first response is to accuse the prophet of having been paid for his prophecy — that is, of having already traded the thing he claims to be transmitting. And late in the play Creon does reverse course, burying Polyneices and setting out to release Antigone from the tomb, and arrives too late. Please treat that sequence as a bargaining history and go through it stage by stage: what could Creon have conceded at each point that would have cost him less than the catastrophe, what could Antigone have offered that she would not have regarded as a betrayal of the burial itself, and which of the two — if either — is the party whose framework makes trade impossible rather than merely expensive. Note also Antigone's own justification for acting: her reasoning that she would not have done this for a husband or a child, since those could be replaced, but only for a brother, since her parents are dead and no other brother can be born. That looks to me like an explicit argument from non-substitutability to non-tradeability, and I want you to say whether it survives contact with the moral-trade framework or whether that framework has the resources to absorb it.

Then tell me where the reading breaks down and I should stop pressing it: which features of the play are distorted by treating Antigone and Creon as bargaining agents rather than as characters whose whole point is that they are not, and whether the play's divine and heroic register has any room at all for the idea of a market in moral goods. My colleague and I disagreed about this after our syllabus meeting and I would rather you took a side than surveyed the options. He thinks the play is a tragedy of bargaining failure — the mutually acceptable deal was available and everyone dies because two people would not take it. I think the play is a refutation of the very idea, showing that certain goods are the kind of thing that must not be priced, so that the counsel of flexibility offered by Haemon and Teiresias is the shallow position the tragedy exposes. Commit to one of those readings, or to a third you can defend better, and give your reasons. Two constraints on the response: please do not quote the play, since we are using a modern prose translation and I have no use for speeches reproduced from memory or for line numbers; and I do not want references to secondary literature or a suggested reading list, just your own account. Work from the structure of the scenes as I have described them, and where you think I have mischaracterized the play, say so and correct me rather than quietly accepting my framing. Close by saying what the dramatization can contribute to the debate over moral trade that an argument in a journal article could not — and be candid about how much of your verdict depends on a contested reading of Antigone's own motives.
