I sit on the five-student committee at my high school that administers a $4,000 college award left by an alumna. Her letter directs us to choose the student whose circumstances most limited what they could do, and who nonetheless made the most of what they had. Our two finalists are as follows. A works twenty-five hours a week in her family's restaurant, transferred here from a school that offered no advanced courses, and finished with a 3.6 while also looking after a younger brother. B turned down a summer research placement to build a tutoring app with two friends; the app failed, his grades fell to a 2.9, and he has since recovered to a 3.5. Three of us think the award clearly goes to A, because B's setback was a bet he chose to make. Two think that charging a sixteen-year-old for a gamble that did not pay off is the harsher and less faithful reading of what the donor meant, since her letter says nothing about fault. Our bylaws permit splitting the award, and one member has proposed doing exactly that.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — one finalist, or a specific split — and give reasons the minority can actually answer rather than restate. Treat the strongest version of the losing side's case honestly. And if splitting would resolve nothing and merely record our disagreement, say so plainly.
