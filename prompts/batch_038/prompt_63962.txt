I teach a 100-minute upper-level political theory seminar, 18 students, third year. They've just finished a unit on democratic legitimacy and nearly all of them think "who gets to vote?" is a settled question with an obvious answer — you enfranchise the members, and then you get on with the interesting arguments. I want an in-class activity that knocks that assumption over from the inside, before anyone in the room says the words "boundary problem" or "the demos" out loud.

What I want them to walk out seeing, in order of importance:

1. That every criterion for who's inside either blows the constituency up to the point of paralysis or leaves out people who are plainly on the receiving end of the rules. I want them to feel the blow-up as paralysis, not as a joke — there's a real difference between "ha ha, everyone on earth gets a vote" and actually trying to run a decision procedure with a constituency of four million for a town of nine thousand and discovering nobody can win anything.

2. That whoever settles the boundary can't themselves have been chosen democratically without already assuming a boundary. Honestly not sure this one lands — undergrads have seen infinite regresses before and mostly shrug. If you think it can be made to land, build something for it; if you think it can't, say so and let the activity carry the other two points instead.

3. That in practice boundaries get fixed by conquest, treaty, inheritance and administrative accident, and then the fixed boundary does the legitimizing work retroactively. This is the one I most want them to be ambushed by.

My half-formed scenario, improve it or bin it: a mid-sized town has to decide whether to expand a wastewater plant sitting right on its border, which discharges into a river running through two other municipalities downstream. Cast of categories I want in the room: year-round residents, people who commute in to work but live elsewhere, non-resident property owners, some long-term residents with no paperwork, seasonal workers, minors, people who'll move there in fifteen years, the plant's corporate operator, and the downstream townsfolk. Not every student needs a named individual — several can hold a whole category.

Two mechanics I'm attached to, tell me if they're bad:

- Partway through, the group learns the plant is actually sited in the neighbouring jurisdiction, not theirs. Everyone who spent twenty minutes arguing about whom to enfranchise discovers they were ratifying somebody else's boundary the whole time. I want the flip-chart record of the room's own moves to make this undeniable in the debrief rather than me just asserting it.
- A seperate round where they have to decide the rule for who decides the rule. I expect some groups will just appoint a chair and move on, which is... actually the point, but I need a lever so that isn't an easy out.

Also please don't let it become a morality play where the obviously correct move is to include everyone. I want at least one role whose genuine interest lies in a smaller demos and who can defend that without being a cartoon. And I'm tempted by a card for someone representing a non-human or not-yet-existing interest, who gets to speak but not to vote — worried it hijacks the session into a future-generations debate. Your call, but say why.

Deliverables: full rules; role cards written out so I can photocopy them onto index cards; a payoff or incentive scheme with no real money that makes exclusion actually sting; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes including the debrief; the debrief itself, mapping specific moments in the room onto the three points above; at least one escalation lever for a group that resolves the second-order round too fast; and a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each thing in the moment.

On stakes: I want the game's outcome to control something real about the rest of the course — which of two contested topics gets the final seminar slot, say. But tell me how to make exclusion sting without cutting a student out of a course decision they have a legitimate claim to be part of, and without anything touching grades. That tension is the bit I havent solved.

Last thing: materials have to be index cards, a whiteboard, a paper register and one handout. No apps, no printed packets, nothing that takes me more than an hour to prep.
