I retired four years ago after a career spent running numerical models of geophysical fluid dynamics, and I have since been reading philosophy of mathematics as an amateur — largely to find out whether all those partial differential equations I manipulated for forty years were about anything. The most recent item I read was a chapter in a trade-press introduction to the subject, whose author is a professional philosopher writing for a general audience. It summarizes Benacerraf's argument as follows:

"Benacerraf's earlier work had already shown that numbers cannot be sets: equally adequate set-theoretic constructions identify the number three with different objects, so arithmetic, read literally, cannot be true. His more famous 1973 paper turns this into a general dilemma for mathematical realism. Take the first horn. If numbers are genuine objects, they must be abstract entities lying outside space and time. But we can know about a thing only by observing it, and abstract objects cannot be observed even in principle — there is no experiment that could reveal a number. So platonism makes mathematical knowledge impossible. Take the second horn. The rival accounts that do make such knowledge easy — the formalist and constructivist views on which mathematics describes what we can build out of symbols — cannot preserve the ordinary meaning of mathematical statements: on them a sentence of arithmetic is either false or about something other than numbers. Both horns are unavoidable, so realism about mathematics is refuted and the only coherent option remaining is to treat mathematics as an indispensable fiction. That is roughly the conclusion Benacerraf endorsed, and it has become the majority view among philosophers of mathematics."

My instinct is that this is mostly sound but that something has slipped in the middle, and I cannot locate it. I would be grateful for a careful audit rather than a general verdict.

1. Work through the passage sentence by sentence, sorting each claim into three buckets: accurate; a defensible simplification that costs nothing important; and a distortion. Where you identify a distortion, say precisely what has been substituted for what — which concept has been swapped, which modest thesis has been inflated, which live disagreement has been presented as settled — and then say how the correction changes what the argument is entitled to conclude.

2. Restate the argument in your own words, in a form that a defender of mathematical platonism would accept as a fair statement of the problem she faces. If you think no such fair statement exists, say so and explain why the problem cannot be stated without begging a question against her.

3. I have seen this described both as a dilemma and as a tension between two desiderata that an account of mathematical truth must satisfy — one semantic, one epistemological. Which framing is correct, and does the difference matter for how much work the argument can do? A genuine dilemma requires the alternatives to be jointly exhaustive, and I should like to know whether that condition is met here.

4. Does the fictionalist conclusion survive the corrections? Set out the main lines of response that remain available once the argument is stated properly, and tell me plainly whether the closing claim about the current state of philosophical opinion is right.

5. One further question, prompted by my own discipline. The first horn as written seems to me to prove far too much: on the same reasoning we could not know about electrons, or about the composition of the earth's core, since we observe none of them directly either. Does the corrected argument carry this consequence, or is there a principled distinction between causal isolation and unobservability that confines the trouble to mathematics? If there is such a distinction, state it and say what does the work; if the argument really does generalize, tell me that rather than softening it.

Please do not supply a bibliography or a reading list — I have no library access and want the reasoning, not the apparatus. But do flag the places in your reconstruction where the scholarly record is genuinely contested, or where you are working from general understanding rather than from a specific text, so that I know how much weight to put on each part.
