So I help run a small reading group on the philosophy of media — mixed bag, a couple of historians, some comms people, two of us from philosophy — and last week I ended up defending a position I'm not sure I can actually defend, so I want a proper map of what would count against it before I open my mouth again. The position is basically Kierkegaard's diagnosis of the press from the mid-1840s, the one he develops in his review of Two Ages and circles back to for the rest of his life: that the daily press doesn't address an already-existing public but manufactures one, that the public is an abstraction to which no actual collection of people corresponds and which no one can belong to or answer to, that it's generated by anonymous, reflective, endless commentary — chatter, roughly, in his vocabulary — which detaches what is said from anybody who could stand behind it or act on it, and that the upshot is a leveling process where concrete individuals get flattened into interchangeable units of opinion and the category of the single individual (someone who is a particular person, responsible, capable of commitment) is eroded. What I'd be defending is the strong structural version: this isn't about bad actors, bias, or ownership, it's about the form the medium takes, and that form is why the press has the kind of power it does.

Here's what I need. Take that as one position and tell me what would count against it, sorted from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, and for each defeater say what the world would have to look like for it to bite — don't just name objections. I especially want you to work through the ones coming from outside philosophy: if the expansion of print in the nineteenth century actually multiplied the number of people who could speak in their own name and be held to account; if anonymous pamphlet and newspaper culture produced durable political commitments and real institutional change rather then chatter; if the same leveling effects show up just as strongly in small face-to-face settings with no press at all — village gossip, professional cliques, court society — so that the press turns out not to be the distinctive engine; if the proliferation of niche and dissenting papers created counter-publics that resisted leveling from within the same medium. For each of those, say whether it would refute the thesis or only show it needs an extra premise about volume, speed, and anonymity, and whether it's a defeater a historian of the period could actually check or one that's only available in principle.

Then three more things. First: what does the position predict that its rivals don't? I have in mind the more optimistic print-sphere stories — newspapers as creating a space for reasoned argument among private people, or print as building mass solidarity and participation — and I want to know whether there's any observation that could come out differently on the two accounts, or whether they're just vocabularies. Second: pull the thesis apart. Which pieces are load-bearing? Can someone keep the abstraction claim (there's no such thing as the public) and give up leveling, or keep leveling and drop the claim that the press is what does it? If it's a bundle, say which member, if false, takes the rest down. Third: be honest about insulation. My friends' line is that the view is unfalsifiable, because anything I produce as a counterexample — a vigorous public debate, an accountable bylined journalist, a movement that changed the law — can be redescribed as more chatter and more leveling, and they add the biographical point that the diagnosis was worked out by a man who had just been mauled by a satirical paper and who then spent the last stretch of his life using newspapers and a little periodical of his own to run a furious campaign. Tell me whether that's a defeater or just a genetic story, whether the redescription worry is really fatal, and whether it matters that the thesis maybe was never meant as an empirical hypothesis but as an edifying or diagnostic category — and if that's the right way to read it, whether that rescues it from counterexamples or just drains it of content. Finally, say which of all this a sympathetic Kierkegaardian would themselves concede as counting against the view and which they'd wave off, and end with your own verdict on how exposed the thing really is. No references needed, and please don't invent citations — I just want the reasoning laid out, and feel free to tell me I should stop defending it.
