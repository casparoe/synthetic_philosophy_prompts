I'm the technical lead for a payment-processing service, and I need help settling an argument that has now consumed three weeks of design review.

Our customer contracts promise a service credit for any "outage." That word is currently defined nowhere in the agreement, and it is causing fights: last quarter we paid credits on two incidents where, in my judgment, nothing was genuinely down. So we are building an automated classifier that ingests per-minute metrics and labels each minute of the quarter as either outage or not-outage, and we bill credits off that label.

Here is where the review stalled. The classifier needs a rule of the form "more than N failed requests in a minute counts as an outage." A colleague of mine — the sharpest engineer I work with — argues that no such rule can be anything but arbitrary, and he gave what I believe is a valid argument. At peak we serve about 40,000 requests per minute, and our counter is an integer, so:

1. A minute with zero failed requests is definitely not an outage.
2. A minute in which all 40,000 requests fail, affecting every customer, is definitely an outage.
3. Between those two endpoints there is a finite list of 40,001 cases, each differing from its neighbor by exactly one failed request.
4. For any k on that list, if a minute with k failed requests is not an outage, then a minute with k+1 failed requests is not an outage either: one additional failure among forty thousand cannot be the thing that separates "fine" from "down."
5. So, by repeated application of (4) starting from (1), no number of failed requests makes a minute an outage — which contradicts (2).

He insists this is not the slippery-slope move our product manager keeps accusing him of. Nobody is claiming that choosing 900 will causally slide us down to 500; the claim is about adjacent cases, each of which is indistinguishable in the relevant respect from the one before it, and he thinks that is precisely why the argument is hard to dismiss. He also says we cannot repair it by picking N, because whatever N we choose, the same argument runs against N and N+1, so our choice of N is a decision we are dressing up as a discovery.

The team has three proposed ways out, and I cannot tell whether any of them escape the argument or merely conceal it.

(a) Drop the binary label in favor of a continuous severity score on the interval zero to one, computed as a smooth function of failure rate, with credits prorated against the score. My suspicion is that this relocates the problem rather than solving it: at some point the score has to be high enough that a credit is owed at all, and a one-thousandth of a point still cannot matter.

(b) Keep the binary label but refuse to fix N in advance. Rank all minutes by severity and let the on-call engineer adjudicate the borderline ones case by case, writing reasons into the postmortem. Two of us think this concedes that there is no fact of the matter and outsources it to one person's mood on a Tuesday night.

(c) Set N empirically: survey roughly 200 engineers at comparable companies, show each of them minute-by-minute traces, ask whether it was an outage, and set N at the median crossover point. Someone objected that this makes our contractual obligations depend on a contingent fact about how a population happens to use a word — and that if we ran the survey again next year and got a different median, the very same incident would retroactively change its billing status.

Three questions, then. First: is my colleague's argument sound, or is there a step that a non-philosopher like me is failing to see — is (4) actually true, and if it is not, why not, given that I cannot point to any specific k at which one more failure suddenly starts to matter? Second: do (a), (b), or (c) genuinely resolve the difficulty, or should we accept something more drastic — that this term cannot bear the weight a contract places on it, and that we should go back and negotiate a different formulation entirely? Third, and most concretely: if you were in my seat, what would you write into the design document on Monday morning?

I would like an actual verdict rather than a balanced tour of the options, since I have to defend whatever we choose in a room full of skeptical engineers. Please also tell me what would change your mind: which of the four premises you would be least surprised to learn is false, what consideration would make you reject my framing of the whole problem, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
