I spent thirty-six years as an analytical chemist, the last twenty of them responsible for method validation and quality assurance in a municipal environmental testing laboratory, and I retired eighteen months ago. Since then I have been reading epistemology with the seriousness of a person who finally has the hours for it, and I have become absorbed by a dispute that I suspect stands closer to my former working life than its practitioners appreciate: the argument over whether what is at stake for a person can affect whether that person knows something. As I understand the field, one camp holds that the word *knows* is sensitive to the practical and conversational setting of the attribution, so that the same subject in the same evidential position may be correctly described as knowing in a low-stakes setting and as not knowing in a high-stakes one. A second camp agrees that stakes matter but insists they matter metaphysically rather than semantically: the high-stakes subject genuinely fails to know, and the reason is that knowledge is the epistemic position one must occupy in order to be entitled to treat a proposition as a reason for action. A third camp, whose position is sometimes called purism or intellectualism, regards both moves as corrupting an ideal, holding that whether one knows depends only on how one's evidence bears on the truth. I have followed the standard thought experiments — the two people who mean to deposit paychecks on a Saturday, one of whom is told the bank will certainly be open and the other of whom is told it may have changed its hours; the case of the traveler whose connecting flight makes hearsay about a destination city either good enough or not good enough.

I would like you to render these positions in the vocabulary I actually think in, which is metrology and laboratory quality control. I am completely fluent there and only autodidactically fluent in philosophy, so please define any term of art you need in the sentence where you need it. The instruments I have in mind are these: the method detection limit as distinct from the reporting limit; expanded uncertainty at a coverage factor of two; calibration traceability to a primary standard; certified reference materials carrying assigned values that do not vary with who is asking; spike recovery and matrix effects; control charts and their acceptance criteria; hold times and chain of custody; and the persistent asymmetry between the cost of a false positive and the cost of a false negative in a compliance determination. Keep in view throughout a case drawn from my own bench work. A drinking-water sample returns lead at 14.8 micrograms per liter against a regulatory action level of 15.0, and the method's expanded uncertainty at that concentration is plus or minus three micrograms per liter. Whether my laboratory may say that the water complies is one question; whether the same number licenses the same statement when the client is a water system serving an elementary school, and a different statement when the client is a bottler contesting a shipment, is another. My working suspicion — which I want tested rather than flattered — is that the philosophers' disagreement is largely about where to draw the boundary of a technical term, that the boundary is drawn by the consequences of error, and that my profession settled the matter pragmatically decades ago by splitting a single measurement into a stakes-independent part (the value, its uncertainty, its traceability) and a stakes-dependent part (the limit against which it is judged and the decision rule for acting on it).

So, first, translate five claims one at a time: contextualism about knowledge attributions; pragmatic encroachment, including the principle linking knowledge to the permissibility of acting on a proposition; the norm governing assertion, on which one may assert only what one knows; purism; and the objection that stakes-sensitivity on either version severs the connection between knowledge and truth. For each, state what maps onto laboratory practice cleanly, what maps only if something is bent out of shape — saying precisely what you bent — and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Whenever you find yourself rendering *knows* as *reports*, or *acts on*, or *meets specification*, stop and say which rendering you chose and what the choice cost. Do not import metrology's presuppositions and present them as translations; if my framework simply has no room for a distinction the philosophers are drawing, that is a finding rather than a license to paper over it. Second, give me the residue report. Which claims left nothing behind, which left a residue, and for each residue: is it a defect in the metrological vocabulary — does it perhaps lack any notion of epistemic standing as distinct from a decision rule, or any place for the agent's own practical situation as against a limit imposed by a third-party regulator? — or is it evidence that the original philosophical claim was less determinate than it sounded? I want particular attention on two spots: whether the dispute about whether the word shifts or the state shifts has any analogue in a laboratory that reports numbers and lets others set limits; and whether the certified value of a reference material is a genuine counterpart to the purist's stakes-independent knowledge, or whether the resemblance is superficial because the certified value is a property of a material rather than a standing of a person. Please aim for roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words under labeled headings, with the residue report last, and with no bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind: I want your reasoning, not your references.
