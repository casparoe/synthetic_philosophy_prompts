I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about the reserved, non-transferable slice of paid parental leave — the portion of a couple's entitlement that the Nordic systems, starting in the early 1990s, allocate to each parent as an individual rather than to the household. The design details are what interest me. Those weeks cannot be handed to the other parent even when both parents would prefer that they be, and if the designated parent declines to take them they lapse entirely: the time off and the wage replacement attached to it are destroyed rather than reallocated to anyone, the child included. Where exceptions exist, they appear to be keyed to incapacity — illness, death, a single-parent household — rather than to the couple's own considered judgment about how best to divide the year.

What I find strange is that this sits inside welfare states that are otherwise conspicuously deferential to families' choices: they pay cash child allowances with no conditions on how the money is spent, they let parents choose freely among schools and childcare providers, and they generally treat the household as competent to allocate its own resources. In this one place the same governments would rather extinguish a benefit than let the household allocate it.

Please reconstruct the theory this design presupposes. Specifically: who is the rights-bearer — the parent, the child, the couple, or the polity's division of care labour — and which of those candidates can actually make sense of forfeiture rather than mere inalienability? What does the practice presuppose about the authority of a household's revealed preferences, and about whether preferences formed under an unequal division of labour can count as legitimate inputs to policy? Which rival views does it quietly rule out: a straightforward child-development welfarism on which total care is what matters and destroying weeks is therefore a straightforward loss, a liberal neutrality that refuses to rank family forms, or an economic view on which a transferable benefit will be allocated efficiently within the household? I would also like you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to say where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, where several incompatible theories would license exactly this arrangement. One comparison I keep coming back to: most entitlements we refuse to let people trade away are either duties, like jury service, or rights whose exercise is thought inseparable from the person exercising them, like the vote. Does the reserved quota place caregiving in that category, and what would have to be true of care work for that to be the right classification?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. If we took the child-outcome justification literally, would we make the weeks tradable, put a price on the transfer, or pay a bonus for an equal split — and what would each of those variants reveal about the commitment the current design is actually making? And is there any coherent account of value on which destroying the benefit, rather than reallocating it, is the rational way to express that commitment?
