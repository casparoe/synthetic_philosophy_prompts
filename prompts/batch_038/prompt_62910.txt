Write a short story of 1,200–1,800 words set in an isolated alpine valley where each farm keeps a signal fire on its ridge, and where the arrangement of the fires — how many, in what order, how far apart — has carried news of avalanche, birth, and death for four generations. A surveyor arrives from the lowlands with a code of her own that answers the same needs just as well.

The story should dramatize David Lewis's account of convention without using philosophical vocabulary: no character may say "convention," "coordination," "equilibrium," or "common knowledge," and Lewis must not be named. Three things have to be structurally present rather than stated. First, the valley code must be shown to be arbitrary — the surveyor's alternative serves everyone's purposes equally well, no one is punished for lighting the wrong fire, and yet the existing code holds because of precedent and because each farmer expects every other to keep it. Second, the code must persist after the need for it has largely disappeared (a telegraph line reaches the valley in the final third), and two characters must disagree about whether what survives is still a convention or has become something else: a test of who belongs. Third, include one night when the fires fail for a reason that is a mistake about expectations rather than a change of heart — someone who would gladly have lit, and who believed the others would too, but who believed the others believed she would not.

End without settling that disagreement.
