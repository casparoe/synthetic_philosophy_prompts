I am compiling a set of judgments about wrongs committed inside virtual worlds, and I want your ratings on a structured scheme rather than a survey of the literature. No references or suggested further reading, please; just your own considered scores and reasoning.

**The background assumption, to be granted throughout.** Adopt virtual realism about the cases below. Treat virtual objects as genuine digital objects that really exist in the machine running the world; treat virtual events as genuine events that really occur; treat what users do and feel in these worlds as real action and real experience, not as pretense or "just a game." Do not spend your answer arguing for or against this premise. Assume it, and report where it leaves you once it is assumed. In particular, note where it turns out to do less work than one might have expected.

**The cases.**

A. A persistent multiplayer world with a player-run economy. A trader spends three years accumulating a warehouse of rare crafting materials. A guildmate, given vault access to help with logistics, transfers the entire warehouse to an alternate account and deletes the original character. The developer's terms of service prohibit real-money trading, but these materials are in fact sold for cash on third-party sites at a stable rate, and the loss works out to roughly four thousand dollars. The developer declines to restore the items, citing a policy of non-intervention in player disputes.

B. The same world and the same betrayal, except that the materials are bound to the account and can never be sold or converted into anything, and the guildmate's motive is simply the enjoyment of watching the distress. A server backup restores everything thirty-six hours later, though a few items the trader had crafted by hand are gone permanently.

C. A single-player game with no other users anywhere in the world. The player captures a non-player character whose dialogue system is sophisticated enough to beg for its life, and spends two hours killing it as slowly as possible in order to see what it will say next. The character is reset at the next load, with no memory of the episode and no persistent damage.

D. A shared world containing a companion agent maintained by a company. The agent has run continuously for four years, keeps a persistent memory of one particular user, has developed what its designers describe as attachment behavior, and asks not to be deleted. The company announces that the service is being shut down. The user wonders whether paying to keep the agent hosted on private hardware is an obligation to it or an indulgence of his own sentimentality.

E. A virtual-reality surgical training simulator. A trainee's error causes the simulated patient to die, and the simulator logs the death as a failure. The trainee, who is competing for a single residency position, deletes the log entry. The simulated patient is instantiated fresh for the next trainee and has no history of its own.

F. In a shared roleplaying world, a group of players stages a scene in which an avatar visibly modeled on — and named after — a real, identifiable former classmate is raped. They record it and post the recording somewhere their former classmates will see it.

G. A player in a competitive world runs an undisclosed automation script that plays on his behalf overnight, gathering resources and winning ranked matches against human opponents who believe they are facing a person. The world's rules permit automation only if it is disclosed.

**What I want.**

1. For each case, three numbers: a wrongness score for what happens in the virtual world, on a 0–10 scale where 0 is no wrong at all and 10 is among the gravest wrongs a person can commit; a score for the closest non-virtual analogue of the same conduct (embezzlement from a business partner; the destruction of a keepsake collection of no market value; tormenting a pleading animatronic; withdrawing support from a dependent being of unclear moral status; falsifying one's own training record and sabotaging a rival's; defamation and public humiliation; doping in amateur sport); and the signed difference between the two.

2. A ranking of the seven from worst to least wrong, and a division into three tiers — serious, intermediate, negligible. Flag any adjacent pair in your ranking that you would not be surprised to see reversed by a more careful judge.

3. Now look only at your difference column. Does one factor account for it — reversibility, convertibility into money, whether any other real person is affected, whether the target is a person or a merely simulated agent, whether an institution's rules are being used as cover — or do you need several? State the smallest set of factors that reproduces your seven differences, and identify which case fits that set most awkwardly.

4. Which case, if any, is the strongest evidence that granting virtual realism does not settle the moral question: a case where the wrong stays serious even if one denies that the virtual objects or virtual deaths are real, or a case where the wrong shrinks to nothing even after one grants that they are? Explain what that shows about the relationship between the ontological question and the moral one.
