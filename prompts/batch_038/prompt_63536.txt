Here's the pattern as I've got it. A woman in her early sixties signs the paperwork: if she ever reaches advanced dementia, no antibiotics for pneumonia, no tube feeding, don't resuscitate. Nobody asks her anything, becuase she isn't in a position to have wishes anyone counts; the paper does the talking instead, and someone who no longer exists gets to decide whether this person lives. And yet my friend says in practice a lot of clinicians quietly don't follow the paper when the patient is comfortable and seemingly content — they treat the pneumonia, they offer the ice cream, they let her enjoy the last stretch. The directive mostly bites when the person is visibly distressed, and then it's used as a brake. Meanwhile the same hospital takes a refusing patient's refusal completely seriously whenever a psychiatrist finds capacity, and capacity is assessed at the moment of decision, so the present self's spoken word is final there. Which would mean the earlier self is sovereign exactly when the later self would have had a bad time anyway, and gets overruled when the later self is doing fine — so maybe she isn't really ruling at all, she just holds a veto over suffering.
