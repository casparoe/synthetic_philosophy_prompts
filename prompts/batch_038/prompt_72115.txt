I am on an eighteen-month secondment with a small civil-society organisation advising a regional water utility and the district councils that oversee it, and we have been asked to produce a short integrity code before the end of the fiscal year. Before I write anything down, I want to find out what I actually believe, because my gut verdicts on the cases we have collected over the past year are not obviously consistent with one another, and my colleague on the secondment — a lawyer, who thinks this is all much simpler than I do — keeps telling me that I am just looking for a permission structure that will let me like the things I already like. Here are the verdicts, in roughly the order we encountered them.

A district engineer accepts, at the close of a project, a hand-woven rug from the women's association in the village whose borewell she connected: fine, and I would go so far as to say that refusing it would itself have been a kind of offence. The same engineer, six months earlier, accepted an all-expenses-paid week at a technical conference in a coastal resort, paid for by the pump supplier then bidding on the next tender: not fine, obviously. But the same supplier has been paying the conference fees of the utility's engineers for fifteen years, everyone knows it, the training is genuinely good, and nobody involved thinks it has changed a single decision — I still say not fine, though I notice I cannot explain why the longevity and transparency of the arrangement should not make it better rather than worse. A council chairperson steers a small maintenance contract to his brother-in-law's firm, at market price, the work is good, and the relationship is declared in the minutes: bad practice, I'd say, but not corruption. Exactly the same facts with a large pipeline contract instead of a small maintenance one: corruption. I am aware that scale is doing unexplained work here and I don't know whether that is a real feature of the concept or an inconsistency I should eliminate. A ward member spends his discretionary development fund almost entirely in the two wards that voted for him: not corruption, that is simply what being elected means. The same ward member does the same thing after promising before the election that he would do it: still not corruption, and arguably better, because now it is honest — which I recognise sits badly with any general condemnation of clientelism. A headteacher takes an envelope of cash from a parent whose child is next on the waiting list for the last available place: corruption, unambiguously, no hedging. A headteacher accepts a donated computer laboratory from the parents' association, whose chair happens to be a parent whose child then gets that last place: not corruption, because the benefit went to the school rather than to her — but this is the verdict I trust least, since everyone knows what the lab was for. A clinic doctor moves an old schoolfriend up the queue: not corruption. The same doctor moves up the local councillor who can get the clinic a generator: corruption. And a judge on a customary land tribunal receives, according to long-standing local practice, a token payment in kind from litigants on both sides: not corruption, and I would push back hard on anyone who said otherwise — yet the moment one side gives more than the other, or gives before the judgment rather than after it, I am ready to call it corruption, and I cannot say what does the work in that distinction.

My tentative principle, such as it is, runs something like this: an officeholder acts corruptly when she converts the impartial authority of her office into private advantage for a particular party, where "private" is meant to exclude benefits accruing to the community to which the officeholder is answerable. It fails on at least three of the cases above. The rug is a private advantage to the officeholder and yet seems permissible; the discretionary fund is an advantage to a community and yet looks like textbook clientelism to most people who write about this; the customary token is given to the judge personally and is nonetheless, I insist, not corruption. So I would like the principle tested and rebuilt rather than defended. Specifically: state one principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precisely enough that I could tell whether a new case falls under it, and go clause by clause saying which of my verdicts motivated each clause and what that clause does. Then tell me whether a single principle can deliver all of these verdicts or whether what I need is a two-part account — one part about the office and its norms, another about the transaction — and if it is two parts, say where the seam runs. Apply the result to cases I have not listed, and in particular to these two: the district engineer leaves the utility and takes a job at the pump supplier two years after her last tender decision, and a supermarket buyer accepts the same coastal-resort conference from a supplier, where there is no public office anywhere in the story but I still feel something has gone wrong, though perhaps not the same something. Finally, be straight with me about the cost. If a simple principle is available that gets most of my verdicts, name the ones it forces me to give up, and say whether the pairs I flagged — small contract versus large contract, envelope versus computer lab, token given after judgment versus token given before — are places where my intuitions are tracking a real distinction in the concept, or places where I am rationalising. I am less interested in what the code should say than in what I am committed to, and I would rather be told that two of these verdicts cannot both survive than be handed a baroque principle that saves everything at the price of saying nothing.
