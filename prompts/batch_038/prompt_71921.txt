I work as a software engineer, and I have no formal training in philosophy, so please forgive any clumsy terminology below. I have been reading around Herbert Simon's idea of satisficing — taking the first option that clears a bar you set in advance, rather than surveying the field and choosing the best — along with the related material on aspiration levels, on maximisers versus satisficers as temperaments, and on fast-and-frugal heuristics. What troubles me is that I appear to apply the idea inconsistently in my own decisions. Below are my verdicts on twelve cases, all of them real or close to real. I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. Choosing a message queue for a weekend project. I wrote down four requirements, tried the first product that met all four, and shipped. I never looked at the alternatives. Fine.
2. Choosing the datastore that our team's core service depends on. I spent six weeks benchmarking five candidates against a weighted scorecard and took the top scorer, even though the runner-up met every requirement we actually had. Also fine, and I would not have been comfortable applying procedure 1 here.
3. Hiring a mid-level engineer. We screened the first forty applications, interviewed until a candidate cleared the bar, and made an offer while the posting was still live and applications were still arriving. Fine.
4. Hiring a staff engineer using the identical procedure — interview until someone clears the bar, then stop. Not fine; the pipeline should have been run to exhaustion. I cannot say what changed beyond seniority and the cost of being wrong.
5. My mortgage. I accepted the first offer from my own bank that met my two criteria and signed the next day, when three or four phone calls would have surveyed the market. Not fine — that was simply careless.
6. Choosing a surgeon for an elective operation. I asked my GP whom she would send her own family to and accepted that name, without comparing outcome statistics or seeking a second opinion, though both were available to me cheaply. Fine. Structurally this looks like case 5 — high stakes, cheap search, no expertise of my own — yet my verdict is the opposite, and I want to be told plainly whether that is a prejudice to abandon or a distinction the principle can capture.
7. Choosing among three job offers, all still live, none under a deadline I could not have extended by asking. I accepted the first that cleared my bar. Not fine.
8. Choosing among three flats in a market where the landlord gives you until that evening and the flat is otherwise gone. I took the first that cleared my bar. Fine.
9. Flat-hunting, eighteen months later. Nothing had met my criteria, so I relaxed two of them and took a flat that missed both. Fine, and I think relaxing the bar was the right move rather than a failure of nerve.
10. Hiring, after six months without a suitable candidate. I have been tempted to relax the requirements and hire someone who misses two of them. Not fine. This feels like a dereliction, while case 9 felt like sensible adaptation, and the two look identical in structure.
11. Buying a mechanical keyboard. Five weeks of research, switch measurements, forum threads, still undecided. Not fine — the most trivial purchase on the list has absorbed the most deliberation, and I want the principle to explain why this is worse than case 5 despite stakes roughly a hundred times smaller.
12. Twelve years with the same text editor, never re-evaluated, though trialling the main alternative would have cost an afternoon and would very likely have shown it better in some respects. Fine. Note that I do not think my justification here is that search was costly; I think it is that the gap between the options was too small to alter anything downstream of the choice.

The case I keep going back and forth on is one level up. I stop deliberating when I judge that further thought would not change my answer — but that judgement is itself arrived at by deliberating, which seems to require a rule for when to stop applying the rule, and so on without end. I should like to know whether there is a non-circular stopping rule or whether the regress is benign in practice.
