Many US states and other electoral systems provide that a race ending in an exact tie may be settled by chance: officials place the candidates' names in a container and draw one, or flip a coin, and the loser is simply out. In one state legislative race a dead heat was resolved this way, with an election official pulling a film canister from a decorative bowl and thereby handing control of the chamber to one party. The same legal order, however, treats a hung jury as a breakdown rather than an occasion for a coin toss, and empowers courts to strike down administrative action precisely because it is arbitrary and capricious — because no reasoning connects the outcome to the facts.

What conception of choice is built into settling an office by lot? Distinguish what the practice strictly commits us to from what it merely sits comfortably with: does the tie show that there is no fact of the matter about which candidate is better, or only that a settled outcome is worth more than a correct one? Is the drawing itself a choice made without preference, or does it push preference up a level — we do prefer deciding to deadlocking, and prefer an impartial mechanism to an interested official's fiat? Then say whether the jury and administrative-law cases really are in tension with it, and describe what electoral procedure would look like under the rival assumption that a tie always signals reasons not yet found rather than reasons exhausted.
