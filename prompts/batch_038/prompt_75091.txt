Two years ago I left a philosophy department in the United States for a faculty of law in continental Europe. Within six months my brother, who has lived in the country I moved to since childhood, became the subject of a criminal complaint over a series of posts concerning a religious community. The complaint was eventually dropped, but it took fourteen months, and over that period the question of whether the state may classify speech as hateful ceased to be a seminar topic for me. I have spent the time since building an argument about the transatlantic divergence in hate-speech law that I must now present publicly, in a room where a fair number of people will hear it as motivated reasoning by an American outsider.

The thesis, stated as compactly as I can. The standard account is that Europe and the United States differ because they weigh liberty and dignity differently: European systems treat the equal standing of group members in public as an interest capable of outweighing expression, whereas the American system treats speech as answerable only to more speech. I want to argue that this is a rationalization rather than an explanation. My claim is that the operative variable is corrective capacity — the degree to which a system possesses an institution outside the ordinary political process that can reliably reverse the state's own classification of a given utterance. Where proportionality review and a supranational court supply that corrector, a system can afford to delegate line-drawing to legislators and prosecutors, because errors are recoverable. The categorical American rules about imminent lawless action and about content and viewpoint neutrality are, on my account, second-best substitutes for a review apparatus the United States lacks, not the expression of a deeper value commitment. Two predictions follow. First, if American courts reviewed speech restrictions in the Strasbourg manner, categorical protection would erode. Second, European hate-speech statutes are tolerable in large part because of the review layer that sits above them, so that transplanting such a statute into a system without that layer produces a different institution rather than the same institution under a different rule.

The audience is an interdisciplinary workshop on freedom of expression at my new faculty. It is mostly continental public lawyers and human-rights practitioners trained in proportionality analysis, subsidiarity, and the margin of appreciation, several of whom have pleaded in Strasbourg and regard dignity-based limits as settled law rather than as a contested position. There is one senior German scholar who works on militant democracy and who will resist any framing that treats post-war European speech law as a contingent institutional adaptation. There are two American First Amendment scholars in the room who will be broadly sympathetic to my conclusion but hostile to my reasoning, since they hold something like the value-based account I am attacking. There is a communications scholar working on platform moderation who cares about whether the argument scales to private intermediaries. And there are graduate students, at least one of whom will ask whether the whole frame ignores the asymmetry between who prosecutes and who is prosecuted.

Please draft my preparation sheet rather than a defense. Give me the eight or nine objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by a combination of likelihood and damage, each phrased in the vocabulary its raiser would use, with a two-minute reply and an explicit note on where that reply is softest. I want you to be concrete about the exposed flanks I can already see: that the Supreme Court is itself the corrector and reverses legislatures routinely, so my claim that the United States lacks one looks false unless I mean something narrower about a court correcting its own categorical rule; that a mid-century decision upholding a state group-libel statute has never been formally overruled, which cuts in both directions and which someone will use against me; that Strasbourg frequently defers to national authorities, so the corrector is weaker in practice than my argument requires; and that the empirical premise linking review capacity to doctrinal form may simply be unfalsifiable as I have stated it. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility with the proportionality people, which single reply has to land for the talk to survive the question period, and which objection I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it is the one about my brother, and I would rather have a prepared answer than a flinch.

Finally, an epistemic request: state how confident you are that these are the objections this room would actually raise, and single out the one prediction about which you are least sure. For each reply you offer, mark whether it rests on a conceptual claim I can defend from the armchair or on an empirical claim about how review institutions behave that I would be asserting without evidence.
