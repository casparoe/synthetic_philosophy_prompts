I'm in my third year of a political theory degree, and this term I'm taking a seminar on the boundaries of the franchise — who may vote, whether voting can be required, and how much a single vote may be worth. Our first assessed piece is a memo of about fifteen hundred words on a statute currently before the constitutional court of the jurisdiction we've been studying. The professor supplied the court's relevant track record in summary form only, and the ground rule for the memo is that we reason from those verdicts themselves, without importing commentary about the judges' politics or the country's history; she has said explicitly that the memo is graded on how well we map the space of possible rules rather than on whether our prediction turns out right. The verdicts so far: a law lowering the voting age to sixteen for municipal elections only was upheld; a law lowering it to sixteen for national parliamentary elections was struck down; compulsory turnout enforced by a purely nominal fine, with no other consequence attached to abstention, was upheld; compulsory turnout enforced by refusing to renew a non-voter's passport and by barring them from sitting civil-service examinations was struck down; the enfranchisement of non-citizen permanent residents in municipal elections after five years' residence was upheld; an extension of the national franchise to permanent residents with as little as one year's residence was struck down, in an opinion that referred both to the national franchise as constitutive of the political community and to the attenuated connection of much of that class; a rule allowing citizens who emigrate to keep voting nationally for fifteen years and then lose the vote was upheld in both of its directions; citizens born abroad who have never resided in the country were denied the national vote, and that denial was upheld; the disenfranchisement of adults under a court-appointed guardian was struck down; a proposal giving each parent of a minor child an additional half-vote was struck down; the blanket disenfranchisement of all prisoners serving custodial sentences was struck down, while a ten-year disenfranchisement of those convicted of buying or forging votes was upheld; and a facially neutral requirement that voters pass a civics knowledge test was struck down.

The pending statute does four severable things. First, it enfranchises in national elections any non-citizen who has held permanent residence for ten continuous years and who arrived in the country before turning eighteen. Second, it makes turnout compulsory for that class on exactly the same nominal-fine terms as for citizens. Third, it provides that the entitlement lapses automatically after five consecutive years of absence from the country. Fourth, it excludes from the class anyone who has ever claimed the exemption from national service that the country makes available to permanent residents.

The memo has to do three things, and I would like help thinking them through before I start drafting. First, identify the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all of the verdicts above; I suspect there are at least three serious contenders, one of which is not a single rule at all but a set of separate standards applied respectively to the boundary of the electorate, to the permissibility of compelling turnout, and to the weighting of votes. For each contender, say which precedent it has the hardest time with, and say whether the track record as a whole forces us to prefer a unitary principle over the patchwork. Second, predict how the court rules on each of the four elements — or on as many as you think are genuinely determinable — identifying in each case which earlier decision is the closest analog and where the analogy strains. My own instinct, which I cannot make rigorous, is that the first element turns on whether the earlier national-franchise ruling rested on citizenship as such or on how thin the ties of that particular class were; and I have no idea at all what the court's treatment of emigrants does to the third element, where five years' absence strips a non-citizen of the vote but fifteen years' absence only strips a citizen of it. Third, name the single further case — a hypothetical statute, not one already on the list — that would most sharply separate the two leading contenders, and explain what principle the court would be committing itself to if it upheld the pending statute in full, as against what different principle it would be adopting if it upheld the first element but struck the fourth. My study group spent ninety minutes on this on Tuesday and mostly talked past one another, so I would rather see the alternatives laid out cleanly and their divergences located than be handed a confident bottom line.
