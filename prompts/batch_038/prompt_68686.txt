**Question:** Explain Molina's division of God's knowledge into three logical moments, and assess whether middle knowledge succeeds in reconciling exhaustive divine foreknowledge with libertarian creaturely freedom.

**Student's answer:**

Molina was a Jesuit writing in the disputes with the Dominicans over grace, and his move was to say God's knowledge has three logical moments. Natural knowledge comes first: God knows all necessary truths and the whole range of logically possible worlds, and none of this depends on his will. Then there is middle knowledge, which is God's knowledge of what every free creature would freely do in any complete set of circumstances God might place them in. Finally free knowledge, which does depend on God's will and covers what he actually brings about, including the actual future.

The order is what matters. Middle knowledge is prior to God's decree, and that is the whole trick: God doesn't have to determine what I will do in order to know it, because the counterfactals about my free choices are already true before he decides anything. So middle knowledge is really God's foreknowledge of the actual future, just located before the decree instead of after it. This is why Molinism is supposed to give God a robust providence — he can select a world in which his purposes get accomplished through genuinely free choices — without collapsing into the determinism the Dominicans were accused of.

On the reconciliation itself, Molinism gets round the problem because God is timeless and sees every moment in one eternal present, so his knowing my choice isn't really earlier than my making it. And the counterfactuals aren't made true by God's decision; they were true anyway and God simply reads them off. But his choice of which circumstances to create is what makes it the case that I would freely choose as I do, which is why the choice is still mine and not his.

The main objection is the grounding objection, which Plantinga presses: if I am never in those circumstances there is nothing that makes the counterfactual true, so it isn't knowable even in principle. Awkwardly, Plantinga's own free will defence relies on this kind of counterfactual. Molinists reply that the truthmaker is just my free nature. This also seems to show that Molinism agrees with open theism that the future isn't fully settled, and that they differ mainly over how much control God can exercise.
