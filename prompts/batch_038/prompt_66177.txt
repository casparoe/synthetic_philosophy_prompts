I run content standards for a mid-size music streaming service and I need a piece of writing that makes an argument I'm not allowed to make in a normal memo. Backstory: for about a year my team has been fighting over where a track's meaning actually lives — is it the audio, or the title/artist/tags we bolt onto it, or the playlist and the app screen that tell you how to take the whole thing? Someone junior dug up an old three-level account I've become weirdly attached to: the inner message (the content itself, the music in the grooves), the outer message (what tells you how to decode it — language, format, the sleeve copy), and the frame message (the bit that just says "this is a message at all, decode me if you can"). The useful wrinkle is that which level carries the meaning depends on who's decoding: to a listener who doesn't speak the language the sleeve is the entire work, to a recommender algorithm the tags are the work and the audio is basically decoration, and if nobody can decode it the content might as well not be there.

Write me a parody of a corporate standards document — title it Metadata & Meaning: House Rules, rev. 7 — issued by a Head of Content Standards who is convinced one sufficiently precise rule can settle the question permanently.

Exactly five numbered rules, one or two sentences each, and nothing else: no preamble, no purpose statement, no sign-off, no glossary, no footnotes.

What I care about:
- deadpan the whole way. It should read like real compliance prose — shall/must/may, named job titles, escalation paths, version numbers — and it must never wink at the reader or explain the joke.
- the five rules get progressively worse. Each one exists to close a loophole the previous rule opened, and by rule five the document is requiring something flatly impossible.
- get the philosophy right. Someone who knows the frame/outer/inner distinction should be able to see the rules keep failing because the levels trade places depending on the decoder, not because anyone drafted them badly. No rule may actually solve the problem, and no rule may say the problem is unsolvable either.
- one rule has to deal with a track whose title is a description of the playlist it appears on, and one has to deal with who is permitted to edit metadata after release.
- don't name any real philosopher, book, or company, and don't cite anything.

Make it funny to someone who works in platform governance and still legible to someone who's never thought about any of this.
