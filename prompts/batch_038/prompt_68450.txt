I'm a junior, and I run the weekly meetings of my school's philosophy society. Next week's session is on what it actually means for a set of beliefs to hang together, and I've been asked to open it aloud, because the group is tired of definitions handed to them on paper. I'd like to open with a sonnet instead: fourteen lines, iambic pentameter as regular as you can manage, in the voice of a bookie at a racetrack who has just been listening to a bettor state his numbers out loud.

The bettor has said that his credence that the favorite wins is seven in ten, and his credence that she loses is five in ten. He hears nothing wrong with that. The bookie does.

Here's what I'd like the poem to do. The octave should be the two tickets being sold, each priced at exactly what the bettor's own numbers license him to pay for a ten-dollar return, so that the arithmetic is real and could be checked on paper by one of my classmates rather than decorative. The turn at line nine should be the moment the bookie adds them up and the reader realizes, at the same instant as the bettor, that the money taken in exceeds the money that can possibly go back out, whichever way the race runs. The sestet should stay inside that fact for a while instead of rushing to a moral. And the closing couplet should concede, without breaking character, the single thing that would let the bettor walk away whole: that the whole scheme assumes his degrees of belief are the sort of thing that shows up in what he'll pay for a ticket.

Four constraints. Don't use the words "Dutch book," "coherence," "probability," or "rational" anywhere in the poem; the numbers should do that naming, and my classmates should be able to reconstruct the argument from the poem alone. Don't let the sure loss be waved off as pocket change, since someone will say on the night that losing two dollars proves nothing about anybody's mind. I'm also aware of the nicety about whether he'd accept a ticket priced exactly at his indifference point or only one shaded a fraction in his favor; if you can carry that in the bookie's voice without turning the octave into a footnote, please do, but don't wreck the music for it. Finally, after the poem, add one short paragraph of plain prose, with no citations or names, saying exactly what the couplet's concession commits me to, and whether the argument survives if the bettor simply never bets at all.

What I care about most is that it reads as a poem first: a real voice, a track, some weather in it. Someone who has never heard of any of this should enjoy it, and someone who has should find nothing to correct.
