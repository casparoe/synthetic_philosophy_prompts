I'm on a tiny evals/red-teaming team at a startup building a math-solving agent, and there's this argument that keeps flaring up in our weekly and I want to walk in next time with something better than vibes. Setup: we auto-score a few thousand competition-style problems, and separately we've been logging the model's intermediate reasoning so a human can check whether the steps were actually sound. Our lead engineer thinks the step-level review is a waste of budget, and his line every single time is "a right answer is a right answer" — sometimes dressed up as "the ends justify the means, we're not grading its character." The person who set up the step review says that's exactly backwards: a model that lands the answer by fluke, by having memorized the problem, or by a shortcut that happens to hold on our distribution will fall apart the moment the distribution shifts, so you have to grade the process. Both of them insist their making an obvious point and I keep feeling like they aren't even disagreeing about the same thing — they're just sliding between several different claims that happen to share one sentence.

So: pretend you're a senior researcher who works on scalable oversight and reward design and who's also read enough philosophy not to be sloppy about it. Take "a right answer is a right answer," plus the "ends justify the means" gloss, and pull apart every distinct precise claim that line could be making. Brainstorm loosely first, then organize. What I want back is a numbered menu running from the near-tautological readings all the way up to ones that would be genuinely radical if somebody actually believed them, and for each entry: a one-line verdict tag (platitude / substantive but defensible / empirically contingent / straight-up false), and which of three kinds of claim it secretly is — a claim about what has value, a claim about what evidence a correct answer gives you about the model, or a claim about what's cheap or feasible to check. I suspect most of the air in our argument comes from people hopping between those three without noticing, so call that out wherever you see it happen.

Then the part I actually care about: say which of your readings, if true, would really license killing the step-level review, and which ones are fully compatible with the process person's objection going straight through untouched. Test them on two cases we've literally hit. Case A: the model writes out a bogus proof — a step that just doesn't follow — but lands on the correct final answer anyway, and it does this reliably across a whole family of similar problems. Case B: every step is correct and well-justified except a sign slip in the last line of arithmetic, so the final answer is wrong. For each case, tell me which readings get the verdict right, which get it wrong, and which simply have nothing to say about it. Also flag anywhere that "right answer" is quietly ambiguous between right-on-this-instance and right-in-expectation-across-the-distribution, because I think that's doing hidden work too, and if two of your readings turn out to collapse into each other, merge them and say why. No citations, paper titles or reading lists please — I won't chase them and you'd only be guessing. Just the menu, the verdicts, and the case analysis. Think carefully, take your time, a long answer is fine as long as every entry is genuinely distinct from the others.
