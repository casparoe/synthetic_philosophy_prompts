I am a reliability engineer. For fifteen years I have built fault trees and event trees for process plants, and last month I had to defend a number in a licensing hearing — an annual frequency on the order of 1e-5 for a catastrophic release — when opposing counsel asked whether that figure described how often such events happen or merely what I was prepared to assert. I could not answer him cleanly, and I have since discovered that philosophers have been arguing about exactly this for a century.

Restate the debate between frequentist, subjectivist, and propensity interpretations of probability in the vocabulary I actually work in: component failure rates fitted from sparse field data, credibility weighting between plant-specific and industry-wide experience, the split between aleatory and epistemic uncertainty, common-cause failure, expert elicitation with its bias corrections, and the requirement that every number be traceable and auditable. Translate as faithfully as you can, and do not settle the debate by assuming that my practice has already settled it.

Then give me the residue report: which of the philosophers' claims survive intact, which survive only at the cost of distortion — say precisely where you had to bend the terms — and which have no expression in my framework at all. I want in particular to know whether the propensity interpretation does any real work for someone who talks, as I routinely do, about a component's true failure rate, and whether the residue exposes a genuine gap in engineering practice or reveals a philosophical disagreement that was less substantive than it looked.
