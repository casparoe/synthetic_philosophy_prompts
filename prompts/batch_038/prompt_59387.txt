I have argued about the legal regulation of sex work on and off for a decade, and I have finally written down my verdicts on concrete cases, mainly because I suspect the principle I think I hold does not actually generate them. Here they are.

1. A client books an independent indoor worker in Amsterdam — a jurisdiction where the general ban on brothels was lifted in 2000 — who sets her own rates, screens him, and can call the police if he turns violent. Permissible.
2. The same client, the same worker, the same evening, in a Swedish-model jurisdiction, where selling is not a crime but buying is, so the session has to happen somewhere neither of them would choose and she cannot report anything that goes wrong. Not permissible, and I want to say the client does something wrong, not merely something that raises her risk.
3. A woman takes indoor work over a warehouse job in a decriminalized jurisdiction because it pays three times as much and is safer than anything else she can get. Her choice looks autonomous to me and the purchase is permissible.
4. The same woman, with identical preferences and identical alternatives, in a jurisdiction where selling is criminal: her choice still looks autonomous to her, but now I think the purchase is not permissible.
5. An employer who knowingly hires a domestic worker paying off a recruitment debt, and a client who knowingly pays a woman working off a debt to a third party. Both impermissible — but I feel the second much more sharply, and nothing in my view explains the asymmetry.
6. A client in a criminalized setting who pays double because he knows she has had no other clients that week. Better than the going rate, and still not obviously permissible.

My guess is that the operative principle is marginal contribution: a purchase is wrong when it leaves the worker worse off against the relevant baseline, and criminalization shifts the baseline by adding detection risk, displacing work into unregulated settings, and severing her access to police and labour law. That principle has two consequences I dislike. First, it makes permissibility a function of how much wrongdoing is already happening around you, so a purchase becomes permissible once enough other people make one more marginal difference negligible — the saturation problem I have seen pressed against voting and carbon footprints. Second, it is silent on 3 and 5, where the wrongness seems to come from the quality of the worker's outside options rather than from what the transaction adds.

So: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, delivers these verdicts? State each precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me which of the six it forces me to abandon — I would rather lose 6 than 2, but say what that costs. Then give me verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a client who lobbies for decriminalization but only ever buys where it is criminal; a worker-owned cooperative in a legal regime that advertises that no third party takes a cut; and a client in a jurisdiction where buying is legal who pays for a cam session performed by a worker in one where it is not. Finally, say what would change your mind: which consideration would push you toward a different principle, or where you think the reasoning above is most likely to fail.
