I cover psychology and neuroscience for a general-interest magazine, which in practice means I spend my working days inside a particular vocabulary: appraisal components and their sequencing, core affect and its dimensions, constructivist accounts of emotional categories, prediction error and interoceptive inference, action tendencies and motivational systems — and, on the measurement side, skin conductance, fear-potentiated startle, pupil dilation, facial EMG, self-report scales, and the well-documented unreliability of each taken in isolation. I also handle the media-psychology instruments: narrative transportation, presence, identification, and the category researchers use for the feeling of being moved, which they treat as something other than sadness.

Your task is to take the paradox of fiction — the puzzle about how we can be moved by characters and events we know do not exist, in the three-premise form it usually takes: we feel genuine emotions toward fictional figures; we do not believe those figures or events exist; emotional response to an object requires belief in that object — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary above. Not an analogy, and not a survey of who said what. I want a working translation I could hand to an affective scientist who has never read the philosophy and who would not find it patronizing.

Begin with a short brainstorming pass before you commit to anything: list the candidate constructs in my vocabulary that could carry each of the three premises, and note for each where you expect it to strain or snap. Then do the translation premise by premise. For each premise give me (i) the closest empirical counterpart, (ii) what you had to bend in order to make it fit, and (iii) whether the resulting claim is testable within that framework, already tested, or not testable in principle. Take the standard philosophical responses — make-believe or pretence accounts, thought theories, simulation accounts, illusion theories, and the move that denies emotion requires belief, as in the work on recalcitrant emotions and on fast, non-doxastic appraisal — and treat them as positions to be translated rather than as a dispute to be adjudicated, saying for each whether my vocabulary can represent it at all or can only represent a caricature of it.

Then the part I actually care about: a residue report, sorted into three bins. Claims that translate cleanly. Claims that translate only with distortion, where you should name the distortion precisely rather than gesturing at it. And claims with no counterpart whatsoever in my framework. For that third bin, tell me whether the gap is a defect in the empirical vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked. Be explicit about two candidates I suspect will land there: the notion of an emotion being genuine rather than merely similar to genuine, and the normative notion of an emotion being warranted or appropriate by its object.

Keep the translation honest, and note that this cuts in both directions. Wherever a construct in my vocabulary already contains a philosophical answer — wherever treating emotion as a natural kind with a discoverable physiological signature, or treating appraisal as a computation over representations that may or may not be belief-like, quietly settles something the philosophers are still arguing about — flag it as such instead of presenting it as neutral translation. I would far rather be told that my framework is doing philosophy by stealth than be handed a tidy mapping that hides the smuggled premises.

Finally, give me a verdict on the question any reader will ask: if the belief premise is empirically false — if physiological and behavioural responses survive explicit, reported knowledge that nothing can harm you — has the paradox been solved, dissolved, or merely swapped for a different question? And identify the single premise that a well-designed study could genuinely put at risk, saying what such a study would have to control for.

Approach this as a philosopher of psychology who has spent real time inside an affective science lab and can move between both registers without condescension toward either. Think carefully before answering. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations: I want the concepts and the argument, not the literature. Aim for the length of a serious methods memo — long enough to be exact, short enough that every paragraph earns its place.
