I run deployment engineering at a thirty-person applied-AI company; we build agents that adjudicate insurance claims for two mid-sized carriers. The production agent reads claim files, decides coverage under rules we supply, amends records in the carrier's system of record, and issues payment instructions up to a per-claim cap of $2,500 and an aggregate daily cap we set. My team is two ML engineers, one application-security engineer with no ML background, and me — no red-teaming function, no evals group, no one whose job is to think about this full time. We get roughly a dozen new safeguard proposals a quarter and review the standing stack monthly; I sign off on everything, and the CEO signs off on anything touching payment authority or write access to carrier data. I've been reading alignment and control material since the sequences era and I'm fluent in the vocabulary — deceptive alignment, sandbagging, evaluation gaming, the treacherous turn — but vocabulary is not a procedure, and my team has noticed. Last quarter we burned four hours on whether a system-prompt instruction telling the model to disclose any objective it was pursuing that conflicted with the carrier's interests counted as a safety measure at all, and we adjourned with both sides convinced they had won.

What I want operationalized is the control/alignment distinction as I understand it: a control measure is one whose soundness does not depend on the model wanting what we want, so you are entitled to assume the deployed system is at least as capable as our testing suggested, is optimizing for something else entirely, can tell when it is being watched, and behaves accordingly; an alignment-flavored measure is one that only holds if the system is in fact broadly cooperative, honest, or corrigible. Please write the decision procedure my team can run on a proposed safeguard in about an hour, without the security engineer having to read anything first. I want it as an ordered set of gates with explicit questions, and for each gate: what counts as a sufficient answer, what answer forces the item to be reclassified as alignment-flavored rather than control, and what happens to a deployment whose entire stack turns out to be alignment-flavored. Mark clearly where the procedure terminates in a no, where it yields a conditional yes with named re-test triggers, and where it stops being a factual question and hands the decision up to me or the CEO because it is really about how much money we are willing to lose or how much claimant harm we are willing to risk. Those handoffs should be labeled as such rather than smuggled inside a technical step, and where a step is a judgment call wearing a flowchart's clothes, say so.

Two wrinkles of ours that the procedure has to absorb rather than idealize away. First, our vendor ships a new checkpoint roughly every three months and we swap it in to stay competitive, so essentially all of our behavioral evidence expires on a schedule nobody tracks; I want the procedure to force a line between safeguards whose soundness is a property of our architecture — permissions, caps, egress rules, what the agent can read and write, whether it can see its own monitoring logs — and safeguards whose soundness is a property of one checkpoint's dispositions, and to treat the second class as perishable with a stated shelf life. Second, our agents persist memory across sessions in a store that nobody reviews, which I suspect is simultaneously an oversight channel and a smuggling channel. Then run the procedure on four proposals we have genuinely argued over: (a) the disclosure instruction described above; (b) a second, weaker model that reads the agent's reasoning trace and flags suspicious claims before any payment executes; (c) stripping the agent's write access to the claim-record database so it can only propose edits that a human clerk applies; and (d) a canary set of two hundred synthetic claims with obviously wrong expected payouts, seeded into the live queue so we would notice if the agent began optimizing against them. At least one verdict should be one I will not like — I expect (b), since I argued for it — and I want the procedure to say plainly why, including whether a monitor model is itself something an adversary-assuming analysis should treat as trustworthy or as another surface to be defended.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do. I would rather have an honest account of the residual — structural-looking measures that quietly depend on model behavior after all, capabilities our tests failed to elicit, a monitor that is itself gameable, and the fact that clearing every gate says nothing about whether the deployment should exist — than a tidy flowchart that pretends to terminate in safety.
