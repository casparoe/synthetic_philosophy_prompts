I spent thirty-nine years designing feedback loops: beam-position monitors, slow correction coils, the whole unglamorous business of keeping a particle beam from drifting into its own vacuum pipe. Since retiring I have been reading my way, rather unsystematically, through the Scottish Enlightenment, and I have come to something that has unsettled me. Hume's economic essays — chiefly Of Money and Of Interest — describe what I recognize instantly as a control system. A disturbance enters (an inflow of specie); for a while the disturbance does useful work, because wages and contracts lag behind prices; and then a negative feedback drains the disturbance back out through trade until the money stock settles at a level set not by how much metal happens to be present but by the industry and skill of the country. He also has the striking image that money is not itself one of the wheels of commerce but the lubricant that lets the wheels turn, together with the claim that a magistrate should nonetheless want the stock of money to keep growing, purely for the sake of the transitional period. I want this rendered as fiction, not as an essay, because I suspect the story form is the only way to make the transitional period felt from the inside.

Please take on the role of a literary writer who also has a working command of eighteenth-century moral and political philosophy, and who has written economically literate fiction before. Think carefully before drafting: I would like you to begin with a short planning section of no more than two hundred words, in which you tell me which claim of Hume's each movement of the story is carrying, and where you think a story risks turning into a lecture. Then write the story itself.

The setting is mine, and I should like you to keep it exactly as strange as I have made it, because I want to strip out every piece of period vocabulary that would let a reader coast. A harbour town of some two thousand souls, deliberately unplaced in time and geography. Its circulating money is brass tokens struck by the owner of the tide mill and redeemable at the mill's store for flour, salt, and nails; the store also issues paper scrip against future deliveries, which the larger merchants pass among themselves at a small discount. Across the water lies a rival port: the town buys grain and dried fish there and sells it millstone grit and coarse wool, and this is the only channel by which tokens ever leave. Then a foreign trader wrecks on the bar; the salvage yields copper ingots and foreign coin, and the miller's mint melts and restricks it, so that the number of tokens in the town's pockets jumps by perhaps a third inside a fortnight.

Rotate among four points of view: the miller's widow, who lives on a ground-rent fixed in writing thirty years ago; a young shipwright paid in tokens by the week; the salvager, who is the first pair of hands to receive the new coin and the first to spend it; and the town's clockmaker, an engineer by temperament, who builds a tabletop model out of leaky buckets, a standpipe, and a siphon in order to explain what is happening to his neighbours — and who is wrong in a specific and instructive way, namely that he believes the buckets can be sealed. Give the clockmaker no more insight than a clever man with no economic vocabulary could have; his model should be seductive and should fail.

Structure the story in three movements: Arrival, Interval, and Levelling. The Interval must be by far the longest, since that is where Hume locates the entire benefit, and it is the part almost nobody remembers. Within it, show me the ordering of who receives the new tokens first and who last; show the widow's rent buying less bread while the paper she signed does not change; show the shipwright's pay rising late and by less; and show real, durable things being made during the interval that outlast the drain — a repaired breakwater, an additional loom in a weaver's garret, an apprenticeship fee paid up front in the year when coin was suddenly easy. Then in the Levelling, carry the tokens back out through the grain trade, so that the town ends with roughly the quantity of brass it started with, is genuinely richer in what it built, and has one household permanently impoverished — and let all three of those things stand in the same paragraph without any character drawing the conclusion.

Constraints, which I ask you to treat as binding. No character may use words such as inflation, monetary policy, liquidity, price level, demand, or velocity; they must reason in their own idiom — dearness, plenty of coin, what a day's labour will fetch, whether the store's scrip is good this month. No authorial note, no footnote, no glossary, no bibliography, and no explanation after the story ends. No real historical persons and no named real books. Aim for between 1,800 and 2,500 words of story. One further request, since I came at this from engineering and may have made Hume neater and more mechanistic than he actually was: in your planning section, add a sentence or two telling me where you think my control-system reading over-systematizes him, or where I have misremembered a title or a claim. I would rather be corrected now than write the wrong thing into my own notes.
