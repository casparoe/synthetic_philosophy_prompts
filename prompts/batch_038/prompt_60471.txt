I spent thirty-five years doing low-temperature transport measurements, and philosophy has been a retirement occupation only since about four years ago. Reading the eliminative materialists on belief and desire, I found myself persuaded — though not by the arguments I expected. What moved me was not the global claim that folk psychology is a bad theory, but a narrower thesis that I now hold: that attitude attribution is an instrument of the same status as assigning a body a single well-defined centre of mass, or speaking of the gene for a trait — indispensable for navigation and prediction, ontologically empty. On this view a mature science of behaviour will not reduce beliefs and desires to neural states; it will cease to posit them, much as chemistry ceased to posit caloric and did not identify caloric with anything.

I should like to know what would count against this thesis. Please treat it as a hypothesis with content rather than a slogan, and address at least the following, saying for each whether you judge it fatal, serious, or merely awkward:

(a) Double dissociations in lesion and imaging work — cases where patients fail one task that seems to require belief attribution while retaining another. Is the existence of dissociable, causally efficacious states tracked by folk-psychological vocabulary a confirmation of that vocabulary's causal taxonomy, or is it precisely what an eliminativist should expect of a crude instrument?

(b) Interpretability results on trained networks, in which internal states are identified that appear to compose and to recombine in inference-like ways when the inputs are varied. If such states multiply and are reliably described in contentful terms by mechanists who have no stake in folk psychology, does that constitute the reduction I deny is coming?

(c) Representation theorems: that any system satisfying certain coherence constraints must be describable as maximising expected utility relative to a probability function. Does a result of that form carry ontological weight against me, or is it the sort of thing an instrumentalist should welcome?

(d) The analogies themselves. Caloric, phlogiston and vital force were positive posits inside theories with independent explanatory structure. If belief attribution is better compared to a coordinate system or a choice of gauge than to a hypothesis, what follows for my view?

Beyond these: what does my thesis predict that its rivals do not, and has any part of that already gone wrong? And I want a candid assessment of a suspicion I have. Whenever someone reports that attitude attribution predicts behaviour well, I reply that predictive adequacy was never in dispute — caloric accounted for a great deal too. If both the apparent confirmations and the apparent disconfirmations are absorbed that way, is the position unfalsifiable? If so, is that a defect here, or is it tolerable, given that the idealisations I worked with for decades were insulated in not dissimilar fashion? Finally, tell me which of these a committed eliminativist would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would wave off: I want the exposure mapped from inside the view, not only from its critics.
