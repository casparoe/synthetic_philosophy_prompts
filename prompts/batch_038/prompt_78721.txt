I've been following the preregistration/replication-crisis arguments since they blew up, and there's one institutional detail that keeps nagging at me that I don't think I've seen anyone on the usual blogs actually sit down with. Let me describe the practice concretely so we're talking about the same thing. In the Registered Reports format — now used by a bunch of journals in psych, biomed, neuroscience, including some Nature-branded ones — you submit your intro, hypotheses, methods and analysis plan *before you have any data*. Reviewers judge the question and the design, and if it passes you get "in-principle acceptance," you register the approved protocol in a public repository, and then you go run the study. At Stage 2 the reviewers are told explicitly that their decision must not depend on how important, novel or conclusive the results turned out to be; they check that you actually followed the approved protocol, look at anything you flagged as exploratory, and the paper is published regardless of outcome or statistical significance. Deviations have to be cleared with the editor in advance. So basically the entire evaluative weight sits on a document written before the world had a chance to talk back, and the data get demoted to something like a compliance audit.

Here's the part that bugs me. The same fields, and often the same funders, run high-risk/high-reward programs that tell applicants to propose things likely to fail, and the prestige system hands out its biggest prizes for overturning a framework. Meanwhile I read Kuhn in college and mostly wrote him off as someone who made "paradigm" do way too much work — but this setup makes his story look weirdly load-bearing again. If a Stage 1 protocol can only be written by someone who already knows what counts as a testable prediction in the field, doesn't the format presuppose that the framework is fixed, shared, and stable enough that the community can agree in advance on what would count as confirming or disconfirming an effect? It seems to presuppose that the interesting failures are failures of execution and inference — p-hacking, HARKing, selective reporting, underpowered designs — rather than failures of the concepts themselves. And then there's this puzzle I can't resolve: preregistration is supposed to be manufacturing clean, documented, unspinnable failed predictions at industrial scale, which by Kuhn's account is exactly the fuel that's supposed to accumulate into a crisis. But nothing happens. The nulls get filed, everyone shrugs, the field keeps going, and nobody converts. My best guess is that a null result isn't an anomaly at all, because a directional hypothesis was never a guarantee the paradigm issued — the theory licensed a bet, not a promise — but if that's right then I'm not sure preregistration could ever produce a Kuhnian anomaly, and I don't know whether that's a feature or a sign the whole enterprise is quietly defining framework-level error out of existence.

So: what theory of scientific change and what theory of evidence does this practice actually presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit view rather than just listing philosophers — what has to be true about the relation between theory and observation for a frozen analysis plan to be the locus of all the epistemic work? Then sort out for me which parts the practice strictly requires versus which it just happens to fit comfortably with, because I suspect "the framework is stable" and "the framework is correct" are doing very different jobs here and I keep conflating them. Is the internal split coherent — Stage 1 gating on importance and novelty, Stage 2 formally blind to novelty and conclusiveness — or is the format holding two incompatible pictures of what makes research worth publishing? And finally, tell me what would have to change in the machinery if incommensurability were real: can you even write a Stage 1 protocol from inside a rival framework, given that you'd have to state your hypotheses in the vocabulary of the community that's going to review it? If the answer is no, is that a defect of the format or just proof that it's a normal-science instrument that was never meant to do anything else? No need for references or a reading list, I just want the argument worked through.
