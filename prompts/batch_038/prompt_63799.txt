I'm in a Tuesday-night reading group that's been working through early Chinese philosophy with nobody in the room actually able to read classical Chinese — two of us did a year or so of Mandarin in college, one's a linguistics PhD student, one's a backend dev, and I'm just the guy who brought the snacks. Last week we hit the White Horse chapter of the Gongsun Longzi using a free English rendering off the Chinese Text Project site (no idea who did the translation, and the chapter titles themselves are given in both simplified and traditional so I assume it's a house translation). Here's the opening exchange and then the bit about requesting a horse, quoted exactly as that page gives them:

"Horse" is how the shape is named; "white" is how the color is named. That which names color does not name shape. Thus I say: "a white horse is not a horse."

Requesting a horse, a brown or a black horse may arrive; requesting a white horse, a brown or a black horse will not arrive. By making a white horse the same as a horse, what is requested [in these two cases] is the same. If what is requested is the same, then a white horse is no different to a horse; if what is requested is no different, then how is it that in one case brown and black horses are acceptable, and in the other they are not? Acceptable and unacceptable are clearly in opposition to each other. Thus brown and black horses are also one in that one can reply that there is a horse, yet one cannot reply that there is a white horse. It is clear indeed that a white horse is not a horse.

What has me stuck is the first one. The premises only ever talk about "horse" and "white" seperately — nothing in them mentions the compound "white horse" at all — and then the conclusion just produces it. So there's clearly a missing premise, and I can't tell what it's supposed to be. Three people in the group gave three different reconstructions and I can't decide whether any of them is fair to the text or whether they're all just making it respectable: the dev says the compound term denotes the intersection of the two extensions, so the claim is that the intersection isn't identical to the horse-extension (a claim about referents, not about animals); I said it sounds like sense and reference, i.e. "white horse" and "horse" don't mean the same thing even though every white horse is a horse; and our linguist said neither of those is right, that the requesting-horse passage shows the point is about the satisfaction conditions of a request rather than about sets at all. Which of those actually fits the words on the page, and which ones quietly turn the argument into something trivially true and therefore pointless? Relatedly: is "that which names color does not name shape" a claim about what a term picks out, or about the *job* a term does — one translation I found renders the same spot as "A horse denotes a shape, white a colour. Describing a colour one does not describe a shape," which feels like a different thesis entirely, and I don't know if that difference matters or is just translator's choice.

Then there's the closing passage, which I find even harder: "White does not fix what is white, this can be put aside. 'White horse' speaks of white fixing what is white. That which fixes what is white is not white. 'Horse' does not pick or exclude color, thus a brown or a black horse can be brought. 'White horse' does pick or exclude color; brown and black horses are excluded by color, thus only a white horse can be brought." (The very last line of the chapter comes out on that page as "That which does not exclude is not that which does exclude," which I assume is a typo or a corruption.) My reading is that bare "white" is uncommitted about which particular white thing you mean, whereas inside "white horse" the whiteness gets pinned to a determinate instance, so the word has changed meaning by being combined — and our linguist says that's basically the intersective/non-intersective adjective thing from her semantics handout ("small elephant," "former mayor," "fake gun"). Is that a legitimate thing to see in the passage, or am I handing Gongsun Long a distinction he'd have no use for? A few more things I'd like help with, since I don't know where to look: does the advocate ever actually answer the objector's reductio in the middle of the chapter (if a horse with color isn't a horse, and there are no colorless horses, then there are no horses in the world)? Someone mentioned Van Norden's suggestion that the advocate is only arguing the sentence *could* be true on a certain reading, and someone else mentioned Feng Youlan reading it as a distinction between abstract and concrete terms — do either of those square with how the dialogue ends, and what about the passage where the objector says "Having white horses cannot be called having no horses, this is what is meant by the separation of white," which looks like the objector borrowing the advocate's own technical move? And finally, the old editorial preface attached to the edition we were reading says the whole point of the book was that names and titles had drifted out of line with actualities and the author wanted rulers to wake up and rectify names and realities. How does a dialogue whose conclusion is "a white horse is not a horse" serve *that* project — is the text performing the very mismatch it's complaining about, or did the School of Names just not care about consistency the way we assume they should?
