I work on a credit decisioning model at a UK fintech — nothing exotic, it scores applications for a small business loan product and we set a cutoff above which things go to a human underwriter instead of being auto-declined. At Thursday's design review our staff engineer put up a slide arguing that the whole fairness kerfuffle is really just the bias–variance tradeoff wearing a social label: there's a curve, you can't be at both ends of it, you pick a point that suits the business and you stop pretending there's a right answer hiding somewhere off the curve. His words were roughly "we've all accepted that you can't have low bias and low variance at the same time, and nobody calls that a moral crisis — this is the same shape of problem." He used it to close down a fairly heated thread: our policy person had been citing the impossibility results (the Kleinberg/Mullainathan/Raghavan one about calibration versus balanced error rates, and Chouldechova's on recidivism instruments) to argue that which criterion we satisfy is a decision about who bears the cost, not a hyperparameter. The staff engineer's response was that the impossibility results are just the formal version of the same tradeoff, so the debate is over nothing — tune the threshold, log it in the model card, move on. The context he didn't mention on the slide, which I think matters, is that our two reference groups have quite different observed default rates, and some of that gap is downstream of who we've historically been willing to lend to in the first place.

Here's the thing I can't quite articulate in a meeting: I know bias–variance cold, and the more I sit with the comparison the more it feels like it's doing work that it can't actually do. In bias–variance I understand what each term is, why it's there, and roughly what happens when I collect more data or loosen the model class — variance shrinks, bias might grow, and I can estimate where I am on the curve from the data itself. With the fairness thing I'm not sure any of that carries over, and I have a nagging feeling that "more data" and "a better model" do something completely different here than they do there. But I've got no philosophy training and I don't want to just assert that, because he's smart and he'll rightly ask me where the analogy actually fails rather than whether it feels right.

So: can you audit the comparison properly? I'd like an explicit mapping first — what in the fairness problem plays the role of bias, of variance, of the model class, of the sample size, of the loss function that makes both terms commensurable, and of "the point on the curve you pick" — and then the places the mapping runs out. Please separate the disanalogies that actually undermine his use of it (i.e. the ones that matter if the analogy is being used to say "this is a technical parameter, not a values question, and no one's position is worse than anyone else's") from the ones that are real but harmless, like the fact that fairness has several competing formal definitions and bias–variance arguably has several too. I also want to know what the source domain is quietly smuggling in that the target doesn't have — things like whether the two quantities are measured in the same currency, whether the tradeoff is a property of our estimator or of the world we're estimating, whether it's something you can locate empirically and move along, and whether there's such a thing as an optimal point given a loss. And then the payoff question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion survive in any form? Is there a weaker claim about tradeoffs he's still entitled to, and is the policy person's framing — that choosing which criterion to satisfy is a claim about who absorbs the error — the one that comes out intact? If the bias–variance analogy is the wrong picture, tell me what a better one would be, and say why it's better rather than just offering it. I know what calibration and equalised odds mean, so you can assume that and skip the definitions — I want the audit of the comparison, not a primer on fairness metrics.
