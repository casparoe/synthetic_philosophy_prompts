I'm the registrar on a mid-sized museum's collections and attribution committee — seven of us, we meet monthly, and basically our job is deciding what gets catologued as an original, what gets a qualifier in front of the artist's name, and what gets hung where. Mostly curators, plus me and a conservator. I've been keeping a log of our calls for a few years now and I've realised I couldn't tell you what rule we're actually running, and we've got a donation proposal coming up that I'm pretty sure the log doesn't settle. Can you work out what we'd have to decide to be consistent with ourselves?

Our calls so far, roughly in order:

1. A drawing that came in attributed to the master. Technical exam showed most of it was by his best pupil working under supervision, with maybe a dozen strokes added by the master, who then signed it. We went with "studio of," not an original by him, and hung it in the works-on-paper room under that label. Nobody thought this one was close.
2. Twelve posthumous bronze casts made from the sculptor's own plaster maquette, authorised in her will, numbered, all cast within two years of her death. We accepted them as originals by the artist, no "after," no qualifier, on the grounds that she'd set the process up herself.
3. A big fabricated wall piece where the artist designed everything and had industrial fabricators cut and weld it; she never laid a hand on it. She sent a signed statement saying it was hers. Catalogued as her work, full stop.
4. A very good 19th-century copy of a painting that burned in the 1940s. We hang it, but as "copy after," and we turned down a request to catalogue it under the original's title and date.
5. A confessed forgery of a Dutch 17th-century interior that had fooled our own curator for years. Kept it, hung it with a forgery label, and we specifically declined to say anything in the wall text about its artistic merits either way.
6. A print where the artist re-struck 30 impressions from her own plate 25 years on, the plate visibly worn. Originals by her, but dated to the later printing rather than to the plate's first use.
7. A painting with about 30% loss, filled and inpainted reversibly by our conservator. Still the work, hung as the work, label says restored. We rejected a proposal to credit the conservator as a co-maker.
8. A canvas so heavily repainted over the decades that the conservator estimated under 15% of the original surface survived. The artist, still living, wrote to us saying it was absolutely her painting and not to be treated as a copy. We kept her attribution but required the label to state the extent of the loss and overpainting.

New proposal, and because it's a donation we can't fob it off:

An artist painted a large canvas in 1998 for a collector. In 2003 it was slashed — four long cuts, one straight through the main figure. The collector handed the wreck back to her rather than claim on the insurance. She didn't restore it. She stretched a new canvas and painted the same composition again, same dimensions, working from her own preparatory drawing and photographs, not tracing; the handling is entirely hers, though she says she changed two things deliberately. About the second canvas she said something to the effect that this is the painting and the first one is finished, it's over. The donor is offering us both objects: the re-made canvas, and the slashed 1998 one, which is now maybe 40% intact with the cuts still visible and unstitched.

So we have to decide whether the 2003 canvas gets catalogued as *the* work — in which case what is the 1998 object, an earlier state of it, a document, something we accession but don't hang as art? — or whether it's a second, numerically distinct work by the same artist that happens to carry the same image, in which case what is the 1998 object and can we hang both. Plus what date goes on the label.

What I want from you: which of our eight precedents is the closest analog and exactly where the analogy breaks down; what the committee should decide if it wants to be consistent with its own record; and what implicit principle each of the two options would commit us to. Be specific about the principles, because my suspicion is that all eight calls are jointly compatible with at least three pretty different ones and I can't tell which we're actually operating with — one of them being something like "the artist gets to settle what her work is," which half the committee assumes and the other half thinks is exactly what's in question here, so don't just take it for granted either way. Then propose one further case, the kind of thing that could plausibly land on our agenda in the next year or two, that would show us which principle we've adopted, and say what each candidate principle predicts about it.

No bibliography and no theorist names needed, I just want the reasoning worked through properly.
