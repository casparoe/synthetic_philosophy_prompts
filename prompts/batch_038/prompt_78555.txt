Stuck on something for my dissertation and I could do with someone poking holes in it. I've built an argument that seems to prove there's no such thing as social explanation. I'm certain that's too strong, but I can't find the bad step, and my supervisor just said "look at the middle premises again", which isn't helping.

(1) Nothing happens in the social world except by way of what individual people do — firms don't strike, states don't invade, people do.
(2) So any true claim about a social entity is translatable, without loss, into claims about individuals: their actions, beliefs and dispositions.
(3) A claim that's just shorthand for other claims doesn't explain anything those claims don't already explain.
(4) So a social-level claim either translates, in which case it adds nothing, or it doesn't, in which case it's false.
(5) There is no genuinely social explanation.

The case I keep testing it on is a bank run: everyone withdraws because everyone believes the bank will fail, and thier withdrawing is what makes the belief true. Someone already told me to just point out that the same run could have happened with entirely different people filling the roles, so no individual-level description captures it — but that looks compatible with (2) to me, since "whoever occupies those roles" is still a claim about individuals, only a general one.

I'd much rather not give up (1), and I can't swallow (5). So where does it actually break, or if nothing does, which of (1)–(4) is cheapest to lose and what do I inherit once I've admitted that? And does the bank run help me here, or have I picked a case that quietly stacks the deck?
