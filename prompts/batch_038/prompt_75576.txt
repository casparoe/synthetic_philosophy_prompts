I spent thirty-eight years in analytical chemistry, the last twenty of them at a national metrology institute, where my work concerned certified reference materials and interlaboratory comparisons: the unglamorous business of making sure that a number produced in one building means the same thing as a number produced in another. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with something of a convert's zeal, and I have become preoccupied by the position, defended by Keith Frankish among others, that phenomenal consciousness is a kind of introspective illusion — that what presents itself to us as an ineffable, intrinsic feel is instead the output of a self-monitoring process that systematically misdescribes its own workings. Coming from a laboratory, I find this considerably less scandalous than most people do. Every instrument I ever owned came with a stated uncertainty; the difficulty with introspection is that it reports with complete confidence and no error bar at all. I should like you to write me a short story that works this idea through in concrete terms.

Here is the apparatus I propose, which you may adjust in detail but should preserve in spirit. The setting is a commercial sensory-testing house — the kind of firm that runs proficiency panels for flavour manufacturers, paint makers and textile dyers, and where a trained human nose or eye is treated, contractually, as a measuring device. The house has developed an implantable adapter, fitted at the senior panel chair's own request for reasons that should turn out to be partly vanity, which applies a fixed permutation to the mapping between wavelength and the subject's own avowals, while leaving everything observable from outside untouched: discrimination thresholds, forced-choice accuracy, colour naming, reaction times, all precisely as they were. Three weeks after activation the chair, a man in his early sixties, files an internal incident report stating that the redness has gone out of red — that he can still perform every part of his job and still describes samples exactly as before, but that the look of things has been drained away. The technical director, who narrates much of the story, must decide what to enter in the record, because an accreditation audit falls due the following month and the auditors will put a question she cannot answer: to what physical standard are the panel's difference judgements traceable? I should also like one invented empirical wrinkle of the sort that used to consume my working life. When panelists rate the magnitude of a difference between two samples on a bounded scale, their responses are normally distributed smoothly across the range; in this man's post-activation data a comb has appeared, in which pairs he once scored as middling are now scored alternately as no difference whatsoever and as the maximum the scale permits. Half the staff maintain this is an artefact of the rating procedure, a response bias induced by his loss of confidence; the other half maintain it is the only honest evidence anyone possesses. The story should keep both readings genuinely available, and should make plain that no further measurement the house could perform would settle which is correct.

On matters of form: between 1,800 and 2,500 words, interweaving third-person narration with the documentary material of the trade — a calibration log, the incident report, the transcript of an exit interview. Two prohibitions. First, the technical vocabulary of the philosophy of mind should not appear at all: no use of the words qualia, phenomenal, zombie, or hard problem. The dispute is to be conducted entirely in the idiom of auditors, panelists and instrument designers, so that a reader who has never encountered the literature can still feel the weight of what is at stake. Second, please do not quote or closely paraphrase any actual philosophical text, do not put words into the mouth of any philosopher living or dead, and do not append references; I am well aware that you cannot look such things up, and a confident invention dressed as a citation would spoil the whole enterprise for me. Invented instruments, procedures, standards documents and persons are warmly welcome, provided they are internally consistent and deployed as a working laboratory would deploy them.

One further note, which you may weigh as you see fit. I came to this literature shortly after my wife died, expecting to find it cold, and discovered instead that the illusionist line carries an odd consolatory force: if the vividness we take ourselves to be reporting was never the sort of thing that could be lost, then perhaps the loss I find myself reporting is not the sort of thing I take it to be either. I do not want a story that endorses that thought, nor one that refutes it. I want a story in which a competent and honest person must decide whether to sign the report, and in which the reader is left, afterwards, unable to say that the decision was obviously the right one.
