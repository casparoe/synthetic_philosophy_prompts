I write software for a living and have no formal philosophy training, so I would like a frank diagnosis rather than encouragement. I assembled the following argument after reading about the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I have come to think it proves much more than I can accept. I cannot locate the step that fails.

First, one definition. Call an observation *feasible* for us if obtaining it costs no more than a polynomial amount of computation in the size of the data we already have — generously read, so that it covers anything our species could run on any machine we could plausibly build.

1. A theory is confirmed by evidence only if that evidence discriminates it from rivals that would otherwise fit the same data.
2. Consider any theory T of some system, on which the system's behavior is given by a function f of its inputs.
3. Fix a large composite number N, and let T′ be the theory that says exactly what T says, except that when the input is a pair of factors of N the system behaves differently. (If you prefer, use any problem you think remains hard even for quantum computers; the choice does no work below.)
4. T and T′ agree on every feasible observation, since telling them apart requires exhibiting a factorization of N, which no feasible process can do.
5. T′ is not objectionable in the way contrived skeptical hypotheses are usually said to be: it is finitely statable, deterministic, general over its whole domain, and it posits no strange entities or physically impossible situations. It differs from T only on inputs we cannot reach.
6. Therefore no feasible evidence confirms T over T′; by (1), T is not confirmed.
7. N was arbitrary and the construction was mechanical, so the same holds for every theory of every system: nothing is confirmed.
8. The construction applies equally to the theories used in making it — including the claim that factoring N is infeasible, and the claim in (1) about what confirmation requires. So if the argument is sound, its own premises are unconfirmed.
9. Nor is this the old problem of induction dressed up in computer science. The barrier here is not logical or physical but computational, of exactly the kind my industry relies on daily, and it yields a rival we can write down explicitly and could never rule out.

I would rather keep (1) and (4) than anything else. The thought that confirmation has to be cashable in evidence we could actually obtain seems to me to be the only thing keeping the word from being empty, and (4) is arithmetic plus an assumption that a large share of the world's infrastructure already bets on. What I cannot accept is (6) through (8), and above all the self-undermining in (8).

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if your answer is that T′ is not a genuine rival, I want to know what makes a hypothesis a genuine rival rather than a merely consistent one, and whether that criterion is itself something we could ever check — otherwise I worry the fix only relocates the problem. And once the computational version is handled, is anything left of the general underdetermination worry, or does the same move dispose of that as well?
