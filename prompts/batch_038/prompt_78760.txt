I serve on the Governance and Elections Committee of a federation of fourteen scientific societies. Every few years we are asked to certify a decision procedure for some particular choice the federation has to make, and over the last decade we have accumulated a record of certifications that I now find I cannot summarize into any single policy. Our counsel briefed us once, at the outset, on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result: given three or more options and unrestricted strict ordinal rankings of them, any procedure that is capable of returning every option and under which honest ranking is always a member's best response must in effect hand the decision to one member. She told us this left only a handful of exits — restrict the shape the options or ballots may take, restrict which options the procedure may return, randomize, concentrate the decision, or live with manipulability — and that we should expect to choose among them afresh each time.

Here is our record, in chronological order, drawn from the minutes.

1. 2013. Choosing among six candidate slots for the annual plenary, we certified a procedure that ordered the slots from earliest to latest and returned the median of members' reported ideal slots, recording that along a single dimension of earliness no member could gain by shading a report.

2. 2015. Choosing among four commissioned designs for the federation's mark, all four having been judged acceptable by the jury, we certified random ballot: one juror drawn at random, that juror's top-ranked design adopted. We recorded that with four options and no natural ordering among them we could find no rule under which exaggerating one's estimate of a rival design failed to pay, whereas under a draw no juror's statement mattered more than another's and misstatement gained nothing. The unsuccessful designers were each commissioned for other work.

3. 2016. At the written request of a small member society, we certified, for a two-cycle trial, a procedure for electing its treasurer from three candidates under which one designated senior member's ballot alone determined the winner. The minutes record that this made any misstatement of preference by anyone else pointless, and that the arrangement would be reviewed. It was not renewed in 2020; the society reverted to plurality, and we noted that in the intervening elections no one had complained of manipulation.

4. 2018. Choosing among six submitted proposals for a biennial prize, where two of the six were near-duplicates and had split the support of the same reviewers, we certified an approval ballot. We recorded that a proposal's chances ought not to be damaged by the entry of a similar rival, that the ballot was nonetheless manipulable, and that the particular manipulation it invited — withholding approval from a close competitor — was at least visible in the returns and could be audited after the fact.

5. 2019. A certification vote of our own committee came out even. We resolved it by drawing lots, recording that the tie was genuine, that no further information could break it, and that any other resolution would have smuggled in a casting vote for the chair.

6. 2021. Choosing among three host cities for a recurring conference, we certified instant-runoff. In the post-election review two delegations acknowledged that they had ranked the strongest rival to their preferred city below its true standing, one of them having discussed the maneuver in advance. We found that the misreports had not altered the outcome but that the order of eliminations had been what made them attractive, and we replaced instant-runoff for this purpose with a Condorcet-consistent method that resolves cycles by discarding the weakest pairwise defeat last. We recorded that the replacement was still manipulable and that we were no longer pretending otherwise. No one raised the possibility of a draw.

7. 2023. Where a search committee had already narrowed a field of eleven candidates for the editorship of the federation's journal to two finalists, we certified plain majority between them, noting that with two options honesty is trivially a best response and that no voter could profit by exaggeration. Two members dissented, arguing that the manipulation had simply migrated to the shortlisting stage, where it was invisible.

8. 2024. Setting the federation's assessment rate from eleven graduated levels, we again certified the median-of-reported-ideals procedure. A member proposed instead that the level be drawn at random from the eleven, arguing that this was the only procedure on which shading could not pay. We rejected the proposal with little debate, recording that a randomly determined assessment would be indefensible to the societies that had to pay it.

The case now before us. The federation must choose one of three candidate sites for a shared instrument facility. The sites differ along two cross-cutting dimensions — travel burden for the member societies, and the quality of the host institution's existing infrastructure — and we have had it confirmed by our counsel that no linear ordering of the three sites renders all members' preferences single-peaked; a majority cycle is a live possibility rather than a curiosity. The three sites are the only ones that have cleared technical review, so no fourth option can be added. The bylaws require that the outcome be a function of the submitted ballots alone and that no member's ballot count for more than another's; they are silent on randomization and on whether we may specify a procedure that narrows the field before the membership votes. Two delegations have announced publicly that they will shade their rankings, and a third has said it will shade in response. One delegation has said that it will withdraw from the facility altogether if its least-favored site is chosen; two members of our committee take that as sincere and two take it as bargaining. We must certify the procedure before nominations close.

What I would like from you:

(a) Lay out the candidate general rules to which our record is jointly consistent. I have in mind at least: one on which strategy-proofness is treated as overriding and the theorem's exits are worked through in some order of preference; one on which lotteries and concentrated power are ruled out for decisions that bind members or allocate money, and manipulable rules are then ranked by other properties; one on which manipulation is read as a symptom of a badly structured choice set, to be repaired by restructuring rather than by changing the rule; and one on which clone- or spoiler-resistance is the operative value. State each precisely enough that it yields a determinate recommendation for every item in the record, and add any further rule that fits the record just as well — including, if you think it warranted, one that turns on giving up one of the theorem's hypotheses in our case rather than on any of the exits our counsel listed.

(b) For each candidate rule, say what we should certify for the facility site, and why. Where a rule's recommendation depends on a contested matter of fact in the case as I have described it, say which fact and what turns on it. I expect at least two of the rules to diverge on the recommendation itself.

(c) Give your best single guess as to what we will in fact certify, with your reasons for preferring it — including which items in the record carry the most evidential weight, which are compatible with every candidate rule and therefore carry almost none, and which of the eight you would be willing to discard as an artifact of a member society's local request rather than a federation norm.

(d) Identify the closest analog among the precedents and explain the respects in which the analogy holds and the respects in which it breaks.

(e) Name a further, hypothetical certification request — specific enough that we could put it on an agenda — that would come out differently under the candidate rules you take most seriously, so that we might settle our own disagreement by seeing how we react to it.

(f) If the record genuinely underdetermines this case, say plainly what principle we would be adopting with each of the available choices — that is, what we would have committed ourselves to the next time a comparable request arrives — rather than pretending that the precedents decide it.
