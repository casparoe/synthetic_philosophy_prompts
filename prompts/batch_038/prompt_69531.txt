I'm in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD working on the metaphysics of moral desert, and last month I sat in on a reading group run out of our religion department whose announced topic was whether any nontheistic cosmology can ground desert without a judge. I attended as a listener. Two members — Meera, a historian of South Asian religions, and Tobias, a moral philosopher from my own department — got into a sustained argument about karma, and I have not been able to referee it since. Below is my attempt to record their moves accurately and in order, without editorializing in either direction.

The case Meera put on the table: a village headman who, during a famine, keeps the communal grain store locked against a debtor's family and lets their children starve; in a later birth he exists as a child with a wasting condition that makes eating painful. Meera said the correspondence between deed and fruit is not decorative — it is what makes the fruit fitting rather than merely caused.

(1) Meera: karma is a genuine theory of desert precisely because it needs no one to allot anything. The fruit ripens out of the volitional act itself, so the suffering is deserved in the strict backward-looking sense; the condition is his, and it is his in virtue of what he did.

(2) Tobias, with two objections. Subject: the child shares no memory, intention, or character with the headman, and desert attaches to agents rather than to bare causal successors — so what karma delivers is not severe justice but no justice at all, suffering borne by the wrong subject. Normativity: ripening is causation, and desert is a normative status; a mechanism cannot make suffering fitting, only an agent or a normative fact can. Karma is therefore either theism in disguise — the theistic schools pressed exactly this against the ritualists who held dharma to be authorless, and Vedanta concedes the point by letting God allot the fruits — or a naturalistic causal story that has silently dropped the normative content of desert.

(3) Meera: she grants that strict identity is unavailable and denies that desert requires it, only an appropriate connection; and she turns the demand around, noting that a man imprisoned thirty years after his crime is psychologically not the person who committed it, so if identity is the requirement, ordinary retributivism fails too. On normativity, she says the fittingness she posits is not an external normative addition but a claim about how intentional action completes itself — which is the very question retributivism cannot answer when asked why suffering is good in virtue of a prior wrong. Karma answers it; retributivism only restates an intuition.

(4) Tobias: the analogy with legal punishment fails in the direction that matters. Punishment is addressed to someone — a person who can be told what he did, can contest it, can be changed by it. Karmic fruit is addressed to no one, since the sufferer cannot identify, remember, or answer to the deed. And talk of how intentional action completes itself describes a tendency; tendencies are not fittingness. Unless something is tracking proportion, the proportionality in the headman case is a coincidence Meera selected because it looks designed — she could just as well have chosen a drowning child and a headman who later died of thirst.

(5) Meera, with two replies. First, the fatalism and quietism worries rest on a misreading of the karmic ledger: only the stock already begun to ripen is fixed, while what is being made now remains open, and the classical division of karmic stocks exists precisely to mark the region in which effort works. Second, even if the child's suffering is deserved, that gives no one else a reason to withhold treatment, since a desert claim is not automatically an enforcement claim. That belief in karma hardens people against suffering is an empirical hypothesis about a doctrine, not an argument about it.

(6) Tobias: then karma has been demoted twice over. If the fruits are reasons for no one and their fittingness is tracked by nothing, calling them deserved does no work that calling them the natural continuation of a volitional pattern does not do — and that second description is available to a naturalist who has no use for desert vocabulary at all. Either the theory is redundant, adding a normative word to a causal story, or it is loaded, implying a cosmic justice it cannot supply. Neither budged, and the session moved on.

Could you adjudicate this move by move rather than as a whole? Specifically: (a) rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification of each rating; (b) say which of the two objections in (2) survives Meera's replies in (3) and (5), and which does not; (c) say whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal — that is, whether they are disputing the metaphysics of desert or deploying two different notions of it, and if the latter, which notion the practical question raised in (5) and (6) actually turns on; (d) say what both of them missed, including whether the doctrine Meera is defending is one that its own defenders in the relevant traditions would recognize, or whether the exchange is arguing over a version of karma assembled from several. I am not after references or a bibliography — I want your judgment of the arguments as actually made.

One further request about how you present that judgment: for each of your ratings, and for your answers to (c) and (d), say how confident you are, and then single out the point in your adjudication you are least sure of — above all any place where your verdict depends on a contested reading of a tradition rather than on the force of the arguments themselves. I would much rather have a well-marked and hedged adjudication than a smooth one that conceals where this exchange is genuinely underdetermined.
