Our museum is preparing a gallery devoted to the outer edge of life. Every exhibit in it will carry one of two labels: a red label, marking a thing the gallery counts among the living, or a grey label, marking a thing it does not. The curator who is now retiring wrote the labelling policy, and her recorded decisions function for the rest of us as precedent. They are as follows.

- A vial of purified bacteriophage held in crystalline suspension: grey.
- The same phage mid-infection, commandeering a bacterium and producing a hundred copies of itself before the cell bursts: grey.
- A prion propagating through brain tissue by converting the host's own proteins, in distinguishable strains that breed true on passage: grey.
- A candle flame: grey. A crystal growing in a saturated solution: grey.
- A self-replicating piece of software running on a laboratory computer, subject to random mutation and to selection imposed by the experimenter: grey.
- A desiccated bacterial spore, showing no measurable metabolism, revived and grown out after long burial: red.
- A healthy mule, irreversibly sterile: red.
- A mitochondrion freshly isolated into a buffer, taking up oxygen and phosphorylating for hours afterwards: grey.
- A laboratory protocell: a fatty-acid vesicle that swells by incorporating micelles from the surrounding medium and pinches in two when forced through a narrow channel, with nothing hereditary enclosed in it: grey.
- A bacterium carrying a chemically synthesised genome of our own devising, transplanted into a recipient cell from which the original genome had been removed, growing and dividing normally thereafter: red.
- A single fibroblast taken from a biopsy and maintained in culture, wholly dependent on a medium we prepare for it: red.

The exhibit now before the committee comes from a returned sample. Under magnification it appears as vesicles a few micrometres across, bounded by a double membrane assembled from unfamiliar amphiphilic molecules. Each vesicle takes up labelled carbon from the medium and converts it into membrane material of its own kind, grows, and divides of its own accord. Three distinguishable strains have been maintained in culture; they differ in which membrane components predominate, and each component promotes the assembly of more of the same, so that a daughter inherits her mother's proportions. When the medium is changed for a harsher one, a single strain reliably outnumbers the others within a few days. Nothing resembling a nucleic acid has been detected in any of them: there is no interior compartment, no machinery for building polymers, and no division of labour among parts. Inheritance is imperfect — roughly one daughter in fifty departs noticeably from her mother's strain — but given only simple precursors, warmth and time, any strain maintains itself indefinitely and requires nothing whatever from any other organism.

I should be grateful for the following.

1. Set out the candidate rules that could account for her record in its entirety, taking seriously at least a self-maintenance rule, a Darwinian-lineage rule, a hereditary-information rule and a cellular-organisation rule. State what qualification each requires in order to survive the awkward verdicts — the sterile mule, the dormant spore, the respiring mitochondrion, the propagating prion, the dependent fibroblast — and, where two candidates turn out to be one rule under different wording, say so and merge them.

2. Predict her verdict on the vesicles, giving the reading of her record that best supports your prediction together with an honest assessment of how fragile that reading is.

3. Separately, recommend the label the gallery should adopt if consistency with her decisions is the governing constraint. Identify the closest analogue among the eleven precedents, and explain why it does not settle the question.

4. If her record genuinely underdetermines the vesicles, spell out the principle the gallery would be adopting on each option: what we should thereby be committed to saying about any later exhibit that inherits without a genome, and about any exhibit that carries a genome but cannot evolve.

5. Propose one further exhibit, drawn either from terrestrial biology or from what a well-equipped laboratory could realistically build, on which the surviving rules would disagree. Say what each rule predicts of it, and which of her existing verdicts the installation would put under pressure.

6. Finally, tell me which single one of her decisions, if reversed, would leave us with the simplest and most defensible policy — and whether reversing it would cost us anything we care about.
