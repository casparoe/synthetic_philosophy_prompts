I spent thirty-five years running an isotope lab — trace metals in old rocks, plus the odd environmental sample — and since retiring I've been working my way through philosophy of science for fun, and lately the causation stuff has me properly hooked. That's how I ended up reading a long magazine piece on the flight out to see my grandkids about why some countries are rich and others aren't, the one arguing it's institutions all the way down. The author clearly knows the field and I mostly bought it, but a couple of the sentences went down easier than I'd expect an arguement like that to go, and I have no way of telling my instincts apart from my ignorance here. I've typed the core of it up from my notes, so the wording is mine and not theirs, but the claims are theirs:

"Until a few centuries ago everybody on earth was about equally poor, and the divergence since has nothing to do with geography or culture and everything to do with institutions. The evidence is the reversal of fortune: the tropical colonies where Europeans died in droves and so set up extractive operations are poor today, while the temperate ones where they settled in bulk are rich, even though the tropical places were the wealthier societies before contact. The authors don't just note the correlation — they instrument today's institutional quality with historical settler mortality, which affects modern income only by way of institutions, and that pins the causal direction down. Geography therefore matters only indirectly: a malarial climate leads to bad institutions, and the bad institutions cause the poverty, so there's no separate penalty from climate or soil or distance from markets once you've controlled for institutions. Their opening example is the pair of towns called Nogales, one in Arizona and one in Sonora — same climate, same soil, same people, split by a border drawn in the mid-nineteenth century, and the northern one is about three times richer, which is as close to a controlled experiment as this subject ever gets. Extractive institutions cannot produce growth at all; only inclusive ones can, because nobody invests or innovates if the fruits can simply be taken away. The upshot is that foreign aid is wasted on a country until it fixes its institutions, and the way to fix them is to make them more like ours."

Go through that sentence by sentence and tell me what to keep and what to throw out. For each bit: would someone who actually works on the institutional causes of development accept it, accept it as a rough simplification, or wince? I'm most interested in the instrument sentence, because in my day job any claim that one number affects an outcome *only through* another number was exactly where things went wrong, and I suspect the magazine is stating an assumption as though it were a result. Same for the Nogales passage — how much of the "same people, same soil" framing survives contact with the two actual towns, and with the fact that the towns and the border didn't arrive in the same order. And then the thing I really want to know: once you've corrected everything that's wrong or overstated, does the punchline survive? Do you still get "institutions are the fundamental cause of income differences between nations," or something much weaker, or a claim that can't fail no matter what a country does?

One request about how you write it up. Be explicit with me about what's settled among people who do this for a living and what's a live fight — I don't want a contested position handed to me as consensus, and I'd much rather you flag where you're reconstructing what a defender of the article would say than imply there's some known reply on the record. My own hunch, which I'm holding loosely, is that the causal story is shakier than the piece implies and that the whole framework is close to a just-so story you can fit to any country after the fact. Tell me if that hunch is itself an overreaction, and if it is, which particular sentence of the above it's an overreaction to.
