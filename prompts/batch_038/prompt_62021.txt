I am a novelist, and I have been stuck on the same page of my fourth book for six weeks — not because of the prose, but because two readers I trust disagree about something the whole manuscript turns on. The novel is set inside a national statistics office in a country that has not banned any word and has quietly stopped being able to say certain things about itself. My two readers — one a historical linguist, the other a political essayist — have been arguing for the better part of a year about what Orwell's account of language and politics actually claims, and their quarrel has become my quarrel, because the two readings imply different interior lives for my characters. The linguist holds what I will call the *medium* reading: Orwell's thesis is a claim about the material of thought, so that shrinking a vocabulary and stiffening a grammar shrinks the space of what can be thought at all; the police apparatus is the wrapper, not the theory. Her texts are Syme in the canteen in Part One ("It's a beautiful thing, the destruction of words"; "We're cutting the language down to the bone"), the stated purpose in the appendix on Newspeak that the language "was designed not to extend but to diminish the range of thought," and the essay's feedback loop — "an effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original cause and producing the same effect in an intensified form, and so on indefinitely." The essayist holds the *uptake* reading: Orwell's thesis is about the political economy of speech — who may say what, to whom, at what risk, and with what chance of being received — and lexical shrinkage is a symptom of a society in which dissent is unsafe and unrewarded rather than its cause. His texts are the essay's insistence that "the decline of a language must ultimately have political and economic causes" together with "The point is that the process is reversible"; the appendix's whole cast in the past tense, describing a language that in 1984 nobody yet spoke as a sole medium and that was expected to finish replacing Oldspeak only decades later — which reads to him like the obituary of a project that failed for political reasons, not a demonstration about the mind; and the appendix's coinage (I paraphrase, since I do not have the book to hand) for speaking on automatic pilot as a duck quacks, which Orwell treats as neither creditable nor discreditable in itself but only insofar as the speaker is or is not on your side — a thoughtless fluency that requires no missing words whatsoever.

To keep them from talking past each other, I have built a small apparatus of my own, and I want the document you draft to be organized around it. I distinguish five ways speech can be foreclosed: (1) *lexical* — no word exists; (2) *semantic* — the word exists but its offending sense has been stripped, as with the appendix's observation that a Newspeak speaker may still say a dog is free from lice but has no way to call a person politically free; (3) *evidential* — the word and the sense survive, but uttering them identifies the utterer, so speech happens only in rooms that have first been checked; (4) *uptake* — the utterance is made, is safe, and is clear, but nobody is positioned to receive it, or no durable copy survives to be received later; and (5) *dispositional* — everything is available and permitted, and the speaker reaches instead for prefabricated strips. Four cases from my manuscript are meant to be the crux-tests, and I want each side to record a prediction about each in advance, in writing, before any adjudication: (i) my statisticians can all still say and think "unemployment" at home, but the office templates have offered only "economically inactive" for nine years — does the medium reading predict conceptual decay in the speaker, and does the uptake reading predict none? (ii) a character who recites forbidden texts perfectly from memory yet cannot deploy a single one of their sentences to describe her own predicament — abundant lexicon, no traction; (iii) an honest, lucid dissenting memorandum that is written, filed, and never read by anyone with standing to act on it; (iv) a scene in which nothing at all is missing — no word, no sense, no safety, no audience — and the meeting nevertheless produces a paragraph of the essay's prefabricated-hen-house prose. I suspect one genuine crux is whether "the range of thought" in Orwell means the set of propositions a speaker is *able* to entertain or the set she is *disposed* to entertain, and I suspect that if the two camps answer differently they are not disagreeing at all — but I do not want you to adopt my suspicion; I want you to test it.

Please draft the joint statement this collaboration should have produced, in three parts: a numbered list of claims both camps could sign without embarrassment (including what both accept about the essay, about the appendix's framing and tense, and about which of my five foreclosure modes the texts actually theorize); a disagreements table giving each residual dispute in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view, with a column marking whether the dispute is substantive, verbal, or about emphasis; and then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them — each paired with the textual or conceptual test both would agree bears on it and with each camp's prediction recorded beforehand. Then commit. I do not want the even-handed register in which every tension is "productive" and the reader is left to choose: tell me plainly whether the able/disposed ambiguity makes part of this dispute merely verbal and which part; tell me which single crux you expect to actually move a side and in which direction, and why; tell me which of my four manuscript cases is the sharpest test and which is a dud; and flag any claim in your agreed-claims section that you suspect is a false peace, bought by vagueness that one camp would repudiate on reflection. Work only from what I have given you and your own general knowledge of these two texts, refer to passages descriptively rather than by page or edition, and send me no bibliography or further-reading list — the point is to settle a disagreement, not to defer one.
