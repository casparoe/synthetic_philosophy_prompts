I cover neuroscience and human behavior for a national magazine, and for about a decade I've been writing about the gap between knowing something and being moved by it. The version of that problem now sitting in my own family has resisted everything I've tried to say about it, so I'd like a rigorous answer rather than a sympathetic one.

My younger brother, thirty-six, is a structural engineer living two hours from where our mother is being cared for. She has a slowly progressive neurological condition: cognition intact, mobility and stamina going, and a prognosis she understands perfectly well and discusses with all of us without euphemism. My brother has declined to move closer and has twice turned down work that would have put him within easy reach of her. He is not in denial and he is not selfish in any ordinary sense — he paid for her home aide before the first invoice arrived, he handles her finances, he reads her clinical notes and can recite them back to me more accurately than I can. What he will not do is be there. Last month I told him he has a reason to move, that anyone in his position has a reason to be near a parent who is going to need him, and he did not dispute a single fact. He said: I have done the thinking you keep asking for. I read the two books you sent, I spent a year in therapy on exactly this, I lived with her for nine weeks in 2022 and kept a journal to see whether something would start answering to it, and nothing did. I know everything you know and I want none of it. So tell me what you actually mean. If nothing in me answers to it, in what sense is that a reason of mine rather than a preference of yours? I have tried the standard replies — that he doesn't really know what it will be like, that he's underestimating how he'll feel later, that if he imagined it vividly enough he'd be moved — and each one collapsed, because in his case they are simply false: he has imagined it vividly, tested it empirically on himself, and reported back. He also offers a conditional that I can't quite classify: if she reaches the point of needing hands-on daily care he will come and stay for as long as it takes, but what she needs now, he says, is money and company, and he will supply the first without limit and the second not at all. Meanwhile our mother has told both of us she doesn't want him to come if it means resenting her, which seems to make the reason disappear from the other end as well, and I can't work out whether it was ever hers to withdraw.

Here is the part that makes this more than a family quarrel. Two years ago I wrote a column defending a junior researcher who knew for three years that her supervisor was fabricating data and never reported it. She had said plainly that nothing in her circumstances could have moved her — reporting meant losing her visa, her lab, and probably her field — and I argued that calling her a coward was unfair, that condemnation of that kind quietly assumes the person could have been moved by considerations that in fact had no purchase on her at all, and that much moral blame is just our own motives projected onto people who don't share them. I still think that column was right. But I cannot see what keeps it from vindicating my brother, and if it does, then everything I say to him is not an assertion but a kind of pressure dressed up as information — which is either a relief or an indictment of me, and I can't tell which. So, three questions, and please take them in order. First: is the sentence I keep repeating to him — you have a reason to be near her — true, false, or not the sort of thing that can be either, and does your answer shift at all given that he has now done everything we normally require before conceding that someone has genuinely weighed a reason? Second: does my column commit me to accepting his verdict about himself, or is there a principled difference between that researcher and my brother that lets me hold both views? Third: what should I actually say to him the next time this comes up, and is it permissible to keep asserting something I may not believe is true of him — or is the honest move to stop asserting it and simply tell him what I want, which feels like a smaller and meaner thing to say to a brother? I'm not looking for boundary language, a script, or a suggestion that we bring in a mediator; I want to know whether the claim survives scrutiny and what follows for how I talk to him.
