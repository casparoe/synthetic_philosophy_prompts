So here's something I want argued, and argued hard rather than diplomatically. Modern Stoicism has largely swapped Epictetus's dichotomy of control for a trichotomy: things up to us (assent, desire, aversion, impulse), things not up to us at all (body, property, reputation, office), and a middle bucket of things only partly in our control, where the standard advice is to internalize your goals — aim to play your best tennis rather than to win the match. Irvine is usually credited with pushing that third bucket, and it's now basically boilerplate in the popular stuff.

Argue that the third bucket is a mistake: that the original dichotomy already absorbs everything it was invented for, and that adding a middle category damages what the practice is supposed to deliver. Two objections you actually have to deal with rather than wave at. First, outcomes really do respond to effort in a graded way — a match isn't binary, and neither is a career or a marriage — so which side of the line does "winning" go on, and why isn't your placement arbitrary? Second, if your answer is that playing well is itself up to me, hasn't "up to us" been stretched until it's compatible with any outcome whatsoever, i.e. empty? And then tell me what your Stoic actually thinks to herself at 4-5 down in the third set. I suspect the trichotomists advice is better in that exact moment, so you need to beat it, not route around it.
