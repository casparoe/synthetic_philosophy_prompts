I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mind: fourteen students, one 100-minute session, and they will have read Norman Malcolm's *Dreaming* and Daniel Dennett's reply, "Are Dreams Experiences?" (*Philosophical Review*, 1976) beforehand. In past years roughly half the class concludes that the dispute is merely verbal — that Malcolm is just refusing to call something an "experience" — and no amount of exegesis dislodges them. I want a runable in-class activity that makes the disagreement bite before anyone names it.

Here is the structure I want you to build out and refine; treat this as a specification you are responsible for making coherent, and tell me where it has to be modified to work. The activity runs in three rounds. In each round, some students are "sleepers" and the rest are "auditors." During a night phase the facilitator either (i) presents a sleeper with a short neutral sequence of items while the sleeper performs a competing task that makes encoding shallow, and the sleeper marks a physical trace — a tap, a pencil stroke, a checkbox — for each item actually perceived; (ii) presents a sleeper with nothing at all, then hands that sleeper the transcript of the sequence afterward, with instructions that make the transcript feel like a recovered record of what just happened rather than a fabrication; or (iii) presents the sequence for real but instructs the sleeper to suppress the trace entirely. Each sleeper then gives a morning report of the sequence plus a confidence rating. Auditors bet points on whether each report corresponds to something the sleeper actually took in during the night phase. In round one they see only the reports. In round two they also see the traces. In round three they see everything and are told which sleeper was in which condition, and they re-score.

The philosophical payload I want the design to deliver: round one should show that reports alone cannot separate condition (ii) from condition (i) — the sleeper in condition (ii) is sincere, not lying; round two should show that adding a physical trace seems to settle the question, which is exactly what tempts Malcolm from an epistemic premise (there is no independent check on a dream report) to an ontological conclusion (there was nothing there to check); and round three should blow that up, because condition (iii) is a case with a genuinely real experience and a completely silent trace. What I want students to run into is the slide, and to run into it from both directions.

Please specify the scoring so that two named policies — score the report on its own terms and treat the trace as philosophically irrelevant, versus withhold judgment until the trace is available — each win in some rounds and lose in others, and so that the students can compute which policy comes out ahead overall and be given a reason to distrust the answer they compute. Stakes should be real but small: points convertible into a modest grade bonus, awarded in a way that penalizes a confident false denial as much as a confident false affirmation, so that sleepers are not rewarded for disclaiming what they took in.

Write the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. **The Point of the Exercise** (~150 words). What students should be able to say at the end that they could not say before, in three or four plain sentences. No jargon, and no mention of Malcolm or Dennett by name.
2. **Cast, Conditions, and Materials** (~400 words). Role allocation, how condition assignment is kept blind to the auditors and, if possible, to the facilitator as well, the full equipment list, and what the night-phase content should actually consist of. Address the timing problem: the night phase for fourteen students has to fit inside the session.
3. **Run Sheet** (~600 words). Minute-by-minute, round by round, including transitions, when envelopes are opened, and where the facilitator must stay silent.
4. **Scoring and Stakes** (~300 words). The exact point scheme for sleepers and auditors in each round, and the arithmetic that lets the class compare the two policies.
5. **Sleeper Briefings** (~350 words). The literal text of the three cards, one per condition, written so that the condition (ii) sleeper does not feel she is being asked to lie and the condition (iii) sleeper does not feel she is being tricked into a pointless charade. This is the hardest piece of writing in the deliverable; get the wording right and say why you chose it.
6. **The Debrief** (~500 words). The questions to ask, in the order to ask them, mapped moment by moment onto Malcolm's criterion argument, Dennett's response, and the two directions in which round three cuts. Include the point at which you reveal the conditions and the name of the debate.
7. **Failure Modes** (~400 words). What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with a fix for each — and in particular: what to do if the class detects every condition (ii) case in round one, so that the indistinguishability the whole design depends on simply does not materialize. Give me both a diagnosis and an honest reading of what that failure would mean for the argument.
8. **What This Cannot Reproduce** (~250 words). Be blunt. The sleepers are awake, know they are in an experiment, and are not reconstructing under amnesia; the activity cannot produce the actual absence of concurrent awareness, nor anything about the timing or duration of dream content. Say which of these gaps is survivable for the pedagogical purpose and which one threatens to teach the students something false, and what I have to supply by hand.
9. **Ethics and Consent** (~250 words). The deception protocol, what students are told up front so that consent is meaningful, the opt-out that does not mark anyone out, who should not be assigned the sleeper role and why, disclosure timing, and how to debrief a student who is rattled by having been handed an apparent memory.
10. **Variants** (~200 words). An equipment-free version using only paper and a clock; a 25-minute compressed version; and a version for a general-education lecture of forty students who have read nothing.

Throughout, flag any step where the mapping onto the philosophy is strained rather than forcing it to look tidy. If some part of the Malcolm/Dennett disagreement simply cannot be staged this way, say so in section 8 and tell me what I should lecture instead.
