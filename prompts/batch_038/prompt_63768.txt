**Draft: On the knowability clause in path dependence**

Path dependence is usually presented as a claim about how the world works: small early events get amplified by increasing returns, and a system ends up in one of several possible states rather than the best one. But the notion of the best one — of the superior alternative that was foreclosed — does all the interesting work in every application, and it is not a worldly feature at all. To say that an outcome is a case of regrettable lock-in is to say that a feasible alternative would have done better, and feasibility here is not merely physical but epistemic: the alternative must have been something an agent in that situation could have identified and chosen. That clause is indexed to a knowledge state, and the index is almost never supplied.

So the classification of a case drifts. Take the keyboard. Paul David's story is that the layout inherited from Sholes's machine and standardized by Remington in the 1870s was frozen in by the interlocking of manufacturing, typing instruction, and the trained habits of typists, and that a better arrangement — Dvorak's — has been unable to displace it for reasons unconnected to its merits. The familiar skeptical reply is that no alternative has ever been shown to be better by evidence that would survive scrutiny, and that the case rests on a handful of interested experiments. My claim is that both sides are describing the same world and disagreeing about the index. Relative to what buyers and typists could know in 1880, the layout may have been entirely reasonable; relative to what a keyboard researcher could know a half-century later, it was a standing mistake; relative to what can now be measured, it may not be a mistake at all. Nothing about the keyboard changed. What changed was the set of alternatives anyone was positioned to identify — and that set is what the word foreclosed ranges over.

The drift runs the other way too. VHS beating Betamax is standardly cited as lock-in, but if the decisive feature was recording length — a genuine preference of the actual market — then the case is one of a better product winning, and the amplification of an early lead is doing no explanatory work it would not have done anyway.

An outcome can therefore be reclassified from benign to regrettable, or back again, with no change in technology, cost structure, or reversibility, purely in virtue of a change in what the relevant agents could have known. If so, the question of whether something was locked in is ill-posed until a knowledge state is fixed — and the grander question of whether history would have arrived at the same place anyway inherits the defect, since arriving at the same place is only remarkable if that place was identifiable in advance.

My friend's objection, which I have not managed to answer: this proves too much and also misses the point. It proves too much, because every counterfactual and every opportunity-cost claim in history is indexed to an assessor's information; I have described a general feature of historical reasoning and dressed it as a discovery about path dependence. And it misses the point, because lock-in was never chiefly a claim about knowledge but about the present cost of reversing course — a standard is locked in when switching costs now exceed the gains from switching, whether or not anyone in 1880 could have known better. On that reading my index-shifting is beside the question: QWERTY's status is fixed by today's switching costs, not by yesterday's epistemic situation.
