So, kind of an odd ask. I got laid off from a data engineering job last spring and, long story short, I'm now a junior plans examiner at the building department of a mid-sized city — I review comercial permit submittals all day. I like it way more than I expected to. Meanwhile I've kept going to this online reading group on Tuesday nights, mostly ex-philosophy-grad-school people, and we just spent a month on the philosophy of computational complexity. And I keep having this feeling that my day job is a better model of what they're arguing about than anything they actually say, but I can't get them to see it and I can't articulate it myself either.

Here's what I want you to do. Take the cluster of claims that keeps coming up in that literature: (1) that the gap between finding a solution and checking a solution isn't a contingent fact about our machines but a structural feature of the world, so that words like "creativity" and "insight" are just labels we put on the search side of the gap; (2) that worst-case intractability is the interesting claim and average-case easiness is beside the point — or maybe the reverse, there's a fight about this and I dont fully follow it; (3) that the results about why the obvious proof strategies for P vs NP can't possibly work (relativization, natural proofs, that sort of thing) tell you something general about the limits of general methods; (4) the advice-string idea, where a whole class of problems gets more powerful because it's handed a short unexplainable hint; (5) zero-knowledge proofs, i.e. that you can get someone to rationally accept that a thing is true without handing them anything they could pass along. Restate each one, as faithfully as you can manage, in the vocabulary I actually work in: prescriptive paths versus performance paths, submittal packages, calculation packages and narrative justifications, the clause that lets us accept alternative materials and methods when they meet the intent of the code, stamped drawings, the fact that the engineer of record produced something I can check in an afternoon but could never have produced myself, back-checks, correction comments, deferred submittals, the energy-code tradeoff approach where a worse wall is allowed if the mechanical system makes it up somewhere else.

Then the part I actually care about, which is the residue report. For each of the five, tell me whether it came across clean, whether you had to bend it, or whether there's just nothing in plan review for it to land on. Be honest about the bending — don't quietly smuggle complexity-theory assumptions into my vocabulary and then congratulate yourself on a successful translation. And then give me your read on the residue: where it's real, is that because a building department genuinely can't express these ideas (we review one building, not a family of instances growing without bound, and nobody here talks asymptotics), or because the philosophical claim was inflated to start with and my inability to say it is evidence against it? Finally — this is the thing I want to bring to the group on Tuesday — is there a distinction my side has that theirs is missing? My sense is that plan review has a developed notion of who is permitted to check what, and on whose stamp, which is about authority and liability and not about time at all, and that the complexity people have no word for it. Tell me if I'm wrong about that.
