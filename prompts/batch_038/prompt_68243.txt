I am revising a draft essay for a graduate seminar whose topic this term is the relationship between empirical moral psychology and normative theory, and I would be grateful for critical comments rather than encouragement. The full draft follows. After it I have listed the specific points on which I want your judgement; please address those, and resist the temptation to begin by summarising what I have written. I am not looking for references or suggestions of further reading — I want engagement with the argument as it stands.

---

**Draft: "The Palate Argument and Its Two Bridging Principles"**

**1. The descriptive claim.** Moral foundations theory holds that human moral judgement is not the output of a single general-purpose capacity for weighing harm or fairness, but of several semi-independent psychological systems, each tuned to a recurrent adaptive problem. The standard list runs to five: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion and sanctity/degradation, with a sixth, liberty/oppression, added later and fitting only uneasily into the customary division of the first two as individualising and the next three as binding. Data collected with the corresponding self-report questionnaire, in a number of countries, suggest an asymmetry between the political camps. People who describe themselves as liberal weight their responses towards care and fairness and register comparatively little on loyalty, authority and sanctity; people who describe themselves as conservative distribute weight across the foundations rather more evenly. The authors of the theory have been explicit that this is a descriptive claim about intuitions and self-attributions, and that it licenses no conclusion about which political programme is correct. That insistence has not prevented the conclusion from being drawn, in both directions.

**2. Two readings.** The first reading, which I shall call the palate argument, is popular rather than academic. If moral cognition consists of capacities for registering morally relevant features of situations, and one group's responses engage more of those capacities than another's, then the first group is attending to more of what there is to attend to. Liberal moral judgement, on this picture, resembles a cookery that recognises only salt and sugar — not mistaken, but impoverished, and reliably wrong precisely where the unrecognised ingredients matter: in questions about institutions, allegiance, deference and the symbolic significance of bodies.

The second reading is that the asymmetry is an artefact of measurement. Either the binding foundations are not moral foundations at all but mechanisms of group coordination that happen to recruit moral vocabulary, in which case the instrument records how much of one's group psychology one is willing to describe in moral terms; or the scores track self-presentation, since in some circles it is creditable to endorse items about authority and sanctity and in others it is not.

**3. What both readings presuppose.** Each rests on a bridging principle that its proponents leave undefended, and in each case the principle is available only at a cost.

The palate argument requires that the foundations be individuated by their moral content — that fairness/cheating names a feature of situations that a view can track or miss. But the theory individuates them functionally. Fairness/cheating is posited because reciprocal exchange was worth stabilising; sanctity/degradation because of pathogen avoidance; loyalty/betrayal because of coalition formation. On a functional individuation, the claim that a view engages more foundations says only that it speaks to more distinct adaptive problems, and it is not evident why that should be evidence of moral insight. To move from coverage to truth one needs the further premise that the adaptive problems map onto the morally relevant features. That premise is a substantive normative thesis, and the questionnaire cannot supply it.

The artefact reading faces a mirror-image problem. If an intuition whose genealogy is fully accounted for by non-moral selective pressure thereby forfeits its evidential weight, the principle bears with equal force on care/harm and fairness/cheating, which are no less products of selection. The liberal who invokes genealogy against the binding foundations, applied consistently, is left with no intuitions to invoke at all: the data do not favour her side, they simply stop speaking.

**4. Conclusion.** The psychology is therefore not normatively inert, but its bearing is indirect. What the scores license, at most, is a claim about the distribution of reliability. For a given moral question we may ask whether the reported intuitions are the output of a system whose function was to track that sort of question, and treat a divergence between function and domain as a reason for caution. This is a modest result, and a reason for neither camp to celebrate.

My questions, in order of importance:

1. Is section 1 an accurate account of the theory and of the empirical claim? In particular: have I mischaracterised the status of the sixth foundation or the individualising/binding division, and have I overstated how well the liberal–conservative asymmetry generalises beyond the samples from which it is usually drawn? If the description of what the questionnaire measures is loose, say so plainly, since the whole of section 3 leans on it.

2. Does the palate argument genuinely collapse once the foundations are individuated functionally? I suspect there is a version that does not: one on which a functional account of a system's origin is perfectly compatible with that system's being a detector of some real feature of the world, so that possessing more detectors means detecting more features, and the objection loses its grip. If such a version exists, what must its defender add in order to block my point — and does the addition reintroduce the normative premise I claim is missing?

4. Is the symmetry claim in the second half of section 3 too quick? It seems to assume that an equally complete and equally damaging evolutionary story is available for every foundation, and I have argued for none of that. Relatedly, I am not confident that I have kept apart the thought that selection explains an intuition from the thought that selection discredits it.

4. Does the conclusion in fact differ from the debunking principle I reject in section 3? A colleague remarked that a claim about the distribution of reliability is the same claim in softer language, and that I have therefore argued myself in a circle. I cannot see whether she is right, and I would like to know what would settle it.

5. Finally, is there an objection to the framing as a whole that I am ignoring — for instance, that the liberal/conservative contrast in the data does not correspond to any coherent pair of normative outlooks, so that nothing in section 2 or 3 has a stable subject matter? If so, tell me whether the essay can be repaired by re-description or whether it needs to be abandoned. Please also mark any passage that is doing no work and should be cut.
