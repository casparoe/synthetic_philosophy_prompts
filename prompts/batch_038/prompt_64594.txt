American federal courts display a pattern that I find hard to state without sounding as though I have invented it. A single plaintiff who can show a concrete and particularized injury to herself — an injury that is hers, not merely one she shares with the public at large — may obtain an order suspending a government policy, and for decades such orders have routinely been framed to bind the government in its dealings with everyone, including millions of nonparties who never appeared in the case. At the merits stage the court asks whether this plaintiff's right has been infringed; that the policy produces enormous aggregate benefits for the population is, as a rule, beside the point, or enters only obliquely as a compelling government interest. Yet the very same court, when asked for a preliminary injunction before trial, must expressly weigh the balance of equities and the public interest under the four-factor test the Supreme Court restated in Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council in 2008 — that is, it must total up harms to people who are not before it — and will deny relief when the aggregate harm to nonparties outweighs the plaintiff's irreparable injury. Elsewhere the system is cheerfully additive: tort damages scale with the number of victims, a class action pools thousands of claims into one fund divided pro rata, the sentencing guidelines treat the number of victims as an aggravating factor, and executive agencies defend regulations by summing statistical lives saved. And at the front door, standing doctrine turns away the person whose only complaint is that a policy injures millions of people diffusely and injures him exactly as much as it injures every other one of them: a grievance he merely shares with everyone else will not get him into court.

What moral theory does this pattern presuppose? I am drawn to the thought that the merits practice is Scanlonian contractualism in institutional drag — justification owed to each person separately, complaints compared one against one rather than summed, so that a million small benefits cannot be laid in the scale against a single infringement. Test that hypothesis rather than assuming it. Does the practice strictly require it, or would a threshold-deontological view, a rule-consequentialist one, or a wholly non-moral account — institutional competence, the bipartite structure of a lawsuit, the absence of any forum in which the millions could be heard — license exactly the same doctrine? Separate carefully what the practice commits the system to from what merely sits comfortably with it, and mark the places where the doctrine is philosophically underdetermined, several incompatible theories generating the same result.

Then take up the tensions head on. If the merits are genuinely non-aggregative, what is the public-interest factor doing at the injunction stage, and does its presence show that the refusal to aggregate is a rule of decision rather than a theory of value? Is the denial of standing to the diffusely harmed the same principle as the refusal to weigh the aggregate against the plaintiff, or a different and perhaps opposing one — the many incapable of being represented at all, rather than incapable of outweighing the one? Why is procedural aggregation in a class action legitimate when moral aggregation is not, given that the pooled fund is divided according to each member's own loss? And what is the status of the rule that trivial injuries are dismissed however many people sustain them: does it commit the system to a threshold below which complaints simply drop out, a threshold that pairwise comparison is supposed to render unnecessary?

Finally, suppose we rebuilt the apparatus on a straightforwardly aggregative theory of justification. Tell me which doctrines would have to be abandoned, which would survive untouched and why, and what new institutions the system would require in order to represent the aggregate honestly rather than by judicial guesswork about the public interest. I want the reconstruction and the diagnosis, not a survey; use the vocabulary of moral theory where it earns its keep, but keep the legal description concrete enough that I can check it against the practice as I have laid it out here.
