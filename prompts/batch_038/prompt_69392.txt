So I chair the naming-and-dedications committee at a small private university — call it Ashgrove, the particulars don't matter — and I've spent three years watching us apply a rule I can no longer articulate a defense of. Our charter language, which I helped draft, runs roughly like this: a dedication embodied in something physical (a building name cut into granite, a statue, a bronze memorial, a stained-glass window, a bench with a plate on it) carries a presumption of permanence, and "the remedy for a dedication that has become misleading is more information, not less." So when a nineteenth-century trustee who funded the science quad turns out to have been a loud opponent of admitting women, we don't touch the name; we mount a plaque next to it, drafted by us, that says what he thought and adds that the university "neither endorses nor repudiates the views described." But the same committee, sitting in the same room under the same charter, will revoke a prize, a fellowship, a named chair, or an honorary degree outright, sometimes retroactively, with a one-line rescission in the bulletin and no plaque at all — and over in the library we don't withdraw books either, we add a subject heading. So stone gets annotated, paper gets annotated, but offices and honours get deleted. Why that pattern and not another?

I have a half-formed hypothesis I want you to test rather than flatter: that the practice presupposes a distinction between a dedication-as-artifact, whose meaning is fixed at the moment it's made and can only ever accrete commentary, and an honour-as-standing-speech-act, which the institution is continuously in the business of conferring and can therefore stop conferring. Is that the distinction the practice actually rests on, or just a rationalization I invented after the fact? And here's the wrinkle that keeps me up: the plaque is itself authored by a committee, itself mounted on the building, and, by the charter's own logic, itself permanent — so in fifty years, when our wording looks as self-serving and dated as the dedication it annotates, we are forbidden from removing it, and the only remedy the policy allows is another plaque. Plaque on plaque. Tell me whether that regress is vicious or benign and what the answer implies, because I suspect it settles whether we think meaning in a dedication is fixed at utterance and merely accumulates gloss, or is perpetually re-authored by whoever maintains the site. Related: does the "neither endorses nor repudiates" formula do any work at all, or does it quietly convert an institutional endorsement into a standing assignment for the passer-by — which would mean the policy presupposes an idealized reader (attentive, literate, present at an hour when the light is good, willing to stop), and that this imaginary reader is doing the moral labor we've assigned to bronze.

For this one I want you to write as a philosopher who works on collective memory and the philosophy of history and who knows the second Untimely Meditation cold — but please don't summarize it at me, and don't hand me a bibliography or reading list; I have those. Start by brainstorming out loud for a few paragraphs about the candidate theories that could license the practice, including the unphilosophical ones (donor relations, the cost of stone-cutting, liability, reputational risk), because I need to know which parts of the pattern are philosophically underdetermined and would be licensed equally well by several views, and which parts are only explicable on one specific view of what the past is for. Then reconstruct the implicit theory as tightly as you can and tell me what it strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits comfortably with, and which rival views the charter silently rules out. Then run the triad over it — monumental, antiquarian, critical — and be honest about whether the triad is doing diagnostic work or is just a labeling scheme I'm fond of: specifically, whether a presumption of permanence is antiquarian piety generalized from a family to a whole campus, and whether the plaque is critical history with its teeth filed down, i.e. whether we perform judgment while formally disclaiming it. Finally, tell me what would have to change if we adopted the rival view that a name on a building is a standing act of endorsement rather than a finished artifact — does the plaque survive that swap, and would every dedication need periodic re-authorization, a sunset clause for memory? Think carefully, take the space you need, and if a step in your argument is a judgment call dressed up as analysis, flag it as such.
