I'm TA-ing an upper-level seminar on the origins of the Jesus tradition this term — 18 students, mostly juniors, one 75-minute block, and the room has whiteboards but no projector, so anything you design has to run on paper and chalk. Two weeks back they got the standard lecture on the criteria of authenticity: dissimilarity (including the double-dissimilarity version), multiple attestation, embarrassment, coherence, plus the Palestinian-environment and Aramaic-traces business. They all nodded along and now think they've learned a method. What I actually want them to leave feeling is that the criteria were never calibrated — that they behave like diagnostic tests whose specificity nobody has ever measured, and whose output quietly collapses when the prior probability is low. Think of each criterion as a little machine that prints "authentic" or "not." What you'd need to trust it is a rate: how often does it fire on material you already know to be invented? Nobody has ever had a labeled dataset for first-century Jesus material, so that number has never been computed, and yet the whole enterprise is run as though the answer came back favorable. And the criteria aren't independent of each other, which is the part my students never clock: a saying is multiply attested *because* it was useful to lots of communities, and usefulness to lots of communities is also what tends to make it look dissimilar from any single one of them — so stacking criteria doesn't multiply your confidence the way they assume it does.

I have a hunch about a two-round structure and I want you to build it out properly, or tell me the hunch is bad and build something better. Round one: a corpus with an answer key, so they finally get to measure a false-positive rate on material where ground truth exists — the standard move is to have me assemble a list of things I've actually said in earlier classes mixed with things I never said, written up in the same flattened register as a gospel pericope, and have them sift it blind with the criteria while I hold the key. Round two: the same procedure applied to real gospel material, except the key doesn't exist and never will, and they have to sit in that. I'd want the round-two items just named by passage, not quoted — we're all reading the same translation and I don't want your approximation of the wording drifting into the handout.

So: give me the full build. The corpus construction protocol (how many items, what mix of real to invented, and crucially how to write the invented ones so they're *fair* — an invented item that's obviously anachronistic turns the exercise into a shooting gallery and proves nothing about the criteria); the rules and any team structure; a scoring scheme with tokens or points rather than real money, that makes them commit before they see the key rather than hedging; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 including the debrief; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific failures — double dissimilarity handing you a Jesus who fits neither his Judaism nor the church that formed around him, embarrassment depending entirely on knowing what a community was sensitive about, coherence verging on circular, multiple attestation conflating "widely spread" with "early." Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover. Two extra things I care about. First, be honest about whether the round-one/round-two analogy actually holds, because I suspect it partly doesn't: my invented items come from one modern mind with no community shaping them, whereas the gospel material was produced by groups with live interests, which is exactly what the criteria were supposed to be detecting — if that gap is big enough to sink the comparison, say so and tell me what to do instead. Second, there are three students in this room whose faith is not a hobby and one classics major who thinks the entire subfield is astrology; I don't want the activity engineered to humiliate either camp, but I also don't want it sanded down until it teaches nothing, so design with that in mind and flag anywhere you had to compromise. No reading list, no citations — I've got the bibliography sorted and I'd rather you spend the words on the mechanics. And at the end tell me how confident you are in your empirical predictions here (that they'll over-trust the criteria, that they'll score near chance on the labeled corpus), and single out the one claim in your design you're least sure of.
