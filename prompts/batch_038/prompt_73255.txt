ok, slightly weird request. I sit on a county advisory panel — its the body that vets which faith-based shelters and food banks we contract with — so it's mostly social workers, two pastors, a rabbi, and me, a lapsed Catholic who turned out to be the one who reads political theory for fun. We kept circling the same argument every meeting, so the chair asked everyone to write down their actual view in advance, in their own words, and mine is below. Trouble is I've been defending this position for two years now and I have a nagging feeling it doesn't hold together, and I'd rather find out from you than from the rabbi on Thursday night. Can you audit it? Please don't explain public reason to me — I've read the material — just tell me which of these pull against each other.

The list:

1. What makes state coercion legitimate is that it's justifiable to the people it gets applied to, not that it's true. I'm a believer myself and I think some of my convictions are true, but truth isn't the currency here.
2. "Justifiable to" means justifiable to an idealized version of the citizen — informed, reasonable, not viewing things through whatever they happened to be raised on. Actual rejection by someone who isn't reasonable doesn't make a law illegitimate.
3. That said, I take the complaint seriously that this whole framework begs the question against religious people: it builds a sectarian epistemology into the word "reasonable" and then labels unreasonable anybody who won't play along. I don't think my view is allowed to do that.
4. I'm a wide-view person: religious and comprehensive arguments are welcome in public political debate, at any point, as long as properly public reasons get offered in due course. Roughly the proviso.
5. But I think the proviso lands unevenly — it's basically always religious people who end up doing the translating. So I have a little doctrine of my own that I've started calling the translator's exemption: if an official offers a public-reason gloss in good faith, we take the gloss at face value and don't ask whether it's what actually moved her. Motive-scrutiny is off the table, both because it's inquisitorial and because if you did it honestly you'd have to do it to the secular officials too, whose public reasons are often just as much of a cover story.
6. At the same time I don't think a purely decorative gloss should count. If the public reason isn't the reason the thing was done, the thing isn't publicly justified, it's just wearing a costume.
7. Judges get held to a tighter standard than legislators in my view. An opinion should be written entirely in public reasons, and I'd support legally barring a judge from resting a holding on scripture — even though I would never bar a councilmember from citing scripture in debate.
8. Case: our commissioners open each session with an invocation from a rotating clergy. I think that's fine.
9. Case: a councilmember votes against a supervised consumption site. Privately she tells me it's because of a conviction about the body she gets from her faith; in the meeting she defends the vote as a claim about what treating people as disposable does to a community. I think the vote is legitimate.
10. Case: one of our contracted shelters wants to keep hiring only co-religionists. Their written explanation leads with vocation and the religious character of the work, and then adds a paragraph about the freedom of a voluntary association to define its own mission and choose the people who carry it out. I think the county can't act on that, and that the vocation language isn't going to cut it.

So: which pairs genuinely conflict, and which ones only look like they do? I'm fairly confident there's at least one in there that's completely fine and I'd waste the whole meeting defending it, so tell me which ones are non-issues and why, rather than flagging everything. Then rank the real tensions by severity instead of dumping them on me equally — I want to know where the load-bearing wall is versus which ones are cosmetic. For each real one, give me the cheapest available revision and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list. And if dropping or rewriting a single commitment cleans up most of the damage, say which one — but don't assume I'd pick it, I might be attached to exactly the wrong thing. Use my own numbering, and no bibliography or further reading please, I'm not going to get to anything before Thursday.
