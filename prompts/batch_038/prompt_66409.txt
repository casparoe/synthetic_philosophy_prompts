I am a software engineer on the infrastructure team at a mid-sized cloud company. I have no formal training in philosophy, so please treat me as a bright but impatient engineer: concrete examples, plain language, nothing I have to look up twice.

In three weeks I have a twenty-five-minute slot, plus questions, at our quarterly research review. I want to prepare for the question period. What I need is a prep sheet, not a defence of my view — I already believe the view, and I need to know how it will be attacked.

**What I intend to argue.** Our team runs an agent that optimises a single scalar objective: roughly, keep cluster utilisation inside a target band while meeting latency commitments. My claim is that we can add a penalty for irreversible side effects without ever writing down what we actually value, and that this is a real engineering option rather than a philosophical promissory note. The mechanism I will describe is:

- pick a dozen auxiliary objectives that are deliberately *not* the agent's real goal (can an operator still reach every node; can the cluster still be drained for maintenance; can we still roll back to last week's configuration; could we still admit a hypothetical new tenant; and so on);
- from any candidate successor state, measure how much of each auxiliary objective remains attainable, and at what cost, relative to two baselines — the state we started in, and the state a do-nothing policy would have produced;
- penalise the agent by the shortfall, summed across the auxiliary set with fixed weights.

The load-bearing claim, and the one I expect to be attacked, is that this set of auxiliary goals approximates a broad class of possible value functions rather than any single one of them. So the penalty is doing work a lone objective cannot: it keeps options open for later human correction, and it does so without my having to settle in advance what the company ought to want.

**The audience.** About thirty people. Mostly production ML engineers who have shipped reward-shaped systems and who treat specification gaming as a Tuesday problem. Two site-reliability leads whose first loyalty is availability. An applied economist from the pricing team. A product director under pressure to show measurable savings this half. And a VP who has read two popular books about AI risk and enjoys asking large questions in front of the room. Nobody has a philosophy background. Several are openly sceptical that "AI safety" vocabulary has any operational content at all, and one senior engineer wrote the objective my penalty constrains, so he will hear the talk as a review of his work. As a group they care far more about whether the thing can be tuned, evaluated and run affordably than about whether it is conceptually coherent.

**What I want from you.** The objections this particular room will raise, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it. Eight or nine entries, each giving: who raises it and how likely that is on a three-point scale; the question as one of them would actually put it, first person and colloquial, not as a seminar question; a spoken reply of roughly ninety seconds that I could deliver without notes; and a short paragraph on where that reply is soft, or where it quietly assumes the thing I claimed not to need.
