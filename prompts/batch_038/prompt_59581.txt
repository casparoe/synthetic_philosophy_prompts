My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the ravens paradox, and I have come away convinced that we talked past each other because we were using the phrase *the hypothesis* to mean two different things. I would like you to turn my half-formed idea into a proposal for a summer workshop in formal epistemology; the workshop funds a twelve-page paper and a forty-minute presentation slot, and I have eleven weeks to work on it.

The idea is this. Almost every familiar resolution of the paradox — the ones that grant the white sneaker some confirming power but insist it is negligible, the ones that appeal to the relative sizes of the reference classes or to background information about how many ravens there are, the ones that reject the equivalence condition outright — assumes that the raven generalization and its contrapositive are a single hypothesis under two descriptions. I want to question that assumption. Suppose instead that confirmation attaches not to sentences, and not to sets of possible worlds, but to something finer-grained that is sensitive to which predicates the hypothesis is stated in: call it a structured hypothesis. Then the observation of a white sneaker bears evidentially on the hypothesis as stated in negative terms but not on the hypothesis as stated in terms of ravens, and the paradox is generated purely by our sliding between the two. The genuinely interesting question, and the one I want the proposal organized around, is whether any such finer-grained notion of hypothesis identity can carry the weight: it must deliver the intuitive verdicts in the raven case while preserving everything else we demand of confirmation — in particular, that two ways of writing an ordinary scientific law confirm alike, and that evidence for a conjunction is evidence for each conjunct.

Please write the proposal with the following components:

1. A precise statement of the research question, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than serving as an invitation to describe the state of play.
2. A short argument that the standard resolutions really do share the assumption I have attributed to them. I am not asking for a survey of them; I am asking you to say whether the attribution is fair, and to flag any resolution you think already escapes it — and if one does, to say what is left for my project to do.
3. The method, laid out week by week across the eleven weeks, including what formal machinery I would actually need to build, and a recommendation on whether to work in a probabilistic framework, a comparative-confirmation framework, or something else, with reasons for preferring that one over the obvious alternatives.
4. What success looks like: a concrete statement of the definition-plus-theorem, or the definition-plus-verdicts, that I would be aiming to have in hand by the end. And what would count as failure, including the two failure modes I am most afraid of — that finer-grained hypothesis identity collapses back into logical equivalence once it is made precise, and that it turns out to be an ad hoc device tuned to produce the raven verdicts and nothing else.
5. An honest assessment of scope: what should be cut to fit twelve pages, and what the single claim is that I most need to be able to defend from the floor during questions.

I will add the citations myself, so please refer to positions and arguments by their content rather than by author and date.
