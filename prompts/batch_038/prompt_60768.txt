Write the joint statement that a "conscious duality" theorist and a "phenomenal unity" theorist about the split-brain patient could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration. The two parties are to be understood as follows. The duality theorist holds, in the tradition of Nagel's 1971 Synthese paper on brain bisection and of the conscious-duality model defended against Bayne in Schechter's 2012 paper in *Philosophical Psychology*, that the commissurotomy patient hosts two co-active streams of consciousness, that this is what the lateralized-presentation experiments actually show, and that the counting question — how many subjects, and possibly how many persons — is thereby put in play. The unity theorist holds, in the tradition of the unity thesis in Bayne's 2008 *Journal of Philosophy* paper and the ninth chapter of his 2010 book *The Unity of Consciousness*, that the patient retains a single fully unified phenomenal field, and that the apparent disunity is better explained by something like the switch model, on which consciousness alternates rapidly between hemispheres rather than being doubled.

Structure the document in four parts:

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered list of propositions both would sign without qualification. These must be substantive rather than trivial: include shared commitments about the surgical and anatomical facts, about what the standard experimental paradigms (lateralized tachistoscopic presentation with fixation control, unilateral manual responding, the report-versus-retrieval dissociations, cross-cueing, the left hemisphere's confident verbal rationalizations of behavior it did not initiate, bilateral redundancy gains) do and do not establish, and about the ordinary-life behavior of these patients outside the laboratory. Where a shared claim is a claim about ignorance or open questions, say so explicitly.

2. **Disagreements.** A table with the columns: topic; the duality theorist's claim in wording that theorist endorses; the unity theorist's claim in wording that theorist endorses; whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal; and which crux it reduces to. Neither side may be permitted to characterize the other in loaded or straw terms, and the two wordings must be strong enough that they genuinely conflict.

3. **Cruxes.** For each crux, give the concrete case, paradigm, or argument that both accept as bearing on it, then record each side's prediction *in advance*, in enough operational detail that a later result could be scored against it. Candidates worth considering: whether a task requiring a single comparative judgment across the two visual half-fields, as opposed to two separate reports, can be performed above chance; what violation or non-violation of the race-model inequality in bilateral redundancy paradigms implies for simultaneous versus alternating consciousness; how much weight each side gives to spared anterior and subcortical commissures, and to inter-patient variation in that sparing; and whether unity of the phenomenal field is a precondition for there being a single subject at all, or whether subjecthood can be secured by embodiment, agency, and diachronic continuity even if the field is disunified. If a candidate crux turns out to be one on which both sides predict the same result, mark it as not a genuine crux and explain why.

4. **Falsifiers and the verbal residue.** State what each side would accept as evidence or argument against its own view, and whether there is any single result both agree would move the dispute. Then say plainly which parts of the quarrel are terminological — in particular, whether "one stream of consciousness" is being used for a maximal phenomenally unified set of states, for a single integrative workspace, or for a single point of view, and whether the unity thesis is a claim about all subjects or only about normal ones, so that split-brain patients are either counterexamples or simply non-instances.

The statement should run roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words, in a formal register, and should be self-contained: do not include a bibliography, and do not require the reader to know the literature beyond the anchors named above. No verbatim quotation from any source.
