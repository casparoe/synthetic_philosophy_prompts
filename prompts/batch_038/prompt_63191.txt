I am a third-year doctoral student in public law at a Finnish university, working on a dissertation chapter about the legal status of compulsory national service. My training is doctrinal, so I can describe the arrangement precisely, but I am much less sure how to name the political philosophy it carries, and my supervisor has told me twice now that the chapter describes the rules without ever saying what they assume. I would like your help with exactly that.

Please take the role of a philosopher of law and political obligation with a serious working knowledge of the ethics of conscription, conscientious objection and the fair distribution of burdens. Think carefully rather than quickly. Before committing to a single reading, begin with an explicit brainstorming stage: list the candidate commitments the arrangement might be resting on, including ones that look unpromising at first, and only then adjudicate between them.

The practice, stated concretely so that you do not need to know our statutes:

1. Under Section 127 of the constitution, every citizen is obliged to participate in national defence, but the implementing legislation lays the obligation on male citizens only. Women may volunteer for the same service, and those who complete it may pursue a military career. A woman who does not volunteer owes nothing whatsoever: no service, no payment, no penalty.
2. For men, ordinary military service lasts 165, 255 or 347 days, depending on the duties one is trained for.
3. A man whose convictions prevent him from performing military service may apply for civilian service instead, which is a fixed 347 days — more than twice the shortest military term, and equal to the longest. Until the current Act came into force in 2008, it was 395 days.
4. A man who refuses both forms of service is convicted and sentenced to a fixed 173 days of unconditional imprisonment, with credit for days already served; such sentences are generally served in full.
5. A man examined at the drafting event and found unfit is exempted altogether and owes nothing at all.
6. For several decades a separate statute exempted members of one religious community, the Jehovah's Witnesses, from both military and civilian service entirely. That exemption was repealed with effect from February 2019.
7. A woman who volunteers for military service and interrupts it after the first thirty days is required to complete civilian service instead: her voluntary undertaking is converted into a compulsory duty.
8. Amnesty International has for years maintained that the extra months of civilian service are punitive and that those imprisoned under point 4 are prisoners of conscience. The state's standing reply is, in substance, that service must amount to an equivalent burden for everyone.

What I am after:

- Reconstruct the implicit theory of political obligation here. Is the duty owed by citizens as such, or by men as men? Does it presuppose that liability attaches to a category of persons rather than to anything they have done or agreed to — and if so, is that the same notion of obligation that point 7 relies on when it binds a volunteer by her own act?
- Explain what the day-pricing in point 3 assumes about the state's access to other people's minds. The authorities cannot see anyone's convictions, so they appear to have priced the exit. Does that presuppose that sincerity is revealed by willingness to bear a cost? If so, what follows about the odd fact that the more scrupulous the objector, the more he is asked to give — and about point 5, where incapacity is free while unwillingness costs 182 additional days?
- Say whether the practice presupposes that days of civilian labour and exposure to military discipline, deprivation of liberty and risk are commensurable at some rate, who is assumed to set that rate, and whether the rate is set to compensate those who serve or to deter those who would opt out. These seem to me to be different purposes, and I cannot tell which the length rule is doing.
- Identify the conception of equality that makes point 1 compatible with a constitutional prohibition on discrimination on grounds of sex. Does the arrangement assume that equality is a shield against exclusion from offices and benefits rather than a rule about the distribution of impositions? Would any other conception license it?
- Say what the 2019 repeal in point 6 presupposes — that accommodation must be individual and neutral rather than communal, perhaps — and whether that sits consistently beside a distinction drawn by sex, which is itself group-based.
- Mark clearly, for each reading you advance, whether the practice strictly presupposes it or merely fits comfortably with it, and indicate where the arrangement is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same rules. I suspect some of what I have listed is brute administrative convenience with no theory behind it at all, and I would like to be told which parts those are.
- Finally, take the reading you judge most defensible and say what would have to change — in the length rule, in the criminal provision, in the sex distinction — if it were replaced first by a view on which conscience confers an exemption that cannot be priced, and second by a view on which equality requires parity of imposition rather than merely of opportunity.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list or citations of any kind; I have no way of checking them and they are not what I need. Argue from the structure of the practice alone. Write in British English, and prefer four sharp claims to a survey: I would rather be given a reconstruction I can argue with than a balanced overview.
