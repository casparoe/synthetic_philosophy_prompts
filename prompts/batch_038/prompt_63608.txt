**Draft (v4): "Paying Not to Know"**

**P1.** Your older sister was tested last year for a familial late-onset condition for which there is at present no cure. She has now been given your result as well, and she has put it in an envelope and mailed it to you, telling you plainly that you may open it or destroy it unopened and that she will not mention it again. A friend of mine says destroying it is transparently irrational: you would be throwing away a true belief about your own future for nothing. This paper argues that the intuition behind her objection, though it has a respectable theorem on its side, does not survive contact with a very ordinary set of facts.

**P2.** The respectable theorem is the one my seminar leader calls Good's theorem, after the statistician who proved it. My gloss: for an agent who maximizes expected utility, who updates by conditionalizing on what she learns, and whose available acts and preferences are unchanged by the learning, the expected utility of choosing after a costless observation is never less than the expected utility of choosing before it. The reason is that whatever the observation turns out to be, the agent could always have simply carried out the act she would have chosen anyway; observation only adds options. Equality holds, on my reading, exactly when the act chosen in advance remains optimal after every possible outcome of the observation. I state this without proof and I am least confident about the equality clause.

**P3.** If P2 is right, the conclusion seems immediate: ignorance can never be strictly better than knowledge, and so the practice of honoring a patient's request not to be told her carrier status must rest either on the sheer cost of testing or on a confusion. The rest of this paper is an attempt to show that there is a third possibility, and that it is not exotic.

**P4.** Here is the case, with numbers. Suppose your clinician puts your prior at one in two. There is an annual monitoring program, P, which costs $1,500 in fees and lost working hours whether or not you carry the variant; if you carry it, the early intervention it makes possible is worth $10,000 to you. So P nets +$8,500 if you carry the variant and −$1,500 if you do not, while doing nothing nets $0 either way. Without the information you should enroll, since 0.5(8,500) + 0.5(−1,500) = $3,500, which beats $0. Now add one more act. There is a guaranteed-issue insurance policy, G, sold only to people with no recorded genetic test on file, worth +$4,000 to you in expectation. If you have been tested and you carry the variant, no version of the policy will be written for you. If you have been tested and you do not carry it, a medically underwritten version, G′, is available and worth +$2,000. (Stipulation: the monitoring program screens for symptoms and does not itself create a record of your genotype. I know this stipulation is load-bearing and I will come back to it.) So if you destroy the envelope you get P and G together, $3,500 + $4,000 = $7,500. If you open it, you get $8,500 in the carrier world and $2,000 in the non-carrier world, an expectation of $5,250. Destroying the envelope is worth $2,250 more, and no money changed hands.

**P5.** The obvious repair is to say that the information was not free after all: it cost you $2,250, so the theorem was never violated. I do not think that is the right description, and this is the part of the paper I want to defend. Nothing was deducted from any account of yours. What changed is the set of acts open to you, and it shrank for a specific reason: someone else now holds the same fact you hold, and their holding of it is a condition of what you may buy. I propose to call this the disclosure ratchet. Knowledge, once acquired in an institutional setting, is acquired by the institution with you, and options that were defined by your not knowing cannot be re-entered afterward. It is a ratchet because it runs in only one direction; there is no corresponding act of un-knowing that restores G.

**P6.** So Good's theorem does not fail in my example; it declines to apply. Its premise is that the post-observation act set contains the pre-observation optimum, and what the example is meant to show is that this premise is not a technical convenience but a substantive claim about how knowledge is socially arranged. In a world where testing is recorded, expected utility maximization can require an agent to remain ignorant, and can require it not because information is expensive but because information makes you visible. If that is right, then the value of information is not a property of the information at all. It is a property of the audience.

**P7.** Two loose ends I should mention rather than hide. First, the theorem also assumes preferences are fixed, and learning that you carry the variant may change what you want; that is a different escape route and I do not think mine collapses into it, but I have not shown that. Second, my case assumed you must choose for yourself. You could instead pay a cousin to open the envelope and telephone you only if you carry the variant, which recovers most of the value of knowing while leaving G intact, and I would like to end the paper by suggesting that the right question is not whether to know but who is permitted to know on your behalf. Whether a cousin who has legal duties of her own breaks this repair, I leave open.

**P8.** The modest thesis, then: the value of a piece of information is not fixed by the proposition it conveys, but by the arrangement under which it is conveyed.
