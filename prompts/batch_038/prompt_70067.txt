I spent thirty-eight years as a physical chemist and have only since retiring begun reading philosophy with any seriousness, so please correct me where my vocabulary fails. Last week the philosophy-of-mind reading group I audit at the local university was visited by a doctoral student in machine learning who gave a live demonstration, and the discussion afterward hardened into an argument between him and the professor who convenes the group. I was the note-taker and have their exchange down close to verbatim. I should like you to referee it.

The demonstration: the student typed into a large language model a short scenario — a robot arm lifts a mug of cold coffee from a laboratory bench and sets it into a dishwasher, which is then switched on — and asked it to list everything true now and everything true in five minutes. The model answered at once that the coffee was sloshing, that the bench carried a wet ring, that the mug would come out warm, empty and inverted, and that a paper label on the mug might have loosened.

Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) The student said this dissolves the frame problem: no one wrote a frame axiom, no one enumerated non-effects, and the model nonetheless produced the mundane consequences. Relevance knowledge is in the weights, learned rather than axiomatized, so the problem was an artifact of insisting on hand-written logic.

(2) The professor said he had run two problems together. The technical frame problem — how to assert that nothing else changes without an axiom for every action-property pair — was largely settled inside logic itself decades ago, by writing one successor-state axiom per property rather than per action-property pair; so the demonstration cannot be a solution to that problem, since that problem no longer needs solving. What the demonstration was billed as solving is the wider problem philosophers took up in the 1980s: how an agent picks out the relevant facts in a situation it has not been told how to describe.

(3) The student replied that this was mere relabeling, and that the wider problem is an inflation of a technical one: if the model reliably yields the right consequences in exactly the situations humans find unremarkable, then whatever "relevance" is supposed to be, the model has it, and there is no further question to ask.

(4) The professor then edited the scenario in front of the group: the mug became a paper cup with a loose lid, and the bench — which forty lines earlier had been described as holding a tray of samples scheduled for the freezer — was otherwise unchanged. He asked the same question. The model reproduced most of its first answer nearly word for word, including the loosening paper label, and said nothing about the samples. That, he said, is the frame problem: the list of consequences is fixed by the shape of the question and by the typical case in the data, not by the situation, when the situation is the only thing entitled to fix it.

(5) The student's closing move was that (4) is an engineering complaint, not a philosophical one — people too miss a relevant fact buried forty lines back in what they have read — and the remedy is better retrieval or longer effective context; and that if the professor insists the failure is principled, he owes an account of what humans do that the model does not, given that human relevance is equally a matter of having been reared among typical cases.

Please rate each of the five moves — clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification apiece, and then say who is more right overall. I am not after a survey of the literature or a list of readings; I want your own verdict, stated flatly and with reasons, including on the two questions I could not settle myself: whether the technical frame problem and the wider problem of relevance are one problem or two, and whether move (4) reveals something principled or only something fixable. If you judge that one party is using a term in a sense the other is not, say so explicitly and say which sense you are adopting when you rule.
