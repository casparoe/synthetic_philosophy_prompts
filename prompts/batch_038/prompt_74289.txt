I teach a 100-minute seminar in a second-year history of philosophy course, eighteen students, on al-Ghazālī's treatment of causation in the Incoherence of the Philosophers. They have read the seventeenth discussion in translation, but nearly all of them arrive with one of two shortcuts: either Ghazālī is a skeptic about induction in medieval dress, or he is simply invoking God whenever an explanation runs out. I want them to encounter the position before they are given it, and to encounter it twice over. First, they should discover from inside the activity that a record of regularities, however long and however carefully kept, licenses expectation without ever delivering the necessity the philosophers wanted from natures — and that this discovery leaves ordinary practice, medicine and astronomy and the rest, entirely intact. Second, they should be made to describe some small deliberate act of their own in terms that leave no residue for them to claim, and then feel how uncomfortable, and how genuinely arguable, that residue is.

Design the activity. I have card stock, colored tokens, dice, sealed envelopes, and a whiteboard, and I am willing to play a role myself, but I would rather the structure do the teaching: no exposition before the debrief, and the words occasionalism, acquisition, and custom, along with the name Hume, stay unspoken until afterwards. Two further constraints. The game must not be winnable by deducing my hidden rule; if the cleverest students can beat it by guessing the pattern, the point is lost. And three or four of them will insist throughout that fire really burns and cotton really has a nature to be burned — give those students a part with actual work in it rather than letting them sulk on the margins.

Please supply: the complete rules and materials list; a scoring scheme in points rather than money; role assignments, including whatever scripted lines I should permit myself and what I should refuse to answer; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief, which maps particular moments in the play onto particular doctrines — the denial that the tie between what is habitually called cause and what is habitually called effect is a necessary one, God's custom and the fact that it admits of interruption, the Ash'arite account of acquisition, and Ibn Rushd's rejoinder in his own Incoherence that stripping things of their causal natures leaves the world with nothing knowable in it; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover from each; and a frank statement of which features of the position the activity cannot reproduce and I must therefore supply by hand. I am most worried about the hidden-producer structure that any such game seems to require, since it threatens to cast Ghazālī as a trickster or a deceiver, which he explicitly is not; I would like the design to push against that temptation rather than exploit it.
