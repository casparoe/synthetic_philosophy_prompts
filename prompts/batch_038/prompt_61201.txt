I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a piece I am currently reporting began in clinical psychiatry and arrived, unexpectedly, at the Second Meditation. The clinical material concerns patients who report that thoughts are being placed in their heads by someone else — a neighbour, a broadcasting agency, a device. What strikes me is the precise shape of the report: they do not doubt that thinking is going on, and they are often strikingly articulate about the content of the thoughts; what has gone missing is the sense that the thinking is theirs. Meanwhile Descartes's argument from his own thinking to his existence is still treated as the paradigm of a certainty that survives any doubt, and it is still wheeled out to do heavy lifting in debates about minds, selves, and machines.

I want you to make the strongest case you can for a position I have been circling and cannot yet defend properly. The position is this: the cogito, taken at its most certain, does not establish that there is a thinker. What survives every attempt at doubt is an impersonal claim — that thinking is occurring here, now — and the move from that to the existence of a subject who owns it is not a further insight but a grammatical reflex, the standing assumption that whatever thinking happens must happen to someone. On this view the assurance people feel when they rehearse the argument is borrowed wholesale from the impersonal core: the first-person term contributes nothing that could be coherently doubted and nothing that is known. It follows that the cogito proves far less than Descartes needed — no persisting self, no substantial soul, no foothold for a system of knowledge.

Argue for it, in a form I could learn from and be answerable to. Set the argument out with its load-bearing premises marked rather than buried in prose, and say exactly what the cogito does prove on this account: the strongest true claim it supports, stated carefully enough that I would not have to retreat from it under questioning. I am aware that the impersonal reformulation has a pedigree — an eighteenth-century aphorist already remarked that Descartes said too much and that the honest formulation runs the way one says it lightens or it rains — but I do not want that observation repeated at me. I want to know whether it can be turned into an argument, and what it costs.

Work with this case, and press it against the position where it cuts the other way: a subject who is intact, rational and introspectively reliable, but for whom every thought arrives tagged as belonging to someone else, so that the impersonal avowal — thinking is going on — carries for him all the assurance Descartes claims, while the first-personal conclusion is unavailable to him altogether. Is his situation genuinely conceivable, and does its conceivability show that the two claims come apart, or does it merely show a malfunction that leaves the ordinary case untouched?

Then take the Cartesian replies head-on instead of listing them: that pathological phenomenology cannot tell us about the logic of first-personal avowal; that the patient who disowns a thought still possesses the concept of ownership through other mental states, so nothing conceptual has been dislodged; that on the performative reading the very act of doubting enacts a doubter and cannot be peeled away from it; and that there is no such thing as stating impersonally that thinking is occurring, because stating it is already the act of a subject. I would also like you to consider whether Descartes's own qualification — that the certainty in question holds only for as long as the thinking lasts — is a concession in my favour or the thing that saves him.

Finish by telling me plainly where the argument is weakest, which premise a competent opponent should attack first, and what I would be overstating if I printed the position as stated. I am not after a survey of who has said what or a list of further reading; I want the reasoning itself.
