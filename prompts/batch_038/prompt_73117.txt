I write novels for a living, which is a weird thing to be doing on the board of a fact-checking cooperative, but seven years ago they liked something I said in an interview about how a sentence can be true and still be a lie, and here I am. We're tiny: forty-odd regional newsrooms pool money, a paper in one town asks us whether a claim in a press release holds up, we dig and send back a ruling plus a short justification they can actually print. Our founder, who's still chair, was a philosophy lecturer before he burned out of it, and the standard he wrote in 2011 has one hard rule: every claim we certify has to trace back through a chain of supporting claims to something on the Ledger — a fixed list, about 140 entries, of sources we treat as terminal and never check. Official statistics, court records, the big peer-reviewed journals, a couple of wires. Nothing else counts as an anchor. It worked while the Ledger was trustworthy and then it stopped. Two of the entries have since been bought by the parent company of a hospital network we cover; one "independent" trade journal is now basically a content-marketing shop; the national statistics office changed its methodology three years ago and nobody touched the entry. So we have three revision proposals on the table and apparently it's my job to write the memo that gets circulated. Proposal A (chair's compromise): keep the termination requirement but make the Ledger a living document — re-vet every entry every two years, and allow a terminal source to be suspended the moment its independence comes into question. Proposal B (our data journalist): scrap the Ledger and grade confidence instead — every link in a chain multiplies the confidence by a fixed factor, she wants 0.85, and we certify whatever clears a threshold, no matter how long the chain runs; someone already noted that if parallel chains are allowed to pool, B turns into C with extra arithmetic, and she hasn't answered it. Proposal C (our two investigative reporters): certify a claim when enough supporting claims that don't share sources form a closed web with no gaps in it — no anchor needed, mutual support does the work; their weak spot is that "don't share sources" isn't measurable, and in the case below two of the three nurses went to school together.

Here's the case that has to be ruled on Thursday, and it's the reason the board stopped pretending the standard works. A regional hospital network advertises a 30-day mortality rate for a heart procedure that's way better than the national figure, and a partner paper wants to publish our verification that the number is misleading — that the network transfers its highest-risk patients out to a tertiary centre before day 30 so they never count against it. What we actually have: three current nurses willing to talk only on background, two of whom trained together and socialize; a transfer log leaked by a former data analyst whose authenticity can only be checked against that same analyst's description of its own format; the national registry's methodology document, which is what defines the measure — and which lists among its contributors a consultant the network paid until recently; and one academic paper using the registry's data, which is where our suspicion about the transfers started in the first place. Every route down ends at a floor that isn't a floor. So the memo has to answer two things. One: what do we rule on the hospital claim — certify, refuse, or certify some narrower sentence, in which case spell out the exact sentence we'd stand behind and what the paper can print. Two: which of A, B or C we adopt, including whether it applies to this case or only prospectively, since retroactively switching the rule is how we'd get accused of shopping for a verdict. If the honest answer is none of them, give me a fourth thing, but it has to be operable by people who aren't philosophers and who have to answer a phone on Monday morning.

Last bit, and this is the part I actually care about. I have no training in any of this, but four years of these meetings has left me suspecting that A, B and C aren't three practical patches on a practical problem — they're three answers to one question, and that question is older than our organization and probably older than the Ledger's 140 entries. If you think I'm right, put it in the memo: name the question in plain language the chair won't experience as an attack, say which proposal answers it which way, and say whether any of the three is a non-answer wearing a policy costume. What I don't want is a survey of verification methodologies. I want the bottom line in the first paragraph, about 800 words, something I can send round Wednesday night without being embarassed, and it has to include a real paragraph on the strongest thing that can be said for whichever option loses — two of the six board members will vote for it and they are not stupid. (I'm supposed to be drafting a novel about a lighthouse keeper's widow, for what it's worth, which is why I'm doing governance work at eleven at night.)
