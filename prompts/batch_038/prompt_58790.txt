I spent forty years in geophysical inverse problems — mostly travel-time tomography, some electromagnetic sounding — and retired three years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy with essentially no training in it, and I've just worked my way through Quine on translation and Davidson on radical interpretation. The odd thing is that reading it I kept having the feeling I already know this problem, only I know a much more precise version of it.

So I'd like you to do a translation job for me, and then tell me where the translation breaks.

First, restate the Davidsonian position — the interpreter working from scratch on another speaker's behaviour, the principle of charity, holism about belief and meaning, and the claim that interpretation is indeterminate — as faithfully as you can into the vocabulary I actually use. Forward operator, misfit, null space, nonuniqueness, regularization, damping and smoothing, resolution kernels, the resolution-variance trade-off, priors, parameter trade-offs, Occam-style inversion where you take the smoothest model that still fits to within the noise. Be as literal as you can stand to be. Some candidate mappings I came up with myself; tell me which are right and which are just me being too pleased with myself:

- charity is a regularizer, something you bolt on to pick a member out of the equivalence class.
- indeterminacy is exact nonuniqueness rather than practical nonuniqueness — a fact about the operator, not about how much data you happen to have collected so far. Is that really what's being claimed? Because those two are completely seperate things and the way I read it the argument slides between them.
- belief/meaning holism is parameter trade-off: you can't invert for one of them while holding the other fixed, you only ever constrain combinations.
- anomalous monism means there is no forward operator from the physical to the mental. If so, in what sense is there an inverse problem at all? An inverse problem with no forward model isn't a hard problem, its not a problem.
- and the one that worries me most: in my field the data is what it is whether or not you've got a model for it. That's the whole reason inversion works. Davidson seems to deny there's any such thing as evidence that hasn't already been interpreted. So does the notion of data itself survive the trip over, or do I lose it at the border?

Second, the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Sort every claim you translated into three buckets: maps cleanly onto my framework; maps only if something gets bent out of shape, and if so say precisely what you bent; and has no counterpart at all in it.

Third, and this is the discipline part: don't let my framework smuggle itself in. Wherever the translation only works because you quietly assumed a detached third-party observer with clean access to the evidence, flag it, even when flagging it makes the mapping look worse than it did a second ago. I'd rather end up knowing whether the residue points at something inverse theory genuinely can't express, or whether it points at the philosophical claim being vaguer than it sounded when I read it. Either verdict is fine with me, I'm not invested in my own analogy working.

No references or reading list please, I've got a stack on the shelf already. Just your reasoning, around 1200 words.
