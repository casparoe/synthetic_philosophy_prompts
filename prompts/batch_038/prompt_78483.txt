I run the in-house training for a community mental health clinic, and once a quarter I get the new peer support specialists for ninety minutes in a small back room with a whiteboard and no projector. Eleven of them this time. Every single one has been on the patient side of the desk, most of them carry a diagnosis they actively use to describe themselves, and two are pretty openly convinced psychiatry is a scam that happens to pay their salaries. The topic I have to teach is Ian Hacking's looping effect — the idea that some kinds of people are interactive rather than indifferent, so that classifying them changes what they are, which changes the classification, and round it goes — and then what that actually implies for the DSM categories these people live inside. They have all heard some version of mental illness is socially constructed about a hundred times and their eyes glaze over, so I can't open with that framing or I've lost the room before I start.

I don't want you to explain looping effects to me, I want a plan for how I explain it. So: the order the pieces have to go in and what genuinely depends on what. My current sketch is to warm up on a non-human kind first (plutonium-239 doesn't care that we named it, quarks don't reorganize themselves in response to a journal article), then draw the interactive/indifferent distinction, then do the loop itself on the board as a cycle, and only then touch psychiatry. Tell me whether that warm-up is a good idea or a trap — I worry it plants exactly the wrong seed, that people who loop are somehow softer or less real than the stuff in the periodic table. Then: one example to carry the whole thing. I'm leaning toward adult ADHD and the short self-diagnosis videos people watch on their phones, because the loop is fast enough that you can watch it close in real time and half the room has skin in the game. Is that the right choice for this audience or will it blow up? And what does a carrying example for this idea have to have and not have — I've got a feeling an example where the only loop is get-a-diagnosis, get-services, feel-relieved won't do the work, and neither will one where the category just quietly died.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I actually need. List them in the order they'll surface rather than the order they occur to me, and say where in the sequence I head each one off instead of mopping up at the end: that it means my diagnosis isn't real and was invented by a drug company; that this is just labeling theory or the self-fulfilling prophecy with fancier vocabulary; that the real content is psychiatry-is-politics; that if every kind loops then no classification can be better than any other so we should just mirror whatever words the client uses; and the mirror image of that one, which is that knowing about looping means we should stop saying diagnostic words at all. Also tell me which of those is the one that's genuinely hardest to dislodge in this room, and whether I should let it stay partly standing. Two more things: give me one check I can run in the last ten minutes — a question, or something small they do — that would show they've got the mechanism, the feedback between the classification and the classified, and not just the general mood of the session. And tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say out loud that I'm leaving out, because I suspect Hacking's whole historical-ontology apparatus and his fugue case study and the multiple-personality memory wars are all off-limits in ninety minutes, plus the question of whether looping makes psychiatry less of a science. If anything in my sketch above is just wrong, say so bluntly.
