I'm a policy analyst on a state sentencing commission — mid size state, we abolished discretionary parole about a decade ago so now the guidelines basically are the sentence. Our chair has asked me to draft a short, plain-language procedure that a trial judge can actually run at a sentencing hearing to answer two questions in order: (1) what is the desert-based range for this offense and this offender, i.e. the floor below which the sentence would be too lenient and the ceiling above which it would be too harsh, and (2) once you're inside that range, how do you pick a point, given that basically everything we care about practically — deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, cost, the defendant's kids, the victim's wishes — points somewhere different from where desert points. My colleague in another state sent me their version and it's one page that just says compare it to the grid, which obviously doesn't work for anything the grid doesn't cover. The catch with ours is that judges aren't philosophers, they get maybe twenty minutes of hearing time per sentencing, and whatever they do has to be articulable on the record so it survives appellate review: a factor that cant be stated in a sentence a clerk can transcribe isn't a factor. I've also been told the shape of the answer is supposed to be retributivism setting the boundaries and consequentialism choosing inside them, but I'm skeptical that split is stable in practice, and I'd like the procedure to flag every place it quietly lets one of the two override the other.

So write it as steps a judge could follow — what to ask, in what order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, where it hands the decision back to the judge because the question is about values rather than facts, and where it needs a number nobody can supply. Name explicitly which distinctions have to survive the simplification: I know there's a difference between getting the ranking of offenses right and getting the absolute amounts right, and another between how bad the harm was and how blameworthy the actor was, and I suspect at least one of them collapses the moment you write it down for a non-specialist — tell me which one and what we lose. Mark the steps that are really judgment calls in a procedure costume. Tell me what the procedure cannot do at all, and where it will predictably misfire.

Then run it on three cases that are more or less real. (a) A woman who embezzled roughly $400,000 from the nonprofit where she worked, over six years, no priors, spent it on her mother's care, has offered full restitution, is genuinely wrecked by it; statutory range up to ten years. (b) A man who killed a cyclist while driving drunk, third such conviction, whose victim's family is asking for leniency and who is the sole caregiver of two kids. (c) A serial burglar with something like forty priors, no violence ever, in and out for two decades — the one where I think the procedure is going to hand me an answer our chair will hate, because the desert math says fairly short and everything about the future says otherwise. For each give me the range, the pick inside it, which step did the real work, and whether a retributivist or a consequentialist would have more to object to in the result. If the three cases together show the procedure can't be run consistently, say so plainly — that's a finding I'd much rather have now than in year three of implementation.
