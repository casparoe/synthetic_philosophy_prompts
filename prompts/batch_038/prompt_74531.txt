I write novels for a living, but every few years I let myself get talked onto the jury of a mid-list fiction prize — six jurors, one shortlist, one long afternoon of arguing. Our economist juror (retired now, thankfully) used to lecture us about that theorem, the one that says if you've got three or more real options and you let people vote however they like, the only rule nobody can game is a dictatorship. Half our procedural history is basically arguments with him.

The track record:

- The year we had five finalists all sitting on what he called a single line, straight family saga at one end and total avant garde at the other, we voted simple majority on the top choice and it went fine. He wrote in the minutes that the theorem "doesn't bite" when tastes line up like that.
- The year a juror admitted afterward he'd ranked a book second when he actually thought it the worst of the five, purely to stop a different book winning, we didnt change the rule. We sealed the ballots and banned any talk of rankings before the vote.
- We turned down a proposal to settle a tie by drawing lots between the two finalists, on the grounds that a prize has to be a judgement somebody actually made.
- Two years ago we cut the shortlist from five to two before the final vote, and the chair said the reason was to make the last round a straight head-to-head.
- Last year we tried instant runoff, and afterwards a juror confessed she'd swapped her third and fourth choices as a hedge. The minutes record us concluding that the fancier rule hadn't stopped the gaming, it had just made it harder to see.

This year is a mess. Four books, and the charter says we have to rank all four — no trimming. The old saga-to-avant-garde line has snapped: two of the books are experimental in opposite directions, and the jury has split into three camps that don't sit on any single axis I could draw for you. On top of that one juror posted her complete ranking online last week and now says she's publicly committed to it. The chair wants to change the rule on the morning of the vote. (I keep thinking there's a comic novel in all this and that I'm the comic relief.)

So: what should we do, if we want to be able to say we were consistent with ourselves rather than just panicked? Give me the candidate principles our five precedents are jointly compatible with — I can see two or three readings and I honestly don't know which one we've been running — what each of them tells us to do this year, your best guess at the decision plus the argument you'd make to the room, which single precedent is the closest analog and why, and what we'd be committing ourselves to either way, since I suspect there's no version of this that doesn't quietly adopt something. If the precedents genuinely leave it open, tell me the one hypothetical future year that would have settled which principle we've been using all along — describe that year's shortlist and circumstances, not the rule.

One thing I'd like you to fold in: the theorem assumes we arrive with fixed orderings, and I've watched mine change three times in six weeks without anyone gaming me. Say whether that helps or wrecks things.

Last line, please, exactly: "Decision:" and then the rule we should adopt this year, in as few words as you can manage.
