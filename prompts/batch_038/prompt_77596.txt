The question in the packet read: Williams and Dawkins argued that appeals to group selection should be driven out of evolutionary biology in favor of selection acting on genes and, for most purposes, on whole organisms. (a) Reconstruct their central argument against group selection. (b) Explain how Hamilton's work on inclusive fitness was supposed to absorb the phenomena that had motivated group-selectionist talk in the first place. (c) Sober and Wilson's multilevel framework is often described as a revival of group selection — is that a fair description? And are a kin-selection account and a group-selection account of the same trait rival explanations, or two vocabularies for one underlying process?

Here is the answer, essentially as submitted:

"(a) The argument is really two arguments stapled together. The first is about heredity. For selection to build an adaptation at some level, the entities at that level have to vary, reproduce, and pass their properties on with reasonable fidelity, and groups mostly fail the second and third conditions: they don't have discrete generations, they bud and merge and split irregularly, and a daughter group doesn't inherit the parent group's composition the way a daughter cell inherits a genome. Genes by contrast are copied with high fidelity and are potentially immortal in the lineage sense, which is why Dawkins separates replicators from vehicles and says only replicators are units of selection in the strict sense — organisms and groups are both vehicles. The second argument is about strength, and it does most of the work. Even granting a group-level benefit, any group of altruists leaves room for a selfish type that collects the benefits and pays none of the costs, and within a single generation that type out-reproduces its neighbors; Dawkins's name for the problem is roughly that the group gets subverted from within by its own members. Between-group selection is slow because there are few groups and they divide rarely, while within-group selection is fast because individuals reproduce every season, so group selection would have to be enormous to keep ahead of that, and there's no sign that it is. Williams adds the methodological rule on top: never postulate a higher level of selection than you have to. Individual selection accounts for nearly all of what needs accounting for, and genic selection gets brought in only for the awkward cases — meiosis, the maintenance of sex, senescence — where what's good for the individual doesn't add up. Since groups aren't replicators, though, the upshot is that group selection isn't merely uneconomical but impossible: a trait that spread because it was good for the group would be a contradiction in terms. And the same goes for the individual, which is only ever a vehicle, so all talk of the individual being selected is loose shorthand that could be replaced by genic talk without loss.

(b) What made group selection attractive was altruism — warning calls, sterile castes, cooperative breeding — traits that cost the bearer and help neighbors. Hamilton showed you don't need groups, you need relatedness. A gene for costly helping can still spread if the cost to the actor is outweighed by the benefit to the recipient discounted by how likely the recipient is to carry the same gene, which gives Hamilton's rule, rb > c, where r is the probability that a gene in the recipient is identical by descent to the one in the actor, b is the benefit to the recipient and c is the cost to the actor. So what the group-selectionists had picked out as a group turned out to be a cluster of relatives, and the welfare of the group was never doing any causal work; what was being selected was a gene whose copies happened to be concentrated among the beneficiaries. The key point is that this isn't a rival mechanism to ordinary natural selection. It's the same gene-frequency arithmetic with a relatedness term added, which is why Dawkins treats it not as a concession to the group-selectionists but as the best advertisement the gene's-eye view ever got.

(c) Sober and Wilson's multilevel framework is usually sold as a revival of group selection, and in a narrow sense it is: they write down explicit trait-group models in which selection runs both within groups and between them, and they show that altruism can increase when between-group differences are large enough relative to the free-riding inside groups — a condition that turns out to be structurally Hamilton's rule again, since relatedness is just a measure of how much groups differ from one another in the frequency of the type. The more interesting claim is that kin selection theory isn't a rival to group selection theory but a special case of it: a model that uses relatedness can be rewritten as a trait-group model and back again without changing any prediction. Once that translation works both ways, the dispute was never about the facts at all, only about notation, and Sober and Wilson say so plainly, which is exactly the point Dawkins had been making all along — that the two camps agreed on everything except vocabulary. So for the last part: a kin-selection account and a group-selection account of the same trait are two descriptions of one process, and the units-of-selection debate was a fight over words that the translation results have now settled."
