I am writing a paper for a senior seminar on the philosophy of spacetime, and I would like your help thinking through the historical episode I have chosen, in a disciplined way. The episode is Einstein's abandonment of generally covariant field equations and his return to them two years later. So that you do not have to reconstruct the chronology, here it is as I understand it. In the 1913 theory Einstein developed with Grossmann (the Entwurf theory), the gravitational field equations are covariant only under a restricted class of coordinate transformations, and one of the reasons Einstein offered for that restriction was a thought experiment about a matter-free region, now called the hole argument. It runs roughly as follows: take a region of spacetime from which matter is absent, with the metric already fixed everywhere outside it; apply a coordinate transformation that is the identity outside the region but moves the points inside it; if the field equations are generally covariant, and if the points of the manifold are individuated independently of the metric fields defined on them, then both the original and the transformed assignment satisfy the equations and agree with all the boundary data, so nothing outside the region determines the field inside it. Einstein took this to be an unacceptable failure of determinism and concluded that generally covariant equations were inadmissible. In November 1915 he reversed himself and presented the generally covariant field equations now associated with the theory, and in his 1916 review paper he answered the hole argument with the point-coincidence line: coordinates are mere labels, and what is physically real are coincidences among events, such as a pointer meeting a mark on a scale or two worldlines crossing, on which diffeomorphism-related solutions always agree.

What I want is an analysis of this episode through the lens of the hole argument in the form it is now standardly stated, that is, as revived in the 1980s by Earman and Norton as an argument against spacetime substantivalism rather than against general covariance, with the analysis running in both directions. First, set the modern argument out as a dilemma and compare it premise by premise with the 1913 version: my suspicion, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that these are not quite the same argument, since the modern one turns on the claim that diffeomorphic models represent physically distinct situations, whereas Einstein may have been making the different claim that his equations failed to determine the metric uniquely from boundary data. Say explicitly which premises are shared, which are not, and what follows if they are not. Second, identify what Einstein actually gave up in November 1915, and whether he was entitled to it: did he abandon a metaphysical commitment about the individuation of manifold points, or merely a gloss on what it means for equations to determine a field? Does the later construction of a well-posed Hamiltonian initial-value formulation of the generally covariant equations show that the 1913 determinism worry was simply mistaken about the mathematics, or does that formulation already presuppose the reinterpretation the worry was pressing? Third, take account of Kretschmann's objection that any theory whatsoever can be recast in generally covariant form, and of Einstein's concession that covariance therefore carries no physical content by itself and functions as a heuristic demand: what does that concession do to the reading of the 1913 inference as an argument from determinism rather than as an expression of a methodological requirement Einstein had adopted for independent reasons? Fourth, and this is the part I find most interesting, explain the asymmetry between the two episodes: the same argument, in modern hands, is treated as threatening substantivalism rather than covariance. What had to change between 1913 and the 1980s for the second horn of the dilemma to become the attractive one, and does the fact that a working physicist rejected the first horn for two years on the strength of this argument constitute evidence about the argument, or only a fact about Einstein?

Please be scrupulous about the mapping: name the players and the premises, distinguish what Einstein is recorded as having argued from what his biographers and commentators have reconstructed on his behalf, and say where the record is thin or contested instead of filling the gap. I am not asking for references or a bibliography, only for the reasoning. When you are done, tell me what would change your mind: which technical or historical consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where you think your own reconstruction is most likely to have failed, particularly if you have inferred Einstein's reasons rather than reporting them. Finally, end your reply with one line, and only that line, beginning Verdict: followed by exactly one of these labels, chosen for the 1913 argument as Einstein deployed it: UNSOUND (a premise of the argument was false); INVALID (the conclusion does not follow from the premises, whatever their truth); SOUND-AGAINST-COVARIANCE (given the metaphysical options Einstein had before him, the inference was correct and his 1915 reversal required an unargued change of metaphysics); SOUND-ONLY-AGAINST-HAECCEITISM (correct, but only against a view about the individuation of spacetime points that Einstein never explicitly endorsed); or NOT-ONE-ARGUMENT (the 1913 and modern versions diverge enough that no single verdict transfers, in which case say which verdict attaches to which).
