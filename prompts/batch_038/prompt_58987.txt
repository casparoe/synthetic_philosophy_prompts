I'm in a third-year seminar on well-being and value theory and we did the experience machine this week alongside a chunk on death and whether it can harm you, and I tried to write an argument for my essay (due Friday, not that I'm stressed) that glues the two topics together. My roommate and I went at it for like two hours and I came out the other side with something that proves way more than I can defend, so before I hand it in I want to know where the leak is. Here's what I've got:

(1) Ask people whether they'd plug into the machine and almost everyone says no, even though the machine life is stipulated to contain experiences just as good as the real one, and the person inside can't tell the difference. (2) The only thing separating the real life from the machine life is that in the real one, things actually are the way they seem to the person living it, and in the machine they only seem that way. (3) So contact with reality is a requirement for well-being: a life goes better for the person to the extent that the central features of it are real rather than merely apparent. (4) If (3) is right, then someone can be worse off because of a fact they never detect — a spouse who's been unfaithful for thirty years, a best friend who's been quietly contemptuous of you the whole time. (5) Nothing in (4) turns on *when* the undetected fact obtains, only on its being a fact about your life, and a fact about your life is a fact about your life whether it holds while you're alive or afterwards. (6) So someone can be worse off because of facts that obtain entirely after they're dead: forgotten within a generation, their work pulped, their name dragged through the mud, their kids ruined. (7) But Epicurus's line is that harm needs a subject who is harmed at some time, and if there's no time at which the dead person is worse off than they were, there's no harm done at all. (8) So (3) has to go, and the machine case doesn't show that reality matters for your good — it only shows that we *want* reality, which is a fact about our desires and not about our welfare. (9) Which means hedonism walks away from the experience machine basically unscathed, which is a conclusion I really don't want.

Can you tell me which step to give up, why that step looked completely innocent when I wrote it down, and what it actually costs me to give it up? I lean toward keeping the machine intuition and biting the bullet on posthumous harm, so spell out what that bullet consists of — if you deny (7) you still owe me an account of *when* the dead person is supposed to be worse off, and I don't see how that goes. Also, is the thing even valid as it stands, or am I smuggling something in?
