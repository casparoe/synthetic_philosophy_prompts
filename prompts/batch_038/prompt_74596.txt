I am preparing a chapter for a volume on perception and epistemic justification, and before I hand it in I would like an audit of the package of commitments I have been defending, because a colleague in the department suggested last week that I am holding things together by equivocation and I could not immediately tell whether she was right. I run a philosophy-of-psychology seminar in which this material comes up every year, and what follows is roughly the position I have been assigning and defending there, so I would rather not discover the problem in a referee's report. The commitments are these. (a) A modest foundationalism about perception: an experience as of an F can confer non-inferential, defeasible, prima facie justification on the belief that there is an F, without the subject needing any further positive reason to trust her senses. (b) My own principle, which I call the Insulation Condition, and which I take to be the substantive addition I am making: justification is conferred by an experience to the degree that its phenomenal character is counterfactually stable with respect to the subject's non-perceptual mental states — that is, the more the character of the experience would change, holding proximal stimulation fixed, as we vary what the subject believes, expects, wants, or wishes, the less justification it confers. Encapsulation is thus not a binary property a system either has or lacks but a scalar one, and justification tracks the scalar. (c) Encapsulation in the classical sense is false, and the evidence that convinces me is not drawn from expertise but from need and desire: the old studies in which coins and food are overestimated in size relative to neutral discs of equal diameter, and the more recent work in which a desired object is judged to be nearer, or a slope steeper, than it is judged by the unburdened or unmotivated subject. I take such results at face value, as reports about how things look, not merely about what subjects say. (d) Where penetration occurs, the experience is downgraded; I want this to fall out of (b) rather than to be an independent postulate. (e) I accept the predictive-processing framework as our best general account of how perception works, but I do not think the priors it posits constitute penetration: those priors are, on my telling, statistical regularities acquired from the perceptual stream itself and sealed off from the subject's beliefs, desires, and conceptual repertoire. (f) Expertise cases — the radiologist who sees the nodule where the novice sees mottled grey, the ornithologist who sees a warbler rather than a small brown bird, the oenologist whose experience of a glass is differentiated by a vocabulary she acquired in adulthood — are, on my view, genuine cases of penetration with genuinely altered phenomenology, and they are epistemically benign, indeed enhancing; the expert's experience is better justified than the novice's, other things equal. (g) I want the distinction between rational and arational penetration to do real work: an influence on experience that the subject could recognize and assess as bearing on the truth of what she sees is different in kind from a mere causal nudge, and I suspect only the former has the interesting epistemic consequences.

Let me add one thing, because it is where I feel least worried and therefore where I am most likely to be blind. I regard the difference between the (c)-type influences and the (f)-type influences as the least controversial element of the package: nobody, so far as I can see, thinks that acquired expertise is epistemically suspect in the way that hunger, fear, and longing are, and I have been treating that asymmetry as a fixed point against which any general account of penetration must be checked. I have also never thought that (a) and (d) are in any tension whatever, since a downgrade seems to me to concern the weight of a justifier rather than its kind; and I do not see how (e) could conflict with (g), since the first is a claim about what counts as perceptual processing and the second a claim about how influences on processing are to be epistemically appraised. If either of those dismissals is too quick, I would like to be told so plainly.

So: go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely look that way. Rank the genuine ones by severity, most damaging first, and for each say in a sentence or two exactly what has to give, and why the pair cannot be reconciled by refinement. For each genuine tension, give me the cheapest available revision together with what that revision costs elsewhere in the package — I want to see the price, not just the repair. Then tell me which single revision would do the most cleaning up across the whole set. Finally, for every repair you propose, say explicitly whether it leaves (b) recognizably intact or replaces it with a different principle wearing its name: my suspicion, which I cannot yet articulate properly, is that several of the obvious repairs would turn the Insulation Condition into a reliabilist clause about the truth-conductiveness of the process, which would no longer be a claim about encapsulation at all, and if so I would rather know that now than be persuaded of it in front of an audience.
