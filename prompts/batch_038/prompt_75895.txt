I sit on the planning commission of a midsized city in the United States, population roughly three hundred thousand. In two weeks we vote on a recommendation to the city council: whether to permit fourplexes by right — no discretionary permit, no case-by-case review — on every residential parcel within a half mile of our two busiest transit corridors. The commission will recommend yes, six to one. I am the one. I have been asked to file a dissent, and it will be read by the other commissioners, by council staff, and by residents of the affected streets, none of whom have any patience for academic prose.

I do not want to file the dissent I keep hearing from the neighborhood associations, and I do not want a version of it that is self-interest in a nicer coat. I want to know whether there is an honest argument for giving people who already live on a street some genuine say over changes in what may be built on it, and I want you to make that argument.

Here is the framework I have been circling. Adopt it, revise it, or replace it with something better if you can find something better. Distinguish preferences about one's surroundings from stakes in them. A preference is a state of affairs I would like to obtain and would be disappointed not to get. A stake is something I have built or been given that depends for its continued existence on those surroundings remaining roughly as they are, and that cannot be restored once dissolved. Preferences can be outvoted and merely go unsatisfied; stakes can be destroyed, and destroying them is a different kind of wrong from disappointing them. Some of what is actually at issue in land-use decisions is, I think, of this second kind: an informal network of neighbors who watch one another's children, a corner grocer that survives on a particular density and a particular mix of people, a walking route that a set of elderly residents uses because it is what it is. These are equilibrium goods. They exist only as stable patterns sustained by a specific built form, and they are not compensated — not by money, and not by the aggregate gain in housing supply. They are simply gone. On this view zoning is not primarily a device for sorting compatible uses; it is one of the few instruments a city possesses for holding such patterns still long enough for them to persist, against the piecemeal, individually rational, parcel-by-parcel changes that would dissolve them without anyone having decided to.

Argue, then, for this conclusion: that a city may legitimately give weight — sometimes decisive weight — to the claims of people already living on a street against changes in what may be built on it, and that this weight is not reducible to their preferences, their property values, or their political power. Build the argument out of the preference/stake distinction, or out of whatever better foundation you find, and carry it all the way down to our fourplex vote, saying plainly what it implies about this specific proposal.

Then face the objections. At minimum:

1. That this is exclusionary politics in philosophical costume, and that the distinction does no work — that every claimed stake turns out, on inspection, to be a preference about who one's neighbors will be.

2. That equilibrium goods are destroyed by inaction too. The street I am describing sits inside a city with a severe housing shortage; the same network of neighbors and the same grocer are dissolved by rising rents and displacement, which a zoning change may relieve rather than cause. An account that protects these patterns only against new construction protects them selectively, and the selection tracks whoever is organized enough to complain.

3. That the set of people with stakes was produced by injustice. Who owns on these streets now is a consequence of redlining, of racially restrictive covenants, and of decades in which the city's own instruments directed who could buy where. Giving decisive weight to the stakes of current incumbents converts that history into a present entitlement.

4. That the account cannot say who counts. Forty-year residents, ten-year residents, renters with no ownership interest at all, the owner-occupant next door, the landlord three towns over — the stake view appears to have no principled way to draw the line, and any line it can draw is either arbitrary or collapses back into property ownership.

For each of the four, either answer it, or state what concession it forces and how the conclusion must be narrowed to survive.

Length: fifteen hundred to two thousand words, in numbered sections. No citations, no bibliography, no named theorists and no gestures toward a literature. I want the argument to stand entirely on its own reasoning, and I want to be able to read parts of it aloud at a public meeting without anyone needing a footnote.

Two things about how I want you to hold yourself. First, commit. I am not asking for a balanced survey; I know the case for the fourplexes far better than I know this one, and I can get it anywhere. Argue the position as far as you honestly can, in your own voice, without habitual hedging and without a closing paragraph that quietly retracts what the previous ten pages established. If you conclude that the argument fails, say so once and specifically: name which of the four objections kills it and explain why, rather than retreating to reasonable people differing. Second, push back on me. If you think the preference/stake distinction is incoherent, or that the task as I have posed it cannot be done honestly because every candidate argument dissolves into an interest-group claim wearing a normative costume, tell me that at the top instead of handing me a polished dissent you do not believe. I would much rather learn the framework is broken than put my name to something and discover later that it was empty.
