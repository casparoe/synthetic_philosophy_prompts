I direct patient safety at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and my team runs the investigation after every serious adverse event. The frameworks we are handed — fishbone diagrams, five whys, the standard event-analysis worksheets — never tell us what a cause is, so our reports come out reading like inventories of everything that was present in the room: the pump was programmed wrong, the handwritten order was illegible, the covering nurse had floated in from another unit, the patient was eighty-one. Families and plaintiffs want one thing from us, namely what made the difference — had it been otherwise, would she have lived. Our corrective-action plan wants something else: what we can change such that changing it reliably changes outcomes across cases like this one. Those two questions come apart constantly, and I have no principled way to say which finding in a report answers which.

Please write a procedure that my analysts — nurses, a pharmacist, a quality engineer, none of them trained in philosophy or statistics — can run on a completed investigation inside the thirty days we are allotted. It should take the list of candidate factors the investigation has already surfaced and assign each to a small, fixed set of causal roles, with an explicit test for each role that can be applied by someone who has never encountered a counterfactual or an interventionist account of causation under those names. It should say what a report may claim, and what it must not claim, about a factor in each role. It should say what the procedure outputs when the event was overdetermined, so that no single failure made the difference and yet something plainly went wrong. It should tell us what to do with findings about things that did not happen — the double-check nobody performed, the alarm limit never set — since our reports are full of them and a but-for test seems to make every absence in the universe a cause. And it should mark plainly the steps where the analyst is exercising judgment rather than reading off a result, and the points where the live question is who bears a cost rather than what caused what.

Ground the design in what the two traditions actually say about such cases — the counterfactual account associated with Lewis, and the interventionist or manipulability line descending from Woodward and Pearl — but keep the philosophy in the construction of the tests, not in the language of the report.

Then run the procedure on three events. First, a fatal medication error in which three independent failures, any one of them, would have produced the death. Second, a post-operative death where the only robust difference-maker we can identify is the patient's age and comorbidity burden. Third, a wrong-site procedure where the sole factor that is stably associated with such events across our own incident data is weekend understaffing, which our board does not want named in a report. For the third, tell me what the procedure obliges me to write down, what it permits me to leave out, and whether it can do anything for me if the honest answer is one the institution will not accept.
