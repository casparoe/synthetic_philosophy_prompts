I took over my family's boatyard on the Exe eighteen months ago. It was founded in 1877, has been in the family ever since, employs eleven people, and its hundred and fiftieth year begins next spring. I am writing because I have become unable to make decisions, and I think the reason is not commercial.

Three things are pulling at me simultaneously, and each gets stronger whenever I attend to it.

The first is that I have stopped throwing anything away. The loft above the paint store now holds a hundred and forty years of order books, lofting patterns, offcuts, the original painted sign, and a bench that came with the yard. Four unfinished hulls from my father's time are occupying the covered shed I actually need for the repair work that pays the wages, and I cannot bring myself to move them, let alone sell them. I have noticed myself arguing that anything which has been here that long has a claim on us simply for having been here. The men call it the museum, and they are not being affectionate.

The second is a story I keep telling. In the bad decade of the 1890s the founder took on work no other yard would touch and mortgaged his house twice rather than reduce his men's hours. I invoke this constantly — at inductions, at difficult jobs, when a quotation has gone wrong — in the form of: he did that, therefore we can do this. I have used it perhaps fifteen times this year. After one such speech a twenty-two-year-old apprentice told me quietly, on the way to the car park, that hearing it made him feel he would never be good enough and that he was probably in the wrong trade.

The third is what I found in the order books this summer. The yard was not, as the family has always said, bought outright from a retiring shipwright in 1877. The founder's partner went under in circumstances our version of events describes as bad luck, and the books show an obligation that was never discharged. That partner's grandson, now in his eighties and living in Nova Scotia, wrote to us in June asking whether we intend to mark the anniversary and, if so, how he and his family figure in it. I feel a strong pull to write the whole thing down, publish it alongside the celebrations, and say plainly that the yard rests on a wrong that was never put right. Then I notice that everything I am, and everyone on the payroll, is downstream of that wrong, and that condemning its origin is not a neutral act for the person standing at the end of the chain.

Finally, a piece of evidence I do not know what to do with. Last winter I decided all of this was self-indulgence and resolved to stop looking backwards: I stopped reading the old books, had the sign taken down, took the anniversary out of the calendar, and told everyone we would organise ourselves around the next five years. Within six months the two longest-serving men had left without much explanation; I ordered the wrong timber for a restoration because the answer was written in an order book from 1994 that I had stopped consulting; and when a surveyor asked about a lean-to wall on the east side, nobody alive could tell him anything, and we paid for a structural survey to learn what a dead man had pencilled into a plan.

I have to settle the anniversary programme and the future of the covered shed by the end of the quarter, and I need to be able to defend the result to my staff, to the man in Nova Scotia, and to myself. My suspicion is that this is an old problem rather than a new one and that it has been thought about carefully by people far cleverer than I am; I am not asking you for a reading list, references or attributions, and please do not supply any. I am asking you to do the thinking.

Please structure your reply in exactly the following sections, in this order:

**The shape of the problem** — about 150 words. State, in general terms and without reference to my own biography, what kind of predicament I am in, so that I can tell whether you have understood it rather than merely sympathised with it.

**The three pulls** — about 500 words in total. Take them one at a time. For each: what it is genuinely worth, what it turns into when it is allowed to run unchecked, and how I would recognise that the corruption has already happened here rather than somewhere else.

**The winter I stopped looking back** — about 200 words. What my experiment shows, and what it does not show; whether it is an argument for looking back more or for looking back differently.

**The anniversary** — about 300 words. A concrete recommendation covering the programme, the order books, the sign, the four hulls and the covered shed, and the letter from Nova Scotia. Say explicitly what I should refuse to do, and why.

**What to watch** — about 100 words. The single failure mode I am most likely to fall into over the next two years, and the first sign of it.

Be decisive wherever the situation demands a decision. Do not conclude that all three pulls deserve respect in their own way and leave me there; if one of them has to be subordinated to the others, say which and accept the cost of saying so.
