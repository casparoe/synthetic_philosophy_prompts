I sit on a municipal heritage commission in a mid-sized American city, and for the past year I have been drafting and redrafting a resolution about something the city did in the 1920s. The work has exposed how badly I need a principle, because my intuitions about which state apologies are owed, which are mere theater, and which are worse than silence do not line up with one another. Here is the tentative principle I have been carrying around: an apology is owed by a state just when (a) the state is the same institutional agent that authorized the wrong, (b) the wrong was done in its name or under its authority, and (c) some present members of the polity still hold benefits traceable to it. It feels right, but it misfires in both directions, as the cases below show, and I would rather have it repaired than defended.

My verdicts. A new democracy that apologizes for atrocities committed by the dictatorship it overthrew: owed, even though not one living citizen voted for the old regime. A former colonial power that offers deep regret for a massacre a century ago but has never used the word apologize: I do not count that as an apology at all, and I think the refusal to say the word is doing deliberate work. Australia's national apology for the removal of Aboriginal children, unaccompanied by any federal compensation scheme: a real apology, and yet incomplete in a way that matters morally and not only politically. The 1988 American statute pairing an apology for wartime internment with payments to surviving internees: this is what the thing is supposed to look like. An apology delivered by a leader who supported the policy at the time and has since changed his mind: worth more, not less, than the same words from someone who always opposed it — which is the reverse of my colleagues' reaction. An apology for a wrong whose victims, perpetrators, and beneficiaries are all dead and whose proceeds have long since dissipated: permitted, perhaps graceful, but not owed. Now the pair I cannot reconcile. An apology issued by a state that still holds and uses the very property it seized, and has refused every proposal to return it: I think this apology is worse than silence, because it lets the state look repentant while converting an open claim into a closed one. But an apology issued by a state that has already returned the property, paid, and rebuilt the institutions it destroyed, so that nothing is outstanding: I think that apology is the only fully appropriate one, and also pointless, almost ceremonial, because it changes nothing for anyone. So the apology that could actually matter is the tainted one, and the untainted one cannot matter — and my principle delivers exactly the opposite verdicts, since condition (c) fails in the second case and is satisfied in the first. A related asymmetry: I think the grandchildren of victims cannot accept an apology on the victims' behalf, because the dead are not theirs to release; but I also think those grandchildren's refusal to accept it does not make the apology inappropriate or unowed. Authority to release and authority to refuse come apart, and I cannot see why.

The case I keep going back and forth on is my own city's. In 1924 the council declared a Black neighborhood a nuisance under a sanitation ordinance, condemned some two hundred houses, paid the owners a fraction of what the land was worth, and used restrictive covenants to keep them out of three adjoining wards; the cleared ground became a municipal park and later a public high school, both still in use, and surrounding property values and the city's tax base have been shaped by the clearance ever since. Two of the nine current council members bought homes abutting the park in the 1990s and had nothing to do with any of it. The resolution before us says the city regrets the suffering caused by the urban renewal policies of its era, and the same majority that drafted it voted down both a reparations fund and a proposal to transfer the vacant parcel beside the school to a descendant-led trust. If we substituted apologizes for regrets and changed nothing else, I would vote yes — which suggests I think the wording matters even when the material facts do not, and I cannot explain why. So: give me a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I can check each clause against the cases above. Tell me which case each clause is there to handle; tell me what the principle says about apologies I have not listed, supplying two or three cases of your own and running the principle on them, including at least one where you expect me to resist the verdict; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have to overturn. If you end up with something complex enough to save every verdict I gave, say so explicitly and tell me what that complexity is costing. Finally, say where you think your own reasoning is most vulnerable: which of my verdicts, if I surrendered it, would let the principle simplify the most; what argument would move you to the view that state apologies for historical wrongs are never owed but only ever expressions of present-day policy; and what would change your mind about the two cases I have flagged as irreconcilable.
