I am in my final year of high school, and I am preparing an essay for a national philosophy prize. My topic is whether supererogation — action that is morally good but not required, and whose omission is not blameworthy — can survive an argument of my own construction. I built the argument to refute a friend's relaxed view of charitable giving, but I now suspect it proves considerably more than I intended, and I cannot tell whether the trouble is a hidden flaw in the reasoning or a premise that was never believable in the first place. The argument runs as follows.

(1) An act is supererogatory only if it is morally good, it is not obligatory, and its omission is not blameworthy.
(2) Suppose, for reductio, that giving $2,000 to an effective charity, where that sum would save a stranger's life, is supererogatory. Then omitting to give it is not blameworthy.
(3) If the omission of an act is not blameworthy, then that omission is permissible.
(4) If an omission is permissible, then any concrete act by which the agent carries out that omission is permissible: leaving the money in the bank, spending it on a used car, and spending it on a concert ticket are all simply ways of not giving it away.
(5) So spending $2,000 on a concert ticket instead of on a life-saving donation is permissible.
(6) Nothing in (1) through (5) mentions a number. The same reasoning applies to a second $2,000, and to a third, and so on, since each is an omission of an act of the same kind and is therefore likewise not blameworthy.
(7) If each act in a series is permissible, then the course of action composed of them is permissible.
(8) Therefore it is permissible for me to spend a thousand separate sums of $2,000 on a thousand concert tickets while a thousand people die for want of them.
(9) That is not permissible; it is monstrous, and no moral theory that entails it can be correct.
(10) So (2) is false, and donations of this kind are obligatory. Because the reasoning generalises to any amount short of what I need in order to live and work, nearly all of my spare resources are owed, and supererogation either does not exist at all or survives only as a vanishing residue at the margins.

The claims I am least willing to give up are (1), (9), and the plain intuition that at least something — running into a burning building for a stranger, say — genuinely goes above and beyond the call of duty. I would rather abandon my friend's optimism than those.

Please tell me where the argument goes wrong, and do so in a committed way: identify the step or steps you judge to fail and defend that judgement, rather than cataloguing every candidate and leaving me to choose among them. If your view is that the argument is valid and that one of its plausible-looking premises is simply false, say which premise and why it is false. I would also like the alternatives priced. I am aware that there are views on which the agent retains a prerogative to weight her own projects more heavily than an impartial calculation would allow, views that turn on the difference between doing harm and allowing it, and views that accept (8) and conclude that supererogation is a myth; I want to know what each of these must concede in order to block the argument, and which of them you think pays least.

Please organise your response under exactly these four headings, in this order:

1. **The step that fails** — approximately 150 words. Name the premise or inference you reject and give the diagnosis in a sentence or two.
2. **Why it looked innocent** — approximately 250 words. Explain what makes that step seem obviously true, including which reading of a term such as "omission," "permissible," "not blameworthy," or "composed of" makes it plausible and which reading makes it false.
3. **The cost of the alternatives** — approximately 350 words. Take the other candidate failure points in turn; for each, say what a defender would have to accept instead and what that concession damages.
4. **Where I should land** — approximately 200 words. Give a definite recommendation: either the strongest version of my argument that you think survives, or the position you think I should adopt, with your reasons.

In sections 1 and 4 please do not hedge between positions or present my options back to me symmetrically. I would rather have one reasoned verdict I can argue against than a survey.
