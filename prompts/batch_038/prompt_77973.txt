I've been running utilization review at a mid-size regional health plan for about six years now — we decide whether a requested service is "medically necessary" and therefore covered. It used to be one reviewer per case, but our state regulator and our own board have been pushing us toward panel review, on the theory that five heads beat one. My deputy is fully converted: he keeps citing some pop book about the wisdom of crowds, and over lunch last week he told me something like "if each reviewer is sixty percent likely to get it right, then five of them are basically always right," and I couldn't work out whether he was making a real point or mangling one. I did a philosophy minor years ago and I half-remember the Condorcet jury theorem from a social epistemology class — each voter more likely right than wrong, votes independent, and majority accuracy climbing toward certainty as the group gets bigger — but I also vaguely remember that people have objections to it that I never properly absorbed. What I want is for you to turn whatever that theorem and its standard criticisms actually establish into a decision procedure my team can run.

Some shape of the operation so the thing is realistic. Panel-eligible referrals run about 200 a month; standard cases have to be decided in 72 hours and urgent ones in 24. A panel is normally two physicians, two nurses, and one non-clinical reviewer, and I chair. Everyone is trained on the same criteria manual and the same internal e-learning module, which I think means that when they get it wrong they tend to get it wrong together — I don't know whether that breaks the independence the theorem wants, or whether shared training is fine so long as the training is accurate, and I'd like a straight answer on that rather than a hedge. They deliberate out loud before voting and our senior physician sets the tone; I've watched nurses change their votes to fall in line, which I assume is its own problem. Some determinations look like clean criterion-application questions (does this patient meet this documented threshold), and others are ones where equally good clinicians just disagree and I'm not sure there's a fact of the matter at all. Our appeals data is the part that keeps me up: overturn rate on appeal is about 8 percent overall, but roughly 40 percent for adolescent partial-hospitalization psychiatric cases and about 35 percent for ultra-rare-disease therapies. Our medical director signs off on everything and cares a lot about like cases being treated alike, which pushes toward panels, but the panels are also where I see the most variance.

So: give me a worksheet, maybe two pages, that a nurse or a physician could actually pick up and work through without any philosophy. I want an intake triage that sorts referrals into panel / single specialist / escalate-to-a-human-because-this-is-a-values-question lanes, then the questions to ask in order with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure simply stops with a no. Tell me how we should aggregate once we're in the room — plain majority, unanimity, or voting on each criterion separately and deriving the conclusion, including what to do when a panel agrees on every criterion and still splits on the answer. Say whether we should ever go to seven or nine reviewers, and be blunt if for one of our categories the honest recommendation is "stop using panels here," even though panels are my whole mandate. Run it on three cases: a routine advanced-imaging request for a patient with uncomplicated low back pain; a partial-hospitalization program for a 16-year-old where the risk assessment is genuinely contested; and a gene therapy for an ultra-rare disorder that nobody on our panel has ever seen. Then flag which steps of your own procedure are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, what the whole thing can't settle, how it will misfire, and what I should watch in our overturn data to catch it misfiring. Please don't send me a reading list or citations — I can't look anything up during work hours anyway and I'm not writing a paper. One inequality of arithmetic is fine if it earns its place, but keep the rest in plain language.
