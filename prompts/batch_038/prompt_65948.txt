I run a 14-person senior seminar on rational choice, one 90-minute session, and we're doing money pumps next week. They've all seen the argument against intransitive preferences and think it's closed — the reflex answer is "I'd just see it coming and refuse." I want an activity that takes that answer seriously and then breaks it.

Design it so the cycle isn't handed to them on a role card but gets induced by the setup itself — some structure of goods and payoffs where they genuinely, in the room, come to prefer A to B to C to A — and where the pumping happens over sequential offers with chips (not real money) and nothing hidden from them: theyre told up front that more swaps are coming and can see the whole menu. The thing I want them to land on is that the loss isn't a symptom of being dumb or myopic; then I want them arguing about whether that makes their preferences irrational or just expensive.

Give me: the exact payoff scheme and the order of offers, and why that order; a precommitment variant where they pick their whole plan at once, and what it shows that the sequential version can't; a debrief mapping moments in the room onto sophisticated vs resolute choice; one question that tells me it landed; and a straight answer about which feature of the real money-pump argument the activity can't reproduce, so I flag it up front instead of getting caught out. Also what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it.
