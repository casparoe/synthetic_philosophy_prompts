I TA a philosophy of psychiatry seminar and I want to hand the students something that sorts them *before* we read Szasz, not after. The problem I keep hitting is that they're answering three different questions as though it were one: whether mental illness is a literal kind of thing (metaphysical/semantic), whether involuntary treatment is ever justified (political), and whether the insanity defence should exist (legal). Someone can say no, no, no and mean three completely different things by it, and half the seminar arguments are just people in different cells shouting at each other.

So: a decision tree, maybe eight to twelve nodes, where every node is a concrete case or a plain claim a student could answer without any idea which position it's steering them toward, and every leaf is a named position. Keep the nodes about concepts of illness rather than about how you feel toward psychiatrists — no node should be anything like "do you think psychiatry does more harm than good."

Cases worth drawing on, use whichever ones actually discriminate: a grief that's lasted two years and now has a diagnosis attached; compulsive hand-washing in someone with an abnormal brain scan who insists he isn't ill, just stuck; a heavy drinker who says the drinking is a choice and resents being told otherwise; someone who was detained under a mental health act, agrees they were never ill, and still thinks the detention was the right call; a defendant acquitted by reason of insanity who later says he was faking the whole time; a condition that is disabling but statistically ordinary (short stature, say); a condition that's statistically way off but harms nobody.

Leaves that have to appear: Szasz's own view — illness as literally a bodily lesion, so that what psychiatry treats are really troubles of living, with treatment reconceived as a contract between consenting adults that either side can walk away from; Boorse-style biostatistical naturalism; Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis; a Foucauldian or social-constructivist line; mainstream psychiatric pluralism, i.e. disorders are real but heterogeneous and no single analysis covers them; psychiatric abolitionism; and the neurodiversity view — but only if that's genuinely a seperate leaf and not constructivism wearing different politics; tell me which. Then add two slots of your own choosing where you think the logical space has a gap worth filling. And place this one: a view one of my students holds that I'll call 'diagnostic nominalism' — whether something counts as an illness is settled by the practice of diagnosing rather than by any underlying state, which makes Szasz not wrong, just answering a question nobody was asking. Either give it a leaf or explain why it collapses into a neighbour.

Two more things. Mark every combination of node-answers that the tree allows but that leads nowhere, and say why the cell is empty — incoherent, or coherent but nobody wants it, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually pull apart; I'd rather admit that up front than let the students think the instrument is sharper than it is. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Needs to fit on two sides of a page, so keep the nodes short.
