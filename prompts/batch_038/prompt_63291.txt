I am preparing a short position statement for a graduate reading group on perception. The format is that each of us circulates our commitments in advance and the rest of the group spends the session attacking them, so I want to know where the weak points are before I hand it over. My commitments concern disjunctivism, the argument from illusion, and what follows for skepticism. Numbered so you can refer to them:

1. Naive realism: in a veridical case of perception, the mind-independent object and its qualities are constituents of the experience itself. The phenomenal character of such an experience is partly constituted by the environment, not by an inner item that could have existed in exactly that form had the environment been different.
2. The argument from illusion therefore fails. In the stock cases — the straight stick that looks bent in water, the round coin that looks elliptical from an angle — the subject genuinely perceives the object, and all that is true is that the object looks bent or elliptical from here. Nothing mind-dependent is directly present to the subject, and no sense-data are needed.
3. I do accept the causal-matching premise that drives the argument from hallucination: for any veridical perception, a subjectively matching hallucination is possible, produced by the same proximal neural causes.
4. I deny the inference from (3) to a highest common factor. The matching hallucination is not a mental state of the same fundamental kind as the veridical perception. Hallucination has no positive phenomenal character of its own; what makes a given hallucination the kind of state it is, is that it is introspectively indiscriminable by its subject from a certain veridical perception, and from no other kind of state.
5. Epistemic disjunctivism: in the good case, the subject's perceptual reason for believing that there is a yellow lemon on the table is the factive reason that she sees that there is a yellow lemon on the table. In the matching bad case, no reason of that kind is available to her.
6. Access internalism about justification: whether a belief is justified depends only on facts the subject can come to know by reflection alone. In particular, two situations that are reflectively indistinguishable for a subject confer the same justificatory status on the same belief formed in them.
7. The hallucinating subject is nonetheless entirely epistemically blameless, and the explanation is that things seem to her exactly as they seem to her counterpart in the good case.
8. The package answers the skeptic about the external world, at least for perceptual belief. The skeptic's step from "I cannot tell by reflection alone that I am in the good case" to "I do not know that I am in the good case" is a non sequitur.

Audit this set.
