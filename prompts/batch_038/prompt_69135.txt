I'm a backend engineer, and for the last year and a half I've had a seat on the technical advisory board of a mid-sized open source foundation — we oversee something like forty projects, mostly unglamorous infrastructure libraries, and I ended up on the board because I maintain one of them, not because I have any governance experience or a philosophy background, which is starting to feel like a problem. The board votes in three weeks on a proposal I keep flip-flopping on, and I've been asked to write the memo that gets circulated to everyone beforehand. Here's the situation. We have a designation called Trusted Maintainer that comes with commit rights across projects, a vote on the technical steering committee, and access to the private security disclosure list. To get it today you need two years of sustained contribution history, a decent run of substantial merged changes, and at least a year of documented triage and code review duty — all of it unpaid, on evenings and weekends, plus you have to show up in person to two of our summits at your own expense. A large cloud vendor has offered to fund a sponsored track: they'd pay stipends to people doing the qualifying work and cover the travel, starting with thier own staff and eventually opening it to contractors. Their pitch is that our requirements only measure who happens to have free time and a financial cushion, and that paying people would widen the pool a lot.

Half the board is thrilled and half thinks it's fatal, and honestly I can see both sides. The skeptical camp says the designation only means anything because it's expensive in a way you can't just buy — if a company can pay people to accumulate the requirements, the badge stops telling us about the person and starts telling us about their employer, and a sponsor with enough budget effectively gets to choose which of its staff end up voting on our steering committee. The other camp says we've been flattering ourselves this whole time: the 'cost' was never screening for competence, it was screening for privilege, and anyway most of our current Trusted Maintainers are already paid by their employers to work on our code, so the vendor is just proposing to make that explicit and extend it to people whose employers won't. There's also the awkward fact that the foundation runs on membership dues and we're broke, and this money would cover the security audits we've been deferring for two years. One thing I keep getting asked in the hallway is whether there's a version where the cost stays genuinely costly but stops being a cost of money and free time and becomes more a cost of skill — I don't know if that's even a coherent idea, so tell me if it isn't.

Could you draft that memo for me? I want it to actually land somewhere: approve, reject, or approve with conditions written tightly enough that a vendor's lawyers couldn't wiggle through them, plus what the first six months would look like and what specific thing would make us pull the plug. I'd also like it to name and explain the underlying idea about signals that only carry information because they're expensive to send, in plain language, since most of the board doesn't have that vocabulary and I only half do — but no reading list and no citations, I'd have no way to check them and it would just look like padding. Please take the sponsor's equity argument seriously instead of swatting it, and if the strongest version of that argument changes the recommendation, then say so and change it. I also want to be able to defend this when someone at the meeting says we're just protecting a club of people who got in when it was cheap, so if there's a transition or grandfathering angle, work it in. And formatting-wise: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose the way you'd write a long email, with the bottom line stated in the first couple of sentences, somewhere around 700 to 1000 words.
