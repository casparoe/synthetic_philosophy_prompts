I am closing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of biological function. The last two sessions set an etiological account against a causal-role one: the first holds that what a trait is for is fixed by the effect that explains its presence in the lineage, the effect it was selected or retained for; the second holds that a function is simply a contribution the trait currently makes to some capacity of the containing system, where the containing system is chosen by the analyst rather than fixed by nature. Neither of our resident partisans has budged. I want the final handout to be a poem rather than a summary, and I want it to be a pantoum, because in this case the form is the argument.

Please observe the following constraints.

1. Five or six quatrains, each rhyming ABAB, with slant rhyme permitted. Follow the pantoum's rule strictly: lines 2 and 4 of each stanza recur as lines 1 and 3 of the next. A repeated line may change its punctuation but never its wording, until the final stanza.

2. The final stanza should end with the poem's first and third lines, in that order, so that the line which opened the poem as a claim about where things come from returns as a claim about what things are doing now. Choose an opening line that can genuinely bear both readings without being rewritten to do so.

3. The inheritance of lines should make the etiological claim visible: what a line is for is a fact about where it has already been. What that same line does in its new stanza should make the causal-role claim visible: identical words contribute to a different effect because the stanza around them, the containing system, is different. Push this as far as the form allows. The same sentence should be doing demonstrably different jobs in different stanzas, not merely surviving them intact.

4. Devote one stanza to the cheapness objection. On a purely causal-role reading every contribution counts, so a line's length, its assonance, its placement, and its share in the mood all become functions at once, and nothing is malfunctioning any more than anything else is functioning. Then, without breaking form, let the other side answer: only one of those effects explains why the line was kept at all.

5. Devote one stanza to malfunction, which is where causal-role accounts are supposed to come unstuck. I want the vestigial refrain: a returned line that no longer contributes to the stanza it lands in. Leave open whether that line still has the function it was retained for, whether it has the weaker status of contributing at something like the typical rate for the poem as a whole, or whether it has no function any more. Do not resolve this; the seminar did not.

6. The central image should be gene sharing in the vertebrate lens: one single-copy gene product doing double duty as a housekeeping metabolic enzyme (lactate dehydrogenase, in the duck's case) and as a structural crystallin whose contribution is transparency. One molecule, two pedigrees of explanation. The etiological question is which effect explains its presence there; the causal-role question is which capacity of which containing system one is asking about. You may name the enzyme and the lens. If you want a second instance, the homology between the main α-crystallins and the small heat-shock proteins is available to you. Name no papers.

7. No philosopher's name may appear in the poem, and the poem may not declare a winner. A reader who has taught this debate should be able to say, stanza by stanza, which account is speaking and which is being answered; a reader who has never heard of it should simply find a good poem about inheritance.

After the poem, add no more than 150 words saying which stanza is doing which piece of work, so that I can check your reading against mine. No citations are wanted or expected.
