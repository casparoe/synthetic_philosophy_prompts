I teach a small grad seminar on how the French Revolution gets explained — class interest, ideology, contingency, the usual three — and every year, without fail, somebody in week five reaches for the line everyone hands to Vergniaud on the way to the scaffold: the revolution is like Saturn, it devours its own children. (I've never managed to find where he actually says it, and I don't want you chasing that either — it's the use of the thing I care about.) It's a beautiful line and I've come to distrust it, because in my seminar it gets recruited for two opposite jobs and I can't work out whether it can carry either.

Use one: a structuralist in the group says the devouring shows the Terror came out of the revolution's own logic rather than out of Robespierre's temperament or the war — an event that eats the Girondins in October '93, then the Hébertists the following March, then Danton and Desmoulins twelve days after them, then the man holding the knife in July, has an appetite of it's own, and that appetite is the explanans, not the explanandum. Use two: another student says the reverse — Saturn swallowed because of a prophecy he had good reason to credit, so the same image shows the purges were defensive pre-emption by people being locally strategic rather than possessed. Same picture, opposite historiographical payload, and both of them waved it at me in the same hour.

There's also a wrinkle from my best student, who reads the myth closer than I do: the swallowed children get disgorged alive and go on to overthrow their father. She says that's what makes the analogy worth anything — it predicts the return, Thermidor, the recalled Girondin survivors, the posthumous vindication of Danton — and then she adds, I think correctly, that an image which delivers both the devouring and the regurgitation can't come out wrong about anything, so it isn't an explanation, "it's a mood."

So: audit it for me. I want the mapping laid out explicitly — Saturn, the children, the prophecy, the swallowing, the stone Rhea swaps in, the disgorging, the father's eventual overthrow — and for each item, what in 1793–94 it's supposed to be, or a blank if there's nothing there. I suspect the stone is the giveaway, and I want to know what the fact that nobody ever mentions the stone tells us about which half of the source people are actually using. Then seperate the disanalogies into the ones that genuinely damage one of the two uses and the ones that are just pedantry (yes, revolutions have no digestive tract; fine; so what?). The ones I can't settle: whose appetite it's supposed to be, given that the Terror ran through the Committee, the Convention, the Tribunal, and half a dozen representatives on mission doing their own thing in Lyon and Nantes, whereas Saturn is one agent with one stomach and one motive; whether "its own children" survives the counting, since the overwhelming bulk of the condemned were peasants, artisans and obscure provincials swept up under the Law of Suspects rather than the revolution's own people, so the analogy is narrating a few hundred famous deaths out of tens of thousands; the ordering, since Hébertists-before-Dantonists looks selective and tactical, which seems to feed use two and starve use one; and the prophecy, since Saturn's fear turned out to be true whereas the Jacobin fear of internal conspiracy was substantially manufactured by the measures taken against it — is that a difference the analogy can absorb, or is it the load-bearing wall?

Then tell me which use, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table; whether my student is right that the regurgitation clause makes the thing unfalsifiable rather than deep; and what a better image would be, if there is one — or whether the honest conclusion is that the line was never doing explanatory work at all and the structuralists and the strategists were both just borrowing its gravity.
