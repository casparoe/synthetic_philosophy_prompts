Last month a citizens' convention in our region voted 58 to 21 to recommend devolving education, transport, and health spending from the national government to a new regional assembly. I co-chaired it, and I've been asked to give the closing remarks — seven minutes, roughly 900 words — to the full convention, the national press corps, and four ministers who spent the weekend arguing against us.

The draft statute contains a clause nobody has defended publicly: each devolved competence lapses after twelve years unless the national legislature renews it by a two-thirds majority, and in the meantime either level may bring any dispute before a joint council empowered to rule on procedure but never on the merits.

The speech has to do two things. First, it should treat subsidiarity as a rule about who carries the burden of proof when a decision moves up a level, not as a claim that smaller governments are wiser, warmer, or more authentic; several delegates want the second version and should be told plainly why it is worse. Second, it must face the delegation from the mountain districts, who spent all weekend explaining that the national government has shielded them from the regional majority for a century and asking why the burden of proof should now run against them.

Write it so it can actually be read aloud, so the ministers are not insulted, and so it ends on the sunset clause rather than skirting it.
