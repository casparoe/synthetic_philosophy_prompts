Explain how each theory handles the sorites, and compare the two with respect to (i) bivalence and the law of excluded middle; (ii) the status of the induction step; and (iii) the explanation each offers of our inability to locate the boundary of a vague predicate. And here is the script I would like marked:

> Both theories start from a common diagnosis of the sorites. The argument from the heap is valid, its conclusion is false, and so at least one of its premises must be false, even though it is not obvious which. They differ over what that falsity consists in, and over how much of classical logic has to be sacrificed to accommodate it.
>
> Epistemicism sacrifices nothing. On the view Williamson defends, "is a heap" has a sharp boundary: there is some number n such that n grains make a heap and n + 1 grains do not. Borderline claims are therefore not semantically defective in any way; they are determinately true or determinately false, and we simply do not know which. Bivalence holds without restriction, and so does the law of excluded middle, and the logic of vague discourse remains fully classical. The obvious cost is that one must explain why nobody can ever know where the boundary falls, and Williamson's explanation runs through a margin-for-error principle for knowledge. Our use of "heap" is plastic, in the sense that had we used the word very slightly differently, the boundary would have fallen somewhere else; and because those nearby ways of using the word are not detectably different from ours, any belief about the precise boundary would be unsafe, and so would not count as knowledge. The boundary itself is fixed by our collective linguistic practice, which nobody has ever fully surveyed. In principle, though, a complete description of that practice — every disposition to apply the term, every past and present use, every relevant causal connection between speakers and the world — would allow us to read the boundary off it. Our ignorance is thus contingent and merely empirical, of the same kind as our ignorance of how many grains are in a particular jar: a matter of not having done the counting.
>
> Supervaluationism, introduced by van Fraassen in order to deal with vagueness and developed for that purpose by Fine, keeps the classical machinery but abandons classical truth. A vague sentence is true simpliciter if it comes out true on every admissible precisification of its vague terms, false simpliciter if it comes out false on every such precisification, and indeterminate otherwise. Borderline claims are neither true nor false, so bivalence fails; and it fails comprehensively. If "this patch is red" is true on some admissible precisifications and false on others, then the same goes for its negation, and hence for the disjunction of the two. The law of excluded middle therefore goes the same way as bivalence, and the supervaluationist pays essentially the same logical price as the defender of a three-valued semantics. What is gained is an account of the sorites: the induction step is not true, since for each of the borderline instances there are admissible precisifications on which the conditional fails, and the argument is accordingly unsound at its second premise.
>
> The two views are usually distinguished by their treatment of penumbral connections, and here supervaluationism has the advantage. Take a patch that is borderline between red and orange, and consider the claim that if the patch is red then it is not green. No admissible precisification makes the patch both red and green, so the conditional is true on every precisification, and hence true simpliciter, even though its antecedent is indeterminate. Supervaluationism can thus deliver a compound as true where none of its components is, which is exactly what a three-valued logic cannot manage, and it is this feature that makes the theory attractive to those who want to keep the logical behaviour of vague language as close to classical as possible without accepting unknowable sharp boundaries.
>
> I find epistemicism the more defensible of the two. It does not multiply semantic entities beyond necessity, since precisifications are not independently identifiable and play no explanatory role that a classical valuation could not play; and it leaves our logic completely untouched, which is a real virtue given how much of ordinary reasoning depends on classical principles. The standard objection — that the sharp boundary is a posit doing no work — seems to me to cut both ways, since the precisifications are equally posits. Epistemicism is the bolder theory, but it is the one that has to give up least.
