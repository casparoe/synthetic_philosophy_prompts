I am assembling a problem set for a first-year graduate seminar on the foundations of probability, and one session is devoted to the Dutch book arguments. My students tend to arrive persuaded that the sure-loss argument settles every question it can be pointed at, and I want to hand them a case that shows how much of the work is being done by assumptions about the wager contract rather than about the agent's mind. The case I have in mind concerns an agent who prices conditional wagers in line with Adams's thesis — her credence in a conditional of the form *if A then B* is nothing over and above her conditional credence in B given A — and who is otherwise impeccably orthodox, with credences that are finitely additive, neither extreme where they shouldn't be, and revised by strict conditionalization. Two conventions for settling a wager on a conditional are available. Under the material convention, the wager pays the full stake in every situation except the one where A holds and B fails; the falsity of the antecedent is no defence. Under the call-off convention, the wager is void if A fails and the stake is handed straight back. A colleague of mine insists that any agent who prices conditionals Adams-style is one short book away from ruin; I think that depends entirely on which convention the bookmaker is writing into the contract, and I want the students to be able to check that by hand rather than take either of our word for it.

So please construct a single scenario that can be run under both conventions, and in which the material convention yields a strict Dutch book — a finite package of wagers that the agent accepts at her own fair prices, on the buying side and the selling side alike, and that leaves her with less money in every one of the four relevant states of the world — while the call-off convention yields nothing of the sort, not even a weak book in the sense of a package that can never gain and sometimes loses. Constraints on the design: the setting should be ordinary commercial or institutional life, a freight desk or an underwriting team or a hospital transfers office, and A and B should be plain empirical propositions about that setting, not coin flips, urns, or lottery tickets; no casinos, and no proposition whose truth anybody in the story can affect by acting. At most three wagers in the package. Her two credences must be given as decimals a student can work with on paper, but please do not reach for 0.5 across the board simply because it is convenient — I want the particular values to be shown to matter, or to be shown not to matter, rather than assumed. Stakes must be small enough that utility is linear in money, so that risk aversion cannot dissolve the case, and she may not be rescued by declining wagers, by being unsure whether she will be offered them, by having opinions about her own future opinions, or by any exotic epistemic situation; the bookmaker may be nothing more than a shrewd human being who knows her prices and her conventions. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

Then give me the demonstration, because the whole point of the exercise is that the design can be audited. Lay out a table of the wagers with each stake, the price at which she accepts it, and her net position in each of the four states, and do the arithmetic explicitly in currency units so that the total is visibly negative in all four under the material convention. Repeat the table under the call-off convention, and either show that no package available at those prices can lose her money in every state or, if you believe one can, produce it. Tell me which states the two conventions disagree about, and which of those states is carrying the book. Address directly what becomes of the third wager once the call-off clause is in force — whether it survives as a distinct contract or collapses into one of the others — and say honestly whether that collapse is a legitimate blocking of the book or a cheap one, since a student will raise exactly this objection. Report how the construction depends on her two credence values: does a strict book exist for every pair in the open unit interval, and what happens to the stakes required as the antecedent grows near-certain or as the conditional credence approaches one? Close with the strongest reply available to someone who holds that the material convention is the only coherent way to read a wager on a conditional and who therefore concludes that my agent is simply irrational, together with your assessment of what that reply concedes, if anything, about what Dutch book arguments are able to establish.
