At a recent departmental discussion of whether Stoic ethics can accommodate political commitment, a colleague ended a graduate student's objection with the slogan "control the controllables": since whether a reform succeeds is not among the things up to us, he said, an ethics organized around that distinction has nothing to say about activism, and the charge of quietism never gets off the ground. I am preparing a handout for my ethics seminar that takes the slogan apart rather than either endorsing or mocking it. Lay out, as a menu I could circulate, the distinct claims it might be making: the bare descriptive point that not every event is caused by you; a prescriptive reading about the allocation of attention; readings that gloss "up to us" as full causal production, as compatibilist origination in one's own assent and character, or as a matter of degree, with things only partly up to us; an evaluative reading on which the claim is really about what bears on living well; and a reading on which the distinction is not about events at all but about how impressions are used. For each, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or plainly false, and whether it is descriptive or normative. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually license my colleague's dismissal, and what the student might reply without abandoning Stoicism. Finally, say whether the archer image the Stoics themselves invoke — aiming well rather than hitting the mark — answers the objection from things only partly up to us, or concedes it.
