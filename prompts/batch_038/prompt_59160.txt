Been arguing with a friend since dinner about Ireland and sortition and I'm not moving him. He says the citizens' assemblies prove randomly chosen people do politics better than career politicians; I say it proves the opposite, because the only reason it worked is that they were never actually incharge. Facts, since you can't look them up: the 2012–14 Constitutional Convention was 66 randomly picked citizens plus 33 sitting politicians and a chair, and it recommended changing the Eighth Amendment. Then a fresh Citizens' Assembly, 2016–18, was 99 randomly picked citizens with no politicians at all, chaired by a Supreme Court judge; it took abortion from November 2016 to April 2017 and recommended repeal, on terms more liberal than the press expected. That report went to a 21-member committee of actual TDs and senators, which kept most of it and trimmed some, and the May 2018 referendum passed 66.4 to 33.6 — almost exactly the mirror of the 67–33 that put the amendment in back in 1983.

Analyse that whole chain as a test of the sortition case, not as Irish political history. Work with a distinction I made up but think is doing real work: call it leashed sortition, where the body deliberates and recommends but can't decide, so the accountability load stays on the elected chain downstream. Tell me (a) whether the value came from descriptive representation or from having no re-election incentives to distort them, given the members had no career to protect but also no power; (b) what the politicians' committee stage does to the story — did the leash save the experiment or domesticate it; (c) whether the near-identical margins mean the assembly moved opinion or just sat downstream of a move already underway, and what that says about sortition as an epistemic device versus a legitimating one; and (d) whether replacing career politicians was ever the point, or whether Ireland shows the theory was really about agenda-setting all along. Where the theory goes silent, say so instead of filling the gap.

Exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion.
