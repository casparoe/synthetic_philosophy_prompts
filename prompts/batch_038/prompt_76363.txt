For several years I have co-run an informal reading group at a research institute where formal epistemologists, decision theorists and a handful of machine-learning researchers sit in the same room. Our next session is on diachronic self-trust, and in particular on van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, the idea that my present credence in a proposition, conditional on my future credence in it being r, ought itself to be r. The room is mixed: perhaps half of the twenty-odd attendees have never seen the principle stated with any precision, and the other half arrive already persuaded that it is either trivially true or a mistake, largely depending on which camp they read as undergraduates. Our last handout, on the Sleeping Beauty problem, degenerated into forty minutes of people arguing about labels rather than about what they would actually say in the cases, so this time I want an instrument that sorts attendees by their verdicts on concrete situations before anyone is permitted to announce an allegiance.

What I need is a decision tree, printable on one side of A4, that carries a reader from a bare disposition to a named position. Build it from the following eight situations, which I have drafted but which you should sharpen, reorder, cut or replace wherever one fails to discriminate: (1) tonight you will be injected with a compound that you know will make you irrationally certain by morning that an unmarked envelope on your desk contains a cheque, and you also know the injection will erase your memory of having been injected; (2) you have worked through the evidence for a contested claim this week and expect to have forgotten the argument by Friday while retaining its conclusion; (3) you expect your future self to have mulled over precisely the same evidence for a further year, with no new data arriving at all; (4) your future credence is partly caused by your present one, because you know that whatever you confidently assert today will be repeated back to you next month by people you trust; (5) an artificial agent knows that a third party will adjust its weights and instructions overnight so that it assigns 0.95 to a proposition it currently assigns 0.4, and it regards that party as well meaning but no expert on the proposition in question; (6) you expect to be told tomorrow which of two indistinguishable replicas in a laboratory you are, in circumstances where your evidence about the world will not change but the indexical content of your beliefs will; (7) you expect a reliable informant to tell you the outcome of a trial, and you also expect that, once you have heard it, you will begin to suspect the informant of lying; (8) you expect your future self to have acquired different tastes rather than different evidence — say, about whether a certain style of architecture is hideous — and the question is whether deference of this kind reaches attitudes that are not evidentially responsive at all. Every node must be a question the reader can answer without knowing where it leads, and no two nodes may ask the same question in different words. The leaves must include: Reflection without restriction; Reflection restricted to cases where the later belief is expected to arise from conditionalising on strictly more of the same evidence with one's faculties intact; the family of views on which deference is owed to the process that will produce the future belief rather than to the future self as such, where Skyrms's generalised Reflection and M-Reflection belong; outright rejection of Reflection, whether on Foley-style grounds about epistemic self-trust or on the grounds that deliberation crowds out prediction; and at least one leaf that you judge nobody in the literature actually to occupy, which you should name yourself and diagnose. Where two leaves answer every one of the eight situations identically, say so in the leaf entry instead of implying that the tree separates them.

Deliver the instrument as a single numbered list of exactly fourteen items and nothing else: no introduction, no preamble about method, no closing summary, and no reference list, since I have told the group that nobody will be asked for citations. Items 1 to 8 are the branch nodes in the order a reader should actually meet them, each giving the situation, the yes-or-no question it poses, and where each answer sends the reader; item 1 must also carry one clause explaining why that question is the trunk and what would go wrong were it placed later. Items 9 to 13 are the leaves, each naming the position, sketching its most awkward commitment in a sentence, and flagging any sibling the tree's own questions cannot distinguish from it. Item 14 is the combination of answers your eight questions permits but no leaf receives — the empty cell — with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it stands empty because such a view would be incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply got round to defending it. Keep every item to four sentences at most and most to one or two. Two further requests: commit to one tree rather than offering me a choice of orderings or a survey of the alternatives, and write the whole thing in British English.
