Some background on my position. I have been reading the rationalist corpus since the Overcoming Bias days, and of all the big speculative claims in it, the singleton hypothesis has always struck me as the least examined relative to how much work it does: the conjecture that advanced technology makes some form of single global decision-making structure likelier than a permanently multipolar future, so that the long-run shape of things turns disproportionately on choices made while the structure is still soft. I think the standard presentation of that conjecture is badly flawed, and I have spent a couple of years developing a revision of it. My claim is that the singleton, if it arrives, will not arrive as a conqueror. It will arrive as an interface. The historical precedent I lean on is not Rome or the British Empire but the unglamorous treaty architecture of technical standardization: the Metre Convention and the standards body it created; ICAO's standards and recommended practices, which states follow less from fear of sanction than because non-compliance means their airworthiness certificates and licences simply stop being recognised anywhere else; the DNS root zone and the arrangements for altering it; the messaging network that carries cross-border payment instructions. In each case nobody rules, and yet everybody's option set is formatted by a single specification. My argument has four moves. First, governance can be secured by controlling the layer at which decisions are expressed, translated, verified, and executed, rather than the layer at which coercion is applied. Second, advanced AI systems will be interface-like in precisely this sense — they will not sit above states so much as become the medium through which states formulate what they want, check what they believe, and carry out what they decide. Third, the question worth asking is therefore not whether some actor will achieve a decisive strategic advantage, which is the framing that attracts all the familiar and quite reasonable objections about how hard world conquest is; it is whether the decision-interface layer will converge on one specification faster than convergence can be contested. Fourth, that convergence is driven by network effects and by the sheer cost of mutual unintelligibility, and crucially it requires nobody's intention — which is exactly what makes it resistant to the tools states normally use against rising powers.

The venue is what makes this difficult. This is a political science department colloquium, not a philosophy or computer science one, and I am the outside speaker. The room will be about twenty-five people: four or five comparativists who work on state capacity, bureaucratic quality, and implementation; two IR realists, one of whom writes about polarity and the other about the security dilemma; an economic historian of nineteenth-century globalization; an international lawyer whose work is on why regimes get complied with; a political theorist who will be sceptical of any account that treats power as a matter of technical format; and a scattering of graduate students, one of whom is writing a dissertation on measurement in cross-national indicators and will be interested in how I would ever know a singleton had occurred. Nobody in the room works on AI. Most have encountered the word singleton once in a newspaper, if at all. As a group they are far more suspicious of concept inflation than of technological speculation: the reflex objection in this room is that I have taken a well-studied phenomenon, given it a science-fictional name, and thereby immunised it from measurement.
