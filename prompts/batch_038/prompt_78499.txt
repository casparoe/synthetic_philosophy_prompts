I write a science-and-ideas column and once a month I co-host a little evening thing in the back room of a pub — call it 25 people, 60 to 75 minutes, a mix of actual researchers, two retired teachers, a couple of regulars who came for the beer and stay for the argument. Next month's topic is degrees of belief, and specifically that accuracy-first line of argument I've been reading around for a piece (you know, the claim that being probabilistically coherent isn't fussy bookkeeping but the only way to avoid holding a set of beliefs that gets beaten no matter how the world turns out, plus all the Brier-score business the forecasting people won't shut up about — my editor has already told me "scoring rule" is unprintable, which is fair). What I want from you is a game, not a talk. The whole point is that they should run into the thing before anyone says the name of it. There's always one options trader in the room who will try to game whatever I set up, and always one person who insists they never have numbers for anything, so it has to survive both.

Design it with three escalating rounds and give me the actual mechanics: rules, a materials list with quantities, what goes on the whiteboard, what I say at each transition, and rough timings. Round one should let them discover on their own that a payoff which simply rewards being right turns everybody into a maximalist shouting 99 percent about everything, and that this feels like a bug rather than a win. Round two should flip it, so that reporting your honest number is literally the best strategy available when the payoff is measured as closeness-to-truth rather than as hits and misses — but the payoff has to be physical and visual, tokens or a big chart on the wall or something, because I am not going to compute forty quadratic penalties by hand in front of 25 people while they watch me sweat. Round three is the trap: I want a bunch of them to end up holding credences that each feel individually sensible but are jointly incoherent across a set of logically hooked-up propositions, and I want me to be able to walk to the wall and produce an alternative set that's strictly closer to the truth under every single possible outcome. Ideally they're the ones who notice what just happened. Give the arithmetic-haters real roles too — scorekeeper, envelope-runner, devil's advocate, whatever — so nobody's a spectator. No money, but make the stakes feel like something: cheap forfeits, bragging rights, a traveling trophy, your call.

Two hard constraints. First, every uncertainty has to resolve in the room. Don't hand me trivia questions whose answers I'd have to look up or take your word for, and don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any named study or historical episode I'd have to fact-check before repeating it on stage — I'd rather have sealed envelopes I prepare beforehand, urns with unknown mixes of chips that people get to draw samples from, dice under cups, spinners, whatever you can justify. If you use sampling, build in a cost for extra draws, because I'd like them to feel both that evidence is worth having and that it isn't worth any price. Second: the debrief. Map each moment of the game onto the piece of the view it dramatizes, then tell me which two or three objections will come up from the floor and how I should answer them without overclaiming — I'm expecting the immodesty one (my own credences always look best to me, so this whole scheme just says I'm right), the "so should I avoid looking at evidence that might upset my nice accurate set" one, and somebody saying you've just proved probabilism is nice, which isn't the same as proving its true. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the rounding, the guy who just says 50 percent to everything, the point where the token maths stops being fun — and how to head each off. And be honest about which feature of accuracy-first epistemology a pub game with poker chips simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand. My guess is that its the voluntariness of belief — nobody actually picks their credences off a menu — but if you think there's a bigger gap, say so and say why.
