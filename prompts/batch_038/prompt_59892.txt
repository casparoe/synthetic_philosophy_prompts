So, context: I'm a novelist, and once a year I teach a seminar in a literature department that's basically "fiction about people doing things nobody wants done." This year the spine of it was races to the bottom — the Moloch framing, positional competition, why decent people keep feeding machines they all claim to hate. I'm writing the final now: 90 minutes, closed book, one essay question, 25 marks, and I want it to be unpassable by recitation.

Because I know exactly what I'll get if I ask anything general. "It's a prisoner's dilemma, individually rational and collectively ruinous, you need monitoring and enforcement or a Leviathan, QED." That answer is not wrong, it's just free, and half the room will produce it without having thought about the case in front of them.

So I want to build the question out of the setup in the book I'm currently working on, which I think has the property I want — the obvious diagnosis demonstrably doesn't rescue it. Here's the scenario:

Forty-odd independent schools in one region, competing for a shrinking pool of fee-paying applicants. Their reputation, and therefore their survival, is driven by placements at a handful of elite universities that admit overwhelmingly on comparative standing — rank, percentile, "how does this cohort look next to that cohort." So the prize is ordinal, and it's fixed. Every head of school knows the workload race is destroying the children; they say so to each other at the annual conference every year. In book one they sign a non-binding compact: capped set homework, capped school day, no weekend provision. All forty sign. Nobody was holding out. Within two academic years every single one of them is quietly over the cap, and — this is the bit I want the question to turn on — parents couldn't verify any of it, hours were self-reported, so complying bought you nothing visible and defecting cost you nothing visible.

Then, in book two, the compact gets teeth: third-party audits, published hours logs, mutual inspection, a real verification regime, and every head is genuinely, sincerely in favour of it. They comply. The audits confirm compliance. And within a year the race is back in a different currency — schools start "suggesting" outside study support, i.e. private tutors, off campus, out of the audits' reach. Tutoring spend becomes the new margin, the hours logs stay immaculate, and the universities haven't changed their selection method at all.

What I want from you:

1. The exam question itself, with the scenario compressed into it — call it 250 words of setup maximum, then the question. Two parts is fine; I like a part that asks for diagnosis and a part that asks them to commit to a fix and predict what happens to it.

2. A model answer, 700–800 words.

3. A marking scheme out of 25 — five or six must-hit points with marks attached, and for each one, the specific wrong turn that shows a student is pattern-matching to "tragedy of the commons" or "prisoner's dilemma, add enforcement" instead of reading the case. I want the A/B boundary stated in terms of moves, not vibes. Tell me which mistakes should cost marks and roughly how much.

Two things I care about most. First, please commit. I don't want a model answer that lays out three plausible diagnoses of the trap and leaves me to decide which one earns the marks — I have to grade thirty of these and I need to know what I'm grading against. Pick the diagnosis you actually think is right, the one you'd defend if a colleague across the table pushed back on it, build the whole scheme around it, and give your reasons. If you think a genuinely strong student could argue for a different diagnosis and still deserve full credit, name that alternative explicitly and say what it would have to demonstrate to earn it. Then flag the part of your own answer you're least sure about, and where I'm most likely to be wrong about what counts as an A.

Second, no citations, no reading list, no names of papers. Its closed book for them and you're not looking anything up either, so don't hang marks on a student remembering who said what — if a concept has a name they might reasonably know, you can use the name, but the marks have to be for the reasoning, not the label.
