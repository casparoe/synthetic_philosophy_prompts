I write literary fiction, and I have been trying to work out whether a quarrel about my own books can be made into a story rather than an essay. Here is the conceit I want you to build. A much-loved novelist has died, survived by an estate with unusual liquidity and an unusual asset: her private declarations of what she meant. The estate now sells these as certificates of intention — notarized, numbered, issued in limited lots to collectors, universities, and one stubborn reading group that has been arguing about her last novel for thirty years. Once a certificate is sold, the estate is bound by it, and the certified reading becomes the official one; the older, unlicensed readings are not banned so much as devalued. The trouble, which the estate is slow to notice, is that the certificates keep making the book worse. The readings people love are the ones she did not intend, and the market begins to pay a premium for intentions that spoil the novel. Two certificates eventually contradict each other, and nobody can say which governs.

Write this as a scene-driven story of roughly three thousand words, in the voice of the person whose job it is to authenticate the certificates and keep the ledger straight. The plot should turn on the final lot — a sealed certificate covering the last line of the novel — and on at least one buyer who has a reason to want it destroyed unread. Let the characters argue, but let none of them win by announcing a theory: the case of the reader who insists the book has outrun its maker, and the case of the estate that an author's word is the only word, must both be felt in what people do and what it costs them. Keep the distinction between what a text means and what it has come to matter running underneath the story without ever naming it, and treat the mechanics — provenance, forgery, priority, the price of a lot — with the seriousness of a real market.

End with the authenticator facing a decision that cannot be settled by looking up what the author meant, and show what she does. I am far less interested in a verdict on the quarrel than in the moment the machinery of intention stops working; if the story resolves the debate by fiat, it has failed.
