Our reading group on the ethics of belief hit a wall last week. Someone was arguing that paying a person to believe something is incoherent — you can't be paid into a belief, the money just gets you an assertion — and when pushed she said "belief aims at truth" and the room nodded and we moved on. Ten minutes later the same line came back for a totally different point: that believing something because it makes you happier can't be rational, even if it's true and even if you know you're doing it for that reason. I want to be able to say next week why both moves were too fast, and my hunch is that the sentence is doing at least four or five different jobs depending on whos saying it.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses "belief aims at truth" could be standing for — as a menu I can bring along, not as one reading you pick and then defend? The axes I've half-noticed but can't sort out: whether it's a claim about when a belief counts as correct versus a claim about what belief is *for* in some stronger constitutive sense; whether it's descriptive of how beliefs actually get formed, and if so what's supposed to happen with self-deception, motivated reasoning, and the friend you can't help believing is innocent; whether it's a claim about what kinds of considerations can be reasons for belief at all rather than merely causes of it; whether it's really a transparency thesis about how deliberation has to proceed; whether it's an analogy borrowed from intention or other goal-directed states, and if so how much weight the analogy is carrying; and whether there's a deflationary version that's just a remark about the evolutionary or social function of our belief-forming habits.

For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude that nobody in the room would deny, substantive but defensible, or actually dubious/false — and then which of them would support the first use and which would support the second use, if any. It doesnt have to be the same one for both, and I suspect the interesting result is that one of her arguments needs a much stronger reading than the other. Then say which reading you think she most likely had in mind, and whether there's a gap between that and the one that would have to be true for her conclusions to follow.

Finally: if none of the readings actually closes either question, tell me what the real crux was in each case — the thing we should have spent the rest of the hour arguing about instead of nodding at a slogan.
