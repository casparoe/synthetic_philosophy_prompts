I teach an upper-level seminar cross-listed between philosophy of biology and metaethics, on the theme of whether teleological talk can be made respectable without minds. It's a closed-book final and I need one essay question worth 25 marks, answerable in 50 minutes, plus a model answer of about 650 words and a marking scheme.

The topic is what I'll loosely call free-floating rationales — the Dennettian idea that a trait can have a rationale, an answer to the question "why this rather than that," that nobody ever represented: the design is explained by a benefit it conferred, but no mind anywhere ever had that benefit in view. The slogan version is reasons without reasoners. The question has to push past the slogan, because half my students will happily recite the intentional stance and etiological function theories back at me and think they've said something.

Here's the twist I want built into the stem, so give students the case in the question itself rather than assuming they know the biology: sea turtle hatchlings crawl toward the brightest low horizon, which for millions of years was the ocean reflecting sky and moonlight; on a developed coast the brightest horizon is now a streetlight or a hotel lobby, and the hatchlings crawl inland and die. Suppose also — this is the part I want the question to hang on — that the coastal population has been crashing for only a few generations, nowhere near long enough for selection to have altered the orientation response, so the trait is currently being winnowed but hasn't been replaced or repaired by anything else.

The question should make students answer something like: is there still a rationale for the orientation response, and if so whose interests does it track — the ancestral environment's, the current one's, or the biologist's? Then the harder half: can a rationale of this kind ever be a reason in the normative sense, a reason for someone, or does it only ever explain why a thing is the way it is? I want a genuine crux here, and the one I have in mind is that a rationale which "floats free" of the organism still seems to need an ascriber to be a rationale at all — so the slogan may covertly relocate the reasoner rather than eliminate it, and a student should be able to say whether the instrumentalist and the realist about such rationales pay different prices for that. Don't state that crux in the stem, obviously; that's what the marks are for.

Deliverables:

1. The exam question as students would see it, with the case spelled out in the stem, timed and marked (25 marks). No references, no "cite the literature" instructions — it's closed-book.

2. A model answer, roughly 650 words, that actually resolves the crux instead of surveying positions.

3. A marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves. I want it itemised: five or six must-hit points, the marks each carries, totalling 25, and for each one a line on how students typically botch it — e.g. sliding from "explains why the trait exists" to "counts in favour of the turtle doing it," or treating the mismatch case as if it settled the question when it only forces a choice between historical and current-fit readings, or declaring the whole notion a harmless metaphor and thereby losing the explanatory work it was doing.

4. A short list of three or four claims that should cost marks outright, the kinds of thing that sound sophisticated but show the student hasn't got it.
