You are a metaphysician working on modality and personal identity who has set and marked a great many final examinations. I teach an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of origin, and I need one essay question for the final: answerable in fifty minutes, worth twenty-five marks.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard survey — the statement of Kripke's doctrine that a person's origins are necessary, Salmon's arguments for it, the counterpart-theoretic rejoinder. Build it instead around a pair of developmental cases the doctrine is rarely pressed against in class:

- Case A. The sperm and egg that actually fused to produce a certain person fuse on schedule, but the zygote splits within its first days and develops into two healthy monozygotic twins.
- Case B. Two distinct zygotes, formed from two different eggs and two different sperm, fuse early in development and give rise to a single chimeric individual.

Have students work out what the origin essentialist owes in each case. The question should require them to keep two components of the doctrine explicitly apart — the necessity direction (no one other than this person could have come from these very gametes) and the sufficiency direction (had these very gametes fused, the resulting person would have been this very person) — and to identify which case threatens which component. It should then press them on the available repairs: restating the doctrine in terms of the zygote rather than the gametes, in terms of a developmental process or causal-historical chain, in terms of the dominant lineage or the majority of the person's cells, or in terms of an indeterminate origin; and on whether any repair still delivers what motivated the doctrine, namely the intuition that a given person could not have had wholly different parents, together with the arguments that trade on necessity and sufficiency working in concert.

Before drafting anything, map the space of responses in a few sentences: for each case, list the repairs available, note which collapse into one another, and note which amount to abandoning the doctrine rather than defending it. Think carefully about whether the two cases are symmetric in the damage they do — I suspect they are not, and I want the question to exploit that asymmetry rather than let students treat the cases interchangeably.

Then deliver: (i) the exam question as it would appear on the paper, self-contained and phrased so that a well-prepared student needs no sources at hand to answer it; (ii) a model answer of roughly 700 words; and (iii) a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a first-class answer must contain, states precisely what separates that from a competent but merely accurate answer, and lists the characteristic errors with the marks each should cost. No bibliography, reading list, or citations in either the question or the model answer.
