So I'm deep in a novel whose central device is that karma is real and mechanical — not vaguely, the way people say it at dinner parties, but literally: the universe keeps a ledger, and what my characters call luck, windfall, illness, and accident is just the ledger balancing. I know the actual doctrines aren't one thing (Jain, Buddhist, the various Hindu schools all cut it differently) and I'm not trying to write a religion, I'm trying to write a machine that a reader will feel is fair even when it's horrible. To get there I've been pressure-testing my own intuitions about who deserves what by scribbling little cases in a notebook and marking them pass/fail, and I've noticed they don't add up to anything I could name. Roughly in the order I wrote them: (1) A man lies about his partner, takes the business, prospers for forty years, dies comfortably in his sleep at 83 — I feel this is a *defective world*, not merely that he got away with it, like a sentence with no full stop. (2) Same man, same lie, exposed at 35, loses everything, spends thirty years paying the partner's family back in small instalments, dies poor and mildly respected — ledger closed, right. (3) A woman does something genuinely cruel at fifteen, turns her whole school against a girl who then kills herself, spends her adult life atoning, becomes the kind of person who'd stop for a wounded animal in the road, and at sixty gets the wasting disease she effectively handed that girl — I think that's unjust, not just sad; she's paid, and she isn't that person anymore. (4) But: a man does something equally cruel at fifteen, forgets it completely, drifts into being a bland and kindly forgetful grandfather, and at sixty gets the same disease — I think this one is deserved. He's *further* from the act than the woman in (3), he can't even recall it, and yet my gut says the ledger's open on him and closed on her. Those two are the ones keeping me up. (5) A man repairs everything, apologizes, makes the victim whole, but only because he was caught, and in private still thinks he'd have done it again given the chance — I feel his repair closes nothing, which seems to contradict (2), where I never asked about his motives. (6) Two drivers, both texting, same speed, same road; one happens to hit a child who runs out, the other doesn't — as a machine I think the ledger should treat them identically, same act same intent, but as a reader I want the one who killed to be carrying something the other isn't. (7) A mill poisons a river for three generations and the grandchildren of the people who profited get the cancer cluster — part of me says the ledger runs through the town and not the individuals, part of me says that's monstrous and each child is being punished for somebody else's act. (8) In my mechanics, a child born with that wasting disease has it because of something she did in a previous life. I want to keep the mechanics *and* I want the reader to feel it's unbearable, and that anyone who says she deserves it is a villain. I have no idea how to have both.

My tentative principle, which I wrote on an index card and dont fully believe: the ledger only closes when the person who did the thing can look at it, and the person it was done to can look at them. That seems to handle (3) and (4) — she acknowledged, and the dead girl is present in her life every day; he never did either — and it handles (5) if you add that the looking has to be for its own sake. But it makes desert depend on a performance of contrition, which would let a slick sociopath who's good at tears wipe his ledger clean, and it says nothing about (1), where everybody's dead, or (8), where nobody remembers anything. So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets my verdicts — stated precisely enough that I could actually run the book on it, with each clause tied to the case that forces it? I'd want its verdicts on cases I haven't thought of: the wrongdoer who dies halfway through an atonement; the victim who refuses to look at them, or is dead and would have refused; someone who harms a person who was harming them first; two people who each wrong the other equally and neither apologizes; a good act done purely to close a ledger. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts have to go — if (3) and (4) can't both stand, tell me which one a coherent account keeps and what I lose by dropping the other, rather than building me something baroque that saves all eight. Also: does any of this actually need the rebirth machinery, or is reincarnation doing no moral work here — could a single-life ledger, or a purely this-worldly one of reputation and self-relation, deliver the same verdicts? And finally, and this is the part I'm least sure I want answered: is whatever you come up with a theory of moral desert I'd have to accept outside the book too, or is it only coherent as fictional machinery?
