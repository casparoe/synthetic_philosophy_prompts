Background, so you know where I'm coming from: I spent thirty-odd years as a plant physiologist — stomata, water relations, a bit of electrophysiology on the side — and since retiring I've been sitting in on a reading group run by an old colleague who drifted over to the cognitive science end of things. Lately it's all predictive processing, free energy, minimal cognition, that sort of thing. He's the one who gets to decide which systems count as doing inference, and I've noticed his rulings look fairly arbitrary to me, though I'm not sure I have the concepts to say why properly. The record so far, roughly in the order he gave them:

- E. coli swimming up a sugar gradient: yes, it's modelling its world.
- A bimetallic strip thermostat in one of our old incubators: no.
- Venus flytrap: yes, and he was keen on this one, because it waits for a second touch before it snaps shut and only bothers making digestive enzymes after several more.
- Physarum, the slime mould, the one that grew a network between oat flakes not unlike a rail map: yes, but grudgingly.
- A river delta redistributing its channels after a flood: no — "just physics, nothing it's like on the inside."
- The adaptive immune system, affinity maturation and all: yes.
- A single photoreceptor cell with its whole transduction cascade, considered on its own: no. When I pushed on this, given he'd just said yes to the immune system, he muttered something about it not being a whole organism and changed the subject.
- A trained recurrent network driving a little robot arm: no, which surprised me. His reason was that it never had a childhood. I couldnt parse that one at all.
- A bee colony: yes, unhesitatingly.
- A hedge fund's trading algorithm: no, instantly, no discussion allowed.

Next week he's asked me to bring two cases from my own field, and I'd like to know what I'm walking into. First: an artificial cell — a lipid vesicle with an engineered light-driven proton pump, which adjusts its own membrane composition to hold internal pH in range. It's fed continuously from the experimenter's buffer, it doesn't grow or divide, and it has no evolutionary history whatsoever; it was designed last Tuesday. Second, and closer to home: a pair of guard cells excised from a leaf and kept alive in a bath, still responding to CO2, humidity, abscisic acid and light, with no plant attached and no pore left to open or shut.

So: what does he say about each, and why? I want you to lay out the candidate rules that fit his ten verdicts equally well — I can think of at least three or four myself, something about a statistical boundary being drawn around the system, something about acting on the world to change your own inputs, something about a history of selection, something about being a whole rather than a part — and say which of those best explains the awkward ones, the photoreceptor and the robot arm especially. Then give me your best guess at each of my two cases under each rule, note where the rules part company, and tell me what single further case I could bring the week after that would force him to show his hand. And if you think one of his past rulings is flatly inconsistent with the rest, say which, because I'd like something to raise at the meeting. I'm not after references or a reading list, definately not — just the reasoning, spelled out.
