Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about the centipede game, and neither of us gave ground, so I would like the argument written up properly rather than left where it ended.

Here is the set-up as we had it. Two players alternate between Take and Pass along a finite chain. The pot grows as it is passed, but whoever takes captures the larger share and ends the game, leaving the other with less than that player would have collected by taking one node later. Backward induction says the first player takes at the opening node, and says so for a particular reason: at the final node the player whose turn it is must take; that makes passing irrational at the penultimate node; and the reasoning walks back to the start. Laboratory play refuses to cooperate. Most pairs pass several times, and taking at the first node is a minority outcome.

My friend's diagnosis, which I will call the horizon objection, is that the experiments do not show backward induction failing. They show that the game as presented to the subjects is not the game the subjects believed they were playing. On his account the entire inductive argument is carried by one assumption: that both players know, and know that the other knows, exactly which decision node is the last. Remove that assumption — suppose each player thinks that after any node the game may be called off from outside with some small probability, before the chain runs out — and even a purely self-interested, fully rational player will pass early, because the prospect of an exogenous ending blunts the certainty of loss that a determinate terminus guarantees at the far end. His conclusion was that experimenters have been testing a game with a commonly known endpoint that no subject actually accepted, since any real participant carries residual doubt about whether the announced interaction is the whole of the interaction.

My reply was that this rescues the principle by emptying it. If its predictions hold only under common knowledge of a determinate terminus, and if that condition is essentially never met in a laboratory or anywhere else, then the principle has no content that observation could confirm; and the one prediction it retains, the immediate take, is precisely the one the data deny. I added a second objection: his variant is not neutral. Once the game can end on its own, passing stops being a gift to the other player and becomes a joint gamble against the house, so the two games do not share a strategic character, and showing that rational players pass in his version shows nothing about the original.

Write an essay adjudicating between these two diagnoses. It should do the following.

First, state the horizon objection as precisely as you can. What exactly does the assumption of a commonly known terminus contribute to the inductive step? Is it doing more work than the assumption of rationality at each node, or than common knowledge of rationality generally? Identify which of these assumptions, if relaxed, does the real damage.

Second, work through the variant explicitly rather than gesturing at it. Construct a short chain of four or six nodes with concrete payoffs of your own choosing, then introduce a per-node probability that the game terminates externally with both players receiving some fixed small amount. Show whether, and over what range of that probability, early passing becomes optimal for a self-interested player. Then say whether the result requires the two players to share the same estimate of the probability, or only requires each of them to have some doubt, and explain why that distinction matters for what an experimenter could actually establish.

Third, take my two objections seriously rather than summarily. Is the unfalsifiability charge fair, or does a principle that yields determinate predictions under idealized conditions retain normative force even when those conditions fail? And does exogenous termination genuinely change the strategic character of passing, or only its surface description?

Fourth, consider at least one diagnosis that neither of us offered — other-regarding or inequality-averse preferences, bounded levels of strategic reasoning, subjects treating the session as one round in a longer relationship, simple confusion about the payoff table — and say whether any of them explains early passing better than the horizon objection does, or whether they are compatible with it and merely located at different points in the same causal chain.

Fifth, end on a verdict rather than a survey. Say which of my claims I should have conceded and which of his he should have conceded. If the correct conclusion is that backward induction survives intact but applies only to games nobody plays, state that plainly, and then say what is left of it: whether it remains a normative standard against which actual choices can be assessed, or has been reduced to a description of an equilibrium concept with no behavioral purchase.

I want no citations, bibliography, or inventory of the literature — reconstruct the argument on its merits, with the formal steps shown where they carry weight. Assume I am comfortable with extensive-form games, expected utility, and the idea of a subgame, but am not a specialist. Somewhere between 1,500 and 2,500 words, and committed throughout: I have had enough of non-committal positions from this debate already.
