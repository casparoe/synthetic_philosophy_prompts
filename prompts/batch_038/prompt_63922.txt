I direct the psychometrics group at a social-research and polling firm. My working day consists of writing attitude items, running confirmatory factor analyses, testing for measurement invariance across groups (configural, metric, scalar, strict), hunting for differential item functioning, and arguing with clients about whether the scale they are fond of measures the construct they think it measures. I am fluent in that apparatus and largely untrained in the political philosophy that keeps turning up in the commentary I read on the side.

The commentary concerns two broad dispositions and the question of whether one is more defensible than the other. On one side, a rooted, predominantly working-class and small-town outlook: loyalty to local institutions, continuity of place, craft, and family; norms of honor and dignity organized around being treated as someone who contributes; suspicion of abstraction and of credentialed gatekeeping; stability and obligation prized over optionality. On the other, a professional-class and mobile outlook: openness to experience, self-reinvention, portability of skill, cosmopolitan rather than local affiliation, and a willingness to be evaluated by standardized, general-purpose measures. Writers on both sides claim that their outlook is not merely different but better grounded — truer to what persons actually need, less dependent on flattering self-description, less parasitic on the other.

I would like that entire argument restated in my vocabulary, as faithfully as possible. Treat the two outlooks as candidate constructs, and treat each substantive claim made on their behalf as a hypothesis about those constructs that I could in principle test: latent dimensions and their loadings, convergent and discriminant validity, factor structure and whether the two outlooks are opposing poles of one dimension or two oblique dimensions or a latent class distinction rather than a trait, item difficulty and discrimination, acquiescence and social desirability, construct-irrelevant variance, and invariance across the groups that hold them.

Then I want the residue report, and I want it to be the main event rather than an afterthought:

1. Which claims translate cleanly — where the target vocabulary lets me say exactly what the partisans are asserting, perhaps better than they say it themselves.
2. Which claims translate only under distortion — where I can produce a sentence in my idiom that looks like the original but has shifted its subject, and where you can name precisely what moved.
3. Which claims have no expression at all — where there is no construct, no parameter, and no admissible inference for what is being claimed.

For category three, address the obvious candidate directly: what would it take, in my idiom, to say that one outlook is more defensible than the other? If your answer is that criterion-related validity is the closest analogue, then say whose criterion, and explain why the selection of the criterion is not itself the thing being disputed. Relatedly, tell me whether any of the empirical results I could actually obtain — scalar non-invariance between the groups, DIF on items about ambition or loyalty or rootedness, group differences in reported life satisfaction or in mortality and morbidity — would license any move in the philosophical argument, or whether deploying them that way is a category error. If it is an error, characterize it exactly rather than waving at it.

Two further constraints. First, police your own translation: flag every point at which one of my technical terms — valid, reliable, well-calibrated, adaptive, functioning, biased, in need of correction — is doing covert normative work, and every point at which a claim from the debate has been quietly converted into a claim about measurement rather than reported as lost. I would rather have a shorter translation with an honest ledger than a complete one that has absorbed the debate's conclusions into my instrumentation. Second, watch for a trap I suspect is there: my framework is very good at representing a group's self-description as a response style or an adaptation, and a translation that renders the rooted outlook as a set of satisficing behaviors and the mobile outlook as accurate responding has already decided the question in favor of the people who wrote and validated the items. Say whether that asymmetry is real, and if so, whether it is a fact about my methods or about who staffs them.

Close with a verdict I can argue with: is the residue best read as a limitation of psychometrics, which deliberately brackets the evaluative question it cannot formalize, or as evidence that the debate, as usually conducted, is not one disagreement but a bundle of empirical, psychological, and normative claims that my vocabulary is useful precisely because it forces them apart?
