I want a short story, maybe 1200–1800 words, that dramatizes Descartes' beast-machine view from the inside rather than sneering at it. Setting: the menagerie attached to a Cartesian academy in the 1660s, whose charter says no bit of animal behavior counts as evidence of thought unless the animal deploys a sign in a way that nothing in its immediate surroundings prompted — the creative use of signs, not cleverness at solving problems. Your protagonist is the clerk who keeps the behavior ledger, and one of his charges (jackdaw, dog, your pick) starts producing entries that satisfy that clause literally, which everyone around him wants to log as mere disposition of the organs anyway. The engine of the story should be the clerk slowly realizing the charter is doing all the work — that he can't tell whether he's applying a criterion or defending a conclusion — and he has to decide what goes in the next entry, knowing the ledger is read by the academys patron. End on the entry itself. Keep the animal dumb in the ways real animals are dumb: no talking-parrot reveal, no inner monologue from the beast, the whole tension is that the evidence stays genuinely ambiguous. No moral, no authorial wink. And start straight into the story — no title card, no preface, no note about what you were going for.
