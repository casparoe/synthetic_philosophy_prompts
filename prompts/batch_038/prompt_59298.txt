My roommate and I have been going in circles for like three weeks about whether game characters are the kind of thing you can wrong, so I built an argument to corner him and I'm now pretty sure it corners me instead. Here it is, and I'd rather know where it snaps than get a survey.

(1) For something to be a moral patient there has to be a way things can go *for* it — it has to have interests.
(2) Having interests doesn't take consciousness, only a real distinction between states that serve the thing's own goals and states that thwart them. (This is the premise I want to keep. It's what blocks the "but they can't feel anything" reply, which I think begs the question, since we don't know what feeling is made of and my roommate's own line — simulating a rainstorm doesn't get you wet, so simulating a subject doesn't get you a subject — assumes that the wetness has an analogue that the goal-seeking doesn't.)
(3) My Sim has goals in exactly that sense. She's got a needs panel, an aspiration to make it to the top of the cooking career, and when things go badly she sulks, complains, revises her plans, and the numbers on her go down in ways she then behaves to fix.
(4) So she has interests, and setting her on fire by removing the ladder from the pool frustrates them. That's a harm to her.
(5) But I quicksaved before the ladder came out. After a while I reload, and in the loaded game she never drowned, never lost the promotion, never got sad. From the only perspective that now exists, nothing bad ever happened to her.
(6) If an act is wrong in virtue of making some patient worse off, and reloading makes it true that no patient was made worse off, then the drowning wasn't wrong.
(7) And it generalizes past reloading: she also exists in ten million other households on other machines, and whatever I do to mine leaves theirs untouched. Nothing I can do affects more than a sliver of her instances, and for every instance that suffers there's a near identical one that's fine.
(8) Worse, the unplayed ones count too on (2). A Sim nobody ever loads, or a procedurally generated villager whose chunk was never rendered, is fully described by exactly the same goal-structure my played one has. If the description is what grounds interests, then the patients include everything that could ever be instantiated, which is more than I can affect by any stretch.
(9) So: any being that comes in fungible, restorable, indefinitely many copies — rendered or not — can't be wronged by anything you do to it, no matter how bad it looks from the inside.
(10) I can't accept (9). I'm pretty confident that if you could cheaply and reversibly torment something copyable, you still shouldn't, and that the ten-million-copies point doesn't license anything.
(11) So something above is false, and by (2) it isn't the thing I'd least like to lose.

Where does it go? I keep suspecting (5) and (6) but every time I try to say why out loud I end up with something like "the version that suffered still suffered," which seems to make the suffering real in a way a reload obviously doesn't undo, and he says that's just homesickness for a timeline that isn't there anymore. I also don't know whether (8) is a separate bug or the same one wearing a hat.

So: name the step you'd cut, say why it looked so innocent before you cut it, and tell me what I'm paying. If you'd rather say the chain is valid and that the price falls on a premise, price it — I want to know specifically what (2) costs me if I have to give it up, and whether there's some weaker version of (2) that keeps the anti-consciousness work but stops (7) and (8) from following. And if you think (9) is actually true and I'm just squeamish, say that and argue it.

Lead with your bottom line — the step, or the premise, in the first sentence — and then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the argument back at me, no bookkeeping about what you're going to do.
