I lead the perception group at a warehouse automation company: state estimation, occupancy mapping, multi-sensor fusion, the standard recursive Bayes filter stack. My training is in engineering and my philosophy amounts to one undergraduate course. I am not looking for references or a reading list — I have no way to chase papers — but for an analysis I can check against machinery I already understand.

The subject is Jeffrey conditionalization: the proposal about how an agent should revise its degrees of belief when an observation fails to render it certain of any proposition. My working suspicion is that this is a problem my field settled long ago — a classifier emits class probabilities rather than a hard label, a range return is noisy, and we fuse — and that the philosophers are reinventing a special case of the Bayes filter. I want to know whether that suspicion survives contact with the actual doctrine. Treat it as a hypothesis under test, not as a premise.

**Part one: the translation.** Restate the doctrine and the argument around it in my vocabulary — state space, prior, observation model, likelihood function known only up to scale, recursive versus batch processing, log-odds, calibration, consistency, divergence-based projections. Work through the components in order: the partition; the probabilities the experience is supposed to hand the agent; the rigidity condition, that is, the requirement that the observation leave conditional probabilities within each cell of the partition untouched; the update formula; the question of what, if anything, constrains those handed-over numbers (I gather critics allege that nothing does, which would make the rule a description of what happens rather than a norm); the rival proposal that the agent should instead become certain of, and conditionalize upon, some proposition describing the experience itself; and whether such an update can be reversed, since I can divide a stored likelihood back out and an agent may have no equivalent operation. For each component, give the counterpart in estimation terms or state that there is none. Do not smuggle: if a component translates only once you add an assumption my framework makes silently and without argument, mark that assumption as an addition rather than presenting it as the translation.

**Part two: a discrepancy I cannot resolve, which I think decides the question.** Take a state space of four cells — the item on the belt is metal or plastic, and heavy or light. Historical data give the prior: metal-and-heavy 0.40, metal-and-light 0.10, plastic-and-heavy 0.10, plastic-and-light 0.40. The hypothesis of interest is that the item is the steel bracket, the metal-and-heavy cell, so its prior is 0.40. Two uncertain observations arrive in sequence: a vision pass that leaves me with P(metal) = 0.70, and a lift test that leaves me with P(heavy) = 0.90. Taking each as an input on its own partition and holding the relevant conditionals fixed, I get P(bracket) ≈ 0.813 when the vision pass is absorbed first, and ≈ 0.681 when the lift test is absorbed first. My filter does not behave this way: if each sensor contributes a likelihood and I multiply, the recursion is order-independent. When I rerun the same two inputs on a prior in which the features are independent — 0.25 in each cell — the two orders agree at 0.63. So either I have misapplied the rule, or the rule's input is not a likelihood. Which is it? Specifically: is the order-dependence a symptom of the fact that a probability handed over on a partition already has the prior folded into it, so that absorbing two of them counts the prior twice — and if so, what precisely is double-counted, and why does the prior dependence between the two features determine whether the pathology appears at all?

**Part three: the residue report.** Sort the components into three columns — what translates cleanly, what translates only at the cost of distortion, naming what is distorted, and what my vocabulary cannot express at all. Then give the verdict I am after: for each item in the third column, is it inexpressible because estimation theory lacks a resource it ought to have, in which case identify the concrete failure mode in my own practice that the resource would catch; or because the philosophical claim was less determinate than it appeared, in which case say so. I am prepared to be told that my field mishandles uncertain evidence and that the objection lands. I am equally prepared to be told that the debate dissolves once evidence is modeled as a likelihood rather than as a probability. Do not hedge between the two.

One loose end. I have encountered the claim that Jeffrey's rule is the unique revision minimizing a relative-entropy divergence from the prior subject to the constraint the experience imposes. An information projection onto a linear constraint set is entirely familiar to me, so I expect this piece to translate suspiciously well rather than badly. Check it: does that characterization actually pick out the rule, is the constraint set an engineer would write down for a soft class-probability output the same set the philosophers have in mind, and does uniqueness survive the comparison?
