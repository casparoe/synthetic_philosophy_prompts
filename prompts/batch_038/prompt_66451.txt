I do a science-and-ideas column and next month I've got about ninety minutes with a metaphysician whose view I don't think my readers will have run into: she says composition isn't all-or-nothing. On her account the planks don't either compose the ship or fail to compose it — they compose it to some degree, and the "special compisition question" is malformed because it demands a yes/no where the real answer is a number between zero and one. She's articulate and quick, and I don't want the piece to turn into five paragraphs of her repeating the slogan at me.

Give me exactly five questions, numbered, one or two sentences each, nothing before or after the list — no intro, no wrap-up, no "you might also try asking about." For each item, spend the second sentence either on the follow-up that closes off her likeliest dodge or on naming the answer you're trying to make unavailable to her, so I can see why you picked it. Commit to these five rather than hedging with alternates or a menu of options.

The set has to cover, in this order: whether degrees relocate the problem instead of dissolving it (if the degrees are sharp we're straight back to hunting for a cutoff, and if they're fuzzy tell me what was gained); counting — how many things are in the dry dock, and whether partial composition lands her with extra objects in a region or just vaguer talk about one, plus the problem of the many; whether she's a realist about the degrees or whether they secretly track our explanatory interests, in which case she's reinvented conventionalism with a scalar gloss; and causation — do the parts' causal powers come in degrees too, or does she think the whole's powers over-determine the parts'. Then the fifth question should be the exit one: what argument or observation would actually move her off the view, pressed hard enough that "that's a research program" isn't a usable answer.
