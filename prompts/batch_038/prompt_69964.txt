A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about tranquilism — the view he defends, according to which the only things that ultimately bear on whether a life goes well are the absence of suffering and the absence of craving: not pleasure, not achievement, not satisfied preferences. I would like a move-by-move adjudication rather than a summary of either position.

(1) He argued that craving is a standing disturbance, so someone who wants little is better off than someone whose cravings keep being met, since each satisfaction is brief while the wanting persists underneath it; hence the good life is one with minimal desire.

(2) I objected that this is preference satisfaction in disguise: not-craving is itself something he wants, so tranquilism smuggles in the very currency it claims to reject — a desire for desirelessness, duly satisfied.

(3) He replied that the absence of craving is a state of a person, not a satisfied desire, and pressed this case on me: two lives identical in pleasure and in suffering, except that one contains a large unfulfilled craving — a film she means to make and never makes — while the other contains no such craving. He says the second is better off, and that I cannot account for the difference by appealing to frustrated preference, since the first woman reports no distress whatever.

(4) I said that concedes my point: craving can matter only because it is, or tends to produce, suffering, so tranquilism collapses into the ordinary claim that suffering is the sole bad thing, with decorative extra steps.

(5) He answered that an unsatisfied but painless craving is a tension in the shape of a life rather than a felt episode of pain, and that if I deny this I owe some account of why wanting anything is ever worth the trouble. He then volunteered his own difficulty — that his view appears to rank the contented prisoner above the free woman who longs for freedom — and asked whether lowering aspiration can ever count as improving a life at all.

Please rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with reasons; say whether any version of tranquilism survives move (4); and identify the assumption we both treated as fixed and should not have. No names or references are needed, just the verdicts.
