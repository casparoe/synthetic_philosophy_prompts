Every year on the Sunday nearest the anniversary our town does a small service at the seafront memorial — the one with the eleven names on it, men from here who went over to France in 1940 and didn't come back — and this year the committee have asked me to give the address. Ten minutes, out of doors, and afterwards tea in the church hall. I said yes in about two seconds and have been quietly panicking ever since.

Who's actually standing there: our branch of the little-ships association, which is now mostly grandchildren, including one man whose grandfather took a fishing smack over and who has told that story at this memorial for thirty years running — and I know, because I looked it up, that the association's own published history says most of those boats were taken across by naval officers and ratings and requisitioning parties rather than by their owners, and I have never had the nerve to ask which category his grandfather falls into. A delegation from our twinned town in France: the mayor, plus four others, and one of the four is an old man whose father was in the French First Army and who has told my colleague that his father is not mentioned once in any English account of this he has ever read. Two classes of Year 9 who did a project on the evacuation, stood at the back with their teacher, cold. The vicar, who wants it hopeful and who has already printed the bit of Churchill's speech of 4 June 1940 about wars not being won by evacuations into the order of service. And two councillors, one of whom has a banner up the High Street with the Dunkirk spirit on it in letters about a foot high.

Here's my problem, and it isn't that the local version gets a few facts wrong. What the myth does is move victory off the battlefield and onto character, and once you've done that you can never lose again. The army came home with the rifles in its hands and left the guns and the lorries and the tanks and the ammunition on the sand; the getting-away depended on nine days of almost flat sea, on hesitations on the German side that nobody could have banked on, and above all on French troops holding the perimeter — forty thousand-odd of the First Army were still fighting house to house in Lille while we queued on the beaches, and then marched out with honours of war into captivity and out of the English story altogether. But if the question is no longer "did you win" and instead "what sort of people were you", the answer is always flattering, and that is why the same phrase now gets wheeled out for floods and pandemics and any other thing we failed to prevent. That's what's on the banner.

I don't want to debunk from the front of a memorial service — partly becuase it's cruel, and partly because it isn't true either, there was real courage in it. But I can't tell it straight, and the old Frenchman and the Year 9s are both within earshot. And the thing that would appal me most is making those eleven instrumental: saying their deaths bought time, or bought the story, or made anything possible. They were twenty and they died on a beach.

So please write the address. It has to be true; it has to still do what a commemoration does for people whose grief has only ever had this one shape; it has to give the French their place in a way that doesn't read as a courteous aside; and it has to leave the phrase on the banner unavailable to its owner without being seen to refuse it, because I do not want to be the man who had a go at his own towns memorial in the Gazette. The children should be able to follow it without being talked down to. Aim for 900 to 1,100 words of speech. Then separately, afterwards, tell me which sentences are carrying the double meaning and doing the real structural work, and what you'd have to change if the room were fifty historians and no descendants at all.
