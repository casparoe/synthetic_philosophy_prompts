**Question (25 marks).** Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence (PB) holds that prospective parents who have more than one possible child should select the child expected to have the best life.
(a) A common objection is that PB's reasons are impersonal, and so cannot ground a duty owed to anyone. State that objection and say what Savulescu can do with it.
(b) Distinguish *impersonal* from *non-identity-invoking*: a reason is impersonal when its force doesn't depend on any particular person's interests being served or set back, while a choice raises the non-identity problem when it fixes which particular people will exist. Give one case where the two come apart, in either direction.
(c) Is Habermas's objection to liberal eugenics a version of the objection in (a)? Why or why not?

Marking scheme I wrote: 5 for stating PB and the impersonality objection accurately, 6 for (a) including what Savulescu can actually do, 6 for a genuine case in (b), 8 for (c).

**Student's answer.**

"Savulescu argues that if a couple can have either child A or child B, and B is expected to have the better life, they ought to have B, and this applies to traits that aren't diseases as well, like intelligence or the capacity for self-control. The objection is impersonal: the duty isn't owed to anybody, because the worse-off child A never exists and so nobody is wronged. Parfit's non-identity problem settles this: choosing A does not harm A, since A's only alternative was never to exist, and existence is worth having for A. So the non-identity problem actually shows PB is a genuine duty — once we see that no one can complain, the only reasons left standing are the impersonal ones, and they point to B. Savulescu can add that A has a right to the best possible life, which the couple would violate.

On (b), impersonal reasons and non-identity reasons always come together, because a reason is impersonal exactly when the choice changes who exists; the one case where they look like they come apart is where the alternative is not to have any child at all, but that is just the same case again, so the distinction collapses.

Habermas objects for a different reason. Parents who program a child's traits treat the child as a product rather than a person, and the child can no longer understand itself as the undivided author of its own life, so the relation between them stops being a relation of equals. But this objection is also impersonal: Habermas isn't claiming any particular child is harmed, he's claiming the practice damages the ethical self-understanding of the species, which is a religious-flavoured claim of the same family as Kass's worries about playing God, and it condemns genetic intervention generally, including prenatal treatment for disease, since that is programming too. So Habermas's objection is a version of the one in (a): both deny that PB's reasons are owed to anybody, and they only disagree about whether that matters."
