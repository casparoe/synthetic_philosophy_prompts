My older sister has spent eleven years running outreach for a community violence-interruption program in a mid-sized American city, modelled explicitly on the Chicago operation that treats violence as though it were an infectious disease. I work in supply-chain analytics and have no training in philosophy or the social sciences, but I have been reading around the long-run decline in homicide on my own, mostly out of stubbornness, and over the holidays she laid out a version of the disease picture that I could neither answer nor accept. Her point is not that the comparison is merely colourful; she uses it to close at least four questions that I had assumed were still open.

First, she says only rates mean anything: when the raw number of killings rises because there are more people, that is like reporting a worse measles season because the town grew, so the headlines about violence climbing again since the mid-2010s are, as she puts it, arithmetic dressed up as news. Second, she says the several-century fall in homicide cannot be moral progress, because it is exactly the pattern you would expect from improved transmission conditions — nobody credits the eighteenth century with an ethical breakthrough because smallpox retreated, and she thinks anyone who reads the homicide decline as evidence of improving human character is making the same error in reverse. Third, she holds that blame is a category mistake: you do not punish a person for carrying tuberculosis, you interrupt transmission, so a policy built on desert is superstition and the honest alternative is contact tracing plus interrupters standing between a provoked young man and the person he intends to shoot. Fourth, and most contested for me, she says the analogy tells us what violence is: only harm passing from person to person is the disease, while poverty, incarceration, and killings by officials are risk factors or social determinants standing behind it — so when critics insist the world has not become less violent because they are counting those other harms, her answer is that they have quietly changed the subject.

Please put the analogy itself under examination rather than taking a side on it. I would like the correspondences worked through explicitly — what in her domain plays the role of the pathogen, the host, the vector, the incubation period, the reproduction number, susceptibility, the reservoir that keeps the thing from ever being eradicated, the vaccine, herd immunity, diagnosis and surveillance, treatment, and the endemic baseline we are supposed to aim for — and then, for each of her four conclusions, which pieces of that mapping are genuinely load-bearing. After that, sort the disanalogies into the ones that bite against those specific uses and the ones that are merely cosmetic. My own suspicion is that a disease comes with a functional norm nobody argues about, whereas what counts as violence has drifted across the centuries in ways that move the numbers themselves, and that the carrier of a disease is not an agent who can be reasoned with, promised something, or made to owe an apology, while a prospective shooter is all three; but I may be reaching. Say what the picture silently imports — a fixed diagnostic category, a non-agential transmitter, an identity between the person and the condition, the assumption that rates are all tracking one natural kind of thing — and then tell me, conclusion by conclusion, which of her four claims survive once those imports are exposed and which were being carried by the analogy all along. If some better comparison fits the same phenomena — a self-reinforcing norm, a coordination failure, an arms race, something I have not thought of — say what it would license instead, and note whether she could keep any of the four claims after giving the disease language up. I am after the reasoning, not a list of further reading.
