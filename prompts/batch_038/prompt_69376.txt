Been reading Strawson's Freedom and Resentment and the stuff written around it, mostly for fun — I'm a ward nurse, not a philosopher — and there's a pattern I keep meeting in real life that I haven't seen anyone describe properly. Is there a name for it?

Someone does you a small injury. A colleague, close-ish, doesn't back you up in a meeting and you're properly stung, and you stay resentful for days. Then the excuse turns up: their mum has just been taken into hospital, they haven't slept in two nights, whatever it is, and you can see it's a real excuse, you're not disputing it. And here's the bit. The resentment doesnt go away and it doesn't stay where it was, either. It moves. Within an hour you're cross about something adjacent instead — that they never apologised, that they did much the same thing in March, that they've been a bit cold generally since Easter. And the odd thing is how it feels from the inside: it doesn't feel like fresh resentment about a fresh item, it feels like the identical resentment you had on Tuesday, same weight, same heat, that has simply found somewhere it's allowed to land. People aren't even being evasive about it. Ask them and they'll say it's the March thing you're annoyed about, and they mean it, and the March thing is real. The intensity carries across intact; only the object changes. And it does seem to be doing a job — giving up the original grievance would mean admitting you'd been unfair for a week, and this route means you never have to.

Two more so you can see the shape of it:

- A friend misses your dad's funeral. You're furious. Then you find out she genuinely never got the message, nobody told her. The fury doesn't dissolve, it reattaches: by that evening you're furious that she hadn't checked in on you once in three weeks, which is true but which you had not minded in the slightest until an hour ago.
- Someone cuts you up on the motorway and you're livid for the rest of the drive. Then you notice a baby seat and a passenger obviously in distress, and you accept it — you really do accept it, you feel a bit ashamed — and you are still, continuously, just as livid, now about their lane discipline over the preceding twenty miles and the fact they never indicated. That second complaint was definately not being felt beforehand.

What I'm not asking about, so you don't answer with any of these:

- Resentment that persists against your own judgement that it's unwarranted. I've come across the discussion of recalcitrant emotions and I know that's the neighbouring territory, but there the feeling stays put on the same object and you disapprove of it. Mine is the reverse: it relocates, and then it gets your wholehearted endorsement in its new home.
- Displacement, the kick-the-dog thing. That's aggression moving to a safer target because the real one is out of reach. Here the original target is still right there and still available, and still felt to be the person you're cross with — it's the justification that moved, not the aim.
- Ordinary resentment of a genuine pattern. If the March thing was resented at the time and simply hadn't come to mind, fine, that's normal moral memory and I'm not interested. The phenomenon is a previously unfelt, previously unnoticed item getting recruited with its resentment already paid for.
- Self-deception generally. Though I want to know how much of it is going on here, because the recruitment seems to happen honestly.

So: does this have an established concept anywhere — in the reactive attitudes literature, the philosophy of emotion, moral psychology, ordinary psychology, doesn't matter where? If yes, give me the term and how it's actually defined, and tell me exactly where I'd be stretching it to cover what I've described, because I suspect I am. If no, say so plainly rather than handing me the nearest thing and implying it's a match, and then coin one: a term, a definition, the conditions that have to hold for an episode to count, and the boundary cases — specifically where it shades into (a) legitimate resentment of a pattern you'd genuinely missed, (b) scapegoating, (c) plain self-deception — and what you'd look for to tell those apart in a real case rather than in a thought experiment. I'd also like to know what the Strawsonian toolkit makes of it: if the participant attitude is at bottom a demand for good will rather than a response to individual acts, does that predict this relocation, explain it, or just not have anything to say?

To be clear, I'm not after a label to win a pub quiz with. The carving is the point — what the thing exactly is, what it isn't, where its edges sit. Write it as prose for as long as you need, but finish with a JSON object using exactly these fields:

- nearest_concepts: array of objects, each with name, what_it_covers, why_it_falls_short
- best_fit: object with name (string, or null if nothing genuinely fits), confidence (high | medium | low), reason
- coined_term: object with term, definition, necessary_conditions (array of strings), how_it_differs_from_best_fit — use nulls for the whole object if you've said an established term really does cover it
- boundary_cases: array of objects, each with scenario (one line), verdict (in | out | borderline), test_that_decides_it
- strawsonian_assessment: string, two or three sentences
- falsifiable_marker: string — something you could actually go and look for that would show this is a distinct phenomenon rather than me pattern-matching badly on ordinary resentment
