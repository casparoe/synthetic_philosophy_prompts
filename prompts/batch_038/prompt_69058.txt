I work in a lab that builds the same control policy twice over — once as a spiking network on a neuromorphic chip, and once as a purely mechanical linkage that drives the same valves and louvres in a greenhouse — so that we can compare their failure modes. At a group meeting last month I asked whether the mechanical device really has states that earn the labels we give the chip's states: the same soil-moisture integration state, the same irrigate-now state. A colleague cut me off with "it's the software, not the hardware." He has since used the line twice more. Once, to dismiss a postdoc's worry that the linkage supports none of the counterfactuals concerning inputs the greenhouse will never actually encounter — the mechanism has, so to speak, never been in a position to respond to most of the transitions the chip's policy specifies. Once, to wave away a visitor's remark that on any sufficiently permissive account of implementation one could find the same policy running in the masonry of the greenhouse wall.

I want to know whether the slogan is doing any work at all. Please set out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that "it's the software, not the hardware" could be standing for, splitting any candidate reading that turns out to be ambiguous between two things. For each, give a one-sentence statement of the thesis with its variables fixed — types or tokens; actual systems or metaphysically possible ones; a claim about what a computational or mental state is, or about what individuates it; a claim about the world or about our descriptions of it; a claim about a single system or about the relation between two systems — and then say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false, and what accepting it would commit one to. Where a reading is supposed to be supplied by the multiple-realizability argument against type-identity theories, say explicitly whether that argument delivers it, and at what strength.

Then, in a separate short section, say for each of the two dismissals above which readings would license it and which would not. My suspicion is that the reading strong enough to answer the visitor proves too much, while the reading weak enough to be safe does nothing at all to the postdoc's worry; I would like that shown rather than asserted, and corrected if it is wrong.

Finally, tell me which reading people who reach for the slogan most often have in mind, whether the intended reading is typically stated or merely assumed, and what makes the sentence feel conclusive even when the reading actually in play is not.

I am not looking for references, a bibliography, or an account of who said what — only the analysis.
