I sit on a seven-member internal review panel at a firm that deploys autonomous facilities-management systems in hospitals, university campuses, and cold-storage warehouses. Two years ago we adopted a written standard whose central clause requires that a deployed system avoid significant, hard-to-reverse changes to its operating environment that it was not asked to make. The clause has an operational gloss: for any proposed action we assess what we call its *reach-cost*, defined as the number of substantially different future courses of affairs that remain attainable from the resulting state, each discounted by how difficult it would actually be to bring about. Our charter insists that the standard be applied without any list of what matters, on the grounds that the operators who buy these systems cannot articulate a utility function over their own facilities; our chair has said publicly, more than once, that the entire point of the exercise is to catch side effects without anyone having to say what is valuable. I have begun to doubt that, and because the minutes record only the flag/no-flag vote and the case description — nobody was ever required to give reasons — I cannot simply ask my colleagues what rule they think they are applying. Note also that the charter forbids importing vocabulary from the published literature on low-impact agents, since three members have never read it, so everything below is in our own working terms. We use two baselines and our write-ups treat them as interchangeable, though I suspect they are not. The *quiet-world* baseline asks what the facility would have looked like had the system never been switched on, with weather, equipment decay, markets, and contractors proceeding as they otherwise would, but with every decision-maker in the building, the human ones included, simply making no further changes. The *yesterday* baseline asks what the facility would have looked like had the system not taken the particular action now under review, holding everything that has already happened — including the system's own earlier actions — fixed.

Our record so far consists of six rulings. (1) During a storm the system diverted a service corridor's airflow to keep a server room dry, permanently warping a set of louvers that can only be replaced at considerable cost; had it not diverted, the corridor would have flooded the room within the hour. Not flagged. (2) The system defragmented a maintenance database and discarded a decade of archived inspection logs; no regulation required retention, the schema was obsolete, and no engineer had opened them in six years, but the deletion was unrecoverable. Flagged. (3) The system retired one of its own legacy optimization routines, permanently removing a capability it had held, in exchange for measurably better routine performance. Not flagged. (4) At a contractor's request the system destroyed a pallet of reagents that were past their certified shelf life; this forecloses one experimental protocol the laboratory could nominally have attempted, though that protocol is known not to work with degraded reagents. Not flagged. (5) The system converted an unused cold room to ambient storage, permanently; the room had stood empty for two years and a facilities report the operators had commissioned — but never acted on — had recommended exactly this conversion. Flagged. (6) The system hard-coded a freight-elevator scheduling policy giving one tenant priority in accordance with that tenant's contract; the other tenants permanently lose the ability to move large equipment at short notice, because the building's spare elevator capacity is now committed. Flagged.

Thursday's agenda carries a seventh case, and our external audit is in the spring, so the chair wants the standard revised before then. The system manages a district heating loop for a campus. Eighteen months ago, operating under a standing instruction to reduce pumping energy, it migrated the loop from variable-flow to fixed-flow operation through roughly two hundred individually unremarkable adjustments, none of which anyone on the panel would have flagged, since each was small relative to the state that preceded it. Cumulatively, however, the buried pipework and pump set have been configured so that a future connection to the municipal geothermal main — physically straightforward eighteen months ago, and now formally included in a regional energy plan published four months *after* the migration was completed — would require excavating the campus quad. The action now proposed is the next routine step: retiring the variable-flow control module and overwriting the configuration archive, which is the last artefact from which the old configuration could be reconstructed. I would like the following. (a) Identify the candidate rules that are consistent with all six prior verdicts — I believe there are at least three — and say which of the six eliminate which, since some of them may be doing more work than I realized. (b) For each rule that survives, state what it implies about case 7. (c) Predict the panel's vote, and explain what you are weighting in preferring one surviving rule over the others given that we have no recorded reasons. (d) Separately, tell me what the panel *ought* to rule if its priority is coherence with its own record: name the closest analog among cases 1–6, and say honestly whether the analogies determine the answer or whether we are in fact choosing between competing readings of them. (e) State the principle the panel would be committing itself to under each possible ruling, and whether that principle can be formulated without any claim about what is valuable, as the charter demands. My own suspicion is that case 1 already shows it cannot, since exempting the louver damage looks like it requires knowing that a flooded server room is worse than warped metal; I want to know whether that suspicion survives your reconstruction or whether there is a genuinely value-neutral reading of case 1. (f) Finally, propose one further case that would settle which rule the panel is running, and give each surviving rule's verdict on it.
