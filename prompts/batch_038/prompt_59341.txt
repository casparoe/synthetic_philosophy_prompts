I help put together a printed growing almanac for our county — a volunteer thing, about two thousand copies, and once it goes to the printers in November we can't change a word of it for a year. Around forty recorders in different villages send in their notes each season and we turn them into the short rules that go in the book (sow this after that, don't prune until...). My problem is I have no idea when a run of seasons is actually enough to print a rule, and we've got a bad one going in this year: broad beans in the ground before Christmas for an earlier crop. It worked eleven years running on my own plot and has now failed twice in three winters. Half the committee want it struck out, half say a couple of bad patches proves nothing.

Can you write me a decision procedure we could run at a meeting without any statistics: the questions to ask in order about a candidate rule, what counts as a good enough answer to each, when a rule gets demoted to a "sometimes" note and when it gets cut entirely, and where the procedure has to stop and say this is a judgement call for humans. Someone once told me there's a whole philosophy problem about the same data fitting several different rules — I'm not asking for a lecture on that, but the procedure should cope with it.

Two things I especially need it to handle. First, our recorders don't use the same words: one village logs a seedling as "sulking" after transplanting, another calls the same thing "checking", and a third means something else again, so I need a test for whether a rule is even about the same thing from one end of the county to the other. Second, the baseline keeps moving — winters are milder then when the older rules were written — and I need some way of telling bad luck from a world that's changed, or an honest note that you can't.

Then run it on three cases: the broad beans rule above; a recorder's rule logged for only three seasons, all in one walled garden, which she's very confident about; and a rule about feeding tomatoes that everyone swears by but nobody can say what it actually predicts. Make sure at least one comes out with an answer the committee won't like, and finish with a short list of what the procedure can't do. British English please, and keep it usable — we meet in a village hall on a Tuesday and we're not going to read anything clever.
