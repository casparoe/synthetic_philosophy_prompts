Our faculty research committee runs a small internal scheme — twelve months, a bit of course relief, enough money for two workshops and one badly underpaid research assistant — and the deadline's in three weeks. I want you to draft the two-page proposal for an idea I've been sitting on since a reading group last term, when a physics grad student politely suggested that what I was doing was literary criticism of scientific texts. Here's the idea. There seems to be a recognizable class of scientific thought experiments that were deployed by their authors as reductios — ways of showing that a position, taken literally, lands you somewhere absurd — and that the subsequent community absorbed in the opposite direction, as the standard vivid illustration of, and often an argument for, the view they were meant to torpedoe. Schrödinger's cat is the flagship: it was constructed in 1935 in the middle of his exchange with Einstein about the EPR paper, precisely to press the point that taking superposition literally forces you into nonsense about a cat, and it is now the default teaching device for *introducing* superposition, with interpretations Schrödinger thought refuted by it claiming it as a badge of honor. EPR itself looks like a second case — built to show that quantum mechanics is incomplete, it ended up making entanglement look like a real physical resource. Maxwell's demon is a third, in a different way: built to press the point that the second law admits of exceptions, and finally cashed out in information-theoretic terms that left the second law looking more secure than before. And the shape isn't confined to physics. Foot's trolley cases were introduced to put pressure on the doctrine of double effect and became a self-sustaining research program that mostly ignores the doctrine; Hardin's commons parable, as I remember it, was an argument for coercion about breeding rights and is now cited almost exclusively as an argument for property rights.

Turn that into a proposal. I need the question stated precisely — and I suspect there are at least two questions tangled together here, something like "does inverted uptake show that a thought experiment fails to transmit its author's argument?" and "is the epistemic content of a thought experiment fixed at deployment or renegotiated in use?", and the proposal should pick one as the live question and say why the other is either derivative or a trap. Then say why the existing positions leave it open: the reconstruct-the-argument view, on which a thought experiment is just a compressed argument and so an inversion is simply a misreading (but then why do the misreadings run so systematically in one direction, and why do they so often produce new results rather than just noise?); the intuition view, on which we grasp something necessary by imagining the scenario (but then authorial intent should be beside the point and none of these cases should be interesting at all); and the mental-model or simulation view, which looks like it has the resources to handle this but has never been pushed on reception. Do this by describing the positions, not by name-checking — I'll add all the citations myself, so please don't invent any. Then the method, and here I want you to argue for the method rather than just announce it: my instinct is that the theory-first paper is the wrong move, and that the work is done by close reconstruction of four or five cases tracking how each experiment gets restated — in textbooks, in lecture notes, in the replies of the people being attacked by it — so that one can build a taxonomy of the shifts (inversion, overextension, "domestication" of a reductio into an illustration, "hostile uptake" where a critic's reading simply replaces the author's), and then ask what general claim about how thought experiments yield knowledge that taxonomy supports. Tell me which cases to use and why those, what happens month by month across the twelve months, where the two workshops fit, what the research assistant is actually doing, and what the deliverable is — an article? a set of criteria a scientist could apply? Also give me a real failure condition, not a fake one: I want to know under what circumstances I'd have to conclude at month eight that the project was a dud. Something like "if every case turns out to have a clean story on which the community simply reconstructed a different but valid argument, this collapses into a footnote about the history of science" would be the right shape; make it sharper than that.
