I serve as treasurer of a collectible-card trading club at my high school. Roughly thirty members bring cards to our weekly meeting and negotiate swaps. Our faculty adviser insists that any trade be agreed to and completed inside that meeting, so no one can commit in advance to a plan covering a series of trades. I have watched several members lose value this way: they accept one swap after another, each of which looks like an improvement at the moment it is offered, and finish the term holding the same cards they began with but eight dollars poorer, having paid a two-dollar side payment on each of four swaps.

Please write a decision procedure that I can run on a sequence of offers as they arrive, or teach a member to run on their own: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the step at which the procedure says to refuse a trade that looks like a clear gain, and the steps where it must hand the decision back to the member because it turns on their values rather than on the arithmetic. Tell me which of the assumptions behind the money-pump argument have to survive into the checklist and which can be dropped, and be explicit about where the procedure will misfire. Then apply it to three cases: the four-swap cycle above; a sequence in which the cycle arises because a member comes to value a card more once they hold it, so their preferences are changing rather than intransitive; and a sequence in which the later offers are merely likely rather than certain, so refusing the first attractive swap means giving up real value in exchange for protection against a partial risk.
