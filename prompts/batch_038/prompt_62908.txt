You are a philosopher of mind who also has a serious working command of contemporary reinforcement learning, and I would like you to treat this as a careful translation exercise rather than a summary. I am a research engineer on a team that builds model-based agents: learned dynamics models, planning in imagination, offline pretraining, tool use at inference time. On a long flight last month I finally read Dennett's *Kinds of Minds* (1996) at the urging of a colleague in my reading group, and I was struck by how close his fourfold taxonomy of creatures sits to distinctions I use every week, though I have no idea whether the closeness is real or just rhetorical. His tiers, as I understand them: Darwinian creatures, whose only reinforcement is the fitness built in by natural selection, which learn nothing during a lifetime and run on fixed reflexes; Skinnerian creatures, which can shape their own behaviour by trial and error within a lifetime but have no inner picture of the environment to consult; Popperian creatures, which carry an internal model and can try candidate actions off in simulation, so that — to borrow the thought from Popper that Dennett is invoking — the conjecture dies instead of the animal; and Gregorian creatures, which can make and import mind-enhancing tools that originate outside the skull, language first and foremost, and then writing, number, diagrams and other people's theories, tools that change what the head is able to do.

Here is the mapping I sketched in the margin, and here is where I already suspect it goes wrong. Darwinian: a fixed objective function and a policy frozen after the training run, with no updates at deployment — which feels like a hand-designed reward and a hand-coded controller. Skinnerian: model-free learning, values or advantages accumulated from transitions the agent actually experiences, with no model of the world anywhere in the loop. Popperian: model-based learning plus planning, a learned transition model used to roll out imagined futures so that bad actions are pruned before the body meets them. Gregorian: this is where I stall. Offline pretraining on a large corpus of human text looks Gregorian from the outside, but from my side it is a change of data distribution, not of architecture; whereas inference-time scaffolding — scratchpads, retrieval, calling a calculator or an interpreter, reading a manual that someone else wrote — looks much more like an external tool that is borrowed and restructured around, which is what Dennett seems to mean. Four further worries. First, Dennett insists the tiers are cumulative, each higher kind containing the lower ones as subsystems, but in engineering model-free and model-based are rival design choices, and an agent can be either, neither or both; does the cumulative ordering survive translation, or does it have to be bent? Second, the Darwinian tier's "reinforcement" is reproductive fitness, and rendering it as a scalar reward signal quietly imports the claim that natural selection optimises one quantity, which I am not sure is true and not sure Dennett would want to be committed to. Third, Popperian standing seems for him to involve inner states that can be *false* — representations with content that can misrepresent the world — while my dynamics model just has prediction error; is that a genuine residue or is misprediction all the falsity anyone ever needed? Fourth, Gregorian standing seems to require that the tool be cultural and externally transmitted, whereas my models are trained on the *products* of culture without using culture at runtime, which makes me wonder whether that tier is simply invisible to a training-time vocabulary and only becomes expressible in a deployment-time one.

What I want is the systematic version of this, not my margin notes. Take the debate Dennett's taxonomy is doing work in — the claim that mentality comes in architecturally distinct kinds rather than in a single continuum of more-or-less intelligence, and that the interesting differences are about what machinery a creature has for letting the world settle its options — and restate it in my vocabulary: environments, policies, value functions, reward specification, learned versus given dynamics, planning versus cached action values, offline versus online data, generalisation and distribution shift, inference-time compute and external tools. Go tier by tier, and for each say explicitly what maps cleanly, what you had to stretch or fudge to make fit, and what has no counterpart at all. Then give me the residue report as the main deliverable: for each item that failed to translate, judge whether the failure is a poverty of my vocabulary (some concept the machine-learning framing genuinely lacks, which I should go looking for) or a sign that the philosophical distinction was less substantive than it appeared (a rebranding of a distinction I already had, dressed up with the names of famous dead theorists), and say what would settle the question. Please do not smuggle my framework's assumptions back in and present them as translations — if you find that a tier only works by assuming that fitness is a reward function, or that a learned model has representational content, flag the assumption rather than relying on it. Finish by telling me how many tiers there would be if the taxonomy were rebuilt from scratch using only distinctions that matter to how agents are trained and deployed, where you would put the cuts, and whether anything in Dennett's picture — comprehension without a comprehender, competence without understanding, the difference between taking the design stance and taking the intentional stance — would survive that rebuild at all. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming the candidate correspondences before committing to any of them; I do not need references, page numbers or a reading list, only the argument.
