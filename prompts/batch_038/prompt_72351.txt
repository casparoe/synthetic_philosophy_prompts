ok so I'm in a nationalism seminar this term and we just spent three weeks on the invented-tradition readings (Hobsbawm) and then Anderson's bit about how the newspaper, the calendar, and the school primer are what actually make a country feel like a thing. My prof kept making this remark that I can't get out of my head: that after a few generations the paperwork proving a custom was invented ends up being the best surviving evidence that the custom is old. Which is hilarious if you picture it as an actual meeting.

For our student history society's end-of-term zine I'm supposed to have a joke piece and I'm genuinely bad at writing them. Can you do this:

The minutes of a single meeting of a government "Committee for the Restoration of National Customs" in a made-up small country, sometime in the late 1970s. They've been handed a deadline — a national day is coming up in eleven months and there is nothing to put on it — and the entire meeting is them assembling a custom that will look like it goes back to before the railway arrived. About 700-800 words, formatted like real minutes: numbered agenda items, attendees listed with their titles and departments, an apologetic line at the bottom about a member who asked for his dissent to be recorded, and a short appendix of budget lines.

The humour has to come from the bureaucratic genre doing its normal job, not from the author nudging the reader. Deadpan throughout, nobody in the room thinks anything strange is happening. Specific things I want in there, none of them spelled out as a point:

- an argument about print runs — how many primers and newspaper supplements it takes before something stops being a proposal and starts being a custom — plus someone worried that the districts in the second printing will feel they celebrated it wrong
- an agenda item where the committee has to settle on the year the custom began, and they argue about which year is most defensible, where "defensible" transparently means whichever year will be easiest to find supporting material for later
- a seperate subcommittee reporting that a folklorist has turned up actual evidence the custom never existed, and this being handled entirely as a scheduling difficulty and a line item, never as a finding
- the moment somebody notices that in forty years the only document about this custom still in an archive will be these very minutes, and the resulting change in how carefully everyone words things
- one sincerely felt paragraph where a member explains why any of this matters, everyone agrees, and the chair moves to the next item

Target the practice and the style of reasoning, not any real country, nation, or group. Please keep the setting fictional with invented place names and invented names for the custom itself — I don't want it reading as a jab at somewhere that exists, and I definitely don't want to have to fact-check whether some real festival was really cooked up in 1974.

Also: please never use the phrases "invented tradition," "imagined community," "socially constructed," or "authenticity" anywhere in it. The committee should be doing all of that without having the vocabulary for it. That's kind of the whole joke, and if they say it out loud it dies.
