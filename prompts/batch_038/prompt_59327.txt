I am revising a seminar paper on the Newton–Leibniz priority dispute, and my last draft meeting ended with my supervisor cutting off a long argument about whether it matters who produced the calculus first by observing that, after all, both men were only standing on the shoulders of giants — the evident implication being that the priority question is idle, since the result was going to be reached anyway and whatever credit is available belongs to the whole community that made it reachable. I am confident the remark is being made to do considerably more work than it can bear, but I cannot yet say precisely what work that is.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims this proverb might be making? I want the readings separated by the job each one would actually do, running from the near-tautological to the genuinely radical, rather than a single favored interpretation explained at length. Please in particular keep apart the following axes, since I suspect they are being slid between: whether the claim concerns the epistemic dependence of a result on prior work; whether it concerns the counterfactual inevitability of the result (would the calculus have arrived, and arrived in recognizably the same form, without either of these two men?); whether it concerns the number of people positioned to reach it; whether it concerns how credit or priority ought to be distributed once it has been reached; and whether it concerns the discoverer's own stature — that is, whether the metaphor concedes that the person doing the seeing is small. On that last point: the sense may well change between the version everyone repeats and the older medieval formulation, in which it is dwarfs perched on the giants' shoulders who see further, and who see further not by being tall but by having been lifted. I have a hunch the modern wording quietly promotes the person on top from passenger to peer, and that this matters for what the line can be used to assert. Tell me if that hunch is right.

For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false; what would have to be true for it to hold; and — this is what I most need — whether it would support the conclusion my supervisor drew, namely that the priority dispute is idle and that credit should be treated as belonging to a community rather than to individuals. If no single reading gets him there, or if only some conjunction of them does, say so plainly and locate the point at which the equivocation has to occur. A related question: I believe the formulation now in general circulation is usually traced to a letter Newton wrote to Hooke in 1676, which is mildly awkward given my subject. I do not want the paper to turn on that, but if the fact that the line entered common usage as a piece of correspondence etiquette rather than as a theory of discovery constrains what it can be taken to claim, I would like that spelled out.

I am not after a survey of the relevant literature or a list of places to look things up; I want the argumentative structure itself. Number the readings, and let each entry carry a few sentences of substance rather than a paragraph of throat-clearing.
