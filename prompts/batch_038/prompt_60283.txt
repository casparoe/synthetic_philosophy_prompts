I am the convenor of a two-day workshop at a research institute, and the opening remarks have fallen to me. I would like them written. Length and register are specified at the end; the substance comes first, because these remarks cannot afford to get it wrong.

**The workshop's subject** is Gödel's rotating universe of 1949 and what it does or does not establish about time. The material the remarks must handle:

- Gödel found an exact solution to Einstein's field equations describing a universe filled with pressureless dust in uniform rotation, balanced by a negative cosmological constant. The rotation has no centre and no axis: every point of that universe is turning, and nothing is being turned around.
- Through every point of the solution there passes a closed timelike curve. An observer can set out along an entirely permissible path and arrive back before departure.
- Gödel worked out what such a voyage would cost in speed and in matter converted to energy and judged it absurd. His argument therefore never rested on anyone actually reaching the past. It rested on the fact that the same equations governing our universe admit a world with no global time ordering, no objective lapse of time, no way of slicing reality into successive presents — and he inferred from the admissibility of such a world that objective becoming is not a feature of physical reality anywhere, our world included.
- Einstein's reply in the same seventieth-birthday volume pressed the other end of the argument: whether a universe of that kind is physically realizable at all.
- Nothing in the sky has since shown any sign of large-scale rotation. Our universe is not Gödel's, so the argument must run on possibility rather than on fact — and whether such a possibility carries any weight at all is the first thing the two days will fight about.

**The room** holds about ninety: some twenty-five mathematical relativists, some twenty-five philosophers of time, roughly twenty graduate students split between the two, and about twenty members of the public — donors and a group of advanced secondary-school students — because the workshop carries a public-engagement grant.

**The difficulty** is two individuals in the first three rows. One has argued in print that extracting metaphysics from exotic solutions is a category mistake: models of the field equations are not candidate worlds, and what matters is which conditions — on energy, on boundaries, on initial data — our universe actually satisfies. The other has spent three decades arguing that relativity dispenses with objective becoming even in perfectly well-behaved, non-rotating models, and that Gödel's conclusion is stronger than Gödel's own route to it. They have never met. Neither will name the other in these remarks; both are introduced later. My own published sympathies lie with the first, which makes neutrality harder rather than easier: the remarks must be ones I can deliver without embarrassment and that the second would not read as a trap laid in advance.

**Requirements.**

1. Real neutrality, not evasive neutrality. Both positions must be stated fairly, in the same vocabulary, at the same level of respect, under a single framing that leaves both disputes live. Note that the two do not merely disagree about the answer; they disagree about what kind of question the workshop is asking, and the framing must be one under which both readings are legitimate.
2. The remarks must keep two things apart that the non-specialists will otherwise fuse: the absence of any objective global present, and the existence of paths that loop back into the past. The second is what makes headlines; the first is what Gödel's inference actually turns on; and the first is arguably already raised by entirely unexotic relativistic models in which the second is wholly absent. Getting this distinction across, without a formula in the room, is the main intellectual job of the speech.
3. Explain closed timelike curves and the failure of a global time ordering in plain language that no relativist would find condescending, and define nothing by analogy to a film.
4. Do not paper over the observational point. Say plainly that the universe shows no rotation, and be honest about what that does and does not do to the argument — because if the remarks skate past it, one of the two will spend the first session correcting me and the other will spend it applauding.
5. End on a single question for the two days, phrased so that answering it would count as progress for both camps and so that neither can answer it simply by restating its position. It must not be a both-sides platitude, and it must not be the question of whether time travel is possible; the workshop is not about that, and the remarks should say so.
6. Include one short light moment early. It must be funny to a relativist and a metaphysician for the same reason, and it must not mock either.
7. Include one or two sentences that survive being lifted out of context for the funder's press release without turning into science fiction.
8. No verbatim quotation from Gödel or Einstein, no citations, no reading lists, no naming of living philosophers or their books. Do not use the phrase about time being an illusion, and do not use the standard two-word nickname for the eternalist picture of time; the workshop exists precisely to avoid both slogans.

**Form:** 850 to 950 words, roughly six minutes spoken. Formal institutional register — sentences short enough to say aloud, no hedging throat-clearing, no rhetorical questions stacked in threes, and no attempt at uplift in the final line beyond the question in requirement 5.
