I run a ninety-minute evening session for a community philosophy group — about eighteen adults, two nurses, a retired schoolteacher, a couple of programmers, no formal training but happy to argue. The topic is how thought experiments persuade, and how they mislead. I would rather not build the session on a case the group has already met, so I have written one:

A phone app identifies bird species from photographs and is certified accurate 99.9 percent of the time. A novice on her first outing photographs a bird at a nature reserve, and the app reports a species never before recorded in the county. A warden who has watched that reserve for thirty years, and whose identifications are right roughly nine times in ten, looks at the same bird through binoculars and says it is a common warbler. Who do you believe?

Most of the group will say the app, and I intend to let them, then turn the single dial — a clause I buried in passing — and let the verdict flip in front of them.

Give me the teaching plan, not the explanation. What has to be in place before "a thought experiment can be leaky rather than merely disagreeable" means anything to these people, and in what order, with the dependencies made explicit? Can my case carry the full ninety minutes, or what does it lack — and should it be run once or twice? Which misconceptions should I expect, and at exactly which point in the sequence do I head each off: I predict "the author cheated by withholding information," "this is just base rates, so it is statistics and not philosophy," and "intuitions are personal, so nothing has been shown either way." How long should I let them be confidently wrong? One closing question that would tell me they have acquired a transferable habit rather than the answer about the bird. And what I should deliberately omit at this level, and announce that I am omitting.
