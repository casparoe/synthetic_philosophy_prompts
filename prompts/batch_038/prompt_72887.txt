I serve on the board of a small private foundation — roughly $40 million in assets, perpetual in duration, no debt, tax-exempt, and a spending policy of 4.5 percent of a trailing three-year average plus a cost-of-living adjustment. One of my fellow trustees, who ran a systematic equity strategy for two decades before retiring, has circulated a proposal to rewrite our investment policy statement around what he calls geometric rationality. Two board members are enthusiastic, one is hostile, and I have been asked to bring a written opinion to next month's meeting, so I would like a memo I can circulate and actually defend.

The proposal runs roughly as follows. Expected-value maximization is the wrong decision rule for anyone who makes many decisions in sequence, because it averages over outcomes the decision-maker will never live through: maximizing the expected value of terminal wealth can license bets that all but guarantee ruin. The right objective is instead the long-run compounded growth rate of the fund, which, under conditions he describes as well understood, is maximized by a Kelly-style growth-optimal allocation of capital. He adds that mean-variance optimization treats volatility as if it were risk, when for a fund like ours the only thing that genuinely matters is falling permanently behind the compounding path of the growth-optimal portfolio; that a perpetual foundation is precisely the case where the long-run argument bites, since we never have to cash out; and that the usual hand-wringing about drawdowns rests on confusing an average across a hypothetical crowd of simultaneous investors with an average across time for the one investor who has to endure the actual sequence. On this basis he wants us to move from our current 60/40 policy allocation to a growth-optimal sleeve of leveraged broad equity, sized by a formula he has supplied, with a leverage cap of 1.5x and no rebalancing more frequent than annual.

Please draft the memo, and have it take a clear position — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific amendments — in a form the board could vote on. If you recommend conditions, make them concrete rather than gestural: numbers, caps, sequencing, and a review horizon. It should engage the proposal at its strongest rather than dismiss it, since I do find the ensemble-versus-time-average point genuinely persuasive and I want to know whether it licenses what he wants to do with it; and it should squarely address the objection my co-trustee who teaches statistics keeps repeating, that this is all just logarithmic utility wearing a disguise and that the argument is doing no real work. Be explicit about what the growth-optimal result requires: what must be known rather than estimated, what the horizon has to look like, what happens to the argument once expected returns have to be forecast, and what our mandatory annual spending does to a sizing rule derived for a fund that never withdraws. Where the proposal's defenders would themselves concede a point, say so. Finally, I would like the memo to distinguish clearly between what is settled among people who work on these questions, what is merely a defensible modeling convention, and what is live controversy — I do not want to circulate a document that states a contested normative claim as though it were the professional consensus, and I would also like to be told plainly if my own enthusiasm for the time-average point is giving it more weight than it can bear.
