I am the credit officer and, in practice, the only underwriting decision-maker at a small specialty finance fund that lends against used equipment to owner-operators in three trades. We have roughly $22 million of net worth, a warehouse line we treat as untouchable, and no ability to raise new capital on a timescale shorter than about two years. We close twenty-five to forty loans a month, ticket sizes between $30,000 and $500,000, terms of twelve to forty-eight months, and our two passive limited partners co-sign anything above $150,000. They are intelligent people who think entirely in expected value: if a deal's arithmetic mean across outcomes is positive, they want to know why we are not doing more of it. My analysts are numerate but have no training in decision theory, I have about twenty minutes per deal, and right now sizing is a matter of feel plus a hard-wired cap nobody can articulate the derivation of. I want to replace that with something defensible.

What I would like is a decision procedure my analysts can actually run on a deal, grounded in the growth-rate tradition of bet sizing — Kelly's 1956 framing and what has been made of it since, including the volume MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba assembled — rather than in expected-value maximization. It should be explicit about the conceptual load-bearing points, and about which of them survive contact with a real credit book: that maximizing expected logarithmic growth is the same thing as maximizing the expected utility of a logarithmic investor, so that the criterion is doing something different from maximizing the arithmetic mean across outcomes; that the objection from ergodicity — that what averages out across many parallel bettors need not average out along the single path one bettor actually lives — is precisely why a bet with a favorable arithmetic mean can still be the wrong size or the wrong bet for us; that the standard discounting of the full growth-optimal stake is really a hedge against error in the estimated probabilities, and that the discount should scale with how thin the evidence for those probabilities is rather than being a fixed fraction; that a book of correlated exposures is not a sequence of independent bets and must be sized jointly; and that the whole apparatus presumes the bet is repeatable, that the stake can be adjusted, and that the probabilities are known rather than merely conjectured. I want the procedure as an ordered set of questions with a stated adequacy condition for each answer, an explicit rule for what to do when an analyst can supply only an interval and refuses to commit to a point estimate, a floor below which we decline rather than fund small, the points at which it terminates in a refusal, and the points at which it stops and hands the decision to me because the remaining question is one of values or of tolerance rather than of fact. Be frank about which steps are judgment calls wearing the clothes of arithmetic; a procedure that hides them is worse to me than one that flags them.

Then run it on three live situations. First, our core book: thirty-six months of internal performance on roughly 210 loans of one type, an observed default rate near 4 percent, recovery of about sixty cents on the dollar in default, priced at nineteen percent — I want the size the procedure yields, and whether we are currently over- or under-committed, keeping in mind that every observation in the sample comes from a period of unusually strong freight rates. Second, a genuinely new class: the same equipment offered to a trade we have never lent to, in a jurisdiction where our recovery experience is untested, and my analyst will give me a default rate of somewhere between 2 and 15 percent but will not narrow it. Third, a one-off distressed portfolio purchase at around $4 million against what the seller's own servicing data suggests is $9 to $11 million of recoverable value, which my LPs find irresistible on the arithmetic mean — but which we would enter once, cannot re-enter, and which would impair our capital base for years if it goes badly. On this third case I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not like, and I want to be told plainly if the reason is that the criterion itself stops applying once a bet is non-repeatable, rather than merely shrinking the stake.

Present the three applications in a single table with exactly these columns: case; evidence available and its limits; the question in the procedure that this case cannot answer; how the input was represented (point estimate, interval, or distribution); commitment the procedure yields, as both a percentage of net worth and a dollar figure; the binding constraint that determined that figure (growth-optimal stake, estimation-error discount, correlation cap, decline floor, sign-off threshold, or criterion inapplicable); verdict; the single input to which the verdict is most sensitive; what the procedure refers to me as judgment; and what it cannot decide at all. After the table, add a short assumption register, one row per assumption the procedure depends on, with columns: assumption; how our book violates it or comes close; what the procedure instructs us to do under that violation; and whether the correction is quantitative or a matter of judgment.
