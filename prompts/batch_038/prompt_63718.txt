I've been sitting in on a colleague's seminar for two terms now — she works on public reason, and she's remarkably consistent about when religious arguments are allowed into politics, but she has never once written the criterion down and I want to pin it before I cite her in something. Here's her track record as I've got it in my notes:

1. Senator arguing against an authorization for military force: invokes Catholic just-war teaching on the floor, plus the intelligence agencies' civilian-casualty estimates, which she says are what actually settle it for her. Fine, my colleague says.
2. Governor signing a near-total abortion ban: signing statement is scripture and nothing else, and she tells reporters she has no non-religious argument. Not fine.
3. Rank-and-file voter, local ballot measure raising the sales tax to fund pre-K: votes yes purely because her congregation teaches that caring for poor children is obligatory, never tells anyone why. Fine.
4. Judge's opinion invoking a natural-law account of human dignity — descended from Aquinas but statable without any appeal to revelation, and plenty of secular jurists have said versions of it. Fine.
5. Candidate at a church breakfast grounding her anti-death-penalty stance in her reading of the Gospels; asked by a reporter what she'd say on the Senate floor, she gives a fallibility-and-deterrence argument she says she genuinely finds convincing. Fine.
6. Backbencher supporting an utterly ordinary pesticide restriction: floor speech leads with his faith, then, pressed by a colleague, he adds a cost-benefit rationale he admits he doesn't buy and says he wouldn't vote for the bill on that basis alone. Not fine.
7. School board adopting a mandatory health curriculum defended entirely in public-health terms; a religious parents' association says that from inside their own commitments they have no reason whatsoever to accept it. Legitimate anyway, she says.
8. Majority-faith legislator's ban on a face covering worn for religious reasons, backed by public-safety reasons he sincerely holds and says he'd vote for on their own. Fine — but when someone in the room pointed out that the women who wear it have no reason of their own to accept the ban, she said that changes the case, and then wouldn't say how.

Her floor speech gives only her tradition's teaching about what may not be done to a person; she says openly that she went looking for a secular argument she could accept and didn't find one. But she does read into the record a staff-drafted human-rights-and-prison-management rationale, saying she doesn't find it persuasive, though she doesn't think it's false either. And she spends a long stretch arguing that the guards' and wardens' own unions have strong reasons drawn from their own stated commitments to want the cap, and that the people it binds are a state agency, not her community.
