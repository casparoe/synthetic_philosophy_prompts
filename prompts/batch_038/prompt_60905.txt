The exam question, as it appeared on the paper, was this:

> Explain how Kripke's account of rigid designation is supposed to generate truths that are necessary but knowable only a posteriori, using "Hesperus is Phosphorus" as your worked example. Your answer should (a) state what it is for an expression to be a rigid designator, and give one example of a non-rigid designator; (b) explain why, on Kripke's view, whether a true identity statement is necessary depends on the rigidity of the expressions flanking the identity sign — in particular, say what goes wrong if one tries to get the necessity of such identities out of Leibniz's Law alone, without the rigidity premise; (c) explain what, on Kripke's account, accounts for the fact that astronomical observation was nonetheless needed to establish the identity; and (d) say whether Kripke's framework also has room for contingent a priori truths, and briefly justify your answer.

Here is the student's script, transcribed:

> A rigid designator is an expression that picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, and picks out nothing in worlds where it does not. Kripke's claim is that proper names are rigid, whereas the definite descriptions that the descriptivist treats names as abbreviating are typically non-rigid: "the teacher of Alexander" designates Aristotle in the actual world, but in other worlds it designates somebody else, or nothing at all.
>
> Now apply this to Hesperus and Phosphorus. Ancient astronomers used "Hesperus" for the bright body appearing in the evening sky and "Phosphorus" for the bright body appearing in the morning sky. Both of these names are rigid designators, and both designate the planet Venus. Identity is a necessary relation, so any true identity between rigid designators is necessarily true. The argument goes through Leibniz's Law: everything is necessarily self-identical, and if a = b then whatever is true of a is true of b, so the necessary self-identity of a carries over to b. Hence "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessarily true, if true at all.
>
> But it was not knowable a priori. The astronomers had to observe the sky and correlate their records before they could establish that the evening body and the morning body were one and the same object. So this is a genuine necessary a posteriori truth, and it shows that the necessary/contingent distinction, which is metaphysical, comes apart from the a priori/a posteriori distinction, which is epistemic. The empirical work was required because of how the reference of the two names was originally fixed: they were introduced by way of two distinct observable appearances, and although those appearances are in fact produced by a single object, nothing in the way the names are used guarantees that this is so. That is why the identity is informative despite being necessary — it tells us something we did not already know about how the two appearances are related.
>
> The same point disposes of the descriptivist's last resort. Suppose the descriptivist tries to save the theory by rigidifying, and substitutes "the actual brightest evening star" for "the brightest evening star". But "actual" is an indexical, and indexicals shift their reference from context of utterance to context of utterance, so "the actual brightest evening star" is not genuinely rigid either: it designates Venus relative to our context and would designate something else relative to another. The rigidity here is only apparent, the escape route fails, and Kripke's argument against descriptivism stands.
>
> As for why the identity is a posteriori rather than a priori, the deeper reason is that there are ways things might have been in which the evening star and the morning star are two different planets; had the observational record come out differently, the astronomers would have found two bodies where they in fact found one. Since "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" have different senses over a common reference, only investigation can tell us whether the senses coincide.
>
> Finally, Kripke thinks the opposite combination is available too. His example is the standard meter in Paris: whoever stipulated that this stick is one meter long knew a priori that it is one meter long, and yet it is contingent that the stick is one meter long, since under different conditions of temperature and pressure it would have been slightly longer or shorter. So the modal distinction and the epistemic distinction cross-cut one another, and three of the four combinations are populated.
