You are a scholar of ancient economic thought who has written on Aristotle's treatment of exchange, acquisition, and coinage, and who is equally at home with the modern monetary theories that get read back onto him. I would like you to think carefully and treat what follows as conceptual engineering rather than exegesis for its own sake: begin by brainstorming, for a few lines, what argumentative work the analogy I describe is actually being made to do, and only then proceed to the audit.

The context is this. I am auditing a graduate seminar in the history of economic thought whose lecturer has a recurring gloss on Aristotle: money, for him, is a measuring instrument — a ruler, a balance, a calibrated standard — that renders otherwise incommensurable goods comparable. The seminar keeps returning to that image, and the lecturer uses it to license three conclusions. First, that money is on Aristotle's account epistemically and economically inert: it records a magnitude that exchange relations already possess, so nothing in the analysis changes if the coin is replaced by any other faithfully calibrated standard. Second, that the stronger claim sometimes made about him — that money does not merely report commensurability but constitutes it — is an anachronistic importation of credit and state theories of money, one that the measuring picture shows to be unnecessary. Third, that when Aristotle says money exists by convention or law rather than by nature, he means only that the unit is conventional, the way inches are conventional while length is not, and emphatically not that money's existence is itself a matter of collective agreement. A friend of mine with an economics background insists that the ruler image is simply what a neutral-veil picture of money looks like, and that Aristotle's own argument undercuts it; the lecturer says she is reading nineteenth-century debates into a fourth-century text.

Here are the pieces of Aristotle I have in mind, and please correct me wherever my recollection is faulty, since part of what I want to know is how much of the analogy rests on my paraphrase. In the Ethics he argues that everything exchanged must be somehow comparable, that money arose for this purpose and becomes a kind of intermediate that measures all things, that the one thing by which everything must really be measured is need, which holds the community together, and that money has come to stand in for need by agreement — with the etymological point that its name derives from law or custom because it is not natural, together with the remark that it lies in our power to change it and render it useless. There is also the proportional schema with the builder, the shoemaker, the house and the shoe, in which the pairing that holds the city together is the diagonal one rather than the parallel ones; the Politics' distinction between using a shoe by wearing it and using it in exchange; the condemnation of the mode of acquisition that makes currency breed from itself as the most unnatural of all; and a chapter I only half remember in which coinage comes about when people settle on a manageable material such as iron or silver, initially weighing it out and later stamping it so that the mark spares them the trouble of measuring.

With that in hand, I would like the following, in this order and without padding. (a) An explicit correspondence table: what in the measuring-instrument source plays the role of the coin, of the measured magnitude, of the unit, of the measurer, and of the act of measurement — and, just as importantly, where the source has a feature with no counterpart in the target, or vice versa. (b) A list of disanalogies, sorted into those that bear on the lecturer's three inferences and those that are harmless ornament. Please attend in particular to whether a ruler measures along a dimension its objects already share while houses and shoes share none; to the passivity and externality of a measuring device relative to what it measures; to the fixity of a unit versus Aristotle's own concession that money can be changed and made useless, and that its worth wobbles; and to the question of what, if anything, money could be measuring given that need is said to be the real measure and need is not obviously a magnitude at all. (c) An assessment of whether Aristotle is himself committed to the measuring image, and if so whether that commitment is load-bearing for him or a leftover from ordinary Greek idiom about weighing and counting — and whether the measuring language can be reconciled with the claim that money is only a stand-in for something non-quantitative. (d) A verdict on the three conclusions: which survive the disanalogies, which were being carried entirely by the analogy, and what repair is available to a defender of the lecturer's reading, for instance by restricting the analogy's scope. (e) A better analogy, if there is one: consider candidates such as a shared language, a public standard of the kind a city fixes for measures and calendars, a standing promise or IOU, the score kept in a game, and a common denominator, then recommend one and say honestly what your replacement itself smuggles in. Flag every point where my summary is wrong, where the argument turns on a contested translation, or where your recall of the text is shaky enough that the reasoning should be treated as conditional.
