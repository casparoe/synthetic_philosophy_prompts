ok so I'm on my school's debate team and we got a round about whether it's legitimate for politicians to argue from their religion — like voting against something just because their church teaches it's wrong. I built this argument to win the round, and then my coach read it and said only "it's self-refuting" and wouldn't explain, which is infuriating.

Background for the case I used: my town had a fight last year about a temple that broadcast its early-morning call to prayer over outdoor speakers. Neighbors wanted an ordinance to stop it. Some argued from noise and lost sleep, some from "if we allow them we have to allow everyone," and the temple argued it has a religous duty to call the faithful and that the ordinance was really aimed at them specifically. Here's what I made out of it:

(1) Laws are backed by force, and forcing people is only legitimate if you can justify it to them with reasons they could accept. Call that the shared-reasons test.

(2) My own addition, the parity rule: a reason fails the test because of some feature it has, not because of where it came from. So if a religious reason fails for having feature F, every secular reason with F fails too.

(3) Reasonable people disagree about basically every substantive moral claim. This isn't a theory I looked up, it's just what my town looked like — both sides of the temple fight were full of reasonable people who'd actually thought about it.

(4) If reasonable people disagree about a claim, then not every reasonable person could accept it as a reason. If they could, they'd already agree.

(5) So by (2) and (4), nothing substantive passes the test, and religious reasons aren't being singled out — they fail for exactly the reason everything else fails.

(6) What's left over is too thin to decide anything, like "treat people equally" or "don't harm people," and every real dispute including the temple one is a dispute about what those even mean.

(7) So the shared-reasons test can't be met. Either it outlaws almost every law, including the ones that protect religious liberty, or it's satisfied only by slogans that settle nothing.

(8) And a rule that rules out every reason rules out no reason. So the temple gets to argue from its duty to call the faithful, the neighbors get to argue from how much they value a quiet morning, and the test was never a constraint on anybody.

I can't accept (7) and (8). I really want to keep (1) and (2) — (2) especially, because a test that only ever bites on religious people and never on secular people whose reasons have the same shape seems like the exact unfairness I was trying to expose. So if (1) through (4) really force me into (7) and (8), something in (1)–(4) has to go, and I don't want it to be (2).

Separate question: how is my coach's criticism even different from my own conclusion? My argument says the test refutes itself. He says my argument is self-refuting. Is he just confusing the arrow with the target, or is there a real problem with my argument that I'm not seeing?

So: pick the step you'd reject and commit to it. Tell me why it looked obviously true to me, what I lose by dropping it, and whether (1) and (2) can survive without it. And tell me what somebody who genuinely believes in the shared-reasons test would say about (3), because I have a feeling that's where they'd attack. Please don't lay out all the possible moves and leave me to choose — I want your diagnosis, with reasons.
