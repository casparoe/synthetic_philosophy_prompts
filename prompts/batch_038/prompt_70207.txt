I serve on the board of a private foundation with a forty-million-dollar endowment that makes perhaps fifteen grants a year, several of them large, illiquid, and effectively irreversible: endowing a program in perpetuity, underwriting a multi-year trial, committing to a building. The board is split in a way that no amount of additional data has resolved. One member wants us to concentrate resources on the option with the highest expected number of lives improved; another holds that a small chance of squandering the endowment outright is worse than that arithmetic suggests, and that the arithmetic is precisely what fails to capture it; a third is by temperament a gambler who thinks we should be taking the long shot and that the cautious member is not being rational so much as timid. We are about to write a decision rule into our governance charter, and I would rather know what I should be demanding of a rule before I start comparing them.

So: produce the desiderata — the specification an adequate theory of rational choice under risk must satisfy — where the purpose is fixed as follows. The theory must yield a rule that a deliberating institution can apply in advance, to one decision at a time, on the information such an institution realistically has; and it must have room for genuine disagreement about attitude to risk that is not really disagreement about probabilities or about how much anyone values the outcomes. Everything below should be read as relative to that purpose.

For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it — a concrete decision problem, a pattern of intuitions about choices, a structural worry about the formalism — say what sort of theory the requirement excludes, and label it as non-negotiable, as negotiable but expensive, or as the taste of a particular camp rather than a genuine constraint. I would like the list to include at least one requirement addressing each of the following, though you should add any you think I have missed and cut any you think are bogus:

- how the rule treats a single gamble in isolation versus as one element of the institution's whole portfolio of risks, and whether the rule can be applied at all when the larger portfolio is unknown or only vaguely known;
- how the rule behaves when the same agent faces a sequence of decisions over time rather than one decision, including whether a rule that is admissible for a single choice can be exploited across a series of choices the agent knows is coming;
- what the rule says about options that are at least as good as another in every possible state of the world and strictly better in some;
- whether the rule is genuinely distinct from ordinary expected utility theory with a non-linear utility function, or merely redescribes the same preferences with an extra parameter — and what would settle that question;
- what the rule owes to situations in which the probabilities are themselves contested, imprecise, or not well defined at all, and whether handling those is part of the same job or a different one;
- what the rule must preserve of the apparent successes of expected-utility reasoning in domains where it is well confirmed, such as pricing insurance and constructing diversified portfolios.

Then the part I care about most. Identify any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. My suspicion — test it rather than indulge it — is that a theory which treats attitude to risk as a third parameter alongside belief and desire, and which therefore must evaluate a gamble as a whole rather than branch by branch, cannot simultaneously be applied to a decision in isolation, remain immune to a money pump across a foreseen sequence of choices, and honor state-by-state dominance. If some such set is indeed unsatisfiable, say which member is cheapest to surrender, what the surrender costs, and whether the resulting theory is still recognizable as the one we wanted. If my suspicion is confused, explain the confusion; that would be just as useful. Finally, tell me which single requirement eliminates the largest number of candidate rules, and whether that should make me distrust the requirement rather than the candidates.

Please organize the response under the following headings, in this order, at roughly the lengths given:

1. **Purpose and Scope** — about 150 words: restate what the rule is for, what counts as a candidate, and what you are deliberately setting aside.
2. **The Requirements** — about 1,200 words, and the center of gravity of the answer: numbered, each with its motivating case, its exclusions, and its label.
3. **Negotiable, Non-Negotiable, and Camp Taste** — about 250 words: consolidate the labels and defend the two or three you think are most often misfiled.
4. **The Unsatisfiable Subset** — about 500 words.
5. **The Most Exclusionary Requirement** — about 200 words.
6. **The Contenders Against the List** — about 400 words: briefly, how expected utility with a concave utility function, a risk-weighted expected utility theory of the kind I described, and a maximin or other ambiguity-sensitive rule fare against the list. Keep this short; the requirements matter more than the scorecard.

Two further constraints. No bibliography or reference list, and no citations: where you invoke a theorem or a named result, say in plain terms what it establishes rather than relying on my recognizing it, and define any technical term at first use. Write the whole response in American English.
