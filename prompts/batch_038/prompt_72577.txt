I am an American who moved to Aarhus eleven months ago, and last week I was asked to sit on the recognition committee of the small independent school where my stepdaughter is enrolled — partly, I suspect, because the newsletter is bilingual and they were short a native English speaker. My Danish husband's reading of the committee is that its practice is simply Janteloven in a school uniform; mine is that it is something else; neither of us can say what. The committee meets Thursday on a case we cannot settle at home, and I would rather argue from its own record than from either of our folk theories.

The precedents, all from the last six years of minutes, which the outgoing chair handed me:

1. The school's robotics team placed second nationally. All nine students went on the front page and were given the assembly slot.
2. Alumni asked the school to reinstate its old tradition of naming a single top graduate each spring. The committee refused, with the recorded reason that it made an example of one child for the benefit of the others.
3. A student who had arrived in Denmark two years earlier speaking no Danish was admitted to a selective upper-secondary programme. Full feature, photograph, interview.
4. A student won a national young soloists prize on violin. She received one line in a list of news items; the minutes note that she had been taught since the age of five by her father, a professional musician.
5. The annual citizenship prize — an individual prize, named and announced from the stage — went to a student who organized a petition that got the municipality to reroute a bus line.
6. A family offered a donation large enough to name the new science wing and asked that the gift be announced alongside the naming. The committee took the money and declined the announcement, on the ground that it would put other families in their place.
7. The school publishes its aggregate results in the national tests but not the per-class breakdown, and has never named a student in connection with a score.

Thursday's case. A fifteen-year-old has won a national individual mathematics competition — rank-order, announced with names. She is the daughter of two university academics, has had private tutoring since she was nine, and her teachers describe her as doing less homework than almost anyone in her year while finding the material easy. So far that reads like precedent 4. But the prize carries a summer scholarship she will be away for; several younger students from the least academic part of our catchment started a mathematics club in her wake last term; and it was those students who wrote to the committee asking that she be featured, saying she had made mathematics look like something available to them. Her parents, meanwhile, have written that they would prefer the school not make a fuss.

What I need:

- Which rules are jointly consistent with precedents 1 through 7? I can see at least three candidates — something about never ranking individuals in public, something about whether the visible cause of a success is the child's own work or her circumstances, something about whether a display lowers anyone watching it — but I cannot tell whether these are genuinely distinct rules or one rule in different clothes, and I expect there are others I have missed. State each candidate as a rule that could actually be applied, and say which precedents it explains and which it leaves unexplained.
- For each candidate rule, what does it imply for Thursday's case, and which precedent is its closest analog?
- On that basis, what should the committee decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, and how much confidence can that recommendation carry? If the record genuinely underdetermines this case, say so plainly; then say what principle the committee would be committing itself to by featuring her, and what different principle it would be committing itself to by giving her a line in a list.
- One question that is mine rather than the committee's: I want to propose a case — ideally something we could decide cheaply next year — that would settle which rule this body is actually running. What should that case look like, and what verdicts under which candidate rules would it force apart?
- Finally, how should I treat the parents' preference for no fuss? Does it enter the analysis above as a reason, or is it only about how a decision gets implemented?

Please keep the analysis anchored in these precedents rather than in general claims about Danish or Australian or American attitudes. The whole point is that the crude labels my husband and I have been trading do not tell us what this committee does. No references needed.
