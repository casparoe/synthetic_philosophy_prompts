Before turning to philosophy I completed a doctorate in mathematical statistics, and I never genuinely left that vocabulary: I think in sampling frames, exchangeable sequences, sufficient statistics, calibration, proper scoring rules, and the coherence constraints that keep a set of bets from being Dutch-booked. The students, for their part, know decision theory cold and know almost no quantum mechanics beyond linear algebra. I should like the Born-rule problem put into my own terms before I prepare the class.

Take the three main lines of attack — the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic derivation, Zurek's envariance argument, and the Sebens–Carroll appeal to self-locating uncertainty in the interval between decoherence and observation — and restate each, as faithfully as you can manage, in the language of inference and decision under uncertainty rather than in the language of branches, amplitudes, and weights. For each line, identify the nearest counterpart notion in my vocabulary, and flag every point at which you are bending a statistical term to fit rather than actually translating it; I would rather have the bending exposed than have a smooth-sounding gloss. Then give me the residue report: which claims have no counterpart in the language of sampling, calibration, and expected-loss minimization at all, and for each such residue item, whether it marks a real limit on what that language can express about a world containing no repeatable experiment, or whether it reveals that the original claim was doing less work than its partisans suppose.

Finally, test a hypothesis of mine rather than indulging it. My working view is that the entire dispute reduces to the question of which measure to attach to an exchangeable sequence when there is no sampling frame generating it — that is, a problem about the legitimacy of indifference principles in the absence of a source of randomness. If that is wrong, tell me where precisely it goes wrong, and in particular tell me whether casting the squared amplitude as a calibration claim or a symmetry claim already presupposes the result it is meant to derive, and at which step in the reasoning the presupposition enters.
