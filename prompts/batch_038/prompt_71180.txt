What I'm going to claim: most work on archival silence — the idea that archives systematically fail to record certain lives, and that the gaps are produced by administrative power rather than by accident — treats silence as an interpretive theme, something you notice, name, and then gesture at in the conclusion. I think that's a category mistake, and that silence is better handled as a measurement problem. The concrete proposal: for a given series, reconstruct from the administrative rules governing it what entries the bureaucracy was *required* to generate (a fee schedule that implies a transaction, a burial ordinance that implies a register line, a return cycle that implies a household), and then treat the expected-but-absent entries as a residual that has structure — patterned by who was subject to which rule, when the rule changed, and which office was enforcing it. I've been calling it docketing the gaps. My pilot is a district-level land and tax registry from a British colonial administration in the 1880s–1900s (I'd rather not get into the specifics here, they're not what I need help with), where I think I can show the gaps aren't random noise: they cluster by tenure type and around the years a particular district office was short-staffed. I want to say out loud that historians who treat silence as purely discursive are missing that the same archive supports a denominator.
