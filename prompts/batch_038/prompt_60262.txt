I write about environment and economics for a general-interest magazine and I'm working on a piece about the standard advice for overused common resources — fence it off, hand out title deeds, done. Pretty much every expert I talk to says some version of that, and I want a counterexample readers can actually follow, sitting high up in the piece.

So: build me a concrete scenario where splitting a shared resource into individually owned parcels makes the outcome worse than leaving it as an open common, even though every owner afterwards is a careful, forward-looking steward of their own patch. Ground rules, please stick to them: realistic setting (herders, a fishery, an irrigation scheme — nothing sci-fi), the scenario itself under 250 words, no transaction costs or missing markets, no rich outsiders or unequal bargaining power grabbing the good parcels, no damage crossing from one parcel to another, no change in anyone's ability to monitor or enforce, and nobody irrational or short-sighted. The reason the divided version does worse has to be visible in the setup, not smuggled in.

Then: (1) walk through what happens under the open common and under division, with enough rough arithmetic that I could check it on a napkin; (2) name the single feature of the case doing the work, and tell me what happens to the verdict if I take that feature out; (3) give me the best reply a defender of privatization would make to my scenario, and say honestly whether the case survives it.

The point of the exercise is to show that "just privatize it" isn't a universal fix, so a scenario that only works because it quietly assumes bad owners, or that simply asserts the collapse, doesnt count.
