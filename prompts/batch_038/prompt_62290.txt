So I'm a junior and for the last two months I've been in this tiny reading group — me, three friends from my school's philosophy club, and a grad student who supervises us on Discord Sunday nights for free — and we've been working through the stuff on joint action and shared intention, and honestly the whole thing has curdled into an argument nobody can win. My friend keeps a running tally of who bit which bullet and the tally has stopped changing. Here's roughly where the three camps landed. Camp one says that when you and I paint a house together or paddle a canoe, there's no extra ingredient beyond individual planning states that happen to interlock: I intend that we do the thing, you intend that we do the thing, our subplans mesh, each of us is responsive to the other's actions and to the fact that the other is responsive too, and that's the whole story — no group mind, nothing irreducible. Camp two says that's just parallel agency with good manners, and that what makes it our rowing rather than two coincidentally matching rowings is a joint commitment that binds us as a body, which is exactly why I'm allowed to be annoyed at you if you stop pulling halfway across the lake and why you can't just quietly opt out without a word — and that obligation-flavored fact can't be cashed out in individual attitudes no matter how elegantly you interlock them. Camp three says both are overthinking it: the capacity to have we-intentions is a primitive biological endowment sitting in individual brains, its not reducible to I-intentions plus beliefs about you, and dragging normativity into the analysis is a category mistake.

What bugs me is that we never actually disagree about any case. Everybody accepts the canoe, the house painting, the walk together, the two strangers who start shoveling the same snowbound driveway without exchanging a word, the flash mob that goes off on cue. Each camp has a stock counterexample to the other two, each camp bites the bullet it's handed, and in six weeks no argument has ever been treated by the other side as even the right kind of thing — which makes me suspect the argument isnt about joint action at all. My hypothesis, and I want you to actually pressure-test it instead of being nice: the three camps are answering three different questions under one heading — what psychological machinery realizes coordinated planning, what makes participants bound to each other and entitled to complain, and what the basic capacity is that development and evolution have to explain — and the shared vocabulary of we-intention is what makes it look like one debate. Alternatives I can't rule out: maybe there's a smuggled metaphysical constraint doing the work, like a demand that everything bottom out inside individual heads that got imported from philosophy of mind and nobody bothers to argue for here; maybe half the stalemate is people using Tuomela's we-mode/I-mode split differently without noticing; or maybe there's a hidden empirical question underneath, because all three camps gesture at the infant-and-chimp literature on joint attention and shared goals as though it favors them, and I definately can't tell whether that evidence could discriminate between the accounts or whether it's just decoration each side hangs its hat on.
