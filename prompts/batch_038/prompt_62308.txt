I spent thirty-four years in charge of an uncertainty budget at a calibration laboratory, which is to say that my working life consisted of deciding how much confidence to place in numbers produced by instruments of known imperfection. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy more or less systematically, and I am presently working through the literature on political epistemology, where Condorcet's jury theorem is repeatedly invoked as the mathematical license for trusting the judgments of groups. Nearly every treatment I have encountered reaches for the same illustration, and I should like a serious analysis of it rather than another rehearsal of the anecdote. The episode is the weight-judging competition Galton reported in a short letter to Nature in March of 1907. At the annual show of the West of England Fat Stock and Poultry Exhibition, held at Plymouth, a fat ox was selected and competitors bought stamped and numbered cards for sixpence each, on which they wrote their estimate of what the animal would weigh once slaughtered and dressed; about eight hundred tickets were issued, of which some 787 yielded usable estimates. The dressed weight came out at 1198 pounds, the middlemost estimate at 1207 pounds, and Galton reckoned the probable error of a single estimate at roughly 37 pounds, or about 3 percent. He also observed that the scatter about the median was asymmetric, with one quarter of the estimates deviating more than 45 pounds above the middlemost value and another quarter deviating more than 29 pounds below it, though in the following paragraph he describes the two halves of the curve in terms that appear to reverse those figures; please do not let your analysis hinge on that inconsistency, which I mention only so that you are not misled by it. His conclusion was that "this result is, I think, more creditable to the trustworthiness of a democratic judgment than might have been expected."

What I want is a disciplined mapping of this episode onto the theorem, and then an honest account of where the mapping fails. Begin by fixing the terms: who exactly constitutes the jury, given that the card-buyers were a self-selected, paying sample at a single livestock show, and what proportion of them were butchers, farmers, and cattle men rather than the undifferentiated public the retellings imply. Then identify the question being voted on, which is where my first difficulty lies, since the theorem concerns a binary proposition with a determinate right answer and a competence parameter above one half, whereas the quantity here is continuous and no individual estimate is either right or wrong in the relevant sense. Say plainly what must be done to a continuous estimation problem before a competence probability can even be defined, and whether any such reconstruction is faithful or merely cosmetic. Next, the aggregation rule: Galton chose the median on the strength of what he called one vote one value, and I should like to know whether that is a Condorcetian move at all, or whether the theorem simply has no purchase on it. Finally, independence, which strikes me as the load-bearing assumption here. Galton's own justification reads to me like a proto-independence argument — the sixpenny fee deterred practical joking, the prize and the joy of competition prompted each man to do his best, and the judgments were uninfluenced by oratory. Assess how much weight that can bear when the errors are those of men at one show, on one day, looking at one animal, each drawing on the same local market's sense of what a dressed bullock typically weighs; in my own trade that is not independence but a shared systematic offset, and no amount of averaging removes it.

Two things follow from that framing, and I should like you to test both rather than endorse them. First, a conjecture of my own that I have not been able to settle: if a majority of individual estimates are more likely than not to fall on the high side of the true value, then a majority-based argument of Condorcet's sort seems to predict that the middlemost estimate will sit high as well, and that is exactly what occurred, the median standing nine pounds above the truth. Does the observed asymmetry in the scatter genuinely license such a prediction, or am I importing a structure the data do not have? Second, and more generally, whether the right formal description of this episode is the jury theorem at all, or rather the description I would have written in the laboratory: an ensemble of imperfect instruments whose random errors partially cancel under averaging while their common bias survives intact, so that the crowd's near-accuracy is a fact about the estimator and not about the democratic competence of any of its members. Conclude by telling me what the episode does to the theorem — whether it confirms it, is an anomaly for it, or, as I suspect, is evidence for a different proposition that the theorem is habitually made to sponsor — and say where the theorem is simply silent. Please close with the epistemic part of the matter: state which consideration or argument would move you to the opposite verdict, and identify the step in your own reasoning most likely to be wrong, particularly if you have relied on assumptions about the composition of the crowd or the correlation among its errors that the surviving record cannot support. Write in American English.
