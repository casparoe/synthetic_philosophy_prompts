--- QUESTION AS SET ---

Both the counterfactual account of causation and the interventionist account analyse causal claims in terms of difference-making. Explain precisely how the notion of difference-making invoked by each account differs from that invoked by the other. In particular, address: (i) what work, if any, the requirement that an intervention be possible does in each account's conditions for a causal claim to be true; (ii) whether the interventionist account incurs a charge of circularity that the counterfactual account escapes; and (iii) whether there are cases in which the two accounts deliver different verdicts even though an intervention on the putative cause is possible in principle. Your discussion must use at least one preemption case.

--- STUDENT SUBMISSION ---

Both accounts agree that to cause something is to make a difference to it, but they locate the difference in different places. For Lewis, causation between two events c and e is analysed in terms of counterfactual dependence: e depends on c if, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred; and the counterfactual is made true by facts about similarity between possible worlds, so that roughly the closest worlds in which c does not occur are worlds in which e does not occur, with the laws of nature held fixed. Causation proper is then the ancestral of dependence, which is what lets Lewis deal with preemption. In the standard early-preemption case, where Suzy's stone arrives first and shatters the window while Billy's stone is in the air behind it, the shattering does not depend on Suzy's throw, since Billy is a backup; but an intermediate event, Suzy's stone's arriving at the window, does depend on her throw, and the shattering depends in the right way on that, so the causal chain is preserved through the intermediate link.

Woodward's account also appeals to what would happen if things were different, but the relevant difference is produced by an intervention rather than by the obtaining of a nearby world. On his view, X is a cause of Y just in case there is some possible intervention on X that changes the value of Y, or the probability distribution over Y, with the other causes of Y held fixed in the appropriate way; the intervention must act as a switching process that takes X off its other causes, must not affect Y except through X, and must not be correlated with Y except through X. For general causal claims the central notion is invariance: a generalization is causal to the extent that it stays true under interventions on the variables that figure in it. Because invariance is a matter of degree and can only be probed over a limited range of changes, Woodward's account explains why some causal generalizations are better than others, which is something Lewis's account has no obvious resources for doing.

On circularity the two accounts are not in the same position, though the gap is smaller than it is usually said to be. An intervention is defined as a process that causes X and does not affect Y except through X, so the definition plainly uses the causal notion it is meant to analyse, and critics have pressed this as a vicious circle. The right reply, I think, is that the interventionist account never claimed to be a reductive analysis in Lewis's sense: its aim is to clarify the content of causal claims by connecting them to manipulation, not to eliminate causal vocabulary in favour of non-causal vocabulary, and an account can be informative without being reductive. Lewis's account does aim at reduction, because counterfactuals are analysed in terms of similarity between worlds and similarity is not a causal notion; the cost is that he owes us the similarity metric, and the closest-worlds machinery ends up carrying as much explanatory weight as the intervention machinery does. In effect Lewis's reduction pushes the difficulty into the semantics of counterfactuals, just as the interventionist pushes it into the notion of a manipulation that is not fat-handed.

That brings me to (iii). I do not think the two accounts can disagree in any case where an intervention is possible, or at least not in any case that matters. If there is an intervention on X with respect to Y that changes Y, then there is a nearby world in which X has a different value and Y has a different value too; closeness is exactly what an ideal intervention is designed to secure, since an intervention changes X and leaves everything else, including the laws and the other causes of Y, as it was. So the interventionist conditional and the Lewisian counterfactual come out true together and the accounts coincide. Disagreement can only arise where no intervention on X is possible even in principle: claims about the very early universe, for instance, or about macro-level variables such as mental states, where any attempt to intervene would have to change so much else that it would be fat-handed. There the interventionist declines to issue a verdict while Lewis's account still says something, since similarity between worlds is defined whether or not anyone could act. On this reading interventionism looks like Lewis's account restricted to the manipulable cases, with a reservation of judgement elsewhere. It is also why the two accounts handle preemption the same way: once the variables are fine-grained enough to include the intermediate event, the structural-equation model says what the chain of counterfactual dependence says.

The residual difficulty is what motivates the agency theory of causation as a secondary quality, on the model of colour concepts, which Menzies and Price developed in order to repair Lewis's account: if the causal notion cannot be eliminated it should be tied to what we can do, and that tie is not merely epistemic. But the proposal inherits the same limitation, since it has nothing to say about causes that no agent could manipulate.
