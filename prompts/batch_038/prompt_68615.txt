I co-organize an interdisciplinary reading group where half the room is moral philosophy and half is security studies, and last week it produced the most stubborn argument of the term. We had been working through the deterrence paradox — the worry that the act a nuclear state must threaten is one it could never permissibly perform, together with the adjacent puzzle about whether you can intend something you know you will not do — and afterward two members, whom I will call Ilse and Desmond, got into it properly. Desmond spent some years in defense analysis and takes the strategic logic seriously; Ilse is a Kantian about killing and takes it even more seriously. I was asked to referee, made a mess of it, and have been asked to bring in an outside opinion. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reproduce it.

Move 1, Ilse. The posture we actually maintain is a conditional intention to destroy cities if we are attacked. Carrying that out would be the mass killing of noncombatants, which is wrong, and the fact that the posture has kept the peace for eighty years does not launder the intention it requires; if it did, every atrocity threatened in advance would be justified by the calm it bought. So the intention is wrong and the posture is wrong, consequences notwithstanding.

Move 2, Desmond. The step from the act would be wrong to the intention is wrong is not free. Intentions are individuated by their conditions, and what our leaders intend is to retaliate if attacked, which is one conditional act whose value includes the peace it has produced. Judging the intention by the wrongness of the consequent in isolation is like judging a promise by the cost of keeping it while ignoring the value of the practice of promising. You have already conceded the policy keeps the peace, so you have conceded it prevents more killing than it could ever perform. If you are going to count killing, count it.

Move 3, Ilse. I am not counting; I am refusing to trade, since some acts are not available to be chosen however good the reasons. But set that aside, because your reply collapses on its own. The package is good only in the branch where the threat is never carried out. In the branch where it is, retaliation accomplishes nothing: our cities are already gone, the aggressor already has what he wanted, and killing his civilians cannot recover anything or protect anyone. So the threatened act is one that the leader, once in it, would have every reason and indeed a duty not to perform, since by then it is slaughter with no aim. An intention you know you would abandon when the moment came is not an intention, and a threat everyone knows is not backed by an intention does not deter. Your own account of why the package is good therefore explains why the package cannot be held together.

Move 4, Desmond. Two errors. First, that branch is not aimless: retaliating preserves the credibility of the guarantee to allies and of every threat made afterward, denies the aggressor his gains, and removes the incentive to try again, so the act has consequences, merely later ones. Second, you have assumed the leader must form the intention while knowing he will abandon it, which misunderstands how commitments are built. The entire technology of deterrence is the manufacture of commitments that are hard to reverse — delegation to field commanders, pre-delegated launch authority, alert states that compress decision time to minutes, forces dispersed so that they cannot be recalled, communications deliberately severed so that nobody can negotiate them back. It is the old chicken-game device of ripping out your steering wheel and tossing it through the window where the other driver can watch. Once the machinery exists, the question is not whether you would want to back down but that backing down has been made impossible or ruinously expensive. The intention is real precisely because you have arranged not to be able to reconsider.

Move 5, Ilse. Then you have described something worse than what I objected to, not better. On your account the moral task of a nuclear state is to convert itself, over decades, into an entity that will destroy civilian populations in circumstances where destroying them serves no purpose — to become a thing whose worst act is not a choice but a mechanism. Cultivating that disposition in yourself and in your institutions is more culpable than the act performed under duress, because it is cold, deliberate, and dispersed among thousands of people each of whom can say he was only holding his post. And notice what the steering wheel concedes: you throw it out only because you cannot trust yourself at the moment of decision, which is an admission that at the moment of decision the right thing is to swerve.

Move 6, Desmond. It is an admission that at the moment of decision the right thing is to be unable to swerve, because the other driver knows this and will not swerve either if he believes you can. Your position has an implication you are dodging: if the conditional intention is impermissible, then a government must either disarm and leave any aggressor unopposed, or bluff — announce a threat it does not back and hope nobody tests it. The bluffer has no intention to do wrong, which is what you want, but the bluff has to survive every test it is put to, which means behaving in every observable respect exactly as though you would carry it out. And unlike a real commitment, a bluff feeds your adversary systematically false information about your behavior, which is precisely the condition under which miscalculation and accidental war occur. The option you think is clean is the one most likely to produce the thing we both want to avoid, and produces it by destroying his ability to predict you.

Move 7, Ilse. A bluff is not the same as a lie about your behavior, and the difference is doing real work: what the bluffer corrupts is the adversary's estimate of one variable, in a game he has strong independent reasons to play cautiously. What the sincere deterrent destroys is the possibility that anyone, on either side, is ever in a position to refuse the atrocity. And you still have not answered the question I asked. You have told me why the intention would be hard to abandon, not why forming it was permissible. Difficulty is not a defense.

I would be grateful if you would go through this move by move and tell me who is right about what, rather than who wins on the whole. Please rate each of the seven moves — from clearly mistaken, through partly right and partly mistaken, to clearly right — and justify each rating; where a move is right in one respect and wrong in another, say explicitly which is which instead of averaging it out. Then tell me what both of them are treating as settled that ought not to be: I have a suspicion that they have quietly agreed on several things that a third party would refuse to grant, but I cannot isolate them. Finally, say which of the two is more right overall and by roughly how much, and what the other would have to give up to be right. Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables: take the seven moves in order with one paragraph each, stating the rating in words at the head of the paragraph, then two or three paragraphs on what both sides left unexamined, and a closing paragraph on who is more right.
