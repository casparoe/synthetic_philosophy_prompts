One of my assigned respondents is a historian of early modern casuistry who will pounce on anything loose, so please treat the historical claims as a priority even though the argument is what I care about most. The section is meant to end up around 2,500 words, so I am not looking for suggestions about how to expand it; I am looking for what to cut, what to fix, and where I have cheated.

DRAFT OF SECTION 3

3.1 The Augustinian machinery. In *De Mendacio* Augustine defines a lie not by the falsity of what is said but by the mismatch between what is said and what the speaker holds in the heart. Someone who asserts a falsehood while believing it true is in error and rash, but does not lie; someone who asserts a truth while believing it false does lie. The liar's heart is double. Very early on he sets jokes apart from the whole inquiry, on the ground that the tone of voice and the manner of the joker make it evident that no deceit is intended, so that a jest, though not true, is not a lie; whether such speech is fitting for the spiritually mature he explicitly declines to take up. He then ranks lies in eight descending grades, from the lie in the doctrine of religion, which is gravest, down through lies that injure without benefiting anyone, lies that benefit one person by injuring another, the lie told out of the sheer lust of lying, the lie told to please one's hearers, and finally lies that shield someone's temporal safety, temporal welfare, and bodily chastity, the last of which he treats as the least grave of all. The verdict on every grade is identical: there is no sort of lie that is not a sin. The hinge case is the former bishop of Thagasta named Firmus, who, when imperial officers demanded a man he was sheltering, answered that he would neither lie nor betray anyone, endured torture, and so impressed the emperor that the fugitive was pardoned. Augustine holds him up as exemplary precisely because he refuses both horns rather than choosing the lesser.

Later, answering Consentius on the case of the Priscillianists, who were reported to hold that one may feign adherence in order to conceal the truth from outsiders, Augustine tightens rather than loosens. He separates concealing the truth from asserting a falsehood: one may fall silent, one may refuse to answer, one may speak figuratively in such a way that the hearer takes a sense the words do not carry. What one may not do is utter what is contrary to one's heart, not even for the instrumental good of gaining access to heretics in order to correct them. Paul's becoming all things to all men is read as compassion rather than as deceit.

3.2 My proposal. The claim I want to defend is that the joke clause is doing systematic work that Augustine never permits it to do. A jest is not a lie, on his own account, neither because its content is harmless nor because the speaker's end is benign, but because the speaker has overtly cancelled the hearer's standing to take the words at face value. Call this the licence condition: an utterance is a lie only where the speaker, having one thing in the heart and saying another, leaves the hearer licensed to expect that the words track the heart. Where the licence is publicly withdrawn, by tone, by convention, or by the acknowledged rules of a shared practice, there is no lie however false the content.

Augustine therefore has a general account available and declines to generalize it, and I want to argue that the declination is principled rather than merely pious. On my reading he thinks a licence can be withdrawn only in a way the hearer can actually recognize, and that recognition depends on a shared practice rather than on private intention. This yields a hard constraint: the speaker cannot withdraw the licence unilaterally and invisibly. A code agreed with a confederate does not bind a third party who was never let in on it, and an ambiguity that only the speaker knows how to resolve is not a withdrawn licence but a trap. That constraint is what makes the Priscillianist case come out as it does, and it is also what later condemns strict mental reservation, since the reserving speaker intends a qualification the hearer has no means of detecting, leaving the hearer's licence wholly intact and the heart still double.

3.3 Aquinas, and the fate of the ranking. Two differences matter for what follows. First, taxonomy. Augustine excludes the jest from the classification as not a lie at all; Aquinas reads it back in as the jocose lie, alongside the officious and the mischievous lie, and grades them by whether they are contrary to the love of God and neighbour rather than by their distance from religious doctrine. The joke is thereby converted from a non-lie into a venial sin. That is substantive, not editorial: it means the licence condition has been absorbed into an account of the lie's end instead of being allowed to negate lie-hood altogether.

Second, the ground of the wrong. For Augustine the wrong sits in the sign: speech was given for the manifestation of the heart, and lying turns the sign against the purpose for which it was given, with the harm to the neighbour following from that. For Aquinas the wrong sits in what is owed, since veracity is treated as annexed to justice and lying as a failure to render another his due. I want to suggest that Augustine's ordering of the eight grades, gravest where the register of the utterance is most constitutive of the practice it belongs to and least grave where it merely shields a body, makes considerably better sense on the sign-theology than on the debt-theory, and that this is part of why the ranking proved so hard for the later tradition to retain and was displaced by the threefold division.

The speculative part, which is the reason this section exists, is this. Broad mental reservation, the case where the intended qualification is recoverable from context, as when a person answers that he does not know while meaning that he does not know in any way he is bound to disclose, is best understood not as a repudiation of Augustine but as an attempt to generalize his joke clause to the courtroom and the checkpoint. On this view the early modern casuists were extending a genuinely Augustinian principle about overt warrant-withdrawal into settings where the audience no longer shared the practice needed to recognize it, and it is the disappearance of the shared practice, not the principle, that makes the extension illegitimate.
