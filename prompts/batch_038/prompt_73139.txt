**Question 3 (30 points; ~600 words suggested).** About a thousand generations ago, a mutation arose in a small, isolated population of lizards producing an extra row of enlarged cusps on the rear teeth. That population had shifted to a diet of hard-shelled snails, and the trait spread through it. Fifty years ago an introduced grass arrived in the same valley; the lizards now get a large share of their calories from its seeds, which they open with the same cusps. Snail-eating has declined but not disappeared.

(a) Say what an etiological (selected-effects) account and what a causal-role account of function each imply about the function of the cusps in these lizards today, and in particular whether seed-crushing is one of their functions.
(b) Suppose a lizard is born with malformed cusps that cannot crush anything at all. Which account better handles the judgment that this organ is malfunctioning, and why?
(c) Does the case give you any reason to prefer one of the accounts overall, or does it only show that they are suited to different questions? Argue for your answer rather than asserting it.

**Student's answer.**

(a) The accounts disagree because they locate function in different kinds of fact. On the causal-role account, the one Cummins developed in *Functional Analysis*, a function of a part is whatever it currently causally contributes to in the containing system, so the cusps have a whole bunch of functions at once: crushing snail shells, crushing grass seeds, reflecting light at a certain angle, adding a little mass to the skull. Cummins doesn't think the overabundance is a problem, because a function attribution is always made relative to some capacity of the system you're trying to explain, so seed-crushing is a function of the cusps whenever you're explaining the lizards' ability to get calories out of the grass. On the etiological account function is a historical relation: the function of a part is that effect of it which explains why the population has it. The cusps are there because crushing snail shells helped the ancestors reproduce, so that's the function, and seed-crushing can't be one no matter how important it currently is, because the grass arrived fifty years ago and nothing has been selected for it yet. It's a by-product of the cusps' shape rather than a function of them. Though on a modern-history version of the etiological account, where you look at what the trait has been doing recently rather than at what originally got it there, the answer might come out differently, since fifty years is arguably recent enough to matter for what the trait is being maintained for. So the honest summary for (a) is that the cusps both do and don't have the seed-crushing function depending on which theory you're using, and that's a good illustration of the two accounts not really being in competition — one is about current causal organization and one is about history, and it's a mistake to expect a single answer.

(b) Malfunction is where they really come apart. The etiological account is usually sold as the one that handles it, since the malformed cusps still have crushing as their function (their ancestors were selected for it) and simply fail to perform it. I don't think that advantage is as big as advertised. Because the account only looks backwards, it can tell you what an organ was for but not what it ought to be doing now; in a population whose environment has changed completely it's committed to assigning functions that don't help anyone any more, and a function that doesn't help can't ground a claim that something is defective. The causal-role account does better here. A malformed organ that contributes nothing has no functions on that account, and that seems right, since there's no capacity of the lizard left to explain by appealing to it. What's wrong with this lizard is better described as the containing system lacking the crushing capacity, which the causal-role account can say directly and the etiological one can only say by way of an ancestor.

(c) The case does give some reason to prefer an account, though not a knockdown one. The etiological account has to say that the thing the cusps now do for most of the lizards' calories isn't their function, which is awkward for a theory that wants to be useful to working biologists, who talk about what traits are for constantly. And it can't say anything at all about traits that have never been selected for — brand-new mutations, engineered artifacts, things built in a lab — so it has to be supplemented by the causal-role account in those cases anyway. If the two have to be combined to cover everything, and the causal-role part is the one doing the work in all the novel cases, then causal-role function looks like the more basic notion and selected effects like a special historical case of it. That's the ordering I'd defend.
