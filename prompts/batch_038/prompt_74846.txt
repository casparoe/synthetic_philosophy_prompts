I am a third-year doctoral student in political theology, writing a chapter on medieval resistance theory and its modern reception. Before I commit a counterfactual exercise to the chapter, I want to see whether it can be done at all, so I am putting you a deliberately over-specified case.

Suppose the following, all of which I stipulate as given rather than as matters to be argued:

- A head of state came to office through a genuinely competitive election and has since, by formally legal means, suspended judicial review, imprisoned the leadership of the opposition, and converted the state broadcaster into a personal instrument. He retains the settled support of a majority of the population. I shall call this *ratified tyranny*, because the standard medieval division between a ruler who lacks title and a ruler who has title but governs badly does not obviously cover it: he has title, his own subjects conferred it, and he exercises it tyrannically.
- The party contemplating his removal is not any of his subjects. It is an operative of a foreign intelligence service acting on the instruction of his own government, which is not at war with the tyrant's polity and has no treaty obligation to protect the tyrant's subjects.
- The method is a remotely delivered strike in which the operative bears no appreciable risk of death and in which, on the intelligence available to him beforehand, nobody else is killed or maimed. There is no collateral death to weigh, so the ordinary proportionality complaints get no grip.
- Part of the targeting decision — identification, timing and the final authorisation window — is made by a machine system trained on the service's own intelligence, so that the operative's contribution is to confirm rather than to determine.
- Both sides of the prudential ledger are known in advance, and quantified to a reasonable confidence. The service's analysts judge, on the basis of comparable cases, that removal will produce a successor more repressive than the incumbent and roughly four years of internal armed conflict; they judge also that leaving him in place will continue a toll of political killings at a roughly constant rate for the remainder of a long natural tenure.

On that case, what would Thomas Aquinas have said? Please do the following six things.

1. Keep two questions distinct throughout, because I think they come apart and most treatments of tyrannicide blur them. First, the *exegetical* question: had this case been put to Aquinas in terms available to him, what verdict would he most plausibly have returned, given his own vocabulary of the common good, of sedition and its limits, of public authority as the condition for killing, and of the dangers of removing a ruler? Second, the *systematic* question: what follows from his principles once the facts he did not have are supplied? Where the two answers diverge, identify the divergence and say which side of it you take to be load-bearing in his thought and which to be a function of thirteenth-century circumstance.

2. Address the jurisdictional mismatch directly. His requirement that killing for the common good be done by public authority is usually read as a requirement about standing within the polity whose good is at stake. Here the agent has public authority, but over a different people. Does that satisfy the condition, because what matters is that he act for a common good rather than from private interest; or does it fail, because authority is essentially ordered to the community over which it is held? Say how much work his views on relations between independent polities, and on any higher authority competent to judge a ruler, can do here, and be explicit wherever the material simply does not decide.

3. Say whether the ratification of the tyranny matters. Given that his subjects conferred the office on him and that a majority continue to support him, is there still a common good of that polity for an outsider to vindicate, or has the people — on his own account of the multitude as a people united by law and common interest rather than as a mere crowd — effectively adopted the ruler's private good as its own?

4. Take a position on the developmental dispute. Readers differ over whether his early commentary on the Sentences is materially more permissive about killing a tyrant than the more guarded treatment in his later work on kingship and in the Secunda Secundae. If that disagreement is real, say which line you would apply to this case and why; if you think it overstated, say so and show how the two are reconcilable.

5. Deal with the machine. Does outsourcing part of the targeting decision to a trained system change the moral description of the act on his account of voluntariness, deliberation and judgement? Specifically: is there an agent here who judges and wills the killing, and if the operative's role is confirmatory rather than determinative, on whom does authorship of the act fall? I want this treated as a question inside his framework, not as a general remark about automated warfare.

6. Settle the status of the prudential consideration. Is the forecast of a worse successor and four years of conflict a reason the act is unjust, or a counsel of prudence that a sufficiently grave injustice could outweigh? The two readings give different verdicts on a case where the forecast is known in advance rather than conjectured, so choose between them and defend the choice.

I will supply the references myself, so please do not produce a bibliography, a reading list, or anything in the shape of a citation: work titles and the topics they treat are quite enough. Write in British English. Aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,200 words, organised under the six headings above.

On the epistemic side, I do not want a balanced survey that closes by observing how complex the matter is. Commit to a verdict on the question in heading 1. Then tell me what would change your mind: which single argument, or which correction to my stipulations, would flip it; and separately, where you think your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong — that is, at which point the view you attribute to Aquinas ceases to be a reading of him and becomes an extrapolation from his principles. Give me a rough indication of how confident you are in the exegetical answer and in the systematic answer respectively; I expect those two confidences to differ, and I would like to know why.

End with one final line, in exactly this form and with nothing after it:

Verdict: <illicit | licit | conditionally licit>

and if you choose the third, state the condition in a clause of no more than twelve words following the label.
