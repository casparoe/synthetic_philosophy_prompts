I am setting the second problem sheet for an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of quantum mechanics, taken by a mixed cohort of third-year philosophy students, final-year physics students, and a handful of first-year postgraduates. The sheet has room for one substantial question worth 25 marks, and students will have roughly 75 minutes for it. I want the question to concern Bell's theorem and locality, but I specifically do not want one that can be answered by reciting the standard narrative about entanglement, hidden variables, and spooky action at a distance. The difficulty should come from making students distinguish the several assumptions that enter the derivation from the empirical claim the derivation is subsequently used to support.

To that end, the question should be built around the following invented model. Let a source emit pairs of spin-½ particles, with Alice and Bob each choosing a measurement direction in a common plane, represented by angles α and β, and each obtaining an outcome in {+1, −1}. Let the hidden variable λ be distributed uniformly over [0, 2π), independently of the settings, and define

A(α, λ) = +1 if cos(λ − α) ≥ 0, and −1 otherwise;
B(β, λ) = −1 if cos(λ − β) ≥ 0, and +1 otherwise.

Call this Model M, and let E(α, β) denote the expectation value of the product A·B over λ.

The question should then present students with the following extract from a fictional student's answer, and ask them to evaluate it. The extract contains four claims, and each should turn out to mix something correct with something defective:

1. "Model M reproduces the quantum-mechanical predictions for the singlet state: when the two directions coincide it gives perfect anticorrelation, when they are at right angles it gives zero correlation, and when they are opposite it gives perfect correlation."
2. "I evaluated the CHSH quantity S at the usual optimal settings (α = 0, α′ = π/2, β = π/4, β′ = −π/4) and obtained |S| = 2, which does not exceed the Bell bound. Model M therefore respects Bell's theorem, and its existence shows that the theorem cannot after all rule out local hidden variables."
3. "Model M cannot be used to signal: each side sees +1 and −1 with probability one half, whatever the other side chooses. Since locality just means the impossibility of superluminal signalling, M is local, and the impossibility of signalling is all that Bell's theorem could ever have established."
4. "In any case, M is deterministic, and the derivation of the Bell inequalities presupposes determinism. A stochastic variant of M, in which λ fixes only the probabilities of the two outcomes, would evade the theorem entirely, because the inequalities constrain deterministic models."

Please produce three things. First, the question as it should appear on the problem sheet: divided into five parts whose marks sum to 25, with the mark allocation visible to students, and stating explicitly which results students may take as given — in particular the quantum prediction E = −cos θ for the singlet state, where θ is the angle between the directions; the bound |S| ≤ 2 for any model whose joint probabilities factorise into a local part for each wing; and the quantum value |S| = 2√2 at the optimal settings. Nothing in the question should depend on recall of the literature or on remembering a formula I have not supplied. Second, a model answer of about 900 words. Third, a marking scheme that allocates all 25 marks, states for each part the specific moves a first-class answer must make, and names the characteristic errors that should cost marks, so that I can mark consistently and so that a merely fluent summary of Bell's theorem does not score highly.

Two of the parts should be quantitative: one should require students to derive or state the correlation function E(α, β) for Model M as a function of the angular separation and to confirm the three values claimed in the first extract, and one should require them to locate the separation at which Model M departs most from the quantum prediction, together with the size of that departure. The model answer should give the exact value of the correlation function and a correct numerical value for the maximal departure. Please check the arithmetic yourself rather than reproducing plausible-looking numbers, and make sure the CHSH evaluation in the second extract is one that a student reproducing it correctly would indeed find to be exactly at the bound.

The marking scheme should also make clear how a first-class answer differs from a competent but second-class one. My expectation is that the distinction lies in recognising that Model M violates none of the theorem's assumptions — it is deterministic, factorisable, and independent of the settings — and that its failure is purely empirical, so that the second extract mistakes satisfaction of an inequality for reproduction of the observed correlations; in distinguishing the screening-off condition that Bell locality really imposes from the much weaker condition of no-signalling, and explaining why the third extract's premise is true but does no work; and in explaining why determinism is not an independent assumption of the derivation, so that passing to stochastic response functions buys nothing. A candidate who makes these points should be credited even without attaching names or dates to them, and the model answer should contain no citations, references, or suggestions for further reading.
