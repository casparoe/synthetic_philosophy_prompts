I sit on the registry committee of a small classic-yacht club, and this year the committee has a problem it cannot solve by looking anything up. My grandfather's 1938 gaff cutter has been in the club's continuous registry since 1961; I inherited it in 2011, and over fourteen seasons I have replaced essentially every plank, both frames on the port side, the keel bolt, the mast, the deck hardware, and the auxiliary engine, always one piece at a time, always in the yard, never letting the hull stop being a hull. Every removed timber went into a neighbor's shed down the road, and last spring he finished assembling a second boat out of them — same lines, same scantlings, same fastenings — and has entered it in the centenary regatta under the original name, on the grounds that his boat has the original material and mine does not. Two committee members say his is the real boat, two say mine is, one says they are both partly right and neither wholly, and I have discovered, in three hours of arguing about it, that I cannot state my own view twice in the same words. I would like to walk in to the next meeting with something that settles what we are actually disagreeing about rather than who is right.

So please write me a decision tree that carries a committee member from the case as I have described it to a named position on how artifacts persist through replacement and duplication. I want roughly nine to twelve numbered nodes and seven to ten leaves. Every node should be a question about a concrete variant of the dispute — what if the replacement had all happened in one winter, what if the neighbor's boat had been assembled ten years before I started work, what if a third boat had been built from scratch on the original drawings and never had any of the old timber — with two or three answer options and an explicit routing for each. Two hard constraints on the nodes: no node may contain a term of art (nobody in this room has read anything on identity, and I want the tree usable by the two members who mostly care about the trophies), and no node may be one whose answer tells you which leaf you are heading toward, so that the instrument sorts people by what they say about cases and not by which label appeals to them. At the leaves, by contrast, I do want the specialist names, each with one sentence saying what the view commits you to in my case and one sentence on the most awkward thing about it. At a minimum the leaves must include a view on which the answer depends on what kind of thing a boat is, a view on which both claimants are equally good candidates so that there is no unique correct answer, and a view on which there is no fact of the matter because nothing in the yard answers to "the boat" at all; the rest of the leaf set is up to you, and I would rather you built the space first and then placed positions in it than start from a list of names.

Three further things. First, work in at least one node about what could have happened rather than what did — for instance, whether the boat could have been rebuilt plank for plank from all-new timber on the same lines and still have been the boat — and at least one node that tests whether the question has an answer that further investigation in the shed could reveal, as against an answer that is settled by what we decide to count; I suspect those two are where our committee is really split. Second, after the tree, list the combinations of node answers that the tree permits but that no leaf corresponds to, and say for each whether that region is empty because the view would be incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply worked it out yet. Third, flag honestly any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate given that they are answered by laypeople about a boat, and end with a short cross-check: run the same nodes against three cases that are not artifacts — a person whose neurons are replaced one at a time, a volunteer fire company that has turned over every member across twenty years, and a congregation that has kept the same building, name, and liturgy while replacing its entire membership twice — and tell me where the tree gives different answers there, since if it does, our registry rule probably should not have been written as though boats were a special case. No bibliography or reading list, please; I will chase down the literature myself once I know which view I am supposed to be arguing with my neighbor about.
