I am in my final year at a high school that offers no philosophy courses, so I have been teaching myself formal epistemology through papers our interlibrary loan service can obtain, and I would like my reasoning checked before I present it to the forecasting club I founded.

The paper that has occupied me most is James Joyce's argument that probabilism can be vindicated on purely accuracy-theoretic grounds: roughly, that if your degrees of belief over a set of propositions violate the probability axioms, then some coherent alternative system would have been more accurate than yours no matter which possible world turned out to be actual, where accuracy is measured by something like the Brier score — the sum of the squared differences between each credence and the truth value of the corresponding proposition, so that a lower total is better and zero is perfect.

Our club scores its forecasts with exactly that measure, which lets me test the theorem against real numbers. In October three members forecast a single proposition A along with its negation. Priya assigned 0.9 to A and 0.9 to not-A; Sam assigned 0.5 to each; Theo assigned 0.95 to A and 0.05 to not-A. Priya's pair is incoherent, and her total squared error is 0.82 whether A is true or false, whereas Sam's is 0.50 either way, so Sam beats Priya in both worlds precisely as advertised. Theo's totals are 0.005 if A is true and 1.805 if it is false.

From this I have drawn two conclusions that some of my members dispute, and I want to know whether the dispute is substantive or merely verbal. First: because the accuracy argument shows that coherence is what accuracy demands, and because Sam and Theo are equally coherent, the argument cannot rank Sam below Theo; not being accuracy-dominated simply is being free of epistemic fault on accuracy grounds, so Sam's flat 0.5 profile cannot be criticized however much evidence she has accumulated. Second: because the lowest possible scores are achieved at the endpoints, the club should instruct members to report probabilities as close to 0 or 1 as they can stomach, since that is what accuracy itself favors; a scoring rule that penalized this would just be measuring the wrong thing, and we are free to pick whichever rule we like so long as it still rewards being right.

Are these conclusions safe? Specifically, I would like to know which step, if any, fails — whether the accuracy-based case for probabilism really has nothing further to say about Sam, whether it licenses pushing forecasts toward the endpoints, and whether the choice of scoring rule is as unconstrained as I have assumed. I am comfortable with the arithmetic; what I lack is a sense of which distinctions the literature treats as load-bearing.
