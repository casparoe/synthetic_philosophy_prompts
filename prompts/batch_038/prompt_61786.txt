I'm writing a novel where two friends come apart over politics, and the whole thing lives or dies on whether any given line of dialogue reads as arguing in bad faith or just reads as a person being a person. So I've been keeping a list of moves with my gut verdicts on them, and I'd like to know what principle is generating the verdicts, if there is one.

1. Quoting the other person exactly, word for word, but dropping the next sentence, the one that would've changed the meaning. Not fine.
2. Restating their sloppy position more sharply and more defensibly than they managed, then attacking that. Also not fine — which bugs me, since on the face of it that's a service to the argument.
3. Asking for a definition, politely, six or seven times running. Not fine.
4. Conceding the point completely and warmly, then changing the subject. Fine, I think. I keep flip flopping on this one.
5. Saying something true about the other's side while knowing perfectly well how it'll be heard. Fine.
6. Getting caught out and immediately, painfully correcting yourself. I want this to retroactively make the earlier move not-bad-faith — call it the goodwill ledger, I know that isn't a real term — but I'm not sure it should rescue move 3 as easily as it rescues move 1.

Can anything get all six, or five out of six? Mostly I want to know whether 1 and 2 are seperable by something other than my say-so, and what your principle does with the case I can't call: a character who sincerely believes every word he's saying, and is also privately relieved that the argument lets him out of lending his brother the money. And if the simpler version of the principle costs me one of these verdicts, tell me which one and make the case for giving it up.
