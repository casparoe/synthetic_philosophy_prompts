I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment sections for something like fifteen years, which turns out to be preparation for exactly nothing in my current job: I direct a small private foundation, and three years ago our board amended its mission statement to a view it labeled tranquilism, meaning that what ultimately matters morally is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering — not the presence of pleasure, not the satisfaction of desires, not achievement, agency, or flourishing. The board had been reading Buddhist-inflected arguments about desire and some population ethics in summary form, and it took the sentence literally. Every panelist agrees with the sentence. No two panelists agree about what follows from it, and we have been deciding by vibes ever since, with the executive director breaking ties.

The disagreements are structural rather than personal, and any procedure you write has to survive all three:

1. One member holds that craving is simply a species of suffering, so the two clauses of the charter collapse into one and we are ordinary negative utilitarians who flinch at the label. Another holds that they come apart constantly — a person can be free of craving and wretched (grief, chronic pain, loss with no wanting left in it), and can suffer precisely because they crave.
2. A second member treats pleasure as instrumentally load-bearing, since contented people want less, and wants funding credit for pleasure-generating programs on that basis. A third thinks this is a backdoor wide enough to swallow the whole view.
3. Nobody can say what to do about programs that reduce suffering by creating or inflaming desire — aspiration, ambition, consumer wants — or that reduce desire by dulling the person.

Constraints, so that what you write is something my panel can actually run: we meet monthly, we have about ninety minutes per proposal, the panel is six people of whom one has any quantitative training, proposals range from fifty thousand to two million dollars, and I have to be able to write a two-paragraph justification for every decision that cites a step of the procedure rather than a mood.

Please write the decision procedure. Specifically:

- The questions to ask of a proposal, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and which answers terminate the review early.
- Operational definitions. My panelists have not read philosophy, and craving is not a variable in anyone's grant report. Give me the substitutes — what a program officer should look for in an application, a budget, a monitoring plan, or an evaluation — and say explicitly where each substitute is a faithful stand-in for the concept and where it quietly changes the subject.
- A treatment of the cases the charter makes awkward: whether satisfying a craving counts as reducing it, and if so over what horizon; whether desires newly created in beneficiaries are a cost to be netted against benefits, and how; whether effects on people who do not yet exist get the same weight as effects on people who do; and whether a reduction in craving achieved by suppressing agency, curiosity, or the capacity to want anything at all clears the bar or is disqualified, and on what grounds.
- The points where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a human being because it concerns values and no amount of evidence will settle it, versus the points where it concerns matters of fact that a better evaluation design could settle. Mark these visibly, and say what the handoff looks like in practice — what I actually put in front of the board.
- Where the procedure will misfire: the kinds of proposal it systematically over-rewards or over-penalizes, and what a self-interested applicant could do to game it.

Then run it on three proposals and show your work, including the numbers or scores you would want if they existed and what you would do in their absence: (a) a mindfulness curriculum in a low-income school district with strong evidence for reducing student anxiety and moderate evidence for raising educational aspiration; (b) an expansion of community palliative care in a region with essentially none, evaluated mainly on patient and family reports of distress; (c) a proposal to distribute a long-acting pharmaceutical agent in a displaced-persons camp that reliably reduces reported distress, appetite, and agitation. At least one of these should come out in a way the panel will not like. Say so plainly, and say why the view commits us to it — or, if you think it does not, show which step blocks the result.

Two further requests. First, be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing procedure's clothing: where the ordering of the questions, the choice of proxy, or the tie-breaking rule is doing substantive normative work that another reasonable tranquilist would arrange differently. Second, tell me how confident you are in the claims the procedure rests on, and single out the one step or claim you are least sure of — the place where, if you are wrong, the procedure yields systematically bad advice rather than merely noisy advice. I would rather have a procedure with its weak joint labeled than a confident one that fails silently.
