I work on integrity and compliance policy at a development finance institution, and I've stopped trusting our corruption risk register because I can't say what makes an entry on it corrupt as opposed to merely bad, merely illegal, or merely foreign. Before I commission anything or wade back into the literature, I want the spec, not a survey. Write me the desiderata a theory of corruption has to meet to be usable for this purpose: the requirements any adequate account has to satisfy, each with the case or consideration behind it, and a mark on which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of a particular camp.

A few things the list has to be built around, because these are where our register falls apart:

- The theory has to sort cases where the same practice is corrupt in one setting and ordinary courtesy in another, without the cop-out of declaring the local version fine just because it's local. So there needs to be a desideratum about universality: what makes a standard of official conduct claimable across settings, and what it costs to claim it. If the answer is that nothing does, say what's left of the concept.
- It has to take a position on secrecy. Compare a customs officer's small "expediting fee" that is openly known, budgeted for by importing firms, and never disguised, with the identical fee demanded behind a closed door. Is the first one less corrupt? If the theory says yes, explain how it avoids letting people launder anything by doing it in public; if no, explain what work the hiding was doing in all those cases where it clearly seemed to matter.
- It has to explain what "corrupt" is doing that "illegal," "self-interested," and "unfair" aren't, or concede that it's doing nothing.
- It has to handle the private side too. We lend to firms, not just ministries, and I need to know whether a family business hiring the owner's nephew is a smaller instance of the same vice as a minister doing it, or a different thing entirely that only looks similar.

Sort at least these cases with the desiderata in hand, or flag the ones the theory can't sort: seasonal gift exchanges between a supplier and a procurement officer where both cultures treat refusal as an insult; a mayor who takes no money and steers a contract to a longtime friend's firm, where the friend's bid turns out to be the best one; a legislator's adult child getting hired by a company with pending business before the legislature; a judge accepting an all-expenses-paid seminar trip from a bar association; unlimited independent spending on behalf of a candidate versus a capped direct donation to the same candidate; a village headman distributing state-subsidized fertilizer along kinship lines he is also expected to honor at home; a wedding gift of livestock that years later buys favorable testimony; a mining company that builds the town's only school and clinic; and a ritual feast where the point is to give away more than your rivals can. Some of these should come out corrupt, some not, and at least one should come out as a case where the theory has to say "this concept doesn't reach that far" rather than forcing a verdict.

Also tell me which distinctions the theory must not collapse — corrupt versus inefficient, corrupt versus cruel, corrupt person versus corrupt practice, corrupt office versus corrupt institution — and which single desideratum knocks out the most otherwise-attractive accounts. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and say which one has to give; that's the part I most want to see.

Format: lead with your bottom line in two or three sentences — can these be jointly met, and if not which requirement is the casualty — then the numbered desiderata with their motivating cases, then a short section on the conflicts and the unsortable residue. A small table is fine for the case sorting. American English please. Hard cap of 700 words; don't pad it with a literature review and don't hand me a bibliography or citations, I'll chase the sources down myself.
