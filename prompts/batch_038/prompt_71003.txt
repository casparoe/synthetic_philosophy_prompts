So I'm in my second year of a political theory PhD and I'm stuck on the seminar paper for a course on democratic legitimacy. We spent three weeks on the boundary problem — the thing where you can't democratically decide who's in the demos without already having a demos to do the deciding, and every principle you reach for to fix that (affected interests, subjection to coercion, whatever) either collapses into a global everything-demos or quietly assumes the boundary it was supposed to generate. My advisor's position is basically that it's unfixable in principle and the honest move is to just admit the founding of every democracy was an undemocratic act, so I've been trying to steelman the opposite: that the candidates can actually be compared and some of them do better than others, not just "all equally hopeless."

Here's what I've got on my list, described in my own sloppy terms rather than by whose name is attached to them, since that's not what I care about:

1. all-affected-interests: anyone whose interests are affected by a decision gets a say in it. objection I keep hearing: "affected" is impossible to specify without already having a moral theory of whose interests count, and it's infinitely expansive causally.
2. all-subjected: anyone subject to the coercive rules the polity makes gets a say. basically the same worry but sharper — since states coerce non-members constantly (borders, immigration enforcement, trade), does this just dissolve into a world demos?
3. federalist nesting: push each decision down to the smallest unit competent to make it, so the boundary question gets answered recursively instead of once. my worry: this just relocates the problem to "who draws the units?"
4. self-constitution / self-determination: the existing demos decides its own admission rules, majority vote, full stop, and we accept that this is a normative primitive rather than a derivation.
5. two-stage bootstrapping: first, everyone plausibly affected by the *question of the boundary itself* votes on who the voters should be; then that group votes on the actual issue. a friend of mine thinks this is clever, I think it just pushes the paradox up one floor and makes the first-stage electorate as contested as the second.
6. boundary by lot: give up on getting a *justified* constituency and draw it at random from a defined resident pool, on the grounds that a determinate arbitrary answer beats an infinite regress, and randomization at least can't be captured by the insiders who'd otherwise rig the line. I have not seen this argued for seriously anywhere, which might mean it's dumb or might mean it's underexplored.

What I actually want from you is a judgment call, not a literature review, so please don't hand me citations or a reading list — I can find those. Rank the six in tiers (say, three or four tiers) by how much you'd trust them to escape the regress without covertly presupposing the very boundary they're supposed to generate, one or two sentences on each saying why it lands where it does. Then give me a second, seperate ranking — a score out of 10 for each on implementability, meaning: if I were actually drafting franchise rules for a real new institution with a deadline and hostile incumbent members, how likely is this principle to survive contact with people who have an interest in drawing the line in their own favor. Finally, put a probability on each of the six being the *correct* answer to the boundary problem, summing to 1 across the whole list, and tell me which single one you'd bet on and what would have to be true for you to be wrong. Feel free to say if you think my advisor is right and 1 through 6 are all just differently-shaped ways of hiding the paradox — but then say which one hides it most honestly.
