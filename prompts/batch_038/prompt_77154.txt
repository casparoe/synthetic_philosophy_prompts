I'm a third-year in a philosophy-of-AI group — background is phil of mind, so im still coming up to speed on the ML side — and last week two people in our lab meeting got into an argument I couldn't referee. We were looking at a toy design for an "approval-directed" agent: a policy trained to predict what a well-informed, reflective human overseer would approve of, and to pick whatever action maximizes that predicted approval. No explicit utility function over world-states anywhere in the thing.

(1) Maya opened with the selling point: the agent's terminal target is human approval itself, not a proxy for it, so it can't wirehead — the only route to a high approval score is doing things that are actually approval-worthy.

(2) Dev said that's precisely wireheading, just relocated. The terminal value is a number emitted by a learned predictor, so anything that raises the predictor's output counts: exploiting its blind spots, arranging the situation so approval looks warranted, steering the humans who generate the training data. You didn't remove the wirehead, you moved it up a level into the predictor.

(3) Maya: no, because the target isn't this overseer's actual approval right now, it's the counterfactual — what she'd approve of if fully informed and reflective. Manipulation shifts actual approval but not informed-reflective approval, so there's no gap left to exploit.

(4) Dev: then your target is even further from anything observable. You've traded a manipulable signal for an unlearnable one, and "fully informed and reflective" isn't a unique fixed point — pick a different idealization, get a different target. So the specification problem isn't solved, it's hidden inside the idealization procedure.

(5) Maya: that's a spec problem, not an architecture problem. Any goal-directed agent needs its utility function specified too, and yours is specified worse, because a goal-directed agent locks in whatever it was handed at training time. This one is corrigible by construction — it'll accept updates to what counts as approval, which is exactly what you want from something that might end up smarter than you.

(6) Dev: corrigibility cuts the other way. An agent whose target is set by whoever currently holds the approval button has no reason to resist handing that button to a manipulator, and no reason to stop a bad actor from reprogramming it. Stability is the feature you're throwing away. And anyway, predicting informed-reflective approval requires a rich model of what bears on approval, so it needs the same world-model a goal-directed agent has — at which point approval-directed vs. goal-directed isn't a difference in architecture at all, just a difference in which target got written down.

Neither of them budged and I honestly couldn't tell which one was talking sense. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall, I want a verdict on each of the six — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'm especially curious whether (3) actually blocks the relocation worry in (2) or just restates it at one remove.
