Right, so I run a small reading group on the philosophy of time — mostly postgrads plus two retired women who are considerably more frightening than the postgrads — and we've just spent three weeks on Gödel's rotating universe. You know the one: the 1949 exact solution of Einstein's field equations where the whole cosmos is a homogeneous swirl of pressureless dust, balanced by a cosmological constant, no expansion at all, and where if you go far enough out and fast enough you can follow a curve that comes round and crosses your own past. What I want from you is something I can hand round at the end of term, because every bit of fiction I can find on this subject turns into a time-machine caper within two pages and my flatmate keeps saying the whole thing is just the block universe with extra steps.

So here's the ask. You're a philosopher of physics who also writes proper literary short fiction — think of the better parts of that overlap, someone who can get the geometry right without ever sounding like a textbook. I'd like a story of roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words set in a world whose spacetime is Gödel's. Before you write it, think properly and do a little brainstorming: sketch three or four possible framings in a couple of lines each (who's the protagonist, and which everyday practice collides with the geometry), say in two sentences which one you'd choose and why, and then go. Keep the brainstorm to under a page; I don't want an essay, I want the story.

A few things it has to get right, because this group will absolutely pull it apart:

1. No machine, no portal, no wizard, no paradox plot. Nobody is trying to change anything or prevent anything or close a loop for the sake of the twist. If the phrase "time travel" turns up at all, it should turn up once, as a shrug.
2. The world should feel like *that* spacetime specifically, not generically strange. No receding galaxies — the matter rotates, it doesn't expand. Homogeneous, so there's no axis and no centre anyone can point at; wherever you stand you are at the middle of the rotation. And no global "now", no way of stacking the universe into shared present moments laid across the flow of dust. Let the characters run into these as facts about navigation, almanacs, weather, inheritance, law — never as lecture.
3. One scene where a stubbornly temporal human institution — a registry of deaths, an outstanding debt, a promise, a pilgrimage, a court that has to work out whether someone is a witness to their own past — collides with the geometry and can't quite cope with it.
4. One real arguement between two characters, neither of them written as an idiot. One makes Gödel's own move: that if a world like this is so much as possible inside general relativity, then time in the sense of an objective lapse, of things genuinely coming into being one after another, is not a feature of reality but something more like an appearance — and that it itsn't an escape to say our universe isn't the rotating one, because the bare possibility already shows the theory hands us no such structure. Worth knowing: Gödel set that argument out as a numbered list of premises and freely admitted several of them were shaky, and I'd love the story to sit near the shaky ones rather than the tidy ones. The other character gets to resist with something worth resisting with — local distinguishability of past and future, the fact that a closed loop doesn't stop an event having happened to someone, the feeling of passage as data rather than noise, whatever you can make convincing.
5. No cameos from real people. No Einstein on his walk, no logician in a cardigan. No dream-reveal, no clock-striking-the-hour ending; end on an image. Dry, restrained, concrete, British spelling, sensory detail rather than interior monologue.

Then, afterwards, give me a short author's note — 250 words, plain prose, no hedging — saying which of Gödel's claims each main episode is meant to be carrying, and where you knowingly cheated. The obvious cheat is the physics of actually getting round such a curve: the distances and the speeds and the fuel are absurd, so if you've fudged that, just say you've fudged it. Be straight with me about the fudges, because the retired women will find them anyway.
