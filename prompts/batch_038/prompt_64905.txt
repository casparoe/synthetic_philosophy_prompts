You are to act as a specialist in early modern philosophy of mind, with a working knowledge of Descartes' Latin and of the scholastic background to his metaphysics of the self. I am preparing a seminar session on the question of what, precisely, the cogito is supposed to establish, and I want your help with a narrow exegetical problem rather than a general survey. Please work carefully and, before you commit to an interpretation, sketch in a few lines the two or three readings of the passages below that you consider live options and what textual evidence would discriminate between them.

Here is the first passage, from the Second Meditation (Adam–Tannery text, with a nineteenth-century English rendering):

"Sed est deceptor nescio quis, summe potens, summe callidus, qui de industriâ me semper fallit. Haud dubie igitur ego etiam sum, si me fallit; & fallat quantum potest, nunquam tamen efficiet, ut nihil sim quamdiu me aliquid esse cogitabo. Adeo ut, omnibus satis superque pensitatis, denique statuendum sit hoc pronuntiatum, Ego sum, ego existo, quoties a me profertur, vel mente concipitur, necessario esse verum."

"So that it must, in fine, be maintained, all things being maturely and carefully considered, that this proposition (pronunciatum) I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind."

And here is a later passage from the same Meditation, which I give only in Latin:

"Cogitare? Hîc invenio: cogitatio est; haec sola a me divelli nequit. Ego sum, ego existo; certum est. Quandiu autem? Nempe quandiu cogito; nam forte etiam fieri posset, si cessarem ab omni cogitatione, ut illico totus esse desinerem."

My questions concern the temporal qualifications in both places — the "each time it is uttered or conceived" of the first, and the "for as long as I think" of the second, where he allows that he might cease to exist the instant he ceased thinking.

First: what logical work is the temporal clause doing? Is the claim here a timeless truth about a persisting subject, a truth indexed to occasions of thinking, or something more radical — that all Descartes has secured is the occurrence of a present thought, with the "I" doing no more than grammatical work? Second: if the conclusion is indexed to the present moment, what entitles the meditator at the end of the Second Meditation to any claim about a self that was there yesterday, or that will be there tomorrow? Memory of past doubting seems the obvious candidate, but memory is precisely what the First Meditation discredited, and I want to know whether you think that objection is fatal here or whether it misreads the scope of the doubt. Third: how does this square with the claim, developed a little later in the Meditations, that the same power that brings me into being conserves me at each successive instant? Does that doctrine repair the gap, or does it merely relocate it?

End with a considered verdict: does the passage license a claim about a thinking substance, about a momentary subject, or about neither — and say what you take the temporal clause to be costing Descartes, if anything. Argue from the texts as given; I do not want a list of secondary sources or a bibliography.
