You're a philosopher of mind who's spent a lot of time around behavioral ecology — basically the person a department calls when the biologists and the philosophers start shouting at each other. Please think carefully and act as a harsh but fair referee.

Here's my situation. I'm in a reading group on minds-outside-skulls and I've been assigned to state and defend my position on whether an ant colony can literally be a mind — the Aunt Hillary thing from *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, colony as the subject and ants as its neurons. I've written my commitments down and I want a consistency audit before I present, because I have a feeling a couple of them can't all be true and I can't tell which.

My list:

1. I'm a functionalist: what makes a state a belief or a pain is the causal job it does, not what it's made of. Medium is irrelevant.
2. I accept the ant:colony :: neuron:brain analogy in roughly Hofstadter's spirit. The colony is the right grain for a subject.
3. But I'm not willing to demote the individual ants to mere neurons. Ants are little agents in their own right — they learn routes, recognize landmarks, and will turn against a bait after one bad experience. They have preferences and crude beliefs.
4. My positive criterion for when a bunch of coupled processes amount to a single mind is what I've been calling the coupling condition: the subunits have to be locked into one shared feedback loop they can't exit, with their interests aligned so that defecting simply isn't an available move.
5. That's why I deny minds to a national economy, the internet, and a traffic jam. No coupling, no exit barrier, nothing it's like to be a market.
6. Speed doesn't bother me. A colony takes hours to relocate and its memories live in pheromone trails and in which workers are standing where, but clock speed isn't the sort of thing a functionalist should care about.
7. There's no central executive in the colony, and I also deny there's one in my own head, so I don't count that as an objection.
8. Colony boundaries are vague — nests bud and split, and in the big unicolonial Argentine ant supercolonies hundreds of nests across a whole region just accept each others workers and share everything. I don't think vague boundaries stop something from being a mind.
9. What tips me over into "this is a mind and not a thermostat" is that a colony can be wrong: it can carry a false belief about where the food is and act on it.

Two cases I made up for the group, and I'd like you to chew on them too, because I suspect one of them breaks something above:

- **Split Nest.** A colony buds; both the parent nest and the daughter nest keep foraging, keep their route memories, and both avoid a toxic bait they'd learned about before the split. Which one is Aunt Hillary? Is this death, division, fission into two minds, or nothing at all?
- **The Trunk Line.** A commuter highway network where the "memory" of last month's roadworks lives entirely in the rerouting habits of individual drivers, all of whom can and do switch routes whenever they feel like it. I say obviously not a mind. But I need to be able to say why that differs from a colony whose memory lives in trails laid down in the dirt by ants who can also switch jobs.
