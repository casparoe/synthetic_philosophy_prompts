Got into a long argument with my brother last week about whether a hypocrite can ever blame you, and I ended up writing down every case I could think of with my gut verdict attached, because I couldn't tell whether I was being consistent or just making it up as I went. Here they are, then I want to know what principle actually generates them.

1. My dad smoked for thirty years and quit when I was ten. He tells me not to start. Standing: yes, completely — about the most legitimate warning a person can give.
2. A coworker who smokes a pack a day tells me not to smoke. Standing: no. It lands as a dodge and I'd basically write it off.
Those two are the pair that started the fight. Same content, same "your blamer did it too," opposite verdicts from me.
3. Another coworker who smoked for years and quit two months ago tells me not to. Standing: yes, but it's faintly obnoxious and I'd rather he not bring it up. So standing, but worse in some other way I can't name.
4. A judge who committed a similar offense decades ago and was never caught, sentencing a defendant for it. Verdict: the sentence can still be the right one and the defendant can still deserve it, but the judge should have recused himself.
5. A few thousand strangers online piling onto someone for a genuinely awful post. Verdict: what they're saying is true, almost none of them have standing, and the pile-on is bad anyway.
6. A bystander who watches a parent yelling at a kid in a parking lot. If he says "that was wrong of you" — standing, yes. If he says "you're a bad parent" — standing, no. I don't know why those come apart for me but they do.
7. My sister, chronically late, blaming me for being late. Standing: no, and I'm entitled to be annoyed about it.
8. My sister, chronically late, blaming me for being late — but this time because she knows exactly what it costs people and hates it in herself and wants better for me. Standing: yes. The only thing that changed between 7 and 8 is what's going on inside her head, which makes me suspect I'm not tracking hypocrisy at all.
9. Someone who did the thing, still thinks it was fine when he did it, and blames you for doing it. No standing, easy.
10. Someone who did the thing, still thinks it was wrong, hasn't managed to stop, and blames you for doing it. Also no standing, I think, but saying that feels meaner and less obviously right.
11. Me, blaming myself for X and blaming you for X while still doing X. Standing: obviously fine, no hesitation. So whatever rule knocks out 9 and 10 apparently doesn't apply to self-blame, and I dont know why.
12. A guy who grew up on the benefits of some rotten system, blaming the system and the people still running it. If he owns his part in it: standing. If he writes like an outside observer who was never touched by it: no standing.
13. Two friends who are both a mess about money, one telling the other to stop lending to his brother. Standing: yes, and it feels like the best kind of blame, almost like advice between equals.
14. Someone who did it, fully reformed, made amends to everybody affected twenty years ago, and now blames you for it. Standing: yes.
15. Someone who's never done it, never been tempted, basically a saint, blaming you. Standing: yes, technically — but the blame does nothing to me. It's cheap.

Three things I want out of you.

(a) Is there a principle, or a small set of competing ones, that generates most or all of these? State it precisely — I want actual clauses, not a vibe — and tell me which case is doing the work for each clause, because I suspect some of my cases are just decorating the principle I already wanted.

(b) Here's a distinction I keep reaching for and can't tell if it's real: having standing vs. the blame being true or deserved vs. the blame being well-expressed. Cases 4, 5 and 15 seem to pull those apart. Is there genuinely a three-way split, or am I smuggling in "I don't like this person's tone" and dressing it up as a normative category?

(c) Take the simplest principle that gets the most mileage, name the verdicts of mine it can't accommodate, and say which one I should give up. If 7 and 8 can't both survive, tell me which goes and why. Then run it on cases I didn't give verdicts for: a friend who ghosts everybody and blames you for ghosting, and sincerely doesn't realize she does it; a parent who lies to his kid constantly (we're going to the park, it's the dentist) and then comes down hard on the kid for lying; someone who blames you for a fault he caused in you; and someone who did the same thing you did, got caught and punished for it, and blames you for the version that went unpunished.

Write the whole thing in American English. And be straight with me about confidence — say how sure you are of the principle you land on, and single out the one claim in your answer you'd be most embarrassed to have gotten wrong.
