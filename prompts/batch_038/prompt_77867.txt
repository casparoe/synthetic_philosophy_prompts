I am drafting a short essay for a workshop on machine ethics and would like comments on it before I submit. The draft follows.

The Handover, Not the Decision

Most work on machine ethics asks how a system should decide: which normative theory to encode, how to represent outcomes, how to weigh them against one another. That framing assumes the decision is where the difficulty lives. I think the assumption is backwards. In nearly every serious deployment — clinical triage, lending, driving, content moderation — the machine does not decide alone. It decides in the presence of a person who could override it, who expects to be blamed if it goes wrong, and who has stakes the system does not represent. The right unit of analysis is therefore the handover: the moment at which responsibility passes, or silently fails to pass, between human and machine.

Shifting the unit of analysis relocates three standard problems rather than solving them, and I mean that as a claim about where our attention is misallocated. The alignment problem stops being a question of whether the system's values match ours and becomes a question of whether its behavior at the boundary is legible to whoever must live with it. The responsibility gap stops being a missing agent to blame and becomes a missing account: nobody can state, in terms the affected party could contest, why this outcome rather than that one. And the problem of moral patients is largely deferred: a system incapable of reasoning about who counts can still be built so that a human who can reason about it is never shut out at the moment it matters.

One concrete requirement follows, and I take it to be the most testable claim in the essay. Any automated decision that bears on a person should ship with a counterfactual handle: the smallest change to the inputs that would have changed the output, stated in the vocabulary of the person affected — if your income two years ago had been this, you would have qualified. That is what converts a decision from something merely explained into something contestable. My understanding is that handles of this kind are now routinely extracted even from opaque models, which would make the requirement a matter of deployment convention rather than open research.

The obvious objection is that this is not ethics at all but interface design, and that I have dissolved a normative question into a usability question. My answer is that the objection quietly assumes the normative content can be settled in advance and then handed over. If it could be settled in advance, we would not need the handover. Precisely because specification lags the environment it governs, the ethical work has to happen at the boundary, in real time, by whoever happens to be standing there.

I recognize the resulting picture looks deflationary: no theory of the good, no decision procedure, no account of moral status. Deflation is the point. A field that keeps trying to build the decision has been building the part that was never ours to automate.

Please give me your comments as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing summary. I most want to know whether the relocation in the second paragraph genuinely reframes those three problems or simply moves them somewhere I have stopped looking; whether a machine learning researcher would accept my claim about counterfactual handles as I have stated it, or whether I have overstated it and in which direction; and where the essay is least interesting, meaning where I am only restating something the field already says under a different name.
