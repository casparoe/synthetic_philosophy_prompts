I'm in the fourth year of a dissertation on the political economy of the later Mill, and I'm trying to pin down a claim in my third chapter that my supervisor thinks I'm over-reaching on. The claim is that the difficulties Mill presses against socialism in the posthumous and unfinished Chapters on Socialism are not specifically difficulties of *state* socialism, but difficulties of any scheme that gives up the wage sanction and the master's disciplinary power; if that is right, then his own preferred alternative — associations of labourers collectively owning their capital and electing and removing their own managers, sketched in the closing chapters of the Principles — is no escape from those difficulties at all, and the Autobiography does not rescue me, since his remark there that his and Harriet Taylor's ideal of improvement went well beyond democracy and earned them the socialist label states an aim rather than a mechanism. To test the claim I want a thought experiment built exactly to specification, partly for my own argument and partly because I help run a Thursday methods seminar on designing cases and would like to hand the students something with a checkable answer.

Here is the specification. One contemporary, realistic case, no longer than two paragraphs, no allegory and no science fiction, in which the members of a worker-owned firm face a live institutional choice, and in which Mill's two sets of considerations — his difficulties of socialism and his objections to the existing order of things — both bite on the same case and pull in opposite directions. Seed it as you see fit, but this is the setting I had in mind: a worker-owned precision-machining shop of roughly a hundred and fifty members in a post-industrial mid-sized city, bidding against two investor-owned firms and one other co-operative for the same OEM contracts, and now weighing merger into a wider federation against an offer from a municipal development authority to take the plant into public ownership with worker representation on the board. Whichever vehicle you choose for the collective alternative must be a genuine one — a public body with real directing power, not a strawman regulator — and the co-operative side must be described institutionally rather than rhetorically: how surplus is allocated among members, how managers are appointed and removed, what the assembly actually does when a member under-performs, and on what terms new entrants are admitted. Two further constraints matter. First, no external sabotage: no capital strike, no boycott, no discriminatory lending, no repression. If the co-operative fails it must fail from inside, for a reason Mill himself would have recognized — the difficulty of tying remuneration to exertion once dismissal has been surrendered, the tendency of founding members to harden into a new master class relative to apprentices and probationary entrants, the problem of accumulation and risk-bearing across generations of the workforce. Second, the public-ownership alternative must actually solve the coordination and incentive problem as described in the case, so that what it incurs is Mill's other cost and not an incentive cost: the whole industrial life of the town under one directing body, the disappearance of exit, and the flattening of variety and individual initiative he expected from a state that employs everybody.

Then show your work. After the scenario, tell me what Mill's late position says about each horn and why, in his own categories rather than in modern welfare-economics vocabulary; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work; and run two ablations — what happens to both verdicts if inter-firm competition is removed and the whole local industry is placed under one federated association, and what happens if effort becomes perfectly observable to the assembly. Finish with the strongest reply available to a defender of Mill's co-operative preference, including whether the real objection is that the case does not test socialism's difficulties at all but only the survival of competition inside a co-operative shell, which would mean it smuggles in the very feature it purports to isolate. Two outcomes would count as cheating: stipulating the verdicts in the description of the case, and dissolving the problem by insisting that a market-based co-operative sector is not socialism by definition. The seminar will immediately raise the large Basque federation of worker co-operatives as a counterexample, so I'd like one sentence on whether the case I've asked for is or is not structurally like it. No references or reading list, please — I have the texts — only the case and the demonstration that it does what I've asked.
