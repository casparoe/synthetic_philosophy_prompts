So I've been sitting in on a political theory seminar this semester — not for credit, I just needed something to do Tuesday nights — and two weeks ago we landed on epistocracy: restricted suffrage, Mill's plural voting, that Brennan book, the enfranchisement lottery thing. I mostly kept quiet because it isn't my field and half the room were grad students. But then it stopped being abstract for me. I'm on the board of a community land trust that's rewriting its bylaws this spring, and somebody put up a proposal where members who finish the weekend training on how the operating budget and the ground-lease math work would get two votes on financial questions instead of one. One vote on everything else. At the meeting, a guy who's been on the board for like fifteen years just said "one person, one vote, that's the end of it," and the room nodded, and we went to the next agenda item. Nobody actually argued about it. I voted with the room and I still can't say whether I should have.

The problem is I can't tell what he was claiming, because it's four words and I can already think of several different things it might be. Sometimes it seems to mean literally each human being gets handed one ballot and no more. Sometimes it seems to mean each ballot counts the same in the tally. Sometimes it's something bigger about nobody having more influence over decisions than anybody else — which would be embarrassing for him, since our city council is elected by district, so my vote does nothing in the next neighborhood over, and our land trust's own bylaws give the resident seats to a fixed number of people and require two-thirds to remove a treasurer, and he's never once objected to any of that. And sometimes it sounds like it isn't about arithmetic at all but about standing, like the claim is that weighting votes says some members matter less as persons. Those can't all be the same principle, and I suspect the reason his line worked is that people heard whichever one they already agreed with.

Can you lay out the readings as a menu for me? I want the distinct, statable claims that sentence could be expressing, ordered from the ones that are basically true by definition up to the ones that are actually radical or maybe false. For each, tell me what it rules out and what it leaves alone — does it forbid the two-votes-for-trained-members thing specifically, or would it also forbid reserved seats, supermajority requirements, proxy voting, district-based elections, or picking some decision-makers by lot? And which readings are consistent with things everybody on my board already accepts without blinking? Then the part I actually need: which reading would he have had to be invoking for his sentence to genuinely kill the proposal rather than just express distaste for it, and is that a reading anyone else in the room is committed to? I don't want a lit review or a list of who said what, and I don't need you to pick a side on whether the proposal is good — I need three or four sharp questions I can ask him on Thursday that make it clear the slogan was doing work it hadn't earned.
