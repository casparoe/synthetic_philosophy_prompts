I should say at the outset that I have no academic training of any kind. I work nights at a freight yard, and over about fifteen years I have built up a habit of reading and thinking on my own, badly and with nobody to correct me. What follows is a question I have not been able to settle for myself, and I have tried to state it as precisely as I can.

Twice in my life I have held something with total confidence and later come to regard it as nonsense: once about politics, when I was twenty-two, and once about my own marriage, when I was thirty-four. This has left me with the uncomfortable sense that I am at present confidently wrong about something, with no way of telling what. Reading around this, I came upon a principle credited to the philosopher Bas van Fraassen and generally called Reflection. My understanding of it, which you should correct if it is mistaken, is roughly this: if I am certain that at some later date my confidence in a claim will be seven-tenths, then it ought already to be seven-tenths now, because to think otherwise is to expect myself to change my mind without learning anything, which cannot be rational. I have also met it put more loosely, as the requirement that my present confidence must fall somewhere within the span of the confidences I expect I might later come to hold. I do not know whether these two versions agree, and part of what I want is your view on which of them a person in my position should actually be using.

My own rule of thumb, arrived at by myself, is this: defer to my future self when the change in my mind will come from finding things out; refuse to defer when it will come from something being done to me. I want you to test that rule rather than accept it.

Here are eight situations, all either live for me or near enough to live that I can judge them honestly. In each, I expect that at some specified later time I will hold a certain confidence, and I want to know how far that expectation should bind me now.

1. Whether a local official now widely admired will turn out to have been corrupt. In five years I expect to have read a great deal more of the record and to be far more confident that he was.
2. Whether the small hauling business I have put my savings into is sound. In two years I expect to be much more confident it was the right decision, having by then sunk still more money into it.
3. Whether raising a child is worth what it costs. In three years I expect to be a father and to be certain that it is; at present I doubt it.
4. Whether the indignation I now feel toward my employer is justified. I am about to begin a medication that I am confident will make me considerably less angry, and I expect that in a year I will disapprove of positions I now hold — because of the medication, and not because of anything I will have learned.
5. Whether my mother's account of what happened in our family in 1978 is accurate. In ten years she will be dead, and I expect that I will then believe her, chiefly because I will be unable to ask anyone else and will not wish to think badly of her.
6. Whether a book I am reading now will have mattered to me. In five years I expect to think it did not, but I also expect to have forgotten most of it by then.
7. Whether the religious community I left at thirty is right about salvation. Were I to go back among them, I am confident that within two years I would believe as they do; I attribute that wholly to belonging and not at all to argument.
8. Whether I will regret the decision I am about to make. At seventy I expect to be confident that I do not, though I expect that confidence to rest partly on having lived with the decision for twenty years.

What I would like, in this order:

First, a bare list of eight numbers between 0 and 1, one per case, giving the weight you think my expected future confidence should carry against my present confidence — 1 meaning I should adopt it now, 0 meaning I should discount it entirely. I want the numbers before any argument, so that I can see where you land without digging.

Second, the eight cases ranked from most binding to least, with a single sentence of justification each; ties are permitted and should be marked as such.

Third, a verdict on my rule of thumb. For which of the eight does it yield the same answer you did, and for which does it not? Where it fails, say what the better dividing line is.

Fourth — and this is the part I find hardest to put — there seems to be a difference between expecting that I will believe something and expecting that I will be right to believe it. Reflection, as I read it, speaks only of the first. Tell me whether that distinction carries the weight I want it to carry or whether it collapses, and if it collapses, what is left to separate case 1 from case 5.

Finally, if any of the eight is a case where the question has no determinate answer at all — where there is no such thing as the confidence I should expect to have — say so and explain why, rather than assigning it a number for the sake of neatness.

I cannot look anything up and would not be able to follow references if you gave them, so please answer entirely from what you already know, in plain language, and do not send me away with reading to do.
