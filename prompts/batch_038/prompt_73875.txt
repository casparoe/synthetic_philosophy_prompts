I convene a third-year module in the history of economic thought and I am drafting the two-hour end-of-term paper. One question on it, worth 20 marks and roughly 35 minutes of writing time, is to cover Hume's monetary essays in the *Political Discourses* of 1752, chiefly "Of Money" and "Of the Balance of Trade"; candidates have also read "Of Interest" and "Of Public Credit", and the question may let them bring either in but must not require it. My department's external moderator has not taught the module, so the marking scheme I hand over has to be usable by someone coming in cold. The obvious question — explain how Hume thinks the quantity of specie adjusts itself across trading nations — is no use to me, because the whole essay could be written from a seminar summary plus the label "price-specie-flow mechanism", and a colleague's suggestion that I set something on Hume's theory of taxes failed for the same reason.

What I want instead is a question that makes students adjudicate a tension I have been pressing all term without resolving. I have been describing Hume as running a two-tier theory of money. On the first tier money is domestic and purely instrumental: it is "none of the wheels of trade" but "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy", and for a kingdom considered by itself its plenty is "of no consequence". On the second tier money is international and substantive: "It is only the public which draws any advantage from the greater plenty of money; and that only in its wars and negociations with foreign states", since foreigners will take no paper, which Hume dismisses as a counterfeit money they will not accept in any payment and which any great disorder in the state reduces to nothing. What stitches the tiers together is his remark that "no bank could be more advantageous, than such a one as locked up all the money it received": shrink the circulating medium so that labour and manufactures stay cheap in foreign markets, and keep the hoard on hand for emergencies. One of my students objected that this two-tier reading is not an interpretation but a patch over a contradiction. If the levelling argument genuinely works — the thought experiments about annihilating four-fifths of Britain's money overnight and about multiplying it fivefold in a night — then any policy that deliberately holds the circulating medium below its natural proportion to labour and commodities should either be undone by the resulting inflow of specie or should produce a hoard that grows without limit; and if that growing hoard is exactly what Hume is after, money turns out to be of consequence to domestic policy too, and the first tier collapses into the second.

Please write the deliverable in three parts: the exam question itself (two or three sentences, answerable in the time allotted and phrased so that it cannot be met by narrating Hume's mechanism), a model answer of about 400 words, and a marking scheme totalling exactly 20 marks distributed over five must-hit points. Open the whole reply with a single sentence naming the crux you intend the question to test. Present the five marking points as a table with three columns — the point, the marks it carries, and the most common way candidates lose those marks — and close with one line stating what an A-level answer does that a B-level answer fails to do. Keep the entire reply, question and model answer and table included, under 950 words.
