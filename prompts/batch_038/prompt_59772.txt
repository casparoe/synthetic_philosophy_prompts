I've just been roped onto the committee of a small pro-European group here in Leeds — mostly people who spent 2016 to 2019 shouting into the wind and have now decided the sensible thing is to campaign for a customs deal plus getting back into Erasmus. I'm writing the lead piece for the newsletter and the subject is the democratic deficit, because that's the objection we get most at stalls. Our treasurer, who's retired and reads a great deal, has handed me what he considers the knockdown reply, and I can't decide wether it's brilliant or whether it's quietly doing all the work for him. His line: the EU is just the Bank of England writ large. We gave interest rates to an unelected committee in 1997 precisely so that politicians couldn't fiddle with them before an election, nobody calls that a scandal, and the Commission and the ECB are the same move at a bigger scale — technical bodies handed a narrow mandate so that elected governments can make commitments they couldn't otherwise be trusted to keep. He says Majone makes the point about the regulatory state and its non-majoritarian institutions, and that Moravcsik goes further and argues there's no democratic deficit to speak of at all because the mandates are narrow and everything's been delegated by accountable governments in the first place. The conclusion he wants me to print is that we should stop apologising: the honest answer is that the EU is a rules club, delegating rule-making is completely ordinary politics, and anyone demanding a referendum on it is applying a standard they'd never apply at home.

So can you audit the analogy properly for me? I'd like the correspondences laid out — Commission, Council, ECB against the MPC; the 2 per cent target against whatever the EU's equivalent target is supposed to be; the Chancellor's remit letter against the treaties; the open-letter mechanism and the fact that Parliament could in principle take the mandate back tomorrow against… something. Then where the mapping breaks, and I want you to be picky about which breaks actually matter for his use of it: is there anything at all in the EU that plays the role of a narrow, measurable, revisable remit, given the right of initiative and the way the competences have stretched decade by decade; what does reversibility look like when unwinding a regulation means unanimity plus Parliament rather than a sentence in a Budget speech; does the credible-commitment story stretch over the bits that plainly redistribute, the CAP, cohesion funding, social security coordination for people like my sister who works across Manchester and Dublin; and how much of the analogy depends on treating the ECJ as just another technical body when it looks much more like a constitutional court. I'd also like to know what the source case is smuggling in that the target simply lacks — a single identifiable principal, a success criterion you can actually measure, an exit ramp — and which of the disanalogies our treasurer can honestly just shrug off, because I'm sure some of them are cosmetic.

Last thing. If the central bank picture is weaker than he thinks, is there a better analogy I could put up at the AGM instead? The obvious candidates are the members' club, the treaty-as-contract picture, and federation-in-waiting, but my suspicion is that each of those licenses its own set of conclusions and I don't want to swap one loaded comparison for another, so tell me what each would commit us to if I used it. And be blunt with me: if the right answer is that his conclusion survives for some policy areas and collapses for others, say which are which and where you'd draw the line, because I'd rather publish something honest than something triumphant. Around nine hundred words is plenty, and assume I know the institutions well but have never read any of the political theory.
