I work in summability theory — divergent series, regular matrix methods, invariant means on bounded sequences, the structure of the set of Banach limits. My training is entirely analytic; I have no background in moral philosophy beyond what one absorbs incidentally, and I am not asking for an introduction to the field. I have been reading an argument in the ethics of infinite populations, and I would like you to restate it in the vocabulary I actually work in, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what did not survive the translation.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it, in loose terms. Please correct me wherever I have garbled it rather than translating my garbling.

Suppose a civilization persists forever and, at every stage of its history, contains people whose lives are worth living and people whose lives are not. Consider two policies that differ in how much welfare they produce at every finite stage, but whose infinite totals are both infinite in the same direction. A totalizing evaluation assigns the two outcomes the same value, cannot rank them, and appears to go silent precisely where a ranking is needed; the worry is that this silence generalizes, so that no policy can be shown better than any other. The repairs I have seen proposed are roughly these three:

(i) compare policies by summing welfare over larger and larger finite regions — successive stretches of time, or expanding volumes of space — and ranking one policy above another if it is ahead from some region onward and stays ahead;

(ii) refuse to demand that the comparison settle at all, and treat such pairs of outcomes as incomparable rather than equal;

(iii) restrict attention to policies that differ from one another only finitely often, so that beyond some point the comparison is decided by a finite sum.

First task: translate. Restate the whole set-up using partial sums and nets indexed by a directed set, regular summability methods, Cesàro and Abel means, invariant means and their non-uniqueness, and the distinction between absolute and conditional convergence. Be explicit about what the ethical objects correspond to. What is the index set, and is it canonical? What is a policy — a sequence, a function, a rule for generating a sequence? What does the total value of an outcome correspond to when the relevant series diverges? And what is the ethical analogue of the fact that one summation method must be selected rather than another, when the available methods disagree?

Second task, and the one I care about: the residue report. For each of the three repairs, say whether it is a move I can state precisely in my own terms, a redescription of something already in my toolbox under a different name, or something with no counterpart in summability theory at all. Then evaluate a conjecture of mine, which I suspect is too neat. I have been thinking that the entire difficulty reduces to conditional convergence together with the rearrangement theorem: if the welfare series converges only conditionally, then any re-indexing of the people can change the total, and what the ethics discussion is rediscovering is simply a fact about series. If that is right, the natural repair is to insist on summation functionals that are invariant under permutations with finite support but not under arbitrary bijections — which would let one treat moving finitely many people around as morally irrelevant while treating the rearrangement of infinitely many as morally loaded. Tell me whether this diagnosis captures the problem, whether it rests on a mischaracterization of what the divergent objects actually look like, and whether the proposed invariance requirement does any work that the standard shift-invariant functionals are not already doing.

A caution on method: do not import assumptions from my framework and present them as findings of the translation. If the ethical argument depends on something my vocabulary cannot express, say so plainly rather than papering over it with a definition that quietly changes the subject. I want a clear verdict on the following question — does the residue indicate that my framework is genuinely missing something, or that the ethical disagreement is less substantive than its practitioners suppose?

Close by telling me what would change your mind. Which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be right, would reverse your verdict on my conjecture? And where in your own reasoning above is it most likely to have failed — which step did you perform by assertion rather than by argument? Please write in American English.
