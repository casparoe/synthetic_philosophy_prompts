My colleague Derek is retiring after twenty-nine years — he taught philosophy of science at a mid-sized university, never famous, lots of undergrads, two books nobody outside the field has read. I've been asked to give the speech at his departmental party. The room: maybe thirty philosophers and a handful of scientists who used to co-teach with him, plus wives, husbands, adult children and one dean who thinks the humanities are a cost centre.

Here's the thing that makes this hard rather than just sweet. Derek's whole life was Lakatos, and specifically the question of whether a research programme is progressive or degenerating — whether it keeps predicting novel facts, or whether it's just grown a protective belt of auxiliary tweaks to dodge anomalies. He used to spring that question on his own colleagues at dinner, cheerfully, and they hated it. So I want the speech to turn it on us: to actually assess our department's last twenty-five years by Derek's own standard. Did we produce novel predictions, or did we mostly bolt on auxiliaries — new subfields, new vocabularies, new applications to whatever was in the news — each time the old programme hit an anomaly? I want that question treated seriously, not as a jokey nod, and I want it to come out with an actual verdict rather than a shrug.

Three constraints, and they're doing the structural work, so please don't sand them off:

1. The speech cannot conclude that it was all degenerating. That would be cruel and would also be false, and Derek would spot the falsity in a second. But it equally cannot just declare us progressive and move on — he'd know I was flattering him. I need the honest middle: name at least one stretch where we clearly were patching, say what the patch cost us, and then say what actually counted as novel, in the demanding Lakatosian sense rather than the "we published stuff" sense.

2. The Lakatosian machinery has to be explained so the spouses and the dean follow it, but without losing the philosophers. Roughly five minutes, and I'd rather not lean on jargon beyond about three terms of art — and if a term survives the cut, it has to earn its keep.

3. He died — sorry, he nearly died — of a heart thing two years ago and is a bit frail, so no jokes about time running out, and nothing maudlin. He's also an atheist and hates being eulogised while alive.

And the closer is the bit I actually need help with: I want it to end on the forward-looking half of the criterion — what would count as a novel prediction from us in the next ten years, and what would count as another auxiliary — so the room leaves with something to be answerable to rather than a warm feeling. That turn is what stops the whole thing being a lecture dressed up as affection, but it has to still read as affection.

British English, please.
