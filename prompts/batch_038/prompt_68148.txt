so a bit of context before the list. I'm not an academic philosopher — I work in health technology assessment, i.e. helping decide which treatments a public system will fund — but for the last couple years I've been in a reading group with some colleagues who are, and next month its my turn to put my own views on the table for everyone to attack. We're doing the numbers problem this term: Taurek, Scanlon on aggregation, the whole debate about whether complaints can be added up. I've tried to write down what I actually believe as cleanly as I can and I want it audited before I present it. Please take on the role of a working moral philosopher who has genuinely thought about the post-Scanlon literature on aggregation and reasonable rejection — not a summarizer of positions. I'd much rather be caught out by you now than in front of six people who've read the papers.

Here's the set, as honestly as I can state it:

(1) I'm a contractualist in Scanlon's sense: an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject. Justifiability to each person, one at a time, is the whole game.
(2) Complaints don't sum. There's no such thing as the total amount of suffering across a group, so a billion people's mild headaches can never outweigh one person's death, no matter how the arithmetic goes.
(3) Taurek is right about that part — no aggregate harms — but wrong that the numbers shouldn't count. In the five-drowning-versus-one-drowning case you save the five, and I don't think that means I've abandoned (2); comparing people pairwise, one against one, is enough to get you there.
(4) When the groups are genuinely tied, five versus five, I think a lottery is the right procedure and not a fudge.
(5) At work I hold something else: a public payer should allocate by cost per QALY and fund whatever buys the most QALYs overall. I've told myself this is compatible with (2) because an institution faces a different kind of question than an individual rescuer does, and because a QALY is a measure of how things go for a person rather than a sum across persons.
(6) I think the strength of someone's complaint is basically a function of how much worse things would go for them under one principle than another, and that these strengths can be compared across people, usually without much controversy.
(7) Part of why I like contractualism is that I take it to be broadly conservative: it should vindicate a lot of ordinary moral thinking and get revisionary only at the margins. I specifically don't want a theory that hands me the wild verdicts that total-utilitarian arithmetic hands me.

Now the ask. Go through these pairwise — or in whatever groupings make sense — and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do. I suspect at least one thing on this list that reads to me like a flat contradiction isn't one, and at least one thing I've been treating as obviously compatible isn't, so please discriminate rather than flagging everything as tension. Then rank the real ones by severity and, for each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs me concretely — if the fix is weakening (2), or giving up (5), or reinterpreting (6), tell me what I actually lose by doing it. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most damage at once. Think carefully and show the work: I'd like you to start with a short brainstorm pass listing which pairings are even candidates for tension before you commit to a ranking. And don't assume I'd rather protect the work commitments or the theoretical ones — I honestly don't know which I'd sacrifice, that's part of what I'm asking you.
