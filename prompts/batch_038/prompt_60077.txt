I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a dissertation on the structure of experiential theories of well-being. Below is the draft of one section of my second chapter — the section in which I attempt to answer the heterogeneity objection to hedonism. It is rough, and I suspect the closing paragraph half-concedes the argument. I would rather have candid criticism than encouragement.

**Draft of §3.2, "Two Heterogeneity Problems" (~1,000 words)**

Hedonism about well-being, as I am using the term, is the thesis that pleasure is the only thing that is non-instrumentally good for a person, and pain the only thing that is non-instrumentally bad for one. The heterogeneity objection begins from a list. The pleasure of a warm bath after being outside in the cold; the pleasure of the first bite of an apricot; the pleasure of watching an argument suddenly fall into place; the pleasure of taking off tight shoes; the pleasure of being laughed with rather than at; the low pleasure of the last mile of a long run. Ask what these share, phenomenally, and nothing comes to mind. Ask again and you may be tempted to say that they share only a name. If that is right, the objection runs, then hedonism's monism is a verbal achievement: "pleasure" picks out a disjunction, and to say that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good is to say that a motley of unrelated states is the sole intrinsic good — which is not a theory of anything.

I want to argue that the objection, as it is usually pressed, runs two distinct questions together. Call them the taxonomic question and the normative question. The taxonomic question asks what property, if any, all and only pleasures share in virtue of which they constitute a single kind of state. The normative question asks what it is about a state that makes its presence good for the person in it. Hedonism is an answer to the second question. The heterogeneity objection is pressed as though the second could not be answered monistically unless the first were answered by identifying a single essence — a felt ingredient, a hedonic tone, some common quality detectable from the inside. That inference is the weak link, and once it is cut the objection loses most of its force.

Consider a determinable such as being colored. Scarlet and cobalt share no specific phenomenal ingredient; there is no look of colorhood over and above the looks of particular colors, and anyone who went hunting for one would find nothing. Yet nobody concludes that color is a sham kind assembled from a convenient list. What unifies the determinates is not a common component but a common location: each occupies a place on a single dimension, a dimension along which instances can be compared, ordered, blended, and traded off against one another. My proposal is that pleasure is a determinable of that sort. Its determinates are wildly heterogeneous and share no isolable ingredient. What they share is a position in the economy of a single subject: each is a state that, from where the subject is, presents its own continuation as worth having. I will call this self-recommending character.

Two objections are immediate. First, that self-recommending character is simply what a desire is — that to be pleased is to want the feeling to continue — so the resulting view is a preference theory of well-being in hedonist clothing, and forfeits everything that made hedonism attractive, in particular its independence from the subject's conative dispositions and its immunity to problems of ignorance and adaptation. My reply is that self-recommendation is not an attitude taken up toward a state but a manner in which a state shows up. In the case I keep returning to — the cold plunge, whose first ten seconds the subject endures with her teeth clenched and whose second phase is not wanted, endorsed, or even noticed, but simply arrives as worth continuing — the difference between the phases is not a difference in what she is for or against. She is for nothing. The difference is in the states. Second, and much more serious: if "presents its own continuation as worth having" already contains an evaluation, the account cannot explain why pleasure is good, because it has built the goodness into the explanans. I have a gesture at a reply — the phrase is meant to describe the phenomenology of the state, much as a sour taste is called sharp without any evaluation of sharpness — but I do not at present believe the gesture, and this is the first place I want pressure applied.

Whether the proposal survives turns on two cases.

Case A. A person has been working intently for an hour in a warm sweater. There is, I take it, a background bodily pleasure throughout that hour; she is not attending to it, has no attitude toward it, and would be surprised to be told about it; yet when she takes the sweater off she registers the loss at once and non-inferentially. If self-recommending character requires attention, Case A contains no pleasure — a heavy cost, since much of what makes a life go well may consist in unremarkable states that were never the object of anyone's attention. If it does not require attention, then "presents" must be read dispositionally: the state presents its continuation as worth having if and only if the subject would take it to be so were she to attend. The account has then become counterfactual, and therefore hostage to every familiar difficulty about what a subject would report under attention — fatigue, distraction, misleading belief, and the way that attending alters the state attended to. I do not see how to hold both ends here.

Case B. A bereaved person has, in the second year, a particular pain — a tightness behind the sternum that reliably arrives on the anniversary — which she would fight to keep, and whose disappearance she would experience as a further loss. She wants its continuation, and it is not a pleasure. So no bare appeal to what the subject wants, at whatever level, can mark the difference between Case A's sweater and Case B's tightness. Something has to be located in the character of the states themselves, which is exactly what the heterogeneity objection said could not be located.

What follows is uncomfortable for the position I am defending. If Case A pushes the account toward dispositions and Case B forbids it to rest on what the subject wants, then self-recommending character cannot be individuated introspectively, and the hedonist's best option is to individuate her kind functionally: pleasures are those states that recruit attention and behavior toward their own prolongation, and whose absence is registered as a loss not mediated by any belief about the loss of something else. That may well be true. But a kind individuated in this way no longer guarantees the intuition that does the real motivating work for hedonism — that a life cannot go well for someone in a way that never registers with her, that well-being is tethered to how a life feels from inside it. I am therefore inclined to conclude that the heterogeneity objection is not answered by the determinable move but relocated. The question stops being what pleasures have in common and becomes whether there is a single dimension of registration along which a life can go better or worse for the person living it. The second question is harder than the first, but unlike the first it is at least well posed, and it can be pursued without pretending that an inventory of felt qualities will settle it.

**What I would like from you**

Please comment on the following five points specifically, under these headings, in roughly 700–900 words total.

1. *The taxonomic/normative distinction.* Does it carry the weight I place on it, or have I merely relabeled the problem? In particular: if a monist answer to the normative question is compatible with pluralism about the taxonomy, what prevents a rival from defining her own determinable — a self-disrecommending character shared by all and only pains — and thereby generating a mirror-image monism? Is there any asymmetry between my move and hers, or does the distinction show that the monism was never substantive to begin with?

2. *The circularity worry in paragraph four.* Is the "sharp" analogy salvageable — can a term simultaneously describe the character of a state and contain an evaluation — or does the account covertly derive its normative conclusion from its own definiens? If it fails, what is the least damaging repair that does not amount to abandoning hedonism for a preference theory?

3. *Cases A and B.* Which is more damaging, and is there any version of self-recommending character that survives both? I am especially interested in whether a modification on which attention is enabling rather than constitutive — the state is always self-recommending, and attention merely makes it available for report — escapes the dispositional problems I list, or only conceals them.

4. *Paragraph six.* Is "the objection is relocated rather than answered" a legitimate outcome for a chapter whose stated purpose is to defend an experiential theory of well-being, or does it concede enough that the remaining chapters have nothing to stand on? Separately: would the hedonist be better off conceding outright that pleasure is a disjunctive kind and arguing that disjunctive kinds can still do normative work?

5. *Exposition.* The draft never states the target thesis in a single sentence at the outset, and I suspect a reader would want one. Tell me where the section first becomes difficult to follow; where "pleasure," "enjoyment," and "pleasantness" are doing distinguishable work while appearing interchangeable; and whether Cases A and B should be introduced before the determinable proposal rather than after it.
