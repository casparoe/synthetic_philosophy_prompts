My partner and I have been having the same argument, in slightly different clothes, for about two years: I do logistics for a regional food bank and we give away maybe 15 percent of what comes into the household, and she thinks that's just what decency costs now, while I keep insisting that going past some line is supposed to count as *extra* — praiseworthy, not owed. **Two ways an act can be beyond the call**

Supererogation is almost always explained by cost. What makes it permissible-but-not-required to walk past a rescue is that rescuing would cost me too much; saints and heroes are just people who pay a price morality doesn't demand of the rest of us. On that picture the boundary between duty and supererogation is a price line, and it moves when the prices move.

But there's a second, independent reason an act can be beyond the call: the act is mine to allocate. In a lot of ordinary cases morality doesn't rank the options at all — it hands the choice over. Which good project I take up, whose birthday I fly to, which stranger's overdue debt I quietly cover. Nothing about the situation picks one out as required, not because the alternatives are cheap for me but because they're equally good, or good in ways that don't compare. Call the first kind sacrifice-supererogation and the second jurisdiction-supererogation.

They come apart in both directions. Forgiveness is the standard example of something praiseworthy and not owed, and it usually costs the forgiver close to nothing — sometimes only the giving up of a grievance they'd be better off without. That's jurisdiction, not sacrifice. Conversely, plenty of extremely expensive acts aren't supererogatory at all: if I'm the only one who can reach the kid in the shallow water and it ruins a pair of shoes, the expense is beside the point.

If the two are separable, the payoff is that only jurisdiction gives you a stable limit on how demanding morality can be. Sacrifice-supererogation is indexed to what people will actually bear, so it slides: as helping gets cheaper for me — better coordination, standing donation orders, a charity that's genuinely effective — acts that were saintly a generation ago become merely decent now. That's a ratchet, and it's bad news for anyone who wants supererogation to be a brake on moral demandingness, because the brake is made out of the same material as the road. Jurisdiction doesn't slide, because it isn't about price. It's about there being no fact of the matter which good option morality prefers.

The cost is that jurisdiction-supererogation is a much smaller category than it first looks. If morality does rank options most of the time — even weakly, even with tie-breaks — then very little is really mine to allocate, and the supererogatory shrinks toward forgiveness, gratitude, and a handful of gestures. I think that's a price worth paying but I'm not sure.
