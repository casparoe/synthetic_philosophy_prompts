I am a high school senior doing an independent study that sits awkwardly between economics and philosophy, and for my end-of-term presentation I have taken on Herbert Simon's notion of satisficing: the claim that an agent who cannot realistically enumerate and rank every alternative may rationally accept the first option that clears an aspiration level rather than holding out for the best one. My test case is a small one from my own school. Last spring our student council raised roughly fourteen hundred dollars and spent two meetings arguing about where to send it; one member insisted that we were obliged to identify the single most effective recipient we could, and my objection was that the hours we spent comparing organizations were hours we were not spending on the fundraiser that would produce next year's money, and that at some point a merely very good destination becomes the right answer. My older brother, who is a second-year economics student, says that objection is confused, and over winter break he walked me through an argument to that effect which I have been unable to answer. He thinks it shows that satisficing is either redundant or absurd, and I am certain the second half of that is wrong, but I cannot locate the step where the reasoning breaks. Here is my reconstruction of it:

(1) To satisfice is to accept the first available option that meets a threshold of acceptability rather than continuing to search for a better one.
(2) The threshold is not supplied by the situation; the agent has to set it, and setting it is something the agent could do better or worse.
(3) If the threshold were itself set by satisficing — by accepting the first level that seems high enough — then that setting would require a threshold for thresholds, and so on without end; therefore the threshold cannot be set by satisficing.
(4) The only way to fix it without a regress is to choose the level whose expected payoff, net of the costs of searching and deliberating further, is greatest.
(5) But choosing the alternative with the greatest expected payoff once all costs are counted is precisely what maximizing prescribes.
(6) So a consistent satisficer is a maximizer who has remembered to include the costs of search in the ledger: satisficing is not a rival account of rational choice, only maximization applied to a more honest description of the choice problem.
(7) And if one denies (6) and maintains that the threshold may be set by something other than a comparison of expected payoffs, then nothing determines where it goes, since for any option, however poor, there is some threshold that option meets; hence every option is permissible.
(8) A principle that renders every option permissible does not guide choice and is therefore not a principle of rational choice.
(9) Consequently satisficing is either maximizing in disguise or it is empty.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to hold on to (1) and to (8), because I do not want to end up saying that any choice whatsoever is rational, and I would like the answer to explain how a satisficer can keep both without conceding (7). I am also unsure whether (3) is entitled to the demand it makes — that whatever principle governs a choice must also govern the parameters of that choice, on pain of regress — and whether (5) is using one notion of maximization or quietly trading between two, since (4) asks a bounded agent to compute an expected net payoff over possible thresholds, which looks like just the sort of computation bounded agents supposedly cannot perform. Finally, tell me whether the following worry of mine has any force or whether I am merely relocating the problem: if (3) really does rule out setting a threshold by anything other than optimization, then it seems to bear equally on the maximizer, who must also decide how much information to gather before choosing, and I cannot see why that decision escapes the regress any better than the satisficer's threshold does. If it does not escape, then (9) has shown too much, and I would like to know what survives once both accounts are in the same predicament.
