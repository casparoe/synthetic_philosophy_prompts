I'm teaching a graduate seminar on chance and credence this spring and I've decided that what my students actually need around week six isn't another paper — it's a sorting instrument. Right now I have two students who both announce that they're "basically with Lewis" and who then proceed to disagree about everything. One of them thinks the Principal Principle is a theorem: you get it for free if you conditionalize on the chance hypothesis and start from the right kind of ur-prior, so there's nothing extra to swallow. The other thinks chance is just a name for a stable pattern in the Humean mosaic and that deference is a further norm you have to accept on faith or on accuracy grounds. They can't tell me what they'd say about the same case, because they've never been asked. And the whole room has memorized the phrase "admissible evidence" without any feel for what it rules out or why anybody would need such a proviso in the first place. So I want a decision tree that sorts people by what they say about cases, not by whose name they like.

Here's what I need. Roughly nine to eleven nodes, each one a concrete situation the student can answer without knowing which branch they're walking down — no node should ask "do you think the Principal Principle is a coherence constraint?" Point blank. Each node gets labeled with the claim that separates its branches, and each leaf gets a name if one genuinely exists in the literature, or the word "unnamed" if it doesn't; do not invent paper titles or coin labels and present them as standard, that's the one thing that would make the handout useless to me. Things I want the tree to actually turn on: whether the principle constrains your initial credences or your later ones; whether you need to relativize to a complete chance theory rather than to your total evidence; what happens in an undermining world, where the true chance theory assigns a miniscule chance to the history that actually happened; what you do when you're uncertain about the chance itself rather than knowing it; whether a violation is flat-out incoherent or merely imprudent or not criticizable at all; and whether a sober conditionalizing Bayesian who treats every chance claim as just another hypothesis about the world owes us anything extra. Please use a case of my own devising somewhere near the root, because I want to see whether the tree handles it: call it the Jinxed Coin. One coin, a million flips, all heads, and the true chance theory of that world says the chance of heads on each flip is 0.5 and also says the chance of the million-heads history is about as close to zero as makes no difference. The student has the complete history in front of them. What's their credence for flip one million and one, and what has gone wrong if the answer isn't 0.5? If you have room, I'd also like one node on a chance hypothesis that makes claims about the agent's own credences — that one always breaks the tree in interesting ways.

Format: lead with a section called "How to use it" (about 150 words, what the handout is for and what it deliberately doesn't measure), then "The nodes" (each one maybe 70 words: the case, the two or three answers, and the splitting claim), then "Paths" (a compact mapping of answer patterns onto leaves — a table is fine here), then "The leaves" (each leaf: name or "unnamed", one sentence of doctrine, one sentence on its most awkward commitment), then "Empty cells" (the combinations your questions make available that nobody occupies, each with a diagnosis of whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered), then "Pairs this tree can't seperate" (be honest — say which two leaves your nodes fail to tell apart, and either name the further question that would tell them apart or concede that it can't be done with cases alone), and finally "What should surprise my two arguing students" (about 150 words: the place where the theorem-person and the axiom-person turn out to be standing on the same node, or where they come apart in a way neither of them expects). No bibliography, no references, no quotations from anybody's paper — I'll add my own citations and I want your own paraphrase of every position. Two thousand to twenty-five hundred words total is about right.
