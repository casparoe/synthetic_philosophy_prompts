I'm a staffer on a national revenue committee whose record on taxing wealth across generations now runs as follows. We raised the estate-tax exemption to roughly five million while refusing to repeal the tax outright. We closed the step-up in basis at death, but permitted deferral for a surviving spouse and for a closely held business until the business is actually sold. We rejected an annual net-worth tax that would have counted family homes. We enacted a generation-skipping transfer tax so that families cannot simply leap over a tier of heirs. We rejected a bill that would have taxed large bequests to charitable foundations at the same rate as bequests to kin. And we capped the permissible duration of dynasty trusts at a hundred years rather than abolishing such trusts altogether.

On the table now is a proposal to repeal the estate tax and replace it with a levy on recipients: each person's gifts and inheritances would be cumulated over their lifetime across all donors, with a per-recipient exemption and progressive rates, revenue going to the general fund.

Predict our verdict, and identify which precedent is doing the most work. My worry is that our record is jointly consistent with at least three principles — something about taxing events rather than holdings, something about the total unearned advantage any one person may accumulate, and something about administrability and liquidity — and that at least two of them point in opposite directions on this proposal. Tell me what each principle predicts about the new bill, give your best guess with reasons for preferring it, and name the further proposal that would most cleanly separate the principles you have in mind.
