Three hours last night, most of it after midnight, arguing with a friend who is a statistician and does not read philosophy, and I came away unable to reconstruct my own position. I am giving a job talk in eleven days and I would rather be ambushed by you now than ambushed in the room.

You are to act as a decision theorist who has both built and refereed the representation-theorem literature on choice under uncertainty, and who has sat through a great many hostile question periods and knows how they actually go. Think carefully before writing, and start by reasoning through where my view is structurally exposed rather than drafting replies first.

**The thesis.** I defend a version of risk-weighted expected utility: preferences are represented by taking the utility of each outcome and weighting it by a function of the cumulative probability of doing at least that well, so that the shape of the weighting function — not the shape of the utility function — carries the agent's attitude to risk. My two distinctive claims are these. First, the weighting function is not a preference parameter to be elicited from behavior but a norm about how much decisional weight an agent owes each branch of a gamble, given that she will live through exactly one of them and not the others; that is what makes the weighting non-arbitrary rather than merely another taste. Second, this norm applies to a choice as the agent faces it, under the description under which she faces it, and not to whole sequences of choices solved in advance as a global plan. From the second claim I argue that sequential-choice money pumps do not show the view incoherent, only that it refuses to rank plans the way the pump assumes; and from the first I argue that risk-weighting, not a bounded utility function, is the right way to refuse Pascalian bets on tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs.

**The audience.** An economics department colloquium, forty-five minutes plus questions. The room is mostly mathematical economists who treat the Anscombe–Aumann framework as the substance and see departures from it as either reducible or unmotivated; two behavioral economists who run experiments on ambiguity and who already use rank-dependent utility with probability weighting, and will therefore ask what is new; a senior theorist who has written against non-additive representations and who will be polite and fatal; a philosopher of probability who thinks probability is only ever about states and will press on whether my "branches" are states; and four or five graduate students affiliated with an effective-altruism center who want to know why I am not maximizing expected value over extinction risk. Nobody in the room works on risk-weighted expected utility directly, and at least two will have met it only through critiques of it.

**Where I am currently bleeding.** My friend pressed two things. She said that over any fixed finite menu of gambles my weighting function is doing no work that a sufficiently bent utility function — or a state-dependent one — could not do, so I have changed the notation and not the subject. And she sketched a three-step sequential choice in which, at each step, I would accept a small payment to alter the gamble, and end up strictly worse off than if I had never started, and said that settles the matter. I defended the first point and folded on the second somewhere around one in the morning. I need to know whether folding was correct.
