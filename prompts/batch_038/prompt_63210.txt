In a history-of-logic seminar this term the sentence "a white horse is not a horse" has been doing a great deal of unearned work, and I have been asked to come to the next meeting with a written account of what it can and cannot be claiming. I would like your help in producing that account.

The sentence is the thesis defended in the best-known dialogue in the small corpus attributed to Gongsun Long, the figure most closely associated with what later doxography grouped together as the School of Names. In our session it was invoked in opposite directions. One student said that it anticipates the distinction between intension and extension — that the two general terms differ in sense even though every white horse is a horse — and therefore proves that these thinkers possessed a genuine logic. Another said that it is either a triviality in fancy dress or an outright falsehood, and therefore proves that what they produced was eristic word-play with no logic in it at all. Our instructor's remark was that the two of them were arguing past each other, and that the substantive question is threefold: which of the available readings is non-trivially true, which of them the dialogue's own arguments actually license, and which of them survive the objections the dialogue's interlocutor raises.

So that your answer need not depend on recalling the text, here are its main moves as best I can reconstruct them from a translation. The first argument separates the offices of the two names: one names the form or shape, the other names the colour, and what names the colour is not what names the form. The second is an argument from requests: if one asks for a horse, a yellow or a black horse will serve; if one asks for a white horse, neither will; if a white horse simply were a horse the two requests would be the same request and the same animals would satisfy both, but they do not. The interlocutor objects that since there are white horses one cannot say that there are no horses, so there are horses after all; the reply trades on a distinction between considering a horse with the white separated off and considering the white horse as horse combined with white. A further argument observes that horses do have colour, hence there are white horses, whereas if horses had no colour there would be simply horses and no basis for singling out the white ones; relatedly, the term for horse is said to involve no exclusion by colour, while the term for white horse does exclude yellow and black horses by colour.

What I want from you is a menu rather than a choice. Please do the following.

1. Set out the distinct precise theses that the single sentence could be expressing. I would expect the list to include at least a semantic reading about the two names rather than the things named; a reading on which the negation denies class membership, so that some white horse fails to be a horse; a reading on which it denies class identity, so that the class of white horses is not the class of horses; a reading on which it denies intensional identity; a mereological reading on which the compound expression denotes horse combined with white, and a compound is not identical with either of its components; and a realist reading on which whiteness and horseness are distinct entities whose combination yields something over and above either. Add any further readings you think the arguments above or the wider corpus support — including one on which the claim is about the standards of correct naming, that is, about whether a name applies exactly to its corresponding actuality and not merely to something that falls under it.

2. For each reading, give a verdict: trivially true, non-trivially true, false, or indeterminate, with a sentence or two of justification. Where the reading turns on the logic of the sentence rather than on the translation, show the formalization. It seems to me that the sentence taken literally quantifies over white horses and denies of each that it is a horse, which is simply false, while the argument from requests establishes at most that the two terms are not coextensional or not interchangeable; I would like that suspicion either confirmed or corrected rather than ignored.

3. Say which of the readings each of the four arguments actually supports, and whether any of them can carry more than the non-identity of two classes or two senses.

4. Say which readings are compatible with the reply to the interlocutor's objection, and whether the separated-off/combined-with move itself commits the dialogue to one of the readings listed in (1) — in particular, whether it makes sense only on the assumption that the nouns in the text behave more like mass terms than like count terms denoting classes, as one influential line of interpretation has claimed.

5. Two side questions, briefly. Elsewhere in the received corpus there is an argument that hardness and whiteness in a stone cannot be presented to the senses together and are therefore in some sense distinct from one another; does accepting that view enlarge or restrict the readings available for the white horse sentence? And does it matter that the dialogue is written as a disputation with a live opponent, so that the thesis may have been maintained for its performance value rather than asserted as doctrine?

6. Close with a short section saying which reading each of the two students in my seminar was relying on, whether the charge of triviality and the charge of falsehood each land on any reading in your list, and whether there is a reading on which the thesis is both interesting and true — and if so, exactly what has to be given up in order to obtain it.

A numbered list for items 1 and 2, with each reading stated as a single precise claim, then short sections for the rest. Please do not spend space on dating, attribution, or the secondary literature, and do not give me references; I want the analysis, not the scholarship. I would rather have more readings cleanly distinguished than fewer ones developed at length.
