I am in my final year of high school, and I am writing a paper for our school's research symposium on whether generations — Boomers, Millennials, my own Gen Z — pick out real kinds, or whether they are journalism with a birth year stapled to it. What I have read so far consists mostly of people pointing at one case and declaring victory, so before I commit to a thesis I want the specification rather than the arguments: what an account of generationhood has to be able to do in order to count as adequate.

Adequacy is relative to a purpose, so here is mine. My paper has to defend treating "belongs to generation G" as an explanatory variable in a survey I am running at my school this spring — a few hundred students, their parents, and their grandparents, one questionnaire, administered over three weeks — and it has to survive questioning from my faculty advisor, who trained as a demographer and does not believe generations exist.

I am not going to tell you what the requirements are, because that is what I am asking you for. Here is the raw material I keep tripping over, in case it shows what an account has to handle.

1. The boundaries actually in use are precise — the postwar boom begins in 1946 and ends in 1964 — yet nothing at all happens to a person between being born on the thirty-first of December and being born on the first of January.

2. My grandmother, born in 1943, insists she is a Boomer because she was there for the sixties. My grandfather, born in 1948, insists he is not one because he was too young for Vietnam. They are using different rules, and I have no way to say which rule is the right one.

3. A classmate born in Seoul in 1998 who moved here at fourteen: is she a Millennial, a member of some Korean cohort, both, or neither? And if the answer is that the account simply covers the United States, is that an account of generations or an account of American magazine categories?

4. The label for the cohort after mine was in general circulation before its oldest members could vote, so something was named before there was anything to name it about.

5. When I regressed attitudes on age, year of survey, and birth year, my software dropped a column and warned me about perfect collinearity, since the third is just the difference of the first two. My own study is worse: one school, one spring, so birth year and age are the same variable in different clothes. Anything I call a generation effect might be an age effect, and I do not know what would tell them apart.

6. Telling people which generation they belong to seems to change how they answer, at least anecdotally, which suggests the category partly makes the thing it classifies.

Write the list of desiderata an account of generations has to meet, with the consideration behind each. Mark which are non-negotiable and which I suspect are merely the habits of one camp — natural-kind talk imported into sociology, or a demand for sharp boundaries that no social category anywhere satisfies — and tell me which single requirement disqualifies the most candidates. Then give me your verdict on a criterion I invented and cannot settle: an account is adequate only if knowing a person's birth year improves your prediction of their attitudes beyond what you get from knowing their age and the year you asked them. Should that be a requirement, or is it a test that no account could pass for reasons having nothing to do with whether generations are real?

Please keep the whole response under 800 words, and do not send me a reading list or citations — I cannot chase them from school, and I would rather have five requirements argued carefully than twelve merely named.
