I have been awarded five years of funding for a small research program in the philosophy of mathematics, with a remit explicitly confined to the ontological status of mathematical objects — the platonism/nominalism debate, broadly construed. The group is one senior researcher (myself), two postdocs, and a PhD student on rotation. Two of us are trained in first- and second-order logic and some model theory; none of us has physics beyond what one absorbs from reading the philosophy-of-science literature; and one postdoc can formalize substantial pieces of mathematics in a proof assistant, which is a resource the program did not previously have. I need to commit to a work plan before the end of term, and I want to defend a prioritization rather than commission a survey.

Here is the shortlist I have drawn up. Please treat it as the object of the exercise:

1. Whether Benacerraf's epistemological objection retains any force once causal theories of knowledge are abandoned. Is there a version of the access problem that survives for a reliabilist or for anyone who does not require a causal connection between knower and known, or has the objection quietly dissolved along with the semantics that generated it?

2. Whether the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument depends on confirmational holism in a way that is fatal to it. Relatedly: whether the responses that accept the mathematical sentences of our best theories while disowning commitment to their apparent referents — the weaseling strategy, and the pretense or make-believe accounts — genuinely defeat the argument or merely relocate the problem to a new question about the status of the pretense.

3. The scope of nominalization. The program was demonstrated on a limited body of physics; the live question is whether it can be pushed into the mathematics current physics actually uses, including the infinite idealizations invoked in accounts of phase transitions and the functional-analytic apparatus of quantum theory, or whether there is a principled reason to expect it to fail. A sub-question I care about: by what standard should viability be judged — the physics nominalism can handle, or the physics it cannot?

4. Whether the explanatory route to platonism is genuinely distinct from the Quinean route. Cases in which a mathematical posit is said to do explanatory work in an empirical phenomenon — the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas being the standard one — are usually presented as a sharper form of the indispensability argument. Is it sharper, or merely the same contested best-explanation judgment with a more vivid illustration? And what would make such an argument decisive rather than another case in which the two sides restate their disagreement?

5. Whether third-way positions have already dissolved the dispute. Do structuralist accounts, in either their realist or their eliminative and modal variants, together with deflationary approaches to ontological commitment, leave a live question about whether mathematical objects exist, or do they show that the debate was never substantive?

6. Whether formalization bears on the question at all. If a theorem is machine-checked by reduction to statements about inductive types or about sets in some foundation, has the ontological commitment been discharged, merely relocated into the metatheory, or left entirely untouched? Is there any argument running from proof-assistant practice to an ontology, and if not, why has the intuition that there might be persisted?

What I want from you is a defended ordering of these six, not a summary of the literature on each. Specifically:

- Give me an explicit dependency map. Which of these questions are upstream of which, in the sense that the answer to one constrains what can sensibly be said about the others? I expect at least one of the six to be a crux that most of the rest hangs on, and I want to know which and why.
- Assess tractability against the actual resources described above. A question that requires reconstructing quantum field theory in nominalist terms is not one this group can settle, and saying so is a legitimate part of the answer.
- Say what turns on each. For every question, would resolving it change what anyone else does — in metaphysics, in philosophy of science, or in the methodology of mathematics — or would the two camps simply absorb the result and continue?
- Be candid about which of the six I should drop outright as overrated, where the field's sense of urgency is not matched by downstream consequences. If a question is malformed, or rests on a distinction that will not survive scrutiny, tell me that rather than ranking it.
- Tell me whether there is a seventh question I have omitted that would displace one of these.
- Address the formalization resource directly. My suspicion is that it is either a powerful new lever on the commitment question or an expensive distraction that will consume a postdoc's three years and produce nothing that bears on the debate. I want you to commit to one of those assessments and argue for it, not hedge.
- Finally, state what would change your ranking: what results, arguments, or developments over the next five years would move an item up or down, and in which direction.

Two constraints on your answer. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I know the literature and cannot check references. And do not pad the response with a history of the debate; assume I am already fluent in it and spend the space on the ranking and its justification.
