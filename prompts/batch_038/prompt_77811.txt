I'm giving the toast at my doctoral advisor's retirement dinner — a statistical decision theorist, about eighty guests: his wife and daughters, four decades of students who mostly left academia, the dean, and perhaps six people who have actually read his work. He has one verbal tic the whole department knows: offered any two options that looked equivalent — restaurants, job candidates, sealed offers — he would say, "I'll take the other envelope." He said it about his own life, most audibly in 1991, when he declined a considerably larger salary elsewhere, and people have been quietly scoring him on that decision ever since.

Write a toast of roughly four minutes built out of the two-envelope exchange argument, run over his career: from inside the envelope you're holding, the other contains either twice as much or half as much, and the arithmetic says switch. The turn must be the actual resolution — that the calculation silently requires a prior over the space of lives one might have lived, and no coherent such prior exists, so the question everyone has been keeping score on was never well-posed. I want that landing as affection and as a defense of him, not as a shrug and not as a mystifying "we can never know."

Two things are off-limits: never name the paradox, and never use the words "expected value," "prior," or "probability" — the seventy-four guests who don't have that vocabulary still have to follow the argument, while the six who do should get one joke to themselves. Equally, don't assert he made the right choice, and don't imply the choice was weightless; that's the whole reason the structure has to be what it is.

Let "the other envelope" recur at least three times, with its sense shifting by the last appearance. He is in the room, he despises sentimentality, and he will heckle, so nothing in it may be hearable as "you wasted your potential." End on the toast itself.
