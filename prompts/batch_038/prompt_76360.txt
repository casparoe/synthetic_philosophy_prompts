Two members of my reading group argued about the Dutch book argument for probabilism after working through the standard case, and they asked me to referee. Neither will give ground.

(1) Nadia set the case up: an agent will buy or sell a one-dollar bet on A at 60 cents, and likewise on B, where A and B cannot both obtain — but he will only trade a one-dollar bet on A-or-B at 30 cents. A bookie who buys the A-or-B bet from him for 30 cents and sells him the A bet and the B bet for 60 cents each leaves him 90 cents poorer no matter what happens. So incoherent credences are a genuine defect, not a matter of taste.

(2) Owen said the conclusion outruns the premise: the book closes only because the agent accepts three separate bets. His own agent keeps exactly the same prices but has a standing policy of entering at most one bet per day. No package can be assembled against him, yet his credences are just as incoherent — so incoherence and exploitability come apart, and the argument cannot be doing the normative work Nadia wants from it.

(3) Nadia replied that the policy is no refuge but a further error: it makes him forgo bets he himself considers favorable, and anyway the bookie can simply write the three bets up as a single compound wager and take the 90 cents in one transaction.

(4) Owen answered that the compound wager only has a price for his agent if he values it by adding up the values of its parts, and that is precisely the additivity in dispute. If he doesn't, no single bet he would accept guarantees a loss, so the link between incoherence and sure loss rests on an assumption the argument was supposed to establish.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify that rating — including checking Nadia's arithmetic in (1) and her claim in (3) that the three bets can be collapsed into one. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are overlooking about what a sure-loss argument can and cannot show.
