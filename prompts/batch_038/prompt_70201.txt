I'm a third-year in epistemology and I'm supposed to be turning a chapter of my quals into something about testimony, which is why this is bugging me. Last week our informal reading group (nominally a social epistemology group, actually a bar argument that meets weekly) had been poking around Hume and Reid on why we believe what other people tell us, and then someone brought up what happened to Dev, a cohort-mate of ours. Dev had flown somewhere for a conference and, the night before his return leg, saw a comment from an anonymous user on a travel forum saying that connecting back through that particular hub required a transit document he didnt have. Dev believed it instantly, rebooked through a different hub at some expense, and the commenter turned out to be completely right — the airline confirmed the rule afterwards. Two people in the group, Ines and Marcus, then got into a real fight about whether Dev was epistemically in the clear, and it went way past Dev. Roughly, here's how it went:

- Ines: the default attitude toward a bare assertion from a stranger has to be withholding. Not disbelief exactly, but no positive credence until you've got a reason — and the reason doesn't have to be a case-by-case investigation, a general inductive track record for that type of source is enough. Dev had neither: he didn't check the airline's own page or call anyone until after he'd already paid to rebook. Believe-first-check-never is how people end up in things they can't get out of.
- Marcus: that standard can't be met and you know it. Almost everything Dev believes about the world before roughly 1900, about other countries, about his own early childhood, comes to him from other people's say-so with no independent check available. If the default is withholding, Dev is unjustified about nearly all of it. So either testimony gets default acceptance, or we're all massively unjustified — and the second horn is a reductio of the view, not a consequence of it.
- Ines: keep the default for actual telling if you like — someone in front of you, whom you can question, who has a reputation on the line and can be held to what they said. An anonymous forum post isn't that. There's no speaker to be accountable to, no shared context to read, and you can't ask a follow-up. It's a different animal, so it gets a different default.
- Marcus: you're relabeling the problem. Where does your evidence come from that anonymous internet content is unreliable? From studies, journalists, other people's reports, other people's war stories. You can't audit that base rate yourself any more than Dev could audit the transit rule. If testimony is allowed to supply your reasons for distrusting testimony, then it can supply your reasons for trusting it too, and your split between "sources I accept by default" and "sources I need reasons for" has no non-question-begging ground.
- Ines: too fast. I don't need independence from all testimony, just independence from this claimant and this claim — and I've got it: I've personally been sent down a hole by confident anonymous posts, several times, and that's my own experience, not anybody's report. Also, in Dev's case the checks were cheap and he knew they were cheap, and he skipped them. And the fact that the commenter happened to be right is beside the point for justification, otherwise every lucky guess counts as knowledge.
- Marcus: if your reasons are your own scar tissue from past posts, then you're not a reductionist, you're someone with a prior built from experience, and Dev could have had exactly the same prior pointing the other way. And on the cheap-checks line: availability of a check doesn't create a duty to run it, because a check is available in principle for almost anything, so your rule turns into "verify everything," which is the skeptic's picture again. Finally, I don't think the outcome is irrelevant — part of what makes any source credible at all is a track record, and track records are just made out of outcomes.

They both asked me to referee and I punted. Can you go through those six moves in order and say for each one whether it lands, half-lands, or fails, with a short reason — I want a verdict on each individual step, not a summary of the dialectic, and please don't just tell me both sides have a point. Then say who's better off overall and, separately, what both of them are leaving out: I keep having the feeling there's a third position on testimony that neither of them actually named, and that a couple of their moves would look different if they'd had it in view.
