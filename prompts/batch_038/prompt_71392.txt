So, weird ask, but here's the situation. I recently moved onto a team building an allocation system — matching and queueing across a bunch of competing stakeholders, think hospital-ish but for logistics — and I got handed the job of writing the "why our objective function is defensible" memo for an external review. I built the memo around Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. The pitch was: accept the Pareto conditions, accept expected-utility rationality for individuals and for the social chooser, and linear aggregation of utilities falls out, so maximizing total welfare isn't a substantive moral bet we're making, it's arithmetic that our critics have to accept too. Our reviewer is a philosopher and I'm told she's coming straight at this, and honestly I've started to suspect I overclaimed pretty badly.

I want a map of the exposure. What would have to be true, or what would I have to find out, for that claim to be wrong? Concretely:

- Which assumption is actually carrying the weight? I keep being told the linear-representation result doesn't settle interpersonal comparability at all, because each person's utility function is only pinned down up to their own positive affine transformation — so "sum them" presupposes a choice of units and zero points that the theorem never makes. How deep does that go? If you grant fixed scales, is what's left still interesting, or has all the content migrated into the assumption and nothing is in the conclusion?
- Does the theorem even get me *equal* weights? My reading now is that the standard formulations deliver a weighted sum with some fixed but otherwise arbitrary weights, and that equal weights need an anonymity or symmetry condition bolted on separately. If that's right, is anonymity the place where all the moral work is hiding, and would a defender admit that?
- What happens when you swap the Pareto condition? I gather there's a difference between the ex ante version and a statewise/ex post one, that the strong version tacitly needs everybody to agree on the probabilities, and that weakening it opens up options combining anonymity with social risk aversion that the strong version rules out. Which variant do the interesting results actually use, and does my memo survive the weaker one?
- Is the whole framing hostage to treating society as a single agent facing a single lottery? Someone on the team muttered something about aggregate risk aversion not being the same thing as social risk aversion, and about the fact that one objective distribution lands very differently on different people depending on who ends up in which state. Genuine defeater or just a slogan?
- And is Harsanyi's own supplementary move — the impartial observer / extended sympathy business, where he tries to manufacture the comparability out of a hypothetical choice behind a veil — the actual target here? If so, what would falsify *that*, and would a defender just concede it was never part of the theorem?
- Finally: any case where the theorem is claimed to be false rather than merely toothless? Results on aggregating beliefs when people disagree about probabilities, or constructions where unanimous Pareto and the vNM conditions all hold but the aggregation isn't linear?

Rank these for me, from "the memo is simply wrong" down to "awkward, needs a footnote." And for each one, tell me whether a defender of the strong reading would concede it as counting against them or wave it off as a misreading of what the theorem was ever supposed to do. I also want the straight verdict on whether this position has been qualified into immunity — if the only safe version is "given comparability and equal weights, linearity is unique," is there anything left worth putting in a design document at all, or should I go find a different justification?

One thing I'd personally find most convincing: if there's a concrete allocation example where everybody's own vNM preferences and a Pareto condition are satisfied but the linear-sum answer is clearly the wrong thing to actually implement, give me that example. I'd much rather have a case than another theorem number.
