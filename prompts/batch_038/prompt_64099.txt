I am teaching assistant for an intermediate epistemology course this term, and for the section on the structure of justification I put together a short argument to hand out as a puzzle: something the students could work through in pairs before I told them how the literature sorts itself out. The trouble is that the more I look at it, the more it seems to prove far more than I intended, and I am now not sure I can defend it in front of a room. It is meant to force a choice among foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism, but as written it looks like it closes all three exits at once and lands on a global skepticism I do not believe and would not want to teach. I want to know where it actually breaks.

Here it is. (1) If a belief of yours is justified, then there is some consideration you could offer, if asked, as your reason for holding it. (2) A consideration can serve as your reason for a belief only if it is itself something you are justified in accepting. (3) So if any belief of yours is justified, then some acceptance of yours is justified, and that acceptance depends for its justification on a further justified acceptance. (4) The dependence relation in (3) is transitive, and nothing depends for its justification on itself. (5) Hence the chain issuing from any justified belief is either finite and terminating, finite and looping, or infinite. (6) Looping is ruled out by (4): a loop makes a belief depend on itself. (7) Terminating is ruled out by (3): the last member would be a justified acceptance depending on no further justified acceptance, and (3) says there is no such thing. (8) The infinite option fails too, since being able to offer a reason is a matter of having it, so an infinite chain requires a believer with infinitely many justified acceptances at her disposal, and no finite creature has that; this is the usual finite-minds objection, and I do not see how infinitism escapes it. (9) Therefore no belief of yours is justified.

I should say what I am and am not attached to. I find (4) and (5) very hard to give up, because they look like nothing more than the logic of a strict ordering plus a trichotomy, and I suspect that if I dropped them I would have to explain what a regress even is. I am vaguest about (2) and (8), but I cannot articulate why; my instinct is only that each of them is doing more work than its phrasing suggests. Two things worry me beyond the conclusion itself. First, (9) condemns beliefs I would never doubt, including the beliefs I form while reading the argument, and it seems to condemn the argument's own premises, so that if it is sound I am not justified in accepting it; I do not know whether that self-undermining is a reductio or merely a bullet to be bitten. Second, the same machinery appears to show that nothing is ever justifiably believed on someone else's say-so, which strikes me as an overreach independent of the skepticism.

So: tell me which step you would cut, or, if you think the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is cheapest to surrender, tell me that instead. Say why the step you reject looked acceptable to me when I wrote it, and what the position you are left with has to pay for the rejection, including what happens to foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism respectively on each of the candidate cuts I have flagged. If more than one repair is available, say which one you would choose and why the others are worse. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, and no tables; you may refer to the steps by their numbers, but do not set the discussion out as a list or a grid.
