I'm trying to settle an argument with a colleague about the sunk cost fallacy. He thinks the textbook line is basically airtight: the money's gone, only forward-looking costs and benefits count, and anyone who lets past expenditure move them is making an error. I keep thinking there's a decent number of real cases where the past spending genuinely changes what you should do now, so honoring it isn't a fallacy at all. My rough rule, which is the thing I want you to stress-test:

a sunk cost is rationally relevant only if the money or time already spent has changed the set of options you actually face now — made some option cheaper than it would be for anyone else, made some option politically impossible, or produced an asset you only hold because you went down that road. Otherwise, ignore it.

Here's seven cases. For each, give a number 0–10 for how defensible the decision is as *rational* — not as understandable, not as forgivable, but as the right call given the reasoning the person is actually doing — where 10 means honoring the past expenditure is straightforwardly correct and 0 means it's the fallacy in pure form. Then rank the seven most to least defensible. Then do a second pass and say for each whether your score and my rule agree or disagree, and where they disagree, which of us is wrong.

1. Tunnel. A city has spent $2B on a tunnel-boring machine and the approach works for a river crossing. The machine jams and can't be freed without spending another $1.4B. The alternative is to abandon it and build the crossing cut-and-cover for $500M — so abandoning is $900M cheaper looking forward. But the council knows that publicly writing off $2B turns the crossing into a scandal and the voters kill the project outright, and having no crossing costs the city roughly $3B over thirty years in congestion and freight delay. They vote to spend the $1.4B.

2. Drug program. A pharma company has put $800M into a compound that failed Phase II for its original indication. Winding down costs $5M. There's a second, unrelated indication where the same compound looks plausible: running that program costs $120M against an expected return of about $260M, and it only pencils out at $120M because they already hold the tox, PK and manufacturing data — a fresh entrant would face $340M and would never start. The board keeps the team intact and funds the second indication. From outside it looks exactly like "we can't let $800M go to waste."

3. Guarantee. A founder has personally guaranteed $1.2M of company debt and the company is failing. Winding up now triggers the guarantee immediately. She keeps it alive five more months on $40k of her own savings, because there's a signed letter of intent from an acquirer that either closes inside five months and clears the debt, or doesn't. Her co-founder says she just can't admit failure.

4. Violinist. Twelve thousand hours and a conservatory degree, and she is not going to have a solo career. She still practises four hours a day, which costs her about $40/hour in freelance work she turns down. She enjoys it, but by her own account not by enough to justify that. Asked why she keeps going, she says stopping would make the twelve thousand hours count for nothing and she can't live with that.

5. Climber. Four hours up, two hundred metres below the summit, turnaround time reached. Descent takes the same time whether he turns now or comes down off the top. He goes for the summit. His reasoning: he knows himself well enough to know that if he turns around today he'll be back next season for another $9,000 and another three weeks off work, and the combined cost and risk of two attempts is worse than the risk-adjusted cost of finishing this one.

6. Department chair. Four years of political capital spent fighting to save a small program that now has three majors in it. He keeps fighting. Asked why, he says that if he stops he'll have to tell the faculty he burned four years of their goodwill for nothing, and that admission costs him the standing he needs for the other fights he can actually win.

7. Grandson. A grandfather has put $30k into his grandson's restaurant over two years. It loses money every month, the location is bad, and the grandson has no plan. He sends another $5k, saying that if he stops now the $30k was for nothing.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — which case is the most defensible, which is the least, and whether my rule survives contact with them — and only then give the scores, the ranking and the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate any of the above back at me. Don't name-check papers or economists either; I want your own judgement, not a literature review.
