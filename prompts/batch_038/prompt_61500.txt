I'm a software engineer, roughly ten years in, currently working on optimization and ML infrastructure. I have never taken a philosophy course. I am fluent in my own vocabulary — I use it daily — and fluent in none of the philosophical one, so where I state Leibniz's position below I am working from summaries. If I have misstated it, correct me first and then proceed; please don't quietly translate a strawman I handed you.

Here is what I take the claim to be. Among all the worlds God could have actualized, one is best. A being of unlimited power, unlimited knowledge, and perfect goodness would actualize that one. Therefore this world is the best of all possible worlds, and therefore the evil in it is not evidence against such a being. I want that argument restated, as faithfully as you can manage, in the terms I actually work in: search spaces and the feasible sets carved out of them by constraints, objective functions, global versus local optima, supremum versus attained maximum, surrogate objectives, and the difference between a quantity you can evaluate and one you can only bound.

Give me the mapping component by component: possible worlds as points in a space; compossibility — or whatever Leibniz's real constraint on which worlds are available turns out to be — as what carves out the feasible set; divine understanding as evaluation of the objective across the entire space; divine will as the selection rule; and evil as what, exactly? A term that is negative at a point but contributes to the global maximum? One component of a vector objective trading off against another? Something my vocabulary has no slot for? Be precise about where the analogy is doing work and where it is decoration.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want:

1. Which of Leibniz's claims translate cleanly, with no loss?
2. Which translate only under distortion — where does either his argument or my framework have to be bent to make them meet, and which one is bending?
3. Which have no expression in my framework at all?

There are four places I expect the translation to grind. Push on them rather than smoothing them over.

**Attainment.** In my world, "there is an optimum" is not free. You need a closed and bounded feasible set, or a coercive objective, or you get a supremum that nothing attains. My understanding is that the standard objection to Leibniz runs: for any world a better one can be conceived, as for any number a greater. If that lands, God faces an open feasible set with no maximizer, and a principle saying a rational chooser must choose the best has no output to return. Does Leibniz have anything to say about attainment? Does casting the issue this way make his reply look stronger than it is, weaker, or just differently located?

**Aggregation.** An objective function presupposes that goods and evils are commensurable and collapse to a scalar. If the value of a world is vector-valued, I get a Pareto front rather than a unique best, and selecting from the front requires an additional preference ordering the framework does not supply. Is Leibniz committed to scalar aggregation? If so, is that a premise he argues for or one he assumes in passing? And is the residue here a defect in my vocabulary — perhaps the goodness of a whole world is genuinely not scalarizable, so my framework simply cannot represent the claim — or does it expose an unsupported step in the argument?

**Evaluability from inside.** I cannot evaluate an objective over a space I am embedded in and can sample only locally. Leibniz's God can. Is his thesis unfalsifiable in my sense of the term, and if it is, is that fatal, or does the argument live somewhere that falsifiability is the wrong test? I want neither the cheap "unfalsifiable, therefore meaningless" move nor a defense reflex; tell me what its honest epistemic status is.

**The Lisbon case.** When Voltaire turned the 1755 earthquake against the optimists, which of three objections was he making: that the actual point is not the optimum, that the objective function is wrong, or that the whole search-space framing is misconceived? These have very different consequences, and if he is making more than one, separate them for me. Then say whether Leibniz and Voltaire were optimizing the same function at all. If they were not, that strikes me as the most interesting thing in the episode, and I would rather you say so plainly than referee a disagreement that is not actually taking place.

Finally, a verdict, because I don't want the fence-sitting version. Is the residue on my side or his? Does the failure of parts of this argument to translate show that optimization talk is too impoverished to hold a claim about the goodness of an entire world, or does it show the claim was never as determinate as its confident grammar suggests? Pick a side and argue for it. If you conclude the residue splits, tell me which specific claims fall on which side and why.
