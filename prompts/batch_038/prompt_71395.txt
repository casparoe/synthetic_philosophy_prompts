I'm drafting a section of a paper on standing to blame and I need a thought experiment built to a fairly awkward spec. The usual cases here are all varieties of the hypocrite — the manager who lectures about lateness while strolling in at ten, the parent screaming about screen time with a phone in her hand — and those are too easy, because every account of lost standing can explain them. I dont want a closet hypocrite either: no secret vice, no self-deception, no hidden double standard. I want a blamer who loses standing without ever having done anything wrong and without anyone being in a position to know it.

Specs:

- One or two paragraphs for the scenario itself. Realistic setting — a workplace, a school, a professional body, a neighbourhood panel, your pick. No sci-fi, no telepathy, no character who can peer into possible worlds.
- The blamer holds a role whose job partly consists in blaming: a chair, a referee, an ombud, a head of department. I want the case to make it live whether occupying an office gives you standing that your own dispositions would otherwise deny you.
- He has never once violated the norm he's invoking, and in everything that has actually happened he's treated like cases exactly alike. Verifiably clean, with a paper trail to prove it.
- But there's a counterfactual situation he simply has never been in — something like the wrongdoer turning out to be his brother rather than a stranger — in which he would have excused the identical conduct, warmly and without strain. The disposition is real; it's just never been tested, so there's no evidence of it for anyone, him included.

Then run the check. State the norm, the conduct, and the two parties precisely, and say what each position predicts: the plain hypocrisy diagnosis, the view that blame is a kind of moral demand and standing is about being entitled to make it, the view that what matters is how you'd treat people across merely possible situations rather than how you have, and a sceptic who thinks all this standing talk is character assessment in a costume. Which one gets the intuition Im describing, which has to bite a bullet, and be honest if one of them dissolves the case rather than answering it.

Then tell me which single feature is doing the work — the untestedness, the special relationship, or the fact that the norm invoked is one of impartiality — and do the removals: actualize the counterfactual and say whether the case has collapsed back into ordinary hypocrisy; swap the brother for a colleague he merely likes; swap the norm for one where partiality is permitted. If any removal makes the intuition evaporate, say so, since that would suggest standing is tracking the norm rather than the blamer.

Finish with the best attack a defender of the actual-conduct view could make: is the case smuggling in knowledge, moral luck, or something else? I need that objection in hand before I put this in front of a room.
