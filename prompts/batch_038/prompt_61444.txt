I co-organize an informal monthly gathering that sits awkwardly between a philosophy reading group and a physics department social: roughly a dozen people, half of them graduate students in cosmology, half in philosophy of science, all of them tired. For the December issue of our self-published zine I have committed to contributing something on Boltzmann brains, and I have decided the right form is satire rather than exposition, because this group has already heard the standard account twice and the second time was worse. Write me a mock welcome packet — around 750 to 850 words, deadpan throughout, laid out like the orientation binder a university hands a new arrival on their first day: numbered sections with headings, one boxed sidebar, one short form the reader is asked to sign and return, and a brief FAQ at the end. The conceit is that the reader has just come into existence as a thermal fluctuation in an empty, exponentially expanding late-time vacuum, furnished with a complete set of memories of a life they never lived, and that the packet is issued by whatever administrative body is responsible for greeting them. Approach this as a working physicist-philosopher who also writes comedy: the jokes must come from getting the underlying reasoning right, not from absurdism for its own sake, and the piece should still be followable by someone who has heard the phrase and nothing else — legibility matters more than insider payoff.

Three things have to hold for it to work on this audience. First, the physics and the epistemology must be accurate in their bones: memories that are false yet internally coherent, an apparent past complete with an apparent entropy gradient, the numerical dominance of freak observers over ordinary ones in the spacetimes where the argument bites, the dependence of the whole edifice on typicality assumptions and on a choice of measure, and the fact that this was originally pressed as an embarrassment for a cosmological model rather than as a discovery about any of us. Second, the satire has to land the self-undermining structure without ever naming it: the packet cheerfully instructs the reader to discount everything they seem to remember and everything they seem to have inferred — including, implicitly, the inference that produced the packet — and every reassurance it offers should be one its own intended reader has no standing to accept. Keep the voice flat and helpful and let the reader do the recoiling. Third, it should also quietly mock the professional practice around the argument: the way a reductio gets recycled into a research program, complete with measures to be repaired, working groups, and a constituency for entities nobody thinks exist. Two specific bits I want in there, if they fit: the signable form should have the reader attest that their memories are probably fabricated, with a pre-ticked checkbox and a note on what happens if they decline; and the FAQ should end with a question to the effect of 'but why should I believe any of this?' whose answer is simultaneously correct and completely empty. Do not cite real papers, quote real physicists, or name real people living or dead — if you need an authority, a journal, or a working-group title, invent one. No winking asides, no exclamation marks, no sentence that tells the reader the situation is ironic.

Before you write the packet, spend four or five lines sketching the structure: which single real feature of the debate you are exaggerating, what the running gag of the document is, where it pays off, and which question you are holding back for the FAQ. Then write the piece. After it, add a short note of no more than a hundred words, in your own voice rather than the comedy one, telling me where you took liberties — which of the packet's premises are genuinely contested rather than settled, where the internal logic strains or where you had to fudge something to keep the joke clean, and above all what would change your mind about the self-undermining objection. Say plainly which consideration would lead you to think that a theory predicting the typical observer is a fluctuation is thereby refuted, versus merely rendered untrustworthy, versus not in trouble at all; and flag the point in your own reasoning where you think you are most likely to be wrong.
