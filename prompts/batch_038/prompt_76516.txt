I have been asked to run a single ninety-minute session on the philosophical problems raised by mind uploading for an audience of palliative-care physicians and hospital chaplains: adults with no philosophy beyond the undergraduate level, no appetite for science fiction, and a professional interest only in the question of when a person has ceased to be there. I will have a whiteboard and no distributed readings. What I want from you is not the session itself but a plan for it, argued rather than merely listed.

Set out the order in which the component ideas must go in, and the dependency relations that fix that order — in particular, I assume that something like functional isomorphism (the notion that a prosthetic component might reproduce exactly the causal profile of the thing it replaces, in and out, without reproducing its substance) has to be secured before gradual replacement is even intelligible, and I want to know whether you agree. Tell me where the identity material belongs relative to the survival material: I suspect these come apart in ways my audience will not expect, and I suspect that teaching them in the wrong order is what produces the standard deadlock.

Identify the single case that should carry the session, and say what features it must have and what features would spoil it. I have been toying with running the whole thing as a rehabilitation technology — a stroke patient whose damaged neurons are replaced one at a time, over months, by prostheses that replicate their input-output profiles exactly — on the grounds that it keeps the discussion inside their clinical world and avoids the beam-me-up framing. Assess whether that framing genuinely earns its keep or merely buys plausibility at the cost of a later distortion, and if it distorts, tell me which detail to alter.

Map the misconceptions, separating the ones they will arrive with from the ones the session will manufacture if I am not careful. On the first side I expect that a copy is a copy and the original therefore dies; that anything simulated is by definition unreal; and that the argument can be closed by asserting that consciousness requires biology. On the second side, my real worry is that once they grant the gradual case they will leap to the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about whether the patient survived, which is not where I want them, and worse, that they will carry that scepticism back into the ward and start treating determinations of death as arbitrary. For each, give me the point in the sequence at which it must be met and the specific move that meets it — not a general exhortation to be clear.

There is a familiar line of argument to the effect that a subject undergoing gradual replacement would notice nothing amiss from the inside, that any story on which his experience silently degrades or vanishes would have him systematically wrong about his own present experience, and that this places the burden on the person who says the lights go out. I intend to use a version of it, and I want your judgment on whether it belongs before or after the identity material, given that this audience has no machinery for telling an epistemic claim from a metaphysical one and will slide between them.

Give me one question I can put near the end whose answers would distinguish genuine uptake from courteous agreement — something whose wrong answers are diagnostic rather than merely incorrect. Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and give me the form of words for the refusal. Please do not simply amputate the metaphysics to fit the clock: distinguish what can be postponed without damage from what cannot, since the chaplains in the room will notice the difference immediately and will read the omission as a position.
