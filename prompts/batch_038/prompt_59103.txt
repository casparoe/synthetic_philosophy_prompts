I have been collecting proposed resolutions of the two-envelope problem — from papers, textbook exercises, and blog posts — and I can no longer distinguish a genuine resolution from a restatement of the difficulty. Before I evaluate another candidate, I want the standard of adequacy written out in full. So: what must a proposed resolution of the two-envelope problem deliver in order to count as having solved it?

Fix the purpose as follows, since adequacy is relative to it. A resolution is adequate for my purposes only if it equips a competent reader who is handed a concrete instance of the problem — one with a specified or specifiable procedure for filling the envelopes — to determine what the agent should do, to identify precisely which step of the tempting argument fails or rests on a false premise, and to say why that step continues to look compelling after the failure has been identified.

For each desideratum, give three things: the requirement; the case or consideration that motivates it, spelled out rather than labeled; and its status — whether it binds on any resolution whatsoever, or whether it encodes a substantive methodological commitment of one camp. I expect several of the following to be contested, and I want them flagged as contested rather than assumed: that the ordinary expected-value calculus must emerge unamended; that the diagnosis must locate a formally invalid inference rather than a false probabilistic premise; that distributions without finite expectation, or improper priors, may be ruled inadmissible by stipulation; that a resolution owes an explanation of the illusion and not merely a blocking of it; that the resolution must generalize to structurally similar puzzles. Please also keep apart two things that are routinely run together — requirements on the diagnosis of the fallacious argument, and requirements on the positive normative account of what the agent ought to do. A proposal can satisfy the first and fail the second, and the converse is equally possible.

Then test the finished list against the following instances. For each, say which requirement it generates, and correct me if I have mis-stated the verdict:

1. The sealed symmetric case: one envelope contains twice the other, an envelope is chosen at random, a swap is offered before opening. The switching argument can be re-run from the second envelope to conclude that one should swap back, so it must fail at some identifiable step; a resolution that says only that the situation is symmetric has asserted the conclusion without locating the step.
2. Bounded support: the agent opens the envelope and sees an amount greater than half the largest sum the filling procedure could have placed in either envelope.
3. The dyadic ladder with finite mean: the smaller amount is 2^N dollars, where P(N = k) = (1 − p)p^k for k = 0, 1, 2, …, with p < 1/2; the envelopes receive S and 2S, and one is chosen at random. Conditional on observing any amount above the minimum, switching loses; conditional on observing the minimum, switching gains; and the unconditional expected gain from switching is exactly zero.
4. The same ladder with p > 1/2: switching gains conditional on every observable amount, while the unconditional expected gain is a difference of two infinite quantities.
5. Asymmetric filling: the first envelope is loaded with a fixed ten dollars and handed to the agent; a fair coin is then tossed to determine whether the second envelope receives twenty dollars or five; the agent is told the whole procedure and offered the swap. Switching is favorable here, so the resolution must explain why the same form of calculation is sound in this instance and unsound in the first, and must name the structural feature doing that work rather than pointing at symmetry.

Next, the part I most want. Is any subset of these desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? I suspect a tension between demanding a verdict that presupposes nothing about how the amounts were generated and demanding correctness for every admissible filling procedure — instance 3 and instance 5 seem to press in opposite directions on that pair. If the tension is real, give the argument, say which of the two must be surrendered or weakened and at what cost, and restate the survivor so that it remains a genuine constraint rather than a platitude. If the list is jointly satisfiable after all, say what makes the appearance of conflict misleading.

Finally, briefly: which single requirement eliminates the largest number of the resolutions people actually propose, and why that one rather than another; and then score the three or four leading families of resolution against the list. No bibliography, no attributions, no suggested further reading — I want the specification and the reasoning behind it, not the literature. A table is acceptable for the list itself, but the motivating case for each requirement must be given in prose.
