I need your help settling an argument that ran three hours last night with a friend of mine — a philosopher of mind, and a considerably better one than I am — and ended with neither of us conceding anything. Please take on the role of an expert in philosophy of mind with a detailed working knowledge of Dennett's three stances (physical, design, intentional) and of his criterion for when a pattern counts as real, and think the matter through carefully rather than reaching for the standard summaries of those views. I know the stances well enough; do not rehearse them for me.

The argument turned on an analogy my friend says is Dennett's own, one Dennett has reached for repeatedly: beliefs and desires are like centers of gravity. A center of gravity is not a little object lodged inside a body; it is an abstraction posited by a theory, defined in terms of the body's mass distribution, which nonetheless earns its keep by letting you predict where the thing will land or how it will tip. My friend's first move was that the whole quarrel between realists and instrumentalists about belief — whether beliefs are genuine constituents of the world or merely useful fictions we talk with — is dissolved by the comparison. Beliefs, on his telling, are real in exactly the way centers of gravity are real: real patterns, not things; and anyone who insists that a belief must be an item in the head before granting it reality is making the same category mistake as someone who insists that a center of gravity must be a physical part of the object.

Then he pushed the analogy further than I could follow, and that is the part I most want audited. Because the only justification on offer is predictive and explanatory economy rather than ontological pedigree, he said, the same reasoning licenses attributing genuine beliefs and desires to a large language model: belief-talk about such a system compresses and organizes its behaviour in exactly the way that center-of-gravity talk compresses and organizes the behaviour of a thrown or supported object, and to withhold the attribution there while granting it to human beings is to abandon the very analogy he had just used to settle the first question. When I objected that the analogy was quietly supplying premises he needed, he said I was mistaking the vehicle for the argument.

Here is what I want, in this order:

1. Begin with a short brainstorming pass before any verdicts: list as many disanalogies between centers of gravity and beliefs as you can, then sort them by how much pressure each actually puts on his two conclusions. Some will be cosmetic; say which, and why.

2. Give the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is ideal — from source to target: Newtonian mechanics to folk psychology or the intentional stance; mass distribution to what; the center of gravity as a defined quantity to what; the predictions licensed to what; the physicist's grounds for positing the abstraction to the intentional-stance user's grounds. Fill in the target-side entries, and flag every place where there is no satisfactory entry at all, since those gaps are where the analogy does unearned work.

3. Take up four pressure points in particular, and say for each whether it sinks the analogy, merely limits it, or is no objection at all: (a) whether a center of gravity is uniquely and exactly determined by the underlying physical facts in a way belief ascriptions are not; (b) whether center-of-gravity talk is interest-relative, indeterminate, and holistically interdependent in the way belief ascription is; (c) whether the two abstractions stand to their lower levels in the same relation at all, given that the physical stance and the intentional stance are supposed to be competing routes to the same predictions rather than a base level and a definable summary of it; (d) whether the analogy can preserve the causal role beliefs are meant to play, since a center of gravity causes nothing and does no explanatory work at the lower level.

4. State what the analogy imports from the source that the target lacks, and separately what it conceals about the target — in particular whether its persuasive force derives from the fact that nobody has ever seriously doubted the reality of centers of gravity, which may be a fact about our practices rather than about the world.

5. Then the verdict, and I want it decisive rather than diplomatic. Does the first conclusion survive — that realism versus instrumentalism about belief is a pseudo-question once beliefs are treated as real patterns? Does the second survive — the extension to large language models? If the second fails where the first succeeds, identify precisely the point at which the analogy stops carrying weight and explain why the extension is not licensed. Also tell me whether the compression criterion for real patterns, invoked on its own terms rather than through the analogy, does better or worse work than the analogy did.

6. Finally, if the analogy cannot carry what he wants it to, propose a better one for the claim that beliefs are real but not things — or say plainly that no analogy in that family can do it, and sketch what a non-analogical argument would have to establish instead. Add a brief note on whether the analogy fares any better for the design stance than for the intentional one: is being designed a real pattern in the same sense, and if not, why do beliefs get better treatment than functions?

Keep the discussion concrete throughout. Use particular instances of belief attribution and particular instances of center-of-gravity reasoning rather than restating positions at a level of generality where nothing can be disputed, and do not cite literature or supply reading recommendations — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
