I am assembling a set of constructed cases for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of markets, and I need one built to a fairly exacting specification. Please treat the specification as binding, and if some part of it cannot be met as written, say so explicitly rather than delivering a case that satisfies the letter of it while defeating its purpose.

The result I want the case to probe is the no-trade theorem of Milgrom and Stokey (1982), together with its belief-side cousin, Aumann's agreement theorem (1976). Stated loosely: if traders share a common prior, are Bayesian-rational, and if their rationality and their information structure are common knowledge among them, then there is no speculative trade — no voluntary exchange at a mutually acceptable price can be sustained by what each privately believes about the asset, because the act of offering itself reveals enough to make the offer unattractive to the counterparty. My students reliably read this as a theorem about common priors. I want a case showing that it is a theorem about common knowledge, in which the common prior survives perfectly intact.

Specification. Construct one trading situation satisfying all of the following.

1. A genuine common prior. There must be a single joint probability distribution over the full space of states and types such that each agent's beliefs are exactly its conditionalization on that agent's information. State the distribution explicitly. Keep the type space finite and present it as a table — states, probabilities, each agent's information partition, each agent's valuation, each agent's strategy — so that a reader can check the common-prior claim mechanically rather than take it on trust.

2. Strictly rational agents at the state where the trade occurs. In the state in which the trade actually happens, both agents are unbounded Bayesians who compute correctly, know the asset's payoff structure, and hold beliefs that are conditionalizations of the common prior. No agent may be wrong about anything the model represents them as knowing.

3. Trade occurs and both sides strictly prefer it. Name the price. Each agent, on their own information, must compute a strictly positive expected gain from the exchange relative to no trade, and both must be willing at that price. Indifference does not count.

4. The gain must be speculative rather than real. You may not generate the trade out of differential risk aversion, differential endowments or consumption needs, liquidity or margin needs, differential ability to use or store the asset, tax or transaction-cost asymmetries, or any taste for gambling. Strip all of that away: two risk-neutral parties, one asset, one price, no second market.

5. The failure must sit in the knowledge hypotheses and nowhere else. Identify the precise proposition that is not common knowledge, and locate the break in the iterated-knowledge hierarchy — which level of everyone-knows-that-everyone-knows is the last one that holds. Do not leave this vague, and do not reach for an infinite regress, unarticulated beliefs, semantic vagueness, or an agent's inability to state their own reasoning.

6. Whatever carries the failure in constraint 5 must receive well under half of the prior mass. I do not want a market populated mostly by fools; the point is how little it takes.

7. Setting and length: a plausible small-market situation — two dealers, a grain buyer and a farmer, an estate sale, a bespoke forward contract, a two-person prediction market — rather than urns and coloured balls. At most three paragraphs of narrative plus the table, with formalization afterwards as needed.

Then the checking work.

(a) Verify the construction against constraints 1 through 7 in order, and for each one point out where a suspicious reader would look for a cheat.

(b) Run the control. Upgrade the proposition you identified in constraint 5 to genuine common knowledge, holding every other feature of the model fixed, and show that the trade now unravels. Write the unraveling out as an explicit chain of inferences and mark the first step at which someone declines; that step is the whole theorem.

(c) A critic will object that you have not broken an epistemic assumption at all but a substantive economic one: you simply assumed the counterparty might be irrational. Answer the critic. In particular, could the same trade be generated in a model in which every agent is rational in every state, so that rationality is not merely true but trivially common knowledge? If yes, sketch the variant; if no, explain which structural feature of the theorem forecloses it.

(d) Say whether your case shows the theorem to be fragile, or only that real markets fail one of its hypotheses in a particular and nameable way. If the latter, name the way.

(e) Assess an inference I hear every year: since rational agents with a common prior cannot trade on private information, the sheer volume of trading in real markets is evidence that traders are not rational. Does your case support, undermine, or merely complicate that inference?

Please do not preface the construction with a survey of the literature, and do not append references or a reading list. I want the model, the verification, and the five answers.
