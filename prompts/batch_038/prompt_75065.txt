A fight broke out at a department dinner last week — me, two people from political science, and a visiting Norwegian — over which of the two is more liberal, Sweden or Norway, and we never got anywhere, partly because I'm now pretty sure we weren't even arguing about the same proposition. I want to build it into a seminar I'm putting together on value-laden measurement in the social sciences, as the running case study, and before I write the thing up I'd like the conceptual geography laid out cleanly. So: what is that question actually asking, and is there anything that could settle it?

Start with the disambiguation, because "liberal" is doing at least three different jobs there and they don't line up. There's the market/classical sense (low regulation, weak unions, light state ownership, free entry — on which both countries look pretty illiberal next to the US and so similar to each other that I doubt any index has the resolution to separate them). There's the personal-autonomy sense (what you may do with your own body, household, speech, and life plans without interference). And there's the American sense where "liberal" just means left-of-center, on which reading both are liberal and the question is trivial. I want you to work through what happens to the Sweden/Norway comparison under each, and to say where the readings genuinely diverge rather than just relabeling the same facts. Two things I keep coming back to. First, there's the paradox — I think a couple of Swedish historians have a label for it, something like "state individualism," but don't worry about getting the attribution exact — that a very large public sector can go together with unusually high individual independence, because the welfare state does the de-familializing work: you don't have to depend on a spouse, a parent, an adult child, or an employer. So does a big state raise or lower individual liberty here, and does that answer depend on whether you're thinking in terms of non-interference or non-domination? Second, look for the discriminating cases rather than the aggregate rankings: one country joined the EU and the other said no twice; both ban the purchase of sex but one got there first and exports the model; both run a state alcohol monopoly so that dimension is a wash; their drug policies point in opposite directions in spirit even though the recent reforms in each have been messy; and their migration regimes both hardened after 2015. Which of these actually discriminate, and in which direction, under which reading of "liberal"?

Then the harder part, which is the bit I want the seminar to chew on: what would settle it? Talk me through whether this is a dispute that turns on conceptual choice — a family-resemblance cluster with no single latent factor behind the indicators, where the answer just tracks the weighting scheme you picked — or whether there's something underneath that both sides are tracking badly, in which case it's substantive and merely hard. Bring in the measurement problems explicitly: ceiling effects when you use instruments designed to tell democracies from autocracies and then point them at two near-identical neighbors; whether these indices are ordinal or secretly cardinal; the reference-group problem in survey self-ratings, since Scandinavians evaluate themselves against Scandinavian norms; and the prior question of whether the locus of the property is the state, the society, or the individuals, since those can come apart (a culture that describes itself as conformist and anti-boastful can still produce people who leave home at eighteen and live alone). Finally, tell me what you think the honest verdict is — do you land somewhere, or do you think the question is underdetermined, and if underdetermined, at which level: conceptual or empirical? I don't want a bibliography or a reading list, and please don't throw statistics at me; if you're not confident about a number or a date, just leave it out and argue from the structure of the case instead. Aim for something a smart third-year could follow who knows roughly where Sweden and Norway are on a map and nothing else, and if you think my framing of the dinner-party fight was itself confused, say so — that would probably be the most useful thing you could tell me.
