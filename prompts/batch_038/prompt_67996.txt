so bit of background — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever, and after a pretty nasty incident last quarter (long story short, someone's migration script truncated a table in prod and we were down six hours) my team switched to blameless postmortems. You write up what happened, no names, no fault-finding, the working assumption is that everyone acted reasonably given what they knew at the time. My tech lead hates this. Not on process grounds though — on feelings grounds. He's been reading around the guilt vs shame stuff and he reckons we've thrown out the one emotion that actually changes behaviour.

His argument leans really hard on an analogy and I want it audited rather than argued with. He says guilt is like a failing unit test: it's about one specific behaviour, it's local, it tells you what to fix, you patch it, the suite goes green, and nobody else ever has to know it happened. Shame, he says, is like a security audit that finds your whole architecture is rotten — it's not about a line you wrote, it's about you being the kind of person who writes lines like that, there's no patch for it, and by design it gets disclosed to everyone. So on his view shame is unusable signal and a mature team should run on guilt alone. Then someone on the reliability team flipped it round on him and said no, guilt is like a WARN log nobody ever reads — it's private, it costs you nothing, you can feel it on Tuesday and do exactly the same thing on Wednesday — whereas shame is like an alert that pages you at 3am, and the reason it works is precisely that it's costly and social and you can't silence it from inside your own head. Same debate, opposite analogies, both of them from my world, which makes me suspect the analogies are doing all the work and neither side has actually argued anything.

So: can you lay out both mappings properly (a correspondence table is fine — what plays the role of the test runner, the disclosure, the silence-ing, whoever it is that observes), then say where each one breaks and which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion it's being used to support? I specifically want to know whether the load is being carried by features of the source that shame and guilt don't share — is shame really global and unpatchable, or is that just what a security audit is like? — and whether "guilt is about the act, shame is about the self" is a real distinction or a slogan people repeat. Then, seperate from the analogies: is guilt actually the better moral emotion, and can a culture choose which one it runs on? Team is the version I care about but I assume the question is the same at any scale. My lead thinks you can just decide to be a shame-free culture or a shame-heavy one, but that seems to have a bootstrapping problem — to choose shame you'd have to already feel ashamed about not feeling ashamed, and to choose guilt you'd have to already be the sort of person whose guilt is worth trusting. Tell me whether that's a real objection or whether I'm overthinking it. British English please, and don't hand me a reading list or citations, I can't look anything up and I'd rather have the reasoning than references I can't check.
