I'm in the second year of a PhD program and co-organizing our department's philosophy-of-technology reading group. This term we've been working through the debate over whether artifacts have politics, and last week a visiting fellow from a science-and-technology-studies institute gave the seminar's opening summary, which amounted to this: technological determinism is not so much refuted as dissolved. On her account, what determinists identify as the politics of a technical object is really a record of the social negotiations that produced it — a durable inscription of decisions made by identifiable people under identifiable pressures — and the determinist error is to mistake the sediment for the structure. Her illustrations were the familiar ones. A clearance standard adopted for reasons of cost and standardization gets retrospectively read as a deliberate act of exclusion. Machinery installed to solve a labor-discipline problem gets retrospectively read as the cause of deskilling. In each case, she argued, you can trace every apparently technological constraint back to a contingent human choice that could have gone otherwise, which means the constraint is social all the way down and calling it technological adds nothing but mystery.

I have tried the standard replies — that designs freeze choices into material form long after the choosers are gone, that artifacts delegate political work to operators who never consented to it — and she was unmoved, though she did offer a concession I want to exploit. She said she would happily grant that some technologies have effects no one intended, and even that some of those effects are stubborn, so long as we do not describe the stubbornness as the technology acting. I think that concession is worth much more than she realizes, and I want to build an argument on it. I need help making the case in a rigorous form, because I am writing a response paper that she has agreed to read and reply to, and I would rather be arguing than trading slogans.

Here is the apparatus I want the argument built inside. I distinguish three tiers according to where the politics of a technical arrangement is located. Tier one: the artifact preserves the intentions of its designers, so a turnstile of a given height enforces a fare policy someone chose. This is the weakest claim, and it is roughly the one the construction-of-technology critique destroys. Tier two: the artifact has political effects that follow from its physical and informational structure rather than from anyone's intentions, and those effects survive the departure of the people who produced them — call this the endurance condition. Tier three: an entire domain of collective decision becomes progressively unavailable as the artifact and its supporting infrastructure settle into place, such that later actors with entirely different values find that the range of choices they can implement has narrowed without anyone having chosen the narrowing — call this foreclosure. I want to argue that tier three, not tier one, is what technological determinism is really about, and that the critique, however sound it is against tier one, leaves tier three essentially untouched.

For the case, use the following, which I have assembled from my own reading and partly invented for this exercise, so please reason from it as given rather than trying to recall a real deployment. A mid-sized city's traffic authority began installing an adaptive signal-control system across a dense corridor network in 2013. Induction loops embedded in the pavement, supplemented by probe-vehicle travel times, feed a controller that allocates green phases in real time to minimize a single published objective: total average delay per vehicle-kilometer on the network. The procurement documents specify that objective function and nothing else; the words equity, accessibility, and mode share appear nowhere in them, and no council vote on any distributional question was ever taken. Two structural features matter. First, the loops detect vehicles of roughly a tonne or more, so a bicycle, a person in a wheelchair, and a pedestrian in a crosswalk are not inputs; a transit vehicle is indistinguishable from four cars, so the objective function counts it as one. Second, the system can only optimize where it can measure, and measurement is densest on the wide, straight, well-instrumented arterials. Over the following years the network learned to favor those arterials: green waves propagated along them, while the parallel grid streets, which are unsignalized or run on fixed-time controllers, stayed uncoordinated. The travel-time advantage that two bus routes once enjoyed over car traffic on the arterials eroded and then reversed; ridership on both fell by roughly a fifth over six years. Commercial permits followed the reliable throughput, clustering on the arterials and thinning on the grid streets. When the authority's own climate plan and accessibility plan were adopted in 2019, their modeling sections assumed signal timings the network no longer produced, and the plans quietly missed their targets. The authority is aware of all of this and does not like it. Turning the system off would require renegotiating a ten-year maintenance and liability contract with a vendor that has since been acquired, and it would mean giving up a measurable metric that three successive administrations have reported to their councils as evidence of competent management. Redefining the objective function is technically possible but requires revalidating every intersection against the original procurement specification. So the system stays. Six years on, three administrations with genuinely different priorities, and the corridor's traffic behaves the same way.

So: argue that this case establishes a version of technological determinism at tier three that the construction-of-technology critique cannot reach. I want you to steelman the critique first, in a paragraph, at the strength my interlocutor actually deploys it — not a caricature, and not a version that already concedes the point. Then show precisely where its force runs out: what it can explain about the case and what it cannot. The burden you must carry is to explain why the endurance condition plus the foreclosure condition together license describing the network as doing something political, rather than merely as a residue of politics already over. Anticipate at least these three rejoinders, since she will make all of them: that the objective function was chosen by people and therefore the whole story is just politics by other means; that the real explanation is budget scarcity and vendor capture, which are social facts wearing a technical costume; and that the argument is safe only because it is trivial, since everything durable is in some sense constraining. Do not retreat to tier one anywhere in the paper, and do not win by redefining determinism until it means nothing more than path dependence — if your argument turns out to be compatible with her position, say so explicitly rather than hiding it. Finally, mark the single load-bearing premise: the claim I am least able to defend, and the specific piece of evidence about the case that would most change your mind. Prose, around thirteen hundred words, no headers unless you need two or three.
