So I'm a second-year history PhD student and my advisor has this running habit where she'll tell you flat out whether a period label is "a real thing" or "a story we tell ourselves," and she's done it enough times that I figure I should be able to predict her, except I keep getting it wrong. Her record so far, roughly in her own phrasing:

- the Middle Ages: real stretch of time, bogus name — nobody in it knew they were in the middle of anything, and the label was minted by later people who needed the previous thousand years to look bad so they could look awake.
- the Renaissance: not a period, a marketing campaign that worked. There's a genuine cluster of changes underneath, but the "rebirth" framing is the actors' own self-flattery, baked into the archive by people with a stake in it.
- the Scientific Revolution: the events are real, the edges are fake. Nobody living through it used the phrase and it only became a standard period name in the last century, and every start and end date you draw makes some neighboring thing look arbitrarily excluded.
- the Enlightenment: real, and one of the few that is — because the people in it named themselves, argued about the name, and organized around it. The label is in the sources, not just on the syllabus.
- the Industrial Revolution: real change, dishonest noun. Something genuinely broke in the numbers, but "revolution" implies a moment and an agent, and it smuggles in a politics about who did it to whom.
- early modern: a placeholder that hardened into furniture. Nobody meant anything much by it, then departments hired against it and archives got shelved by it, and now it has a shape purely because it has a budget.
- the long nineteenth century: she actually likes this one, says a label that admits its stretchy is more honest than one pretending to be a discovery.

Now a colleague has proposed renaming our 1500–1800 survey. Instead of "Early Modern Europe" it would be "The Age of Extraction, 1492–1830s." His pitch: the old title names a region and a vibe, the new one names a mechanism — the sustained pumping of silver, sugar, land and labor out of the Americas and into Europe and China — with hard endpoints on both sides, the first crossing going in and the Spanish American republics independent plus British slavery abolished coming out. He also insists it isn't polemical, it's descriptive, because "extraction" is just what the ledgers say happened.

Where does my advisor land? I want more than a yes or no. Lay out the different rules that are compatible with everything above — I can see at least three, something about whether contemporaries named themselves, something about whether there's a mechanism with defensible edges, something about whether the word carries a verdict on the period — and say what each one predicts for his title, including any rule I haven't thought of that also fits her whole record. Then give me your single best guess with reasons for preferring it over the others, tell me which of her past rulings is the closest analog and exactly where the analogy strains, and name one further label, real or invented, that would best separate the candidate rules if I could get her to rule on it. And if she does say no: is there a version of the title that keeps the mechanism but survives her objections, or is the mechanism itself the problem?
