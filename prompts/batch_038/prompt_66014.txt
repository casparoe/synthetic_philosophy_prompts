I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in practical ethics, and our closing unit concerns the moral status of insects and the ethics of pest control. For the final I want a single essay question, answerable in sixty minutes and worth twenty-five marks, that a candidate cannot pass by rehearsing the standard positions in animal ethics.

Build the question around the following proposal, which you should present to candidates in the stem. A regional agricultural authority intends to rank pest-management regimes — broad-spectrum spraying, targeted baiting, mating disruption, sterile-insect release, and land left fallow — by a single figure of merit: for each regime, sum over the affected animals the product of the probability that they are sentient, the number of individuals, the intensity of their suffering, and its duration. The authority treats the regime with the lowest such sentience-weighted harm score as the one it is morally required to adopt. The question should ask candidates to evaluate the procedure rather than the regimes, and it should be framed so that the interesting work lies in noticing what the procedure assumes but cannot deliver: that insects spared a spray would otherwise have lived lives worth living rather than dying painfully anyway from starvation, parasitism, or predation; that the individuals existing under each regime are the same individuals, so that selecting a regime harms some of them; that the probability of sentience is one number rather than a stand-in for disagreement about which theory of welfare applies at all; and that scaling moral weight by a probability yields quantities that may be summed across individuals without raising any aggregation problem.

Then supply a model answer of about seven hundred words and a marking scheme. The scheme should rest on four or five must-hit points with marks apportioned to a total of twenty-five, and for each it should say what a merely competent answer does that a first-class answer does not — in particular, where candidates tend to collapse the nonidentity issue into the harm issue, or assume without argument that discounting for uncertain sentience is exactly parallel to ordinary risk assessment over harms we know are real. Close with a note on how to mark the answer that concludes only that our ignorance calls for precaution, since I expect that from most of the cohort; say what such an answer must go on to establish before the conclusion is earned, and what it should be penalised for leaving out.
