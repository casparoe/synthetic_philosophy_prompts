My flatmate is doing a PhD in quantum foundations and he cornered me after dinner with an argument I still haven't been able to shake, three weeks on. I'm not a philosopher — I write backend services — but I can usually feel when something has gone wrong, and here I can feel it and I can't locate it. He insists every single step is something he's independently committed to, and that the conclusion is just the bill coming due. Here's his chain, roughly as he gave it to me:

(1) Everett is right: no collapse, and when a system decoheres every outcome happens, each in its own branch.
(2) Quantum mechanics assigns nonzero amplitude to every macroscopic history that isn't strictly forbidden by a conservation law or superselection rule — including freak ones, like all the air in a room ending up in one corner, or the atoms of a corpse reassembling into something intact.
(3) So for any catastrophe C that doesn't strictly forbid continuation, some branch contains a person existing after C who is continuous with me.
(4) Personal identity isn't what matters prudentially; what matters is psychological and physical continuity and connectedness, and that comes in degrees.
(5) Everettians already accept that after a fission-type event both successors are me — nobody thinks an ordinary quantum measurement annihilates me just because it makes two people.
(6) But identity itself isn't a matter of degree: a future person either is me or isn't. So however thin the continuity in (3), if there's any at all, that person is me.
(7) Therefore, for every catastrophe C, there's a branch containing a person who is me and who survives C.
(8) And "I survive C" is true or false full stop, not true-relative-to-a-branch. By (7), it's true, for every C.
(9) Amplitude tells you how much of the multiverse contains an outcome, not whether that outcome occurs — and by (8) occurrence is what my prudential reasoning cares about. So I should be exactly as averse to a 99.9999% fatal risk as to a 0.0001% one, which is to say not at all.
(10) Put your name where mine was and the same argument runs, so nobody is ever at risk from anything, and safety engineering, insurance, and cautious betting are all resting on a category mistake.

He says (10) is the point: a theory that tells you to walk into traffic is refuted by the fact that it tells you to walk into traffic. I think that's too easy — I've heard there are decision-theory fixes floating around for Everett and I don't know whether they'd touch this — and I don't want to reject (1) just because the conclusion is ugly. If I have to pick, I'd most like to keep (1) and (4); (4) is the part that actually convinced me of anything. But tell me if thats just stubbornness. So: name the step where it breaks, or if it doesn't break, name the premise that's cheapest to surrender, and then price the options for me — I want to know what a person gives up by cutting (2) versus cutting (6) versus cutting (8), since they seem like very different kinds of retreat. And be honest about your own confidence: which of these steps are you least sure about, where is your diagnosis most likely to be wrong, and what argument or what piece of physics would push you to a different verdict than the one you give me?
