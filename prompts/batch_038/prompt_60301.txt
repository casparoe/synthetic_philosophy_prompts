I'm in year 4 of an epistemology PhD and my chapter is about epistemic dependence — roughly the Hardwig line, where in any mature technical enterprise the community knows vastly more than any single member could ever check for themselves, so most of what you're prepared to call your own knowledge you actually hold on the authority of something else. My advisor thinks my aviation examples are engineering cliche and told me to either make one of them carry real philosophical weight or cut them, so: help me make Air France 447 carry weight. I'll stipulate the facts so we don't have to relitigate them. Night of 1 June 2009, A330 from Rio to Paris, ice crystals obstruct the pitot probes, the airspeed readings go temporarily inconsistent, the protections drop out and the aircraft reconfigures into alternate law and hands itself back to the crew. The two junior pilots at the controls pull up. The aeroplane stalls somewhere around 38,000 feet and stays stalled for three and a half minutes all the way down into the Atlantic, 228 dead. The stall warning sounded and, per the investigation, was never diagnosed as a stall.

First I want the mapping done properly, not gestured at. Run the episode through Bainbridge's ironies of automation — the operator is left supervising a system they're no longer permitted to practice on, so their competence decays in proportion to how well the automation works, and it hits bottom exactly when the rare case arrives that needs it. Then run it through Hardwig: who or what is the testifier here, and do the crew stand to the flight computers the way a physicist stands to a colleague's data? My worry is they don't, because Hardwig's dependent believer is trusting someone who has beliefs and is more likely to be right, and an Airbus isn't testifying to anything, it's just executing a control law — but I can't tell whether that's a fatal objection or just a reason to widen the concept, and I'd like that argued rather than assumed. Second, and this is the part I actually think is interesting: decide whether the crew's failure was epistemic or a failure of skill, because the two diagnoses imply opposite remedies. If they didn't know the aircraft was stalled, that's a transparency problem, fixable with better displays and better modes; if they knew and couldn't recover it, that's a practice problem and no amount of display redesign touches it. Bring the understanding literature to bear — the idea that understanding isn't a heap of true beliefs but a grasp of dependency relations, being able to answer what-if questions and to intervene. On that picture did these pilots have understanding of the stall and lose access to it under load, or did the automation mean they never had it? And does that question even stay coherent when you ask it about a person-plus-machine instead of a person? Take the last bit seriously: the extended mind people say the coupled system is the cognitive unit, their critics say that's a coupling-constitution confusion, and I genuinely can't work out which side this crash helps.

Third, tell me where the theories go quiet or actively misdescribe the case, because I'd rather have that than a tidy fit. I suspect the stock "automation complacency" reading of AF447 is just wrong — these weren't passive monitors lulled by a reliable system, they were hand-flying the thing badly for minutes, with a warning sounding — and if Bainbridge's ironies predict complacency and this crew wasn't complacent, I need to know whether that makes the case a confirmation, an anomaly, or evidence the theory was never really about cases like this. Also: Bainbridge was writing about process control operators in plants and refineries, where the automation is built by designers with an incomplete model of the process. How much of that actually transfers to a certified flight control system with a defined degradation ladder? Finally, the part I need for the chapter: does any of this license the claim that the same dynamic is now running in epistemic work with LLMs — grad students writing code they can't debug, junior people producing prose whose reasoning they never had to reconstruct? Give me the disanalogies as honestly as the analogies. An autopilot at least announces its own disengagement, with a chime and a flight mode change, and a wing will shake the stick at you; a language model never tells you you've left its competence envelope, and there's no ECAM message for "you are currently in a state you cannot see." Does the silence make the erosion worse, or just differently shaped — the harm showing up late, in the moment you have to do it without the tool, instead of immediately and publicly? And a related policy point worth chewing on: the US response to a nearby stall accident that same winter was to raise the minimum flight experience for airline first officers to 1,500 hours, i.e. a quantity-of-experience fix aimed at what was arguably a quality-of-understanding problem. If we're going to respond to AI dependence the same way, what would the 1,500 hours even consist of? I don't want a bibliography or a list of further reading, I've read the obvious stuff — I want a real case analysis that runs in both directions and ends with an actual verdict on whether the aviation analogy supports the pessimism about understanding or is mostly doing vivid, fatal, photogenic rhetorical work for it.
