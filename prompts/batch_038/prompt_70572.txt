I moderate a forty-five-minute recorded conversation for a public-affairs philosophy series; the audience is educated but not specialist. Next month's guest is a political theorist who defends a remedial account of secession: the right to leave arises from grave and persistent injustice rather than from a majority's wish to go. Her version carries two distinctive conditions, which she insists are not decorative. The first is an exhaustion condition — a group acquires the right only after it has made a serious, publicly documented offer of continued union on substantially devolved terms and the central state has refused it or hollowed it out. The second is a reciprocity condition — the seceding unit must entrench an equivalent exit right for any territorially concentrated minority inside its claimed borders, and must accept the division of assets and debts set by third-party arbitration rather than terms of its own proposing. She has two signature deflections: she dismisses the perverse-incentive objection as an empirical worry nobody has the data to press, and she treats referendum majorities as symptoms of the right rather than sources of it.

Draft ten to twelve questions in the order I should ask them, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Coverage must include: what separates refusal from legitimate foot-dragging, and who is competent to make that call where no impartial adjudicator exists; whether the reciprocity condition is enforceable or merely declaratory, especially where the internal minority that would benefit is itself the locally dominant group; the well-treated but politically voiceless population, and whether systematic exclusion from effective representation counts as a grave injustice; the moral-hazard objection, with a follow-up built to survive her "nobody has the data" reply; why a durable majority preference carries no independent normative weight, and how she reads the Canadian Supreme Court's 1998 Secession Reference — a duty to negotiate triggered by a clear majority on a clear question — as consistent with that; what third parties may do about recognition, sanctions, or asylum once a secession she deems unjustified has succeeded and stabilized; and how much territory the group gets when it is not a majority across all of what it claims.

Each question must be short enough to ask aloud in two sentences, carrying just enough of the argument that a non-specialist follows it, and none may invite her to restate the view or discuss her biography. Flag the two or three places where she has a stock answer ready, and say how the follow-up closes it. Finish with a single question asking her to name a case, real or constructed, where her theory's verdict is one she personally finds unwelcome.
