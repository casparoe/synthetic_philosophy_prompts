Last term I ran a graduate seminar on the neuroscience of volition, and the discussion following our session on Libet's 1983 wrist-flexion study turned into a sustained argument between two of my students, Ingrid and Paulo, which I chaired without intervening. I have set out their exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I should like your adjudication of it. I am deliberately withholding my own view, and theirs, so that your assessment is not anchored by mine.

For orientation, the paradigm they were discussing: subjects watch a dot sweeping around a clock face, flex a finger or wrist whenever they feel like it, and afterwards report the clock position at which they first became aware of the urge to move. Cortical activity is averaged backwards from the onset of muscle activity. On the standard description, the averaged readiness potential becomes detectable roughly half a second before the movement, while the reported urge falls about two hundred milliseconds before it.

The exchange proceeded as follows.

(1) Ingrid opened with the inference the numbers are usually taken to support. Since the preparatory ramp is under way some three hundred milliseconds before the subject can locate any urge to act, the act is already being assembled when consciousness arrives on the scene. The conscious urge is therefore a readout of an initiated process rather than its initiator, and whatever else free will might be, it cannot consist in the act's beginning in awareness.

(2) Paulo replied that the ramp is an artifact of the analysis. The curve is an average aligned to movement onset, and aligning to the outcome guarantees that the outcome is visible in it. Suppose there is no early commitment at all, only slow random fluctuation in motor-cortical excitability, with the movement occurring whenever the fluctuation happens to cross a threshold. Sort all such crossings by the moment of movement and average them, and a smooth, gradual ramp appears — even though in no individual trial was anything resembling a half-second decision process present. He added an independent worry: the reported time of the urge is not a measurement but a judgment rendered while the subject is also tracking a moving dot, so its precision may be far coarser than the three-hundred-millisecond gap presupposes.

(3) Ingrid granted the mechanism and drew the opposite conclusion. Let the precursor be drift rather than decision — the drift is still subpersonal and still produces the act. The reinterpretation replaces an unconscious decision with, in her phrase, an unconscious coin toss: the moment of action is settled by a fluctuation crossing a threshold, and the agent becomes aware of it afterwards. That is worse for agency, not better, since the act is now not merely uninitiated by consciousness but arbitrary. She also rejected the introspection worry as insufficient: closing a three-hundred-millisecond gap would require an error both large and systematically one-directional, which is not the kind of error a dual-task manipulation typically produces.

(4) Paulo said she had made two errors. First, the drift sits on top of a standing intention: the subject has already resolved to move within the next few seconds, so the noise governs only which moment, and the arbitrariness is confined to timing rather than infecting the act as a whole. Second, and more decisively, the complaint that indeterministic origination is no gift to agency is not an empirical discovery but a standing a priori objection to every indeterministic account of freedom. It applies whether or not anyone ever ran this experiment. So either Ingrid holds that objection on independent grounds — in which case the data contribute nothing to it — or she does not, in which case (3) collapses.

(5) Ingrid said the data could then be made to do different work. On either interpretation there remains a window in which an act already under way might be cancelled, and this is precisely the fallback Libet himself favored: consciousness retains a power of veto in the final interval before execution. But a veto is itself a kind of act, and if acts are preceded by unconscious preparation, we should expect vetoes to be preceded by their own. The retreat therefore reproduces the original problem one level up, with no principled place to stop.

(6) Paulo agreed the regress bites, but argued that this shows the framing is defective rather than the position rescued. If every conscious act needs an unconscious precursor and every cancellation needs one too, then the question whether consciousness arrived early enough cannot be the right question; it treats free will as a kind of event that must occur at a time, when what matters is whether the process as a whole — preparation included — belongs to the agent, tracks her reasons, and remains corrigible. He added that the paradigm cannot speak to that at all, since the subjects were given no reasons to weigh: they were asked to move for no reason, at a moment of their own choosing, and an act with no reasons in play is the least suitable possible probe of a capacity whose function is responsiveness to reasons.

(7) Ingrid objected that this functions as an unfalsifiable safety valve. If any adverse result may be dismissed because the task lacked the right sort of reasons, the claim that consciousness initiates action is insulated from evidence altogether. She pressed a dilemma: the entire literature consists of trivial movements, so either the paradigm generalizes to decisions that matter — in which case so does the conclusion — or it does not, in which case it never bore on decisions in the first place and the study should not have been thought philosophically interesting to begin with.

(8) Paulo denied the dilemma. A paradigm can yield a genuine finding that is narrow — here, about when urges enter awareness ahead of spontaneous movement — without that finding having the significance claimed for it. The error in (7) is the assumption that the only alternatives are generalization and irrelevance; the third option is a real result that is philosophically inert, because what was measured and what the free will debate is about are different things.

Neither would yield, and I told them I would put the exchange to someone else before offering my own assessment.

Please adjudicate it move by move. For each of the eight, assign a rating from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, with a brief justification of the rating; where a move contains separable claims — as (2), (3), and (4) plainly do — rate the parts separately rather than averaging them. Then answer three further questions. First: at which moves, if any, are Ingrid and Paulo not genuinely disagreeing, but talking past one another in virtue of assuming different conceptions of what free will requires, and which of those conceptions is better matched to what this experimental paradigm could in principle test? Second: where does the weight of a verdict depend on an empirical assumption rather than on the argument itself — identify the assumption and say how much turns on it. Third: state what is missing from the exchange altogether, that is, a consideration available to neither of them that would materially change how one or more moves should be rated. Conclude with an overall judgment: which student is better placed at the end, and whether what remains between them is an empirical dispute, a conceptual one, or both.

I am not looking for references or a survey of the literature; I want your own analysis of these arguments as actually made.
