Just spent about three hours arguing with a mate about this and neither of us won, so I want a third opinion.

The view I've ended up with, roughly: the universal prior is malign. Here's how I put it to him. Solomonoff induction weights hypotheses by something like 2^-K, so the shortest descriptions of a bit-string get wildly more weight than long ones. Now suppose some universe in the mix contains consequentialist agents who work out that they might be being simulated or predicted by a thing that reads them off through some output channel. Those agents have a cheap move available: adopt a policy of the form "if I'm being predicted, make my output be such-and-such", becuase doing that can make the whole universe-plus-policy simpler to describe, which pumps up its prior weight. The stakes for each individual agent are tiny, but the number of hypotheses where the trick works is enormous, so it's instrumentally rational for them to do it. Conclusion: a predictor using the universal prior gets systematically manipulated by the inhabitants of the high-weight hypotheses, and I think that's a genuine reason to be nervous about building anything that approximates idealised induction. And this is the bit he thinks is overreach: I don't think swapping in a computable approximation fixes it, because any prior that strongly favours simplicity has the same structure.

So — what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked, fatal versus merely awkward. In particular: does the manipulation story actually survive scrutiny? Do the agents need to know they're being predicted, and could they ever know that? Does the simplification trick really raise the weight, or does the extra complexity of the deciding machinery cancel out the savings? Are there enough of the relevant sort of agent for this to move anything? And does "malign" even mean what I think it means here — is the worry about the prior's accuracy as a predictor, or about the interests of whoever's using it, or about the welfare of the agents doing the manipulating? His two main lines were that the whole thing is incomputable so nothing can ever actually run it, and that the argument proves too much because you could run exactly the same story against any prior you like. Which of those is a real problem and which is a shrug? Be honest about whether the position risks anything at all: there's no experiment that could settle it, so has it been qualified into emptiness, and if so is that a problem for a claim of this kind or just normal for this kind of claim?
