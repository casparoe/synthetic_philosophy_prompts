I chair the charter review commission of a city of about 190,000 in the intermountain West, and I need a written opinion I can circulate to the other eight commissioners before our Thursday session. Two years ago our voters approved a charter amendment creating a Land Use Citizens' Assembly: forty-eight people drawn by lot, convened for each area plan covering land within a half-mile walk of a transit station, deliberating for six weekends and issuing a recommendation that the council may set aside only by a two-thirds vote accompanied by published reasons. The amendment says the assembly shall be drawn from the community affected by the plan, and it does not define that phrase. Our first assembly — for the Marbury Corridor plan, which would allow four-to-six-story housing on land now zoned almost entirely for detached homes — convenes in eleven weeks, and the staff have to publish the sortition procedure in about a month. What is blocking us is not the mechanics but the phrase.

Five candidate pools are now on the table, and the commission has been arguing past itself for three meetings. First, residents of the plan area: roughly 9,400 people, of whom about 46 percent rent, and who bear the traffic, shadows, and displacement pressure most directly. Second, all city residents, on the theory that a corridor plan redistributes citywide opportunity and that the current residents of the area are, in effect, a small committee deciding for all of us. Third, the roughly 31,000 people who commute into the corridor for work but live in other jurisdictions; many of them were priced out of this city, and several of their home towns have told us in writing that they consider our zoning an export of our housing problem. Fourth, former residents of the corridor who were cleared out by the 1998 redevelopment and now live elsewhere in the metro; about two hundred of them have organized and claim a seat as of right. Fifth, a councilmember's proposal to reserve a small number of places for the people who would move in if the plan were built — future residents, who by definition cannot be listed on any roll — to be filled either by advocacy organizations or by a rotating ombudsperson. Our city attorney adds that seating non-residents on a body whose recommendation the council can override only by a supermajority is legally unsettled here and could draw a challenge, and that a challenge filed after the sortition would be far more damaging than one filed before it. Meanwhile we keep sliding between two different questions without noticing: who is governed by this plan, and who stands to gain or lose from it. Those come apart, and I do not think we can adopt a defensible rule until we say which one the charter's phrase is tracking, or explain why the commission needs both.

Please draft the commission's opinion — call it 900 to 1,200 words, in a register my colleagues would recognize as ours rather than as a policy brief. It should recommend a concrete composition rule for the Marbury assembly: which pools feed the draw, how many seats each contributes, whether any seats are reserved or non-voting, what happens to the 1998 displaced group and to the future-resident proposal, and what the draw runs on given that we have no reliable address list for commuters. It should also state the criteria in a form we can apply to the next area plan without reopening this fight, and it should tell us what to do if a challenge is filed anyway — a fallback that does not require re-running the sortition from scratch. Treat the strongest case against your own recommendation seriously and at length, not as a strawman: I expect that case to be that legitimacy requires the people who will have to live under the plan and are bound by it to be the ones deciding, that adding commuter and regional seats hands a hostile outside majority control of a neighborhood's future, and that the whole enterprise collapses in trust if residents conclude the assembly was stacked. One of my colleagues has taken to invoking the right to the city — Lefebvre's phrase, from 1968 — as though it settled the matter; I want the memo to say what such a slogan could mean as an operative rule about who gets drawn, or to say plainly that it cannot. Say also which parts of your recommendation the commission can adopt as charter interpretation and which really require a vote of the electorate, and where you think we will pay politically for it. What I would find most persuasive is a memo willing to name, in one or two sentences, exactly whom we are excluding and why that exclusion is defensible; what I do not want is a call for broader outreach, a restatement that every stakeholder should feel heard, or a recommendation to defer the question to the next charter cycle.
