I'm helping run a weekend workshop — about 25 people, roughly half philosophy of mind grad students, half working ML engineers — on whether the whole competence-without-comprehension business (Dennett's inversion, the Darwinian version and the Turing version) actually tells us anything about the systems we build now. The first hour always goes the same way: the philosophers say comprehension must involve something extra, the engineers say look, it demonstrably *does* the thing, and both sides think the other is just refusing to look at the evidence. I've decided the real problem is that they aren't even disagreeing about the same claim. So I want to hand everyone a decision tree at the door that sorts them by what they'd say about concrete cases rather than by whose name they drop.

Here's what I need. Build the logical space first — seven to nine branching questions, each one a case or a claim someone could answer without having any idea which leaf it's steering them toward — and only then locate the actual positions inside it. Please make sure these splits show up somewhere in the tree, in whatever order you think is most diagnostic: whether the gap between competence and comprehension is explanatory (a limit on what we can work out) or ontological (something present in the one case and absent in the other); whether comprehension is a third-personal functional matter or a first-personal one; whether a complete algorithmic-plus-physical description of the competence exhausts the facts; whether the competence in question is narrow and brittle or systematic and compositional, and whether that distinction is supposed to be doing any deep work or just marking a difference of degree; whether comprehension is itself just a further species of competence, so that the inversion goes all the way down and nothing ever has to be explained by positing prior understanding; and whether the whole dispute is verbal. The leaves have to include, at minimum: the full eliminativist who thinks comprehension is competence all the way down; the skeptic who thinks invoking selection or learning to account for structured competence explains nothing at all and just relabels the thing to be explained; the syntax-isn't-semantics or first-person-gap view; a mysterian or property-dualist leaf; the view on which comprehension isn't in the system at all but is something an interpreter does when it takes the system's behavior in a certain way; and a deflationary leaf where the question just doesn't have an answer independent of our interests. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate, and don't paper over it — I'd rather have one honest note than a fake clean cut.

For the branching questions, use cases like these (pick and adapt, you don't need all of them): a robot arm that reliably catches a dropped egg and whose designer genuinely can't say how it does it; a model that aces the benchmark and then falls over on a trivial paraphrase; a chess engine that plays perfectly and never has anything like the concept of a threatened square; a lookup table large enough to hold its side of any conversation; someone who rides a bike flawlessly and cannot articulate the skill; a child who uses a word correctly in every context but can't define it. Also point out which combinations of answers nobody seems to hold, and for each one say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it's simply unexplored — I especially suspect there's a live unoccupied spot for someone who thinks the gap is ontological but purely third-personal, and I want to know if you agree. No citations and no reading list, just names where a position actually has one. Format: open with one or two sentences naming the single question that does the most sorting work in your tree, then give the tree itself as a compact indented outline, each node question under about 25 words and each leaf under 30. Cap the whole thing at 700 words — I'm printing it single-sided.
