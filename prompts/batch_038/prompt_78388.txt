You are an expert in eighteenth-century political philosophy — specifically Rousseau's moral psychology and his account of the institutions that shape public opinion — who has also worked for years as a professional dramaturg and has sat on public arts-funding panels. Please think carefully and work through the problem in stages before you produce the final deliverable.

I am in my fourth year of a PhD in political theory, writing a dissertation on Rousseau's treatment of the institutions that form and deform public sentiment. I also serve, unpaid, as the dramaturg on the seven-member programming committee of a municipally subsidized civic theater in a town of about forty thousand people. Roughly forty percent of our operating budget comes from the town council's culture committee, which last season opened a review of our subsidy after a production it considered gratuitously humiliating to a group of local officials. The council has now asked us to adopt a written programming charter: not a mission statement, but a defensible procedure that lets us explain each season's choices in two pages a non-specialist can follow. Our artistic director holds a veto over any decision but is now required to give reasons in writing.

Our current practice is a single question — is it good art? — plus whatever the budget line permits. The result is a season nobody can reconstruct afterward, and arguments that recur every month in slightly different disguises. I want the charter grounded in the arguments Rousseau actually makes in his letter to d'Alembert on the theater, written in reply to d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article on Geneva, because those arguments are much more interesting than the caricature of Rousseau as a censor, and because several of them are testable questions about a specific proposed production rather than slogans.

Please do the following, in American English:

1. Open with a short brainstorming section (clearly marked as preparatory, not part of the charter) in which you excavate the distinct claims Rousseau makes, and sort them into four families: claims about what performance does to the passions of an audience, including his treatment of the old defense that tragedy purges them; claims about what a standing theater does to the time, money, and habits of a small polity; claims about the relation between performer and spectator; and claims about what could stand in for theater altogether. For each claim, say in a line or two whether it is conceptual or empirical, and whether it survives translation into our setting — a modern regional house with professional actors, ticket-buying audiences, no sovereign assembly to compete with, and a civic life organized around commuting and school calendars rather than public squares.

2. Build a translation table: Rousseau's claim, the operational question the committee asks about a proposal, what counts as a sufficient answer to that question, and whether an insufficient answer is an outright stop, a required modification, or a referral to the committee because it is a matter of value rather than fact.

3. Write the procedure itself as an ordered sequence of steps a volunteer with no philosophical training can run in about thirty minutes per proposal, with explicit stop conditions and with every value-laden step tagged [JUDGMENT] and assigned to a named decision-maker. Specify what gets written down at each step, since the two-page justification for the council has to exist.

4. Tell me what to do with the claims a contemporary committee must reject or quarantine — in particular Rousseau's remarks on the actor's trade and on women on the stage. I do not want them silently dropped, and I do not want them smuggled in as disguised aesthetic criteria. Give each a stated disposition (reinterpretable, quarantined, or overridden), and where you reinterpret, say what interpretive work is being done and what is lost by it.

5. Run the procedure end to end on three proposals, showing the answers recorded at each step and the resulting verdict and written justification:
   (a) a present-day revival of Molière's The Misanthrope transposed into our own council chamber, which the director wants staged so that the audience laughs hardest at the one character who refuses to flatter anyone and leaves convinced that plain-speaking is a species of self-indulgence — I proposed this one and I still love it;
   (b) an immersive promenade piece marking the tenth anniversary of the closure of the town's largest employer, staged in the market square at dusk, with two hundred residents paid a stipend to take part, no professional actors, no tickets, a single performance, funded entirely out of the subsidy;
   (c) a well-made new drama by an established playwright about a much-loved local physician, a four-week run with a professional cast already presold to capacity, whose design progressively implicates the audience in the physician's cruelty, and whose marketing is leaning on audience reports that the experience is cathartic.
   At least one of these should come out against my preferences. For that case, state what evidence would have to change to reverse the verdict, and diagnose where the procedure is weakest in reaching it.

6. Close with a candid section on what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire systematically, which pairs of proposals it cannot rank, which of its empirical premises the committee has no way of checking before opening night, and what to do about those (staged reading, community workshop, a pilot season).

7. Append a one-page version the committee can hold in hand at a meeting.

Constraints: Rousseau's own vocabulary is admissible only with a one-line gloss in parentheses, since council members will read the memos. No bibliography, no citation apparatus, no page numbers, and no verbatim quotation unless you are confident of the exact wording — paraphrase instead, and attribute nothing to Rousseau that you could not defend as his argument rather than a common impression of it. Finally, flag any place where you think I have mischaracterized the letter or asked the procedure to carry a load it cannot bear.
