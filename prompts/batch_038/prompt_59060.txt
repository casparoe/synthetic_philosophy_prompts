I am giving a seminar talk next month at an experimental-economics lab. The audience is behavioral and experimental game theorists, most of whom have personally run centipede-style, trust, or bargaining experiments; there are two or three formal theorists in the room but no epistemologists. Their shared starting points: common knowledge of rationality is a useful fiction at best; backward induction has been descriptively refuted by the data; and a position earns a hearing only if it makes predictions they could, in principle, take to the lab. They are courteous, but their reflex is to ask what my view rules out and how they would identify it.

My thesis: the centipede game does not refute backward induction, because the standard argument equivocates between two distinct claims — that a rational player takes at the final node, and that a player's belief in the other's rationality survives having observed a pass. Once these are prised apart, the so-called paradox is not about the irrationality of subjects but about belief revision at off-path nodes: a pass is evidence not that my opponent is irrational, but that my assumptions about their payoffs or their information are wrong, and on most readings that inference licenses continuing rather than taking. So backward induction is not falsified by early passing; its premises are destroyed by the very observation the argument needs in order to get started.

Separately flag the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, and explain why that one is dangerous. No reading list, no citations, no named authorities — I want the arguments themselves.
