I work on payments infrastructure for a Manchester fintech, and my philosophy is entirely self-taught from evening reading, so please excuse the absence of technical vocabulary — the absence is rather the point of what follows. For the past year I have been arguing with a friend who is a tax barrister about inheritance tax, dynastic wealth and what equality of opportunity could actually mean in practice, and somewhere in those arguments I became convinced that a single pattern runs through the debate on both sides. I can describe it, I cannot find a word for it, and I would like to know whether there is an established concept that captures it, whether some existing concept nearly captures it, or whether it is genuinely unnamed.

The pattern is this: the amount of moral heat a mechanism of advantage-transmission attracts seems to track how administratively tractable the mechanism is, rather than how much advantage it actually transmits. Take two ways of moving £2 million from a parent to a child. In the first, the parent dies and the child receives the money; the country then has a furious argument about whether that is just, whether it is double taxation, whether the child deserved it, and what it does to the society that permits it. In the second, the parent spends the same £2 million over twenty years while alive: private schooling from the age of four, tutors, a deposit on a flat in the right city, an unpaid placement arranged through a friend, and an accent and a manner of self-presentation that gets the child through interviews. That second route provokes no argument at all — it is usually described as good parenting, and if it is mentioned in the debate it is mentioned as a reason to be relaxed about the first. I found this so strange that I tried to model it for my own amusement, crudely, in a spreadsheet: same money, two transfer schedules, and my sketch suggested the second route moved the child's lifetime earnings prospects further, and earlier, than the first. What I keep noticing is that the thing everyone agrees is the decisive channel of privilege is the one nobody proposes to touch, and the reason given is almost never that it is fair or that it matters less; the reason given is that you could not write a rule about reading bedtime stories.

Two further observations, and I am honestly not sure whether they belong to the same phenomenon or are separate ones. First, there is a slide I cannot stop seeing: from "no policy could realistically address X" to "X is not a matter of justice". Genetic endowment, the fact of being born into a country with a strong currency, the compounding effect of two graduate parents on a child's vocabulary — these get waved away, when they get mentioned at all, precisely because they are not addressable, and the infeasibility seems to do normative work rather than merely practical work. I know "ought implies can" is somewhere in the neighbourhood, but that principle is about whether an agent can be required to do something, and what I am describing is about whether a state of affairs counts as unfair. Second, the pattern runs in the opposite direction too, in a way I cannot explain: within the inheritance debate itself, the mechanism is accepted without argument at one scale and becomes scandalous at another, and the difference people point to is the size of the number, which is exactly the property that is easy to put on a form and easy to divide. So my questions are these. Is there an established concept for the way the intensity — and indeed the very possibility — of a distributive argument is allocated by administrative tractability rather than causal contribution? If so, does my description actually fit it, or only approximately, and where does it strain? If the nearest neighbours are things like the old joke about the drunk looking for his keys under the streetlight because that is where the light is, or the management slogan about measuring what you want to manage, or whatever tax commentators call the question of whether a rule can be collected at all, then please tell me how each of those differs from what I have described, since all three are about searching, managing or collecting rather than about who is blamed and what counts as an injustice. And if there is no name, I would like you to supply one, with a definition sharp enough to generate verdicts: what it excludes, what its boundary cases are, and whether it generalises beyond wealth transmission — to carbon pricing rather than consumption, to AI safety debates that cluster around capabilities that can be benchmarked rather than harms that cannot, to health policy and the things that show up in a dataset. Please do not simply hand me a label to win an argument with my friend; the label is the least interesting part, and what I want is the carving — what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges lie, and what would count as a counterexample to it. I am also not after a bibliography: I have no library access and would only misremember the references, so give me the conceptual map rather than the citations.
