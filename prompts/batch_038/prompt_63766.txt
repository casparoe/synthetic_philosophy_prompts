So, bit of an odd request but here goes. I'm a postdoc and in about three weeks I'm giving a 25-minute talk at a joint philosophy-of-physics / philosophy-of-mathematics colloquium, with another 15 minutes for discussion, and my line is basically that Wigners puzzle is an artefact of how we keep score rather than a fact about the world. My argument runs roughly like this: (1) we only ever interrogate the mathematics we retained — areas that never paid off physically get abandoned, absorbed, or filed away as curiosities, and nobody gives talks about the unreasonable ineffectiveness of the maths that didn't work; (2) the celebrated matches are almost always cases where the mathematics got bent towards the physics or the physics got tidied up to suit the mathematics, over decades, by people willing to adjust both sides — Riemannian geometry, Hilbert spaces, Lie groups, all of it entered physics through a lot of manual labour rather than arriving pre-fitted; (3) the cases that look too good to be constructed that way — the same structure recurring across totally unrelated domains, the way the diffusion equation turns up in heat and in option pricing, or the way the statistics of random matrices turn up in nuclear spectra and in the zeros of the zeta function — are explained by the fact that there are far fewer simple, stable, local, linear things you can write down than there are domains to write them about, so reuse is exactly what you'd expect; and (4) therefore there's no leftover fact demanding a metaphysical explanation, and positing a special harmony between the mind and the world buys nothing at all.

The audience is the problem, because it's a mixed room. There'll be two working theoretical physicists, one of whom does precision stuff and will immediately say that selection can't touch the quantitative side — that the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron comes out right to something like a part in a trillion, that nobody tuned that, and that my story explains why we kept QED but not why the numbers land where they land. There's a fairly well-known structural realist who will want the cross-domain recurrence in (3) to be *evidence about the world's structure* rather than a counting fact, and who will argue I've explained the recurrence away at the cost of making my own view empty. There's a historian of mathematics who thinks my (2) gets the chronology backwards and will bring out the pure-first, applied-later cases, including Hardy boasting that number theory would never be good for anything. And there's a numerical analyst who does PDEs who thinks the whole framing is a category error, because in their world the mathematics mostly *doesn't* work — stiffness, blow-up, mesh dependence, turbulence, ill-posedness — and what looks like effectiveness from the outside is really just error control won at enormous cost. Plus a pile of grad students, at least one of whom is an instrumentalist and will try to claim me for their side, which I don't want.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defence of the view.
