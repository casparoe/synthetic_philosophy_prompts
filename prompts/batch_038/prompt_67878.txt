I'm the program officer at a small community foundation — I take the minutes and I don't vote, so I'm a bystander to this one. Our grants panel has six seats and eleven applications came in this round with money for four. Ingrid was out of the country for the meeting; everyone knows how she'd have gone (no on both counts). The proposal that blew up was about $40k to convert a decommissioned school bus into a mobile legal-aid clinic. Our bylaws say the panel funds an application only if its both "feasible" and "within the foundation's mission," and the mission statement is about improving health outcomes for low-income residents, so there was a genuine argument about whether legal aid counts. Votes as cast: Marta and Dev — feasible yes, mission yes. Okonkwo — feasible yes, mission no (he says legal aid isn't health). Priya — feasible no, mission yes (she says housing stability is health). Tess — no and no. So each premise carried 3–2, which under the bylaws means fund; but if you just ask the room "fund it?" it's 2 to 3 against. I think this is the doctrinal paradox or discursive dilemma or whatever it's called. Marta says the premise votes settle it. Dev says we've manufactured a group belief that three of the five people in the group reject, and now the minutes are going to record that the panel found the project feasible and within mission, which most of us don't think. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Marta: the bylaws are explicit, the funding question isn't a free-standing question anyone gets a separate vote on — it's just whatever the two premises make it. Taking a conclusion vote is voting on something already settled. Also, applicants deserve reasons, and a bare conclusion vote gives them nothing. (2) Dev: with a single yes/no question at least the winning side is a position a majority actually holds. Here the collective ends up committed to the conclusion while a majority rejects it, and the collective also holds both premises — so the board is perfectly coherent and, by three-fifths of its own lights, wrong. Plus Okonkwo, Priya and Tess get described in the record as endorsing findings they don't endorse. (3) Marta: that's an argument against majority voting in general. In any 3–2 split the two losers are "misreported." There's nothing special about this case. (4) Dev: there is. You get a different answer just by slicing the premises finer. "Feasible" was really two questions — does the plan work, and does it fit $40k and 18 months? Vote those separately and "does the plan work" passes but "does it fit the money and the timeline" fails 2–3, and the whole thing dies. So a choice made years ago about how to phrase the bylaws is quietly decisive, and nobody at the time was thinking about it. (5) Marta: then amend the bylaws to name the sub-questions in advance. But conclusion-voting is gameable too — ask "fund this one" versus "fund this one rather than renewing the clinic across town" and you get different results — except premise-voting is gameable in the open, where you can see the cut being used and object to it. And your "it was really two questions" move assumes you know what people meant. The votes are the only data we have; start substituting what people really thought for what they marked and you've got mind-reading instead of a procedure. (6) Dev: Okonkwo told him afterwards that he thinks the money and the timeline don't work at all, and he voted feasible-yes only because he didn't want the minutes saying the panel doubted the operational plan, which he thought was excellent — he figured the mission objection was the honest reason to kill it anyway. So the 3–2 on feasibility exists purely because one member answered a different question than the one on the ballot. Correct for that and both methods say don't fund, meaning there was never a paradox, just a badly worded question. Marta's rejoinder was that under her rule the panel is still bound by the votes actually cast, and that if people get to retract ballots based on what they meant we don't have a procedure, we have a seminar — and anyway if Ingrid had been off the plane both premises tie 3–3, ties fail under the bylaws, and it dies regardless, so the whole "paradox" is an artifact of one empty chair.
