I'm writing a short paper for a phil-of-science seminar and I'd like you to draft the actual essay for me, a couple thousand words or whatever it takes. The topic is the pessimistic meta-induction, but I don't want another lap around the usual suspects — phlogiston, caloric, the ether — where the essay ends up just rehearsing the pessimist's list and then the divide-and-conquer reply that seperates the parts of a dead theory that were doing the predictive work from the idle parts. I want the whole thing to turn on a case that isn't in anyone's textbook, because I think the standard cases have been cherry-picked and nobody ever says so out loud.

Here's the case. I cooked it up myself (it's a composite, details fudged), so please treat what follows as the complete historical record and don't go looking for the real theory or trying to correct the physics:

In the mid-1800s, people measuring heat coming out of the ground develop a theory on which a planet holds a fixed stock of "residuum" left over from its formation, which drains outward at a rate set by the geometry of the body — radius, thickness of the outer shell. The theory is a real quantitative machine. It yields a law relating body size to surface heat flux that holds across a wide range of bodies for about sixty years, it predicts how long volcanic activity should persist in a body of a given radius, and it hits a couple of specific cooling-rate numbers that everyone at the time finds impressive. Then radioactive decay is discovered, and within a couple of decades the residuum is written off as a fiction: the flux is now attributed entirely to ongoing decay of unstable elements in the interior, and the old size-law gets re-derived from the new physics as a sort of coincidence of the regime everyone happened to be measuring in. Textbooks list the residuum alongside phlogiston as a classic dead posit. Then a few decades after *that*, better flux measurements and isotope work show that decay accounts for only about half of what's coming out, and the missing half is precisely the primordial component the old theory posited — the size-law was right all along, and right for roughly the reason originally given. But here's the part I care about: what comes back is not a substance or a stored fluid. It's just the body's initial thermal content plus slow secular cooling, the surviving law is now derived from boundary conditions rather than from any quantity of residuum, and the specific number the old theorists took to measure the size of the stock has no counterpart at all in the new derivation.

So. The essay should argue about whether boomerang cases like this one help realism or hurt it. Give the pessimist's best counter, which I think is something like: "it came back later" is a disjunction you can always satisfy after the fact, since for any discarded posit someone can tell a story in which a descendant of it turned out to be real, so reinstatement can't carry evidential weight. Also take seriously a third position, that once you let partial and structural returns count, the pessimistic induction and the no-miracles argument both go soft in the same way and the whole debate loses its grip. And consider whether the realist even needs my case — maybe the working-posits move already does the job and the boomerang is a distraction.

Then say what the pessimistic induction looks like once you apply that criterion across the history of science rather than across the handful of famous corpses: weaker, stronger, or just differently shaped. Finish with a stated verdict on whether the possibility of reinstatement defeats the pessimistic induction, and don't end on "it's complicated."
