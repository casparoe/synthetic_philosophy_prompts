I teach an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence: sixteen students, roughly half philosophy graduate students and half CS undergraduates who have trained models and run linear probes but have never read philosophy of mind. We meet once a week for 100 minutes. In week six we take up the question of whether neural networks literally have representations, and I want to open that session with a hands-on activity rather than a discussion of the reading. Please design it.

The intellectual target is this. My students slide between at least four claims that get collapsed into "the network represents X": that X can be decoded from some internal activity; that the decoded activity is actually used, in the sense that intervening on it changes what the system does; that there is a determinate fact about which content, among rival candidates, the activity carries; and that the system behaves as though it had X, which is supposed to license the ascription whether or not any of the first three hold. They also assume the first is the interesting one, when it is the weakest. The point of the session is not to teach them what a representation is but to make the realism question bite: when a team of interpreters produces documentation of a system's internals, is that documentation a report of something already there, or an instrument for prediction that we should not mistake for a report? I want them to run into the distinctions from the inside, in that order, before anyone says the word.

So build me a black box. It must be implementable with index cards, envelopes, a cardboard divider, and the students themselves. I have one laptop but would rather not depend on it, and I want the box re-runnable in future years without software. Keep the input space small — a couple of dozen cards — and require that the box return an output within a minute. Design the internals so that the following happen because of the rules you give, not because I announce them:

1. At least one internal slot is reliably decodable by the interpreters but causally off-path: setting it by hand changes nothing about the output.
2. At least one slot carries different contents in different regions of the input space, so that any single-content gloss the interpreters write down is true only of a subset of what the box does.
3. There is a genuine tie: two rival glosses of some slot, incompatible in what they say the content is, that make exactly the same predictions about everything the interpreters can observe or intervene on. The teams should write both down, notice that neither is disconfirmed, and have to argue about whether anything could break the tie.
4. The box exhibits at least one systematic behavioral regularity that no single slot tracks, produced by the combination of others — something that tempts the interpreters to postulate a slot that does not exist, so that they discover behavior alone over-ascribes.

Structure the session around a small team that runs the box from behind the divider according to a lookup procedure you specify, and several competing teams of interpreters. Give the interpreters a budget and a price list: a generous number of free input–output queries, costlier readouts of individual slots, costlier still forced interventions on a slot. Give them a scoring scheme — course credit, candy, bragging rights, no real money — that rewards accurate documentation of the internals and penalizes confident wrong glosses, ideally by having each team stake points on held-back test inputs at the end. The budget should force trade-offs, so the teams find they cannot read everything and must theorize under an evidence constraint. Specify the lookup tables, the card contents, the budget numbers, and the scoring in enough detail that a competent TA could assemble the whole thing in an afternoon, and give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes.

Then the debrief. Map specific moments of the activity onto the positions: what an instrumentalist about representations says about the teams' documentation and why that is not obviously wrong; what the realist says, and which of the four engineered phenomena she leans on; where the off-path slot pushes toward a use-or-causal-role requirement on having a representation at all; where the multi-content slot raises the question of whether a polysemantic unit is many representations, one disjunctive representation, or none; and whether the tied pair is an indeterminacy in the facts or merely a limit on the interpreters' evidence. Be explicit about the access my interpreters lack that real interpretability researchers have — above all, any access to how the box acquired its dispositions — and tell me what a team could do with that access, and whether having it would settle the tie.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: where the box team will improvise off-script, where a clever interpreter will short-circuit an intended discovery, where the scoring will reward the wrong behavior, and how to patch each. And be frank about the one or two features of actual neural networks this apparatus cannot reproduce at all, which I will have to supply by talking rather than staging — I would rather know that beforehand than discover it in the room.
