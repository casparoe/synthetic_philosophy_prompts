I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student, mostly mind/language, and I've been moonlighting on a small evals-and-documentation project at a lab that builds large language models. Half my job is arguing with ML people about whether it's OK to write "the model believes the user is angry" in a technical report; the other half is watching everyone do it anyway and not see what the fuss is about. Someone on my team joked that we should just publish an official house style guide settling the matter once and for all, and I want to actually write that joke document as satire — but the kind that's funny to people who know the literature, not the kind where the joke is just "philosophers argue about nothing and slow everyone down." Act as a professional philosopher who has also spent a few years writing internal engineering docs, and think carefully before drafting: put a few lines of brainstorming at the very top naming which specific features of the practice you're exaggerating and why they're real features rather than ones you invented, then write the piece. I'd much rather have the exaggeration be a plausible escalation of something that actually happens than a strawman.

So: write it as if it's a genuine internal document — something like the Meridian Language Systems Style Guide for Attributing Mental States to Models, Version 2.3, with an owner line like "Applied Epistemology & Model Documentation" and an effective date. Numbered rules, approved vs. deprecated phrasings, a decision tree, a short FAQ, at least one sidebar. Around 800–1000 words. The voice should be deadpan corporate-technical-writing, exactly how real style guides sound ("Do not use X. Use Y instead."). Never wink at the reader — the irony has to live entirely in the content, and a reader should be able to tell what's being ironized without you ever signaling it.

The target is the practice, and specifically the unexamined slide between treating belief talk about models as a harmless predictive shorthand and treating it as a claim about what the system is really like. The rules should encode actual philosophical positions and the friction between them, so that someone who knows the stance framework recognizes the guide trying — and failing — to legislate a dispute that isn't settleable by fiat. A few things I'd want in there: a rule asserting that adopting the intentional stance is a predictive strategy and not an ontological commitment, sitting uncomfortably next to a rule that treats belief attributions as fact-checkable and subject to correction; a qualification rule requiring that a system count as an intentional system — i.e. that attributing beliefs and desires beat the design stance and the physical stance at predicting its behavior — before any mental-state language is licensed at all, with the awkward consequence that whether the model has beliefs becomes a benchmark-dependent, quarterly-revisable matter that can be revoked; a holism rule saying you can't attribute one belief without attributing a sufficiently coherent surrounding set, colliding with the fact that the same checkpoint gives contradictory answers depending on how you phrase the prompt, so the guide needs to distinguish "belief of the checkpoint" from "belief of the context window" and assign ownership of each; something on desires and the ubiquitous "wants to be helpful" locution, including whether the training objective is allowed to count as a desire or has to be filed under a different heading; something on the personal/subpersonal distinction and which of the two levels the guide is even regulating; and an escalation procedure for the case where the design stance and the intentional stance disagree about what the system will do next, with a named committee that has to pick a winner. Please also include a deprecated-phrasings table of genuinely tempting things people write ("the model understands," "the model knows it failed," "the model is confused," "the model refused because it believed…") each paired with the bureaucratically correct replacement — and make at least one replacement so hedged that it turns out to say nothing at all. That should be the quiet punchline, and it shouldn't be flagged as one.

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list — I have the books. I just want to know where it's safe and where you're guessing.
