I read a piece in a magazine over the weekend arguing that we should replace most elections with citizens' assemblies chosen by lot, and it made the historical case like this:

"Athens ran itself on lotteries for two centuries: the Council of 500, the big juries, and just about every magistracy were filled by random draw for a single year, and no man could hold the same post twice. It wasn't mainly a device for keeping factions out of office -- Aristotle's arguement was that election is oligarchic by its very nature, since it always rewards wealth and name recognition, so a city that votes for its rulers isn't really a democracy at all. That's why sortition is the term-limits reform we should have had from the start: single non-renewable terms mean no incumbents, no campaigns and no money in politics, because there's no next election to worry about. And since a randomly picked body is a statistical mirror of the public, what it decides after hearing the evidence just is what the public would have decided. Sortition doesn't cap the career politician -- it deletes the job."
