**Context and purpose.** I teach a graduate methods seminar on inference from material remains. The recurring failure mode in the room is that students argue about conclusions ("these objects are accounts" / "these are offerings" / "these are dice") while remaining unaware that the real disagreement concerns which *classes* of evidence are allowed to license which *kinds* of claim. I want a sorting instrument that surfaces the inferential commitments underneath the conclusions, so that I can put two students who reached the same leaf on opposite sides of a table when their reasons differ, and put two students who reached different leaves together when their reasons are nearly the same.

**The case.** The site is constructed for teaching and corresponds to no actual excavation; that is deliberate, since I do not want the exercise to become a test of recall about a famous real assemblage. Present it to students in exactly these terms.

Tell Ardum is a mound settlement in a semi-arid river valley, occupied continuously for roughly six centuries, with no deciphered writing, no monumental figurative art, and no building that can be securely identified as a temple. The excavations have produced the following observations.

1. Approximately 14,000 small fired-clay objects fall into 23 recurring shapes. Eleven shapes are common and standardized in dimension to within five percent; twelve shapes are rare, occur only in the last two centuries of occupation, and vary widely in size.
2. Two of the common shapes bear incised marks on one face. The marks draw on a repertory of about 30 signs; no object bears more than four signs; roughly 300 marked objects are known. No two marked objects bear the same sequence, except that approximately 150 of them form pairs whose sequences differ in exactly one sign.
3. The common shapes occur in every excavated house, in middens, in floor-fill, and in a large building near the site center. In that building they occur in concentrations on plastered floors and inside one collapsed ceramic vessel. The building has no hearths, no storage pits, unusually thick walls, and a central location; it is conventionally described in the report as non-domestic.
4. The rare shapes occur only inside that building, only in its final phase, and in deposits beneath the last floor.
5. About a third of the common objects show edge rounding and surface polish consistent with repeated handling. The marked faces of marked objects show no wear, and the marks were made before firing.
6. Some objects carry ochre residues on unmarked faces. The vessel mentioned in (3) also contained charred grain.
7. Forty kilometers away, in a valley with no other evidence of contact with Tell Ardum — no shared pottery fabrics, no shared burial practice — a hoard of some 300 objects was recovered from a collapsed rock-shelter with no datable stratigraphy. It includes three of the common shapes and one rare shape bearing two marks from the repertory in (2).
8. The shape repertory is stable for the first four centuries. In the last two centuries, two common shapes disappear and one new shape appears. Marks appear only in the last century and a half.
9. No object has been found in a burial. No object bears a suspension hole or any attachment wear.
10. Experimental replication shows the common shapes can be pressed from simple molds in under a minute each; the rare shapes cannot be molded and require hand-forming.
11. Fewer than two percent of recovered objects are fragmented, and no molds, wasters, or kiln failures have been found anywhere on or off the site.

**Deliverable.** A decision tree of exactly seven nodes that sorts a respondent from these observations to a named interpretive position. Five of the nodes are mandated, because they are where my students actually part company; you choose the remaining two.

- (a) Whether the standardization in (1) requires centralized control, or can arise from convention, or from functional requirement alone.
- (b) Whether depositional context — midden, floor, sub-floor, vessel — discriminates among uses here, given (11).
- (c) Whether the marks in (2) encode language, encode non-linguistic symbolic information, or encode nothing.
- (d) What the off-site hoard in (7) licenses: shared meaning across distance, movement of objects without movement of meaning, or independent convergence.
- (e) Whether the production evidence in (10) and (11) bears on manufacture or on curation and deposition.
- (f) and (g): your choice. One of these two should concern whether the "non-domestic" description of the large building in (3) functions as a premise of the tree or as a conclusion that the tree itself must test.

Each node must offer two or three answer options, phrased as questions a respondent could answer from the observation list without having heard of any of this literature, and phrased so that the wording does not reveal which position it leads to. No node may use vocabulary that names a leaf, and no node may simply ask which conclusion the respondent prefers. Under each node, cite the observation numbers that do the work, and mark with an asterisk any node whose answer depends on a background assumption imported from outside this evidence set, stating the assumption in one clause.

**Leaves.** The tree must be able to deliver each of the following. You may rename them, but not drop or merge any.

1. Quantitative record-keeping: the objects denote quantities of commodities, and the system is a developmental precursor to writing.
2. Bilateral counterfoil: each marked object was one half of a pair held by two parties, recording an agreement rather than a state revenue.
3. Votive: the objects were made for deposition, and the concentrations and sub-floor caches are offerings.
4. Aleatory: the objects were thrown, and their standardization is what fairness in throwing requires.
5. Implement: the objects are tools of a craft or domestic activity, their shapes dictated by use, and our typology wrongly unifies them.
6. Emblematic: the marks denote group, household, or owner identity — non-linguistic signs, not words or numbers.
7. Deflationary aggregate: there is no single system; the shape classes are artifacts of modern classification and the objects belong to several unrelated practices.
8. Licensed agnosticism: this observation set underdetermines all of the above, and no leaf is currently warranted.

For each leaf, give: the answer patterns that reach it; one sentence on its most awkward commitment, naming the observation it must explain away, dismiss as coincidence, or leave dangling; which observations it treats as decisive and which it treats as noise; the single further piece of evidence about this assemblage that would most strengthen it; and the single one that would most damage it. These last two must be evidence obtainable in principle from further excavation or analysis here, not from analogies to known sites.

**The logical space.** Before locating the positions, characterize the space the nodes generate. Since full enumeration is unwieldy, describe its structure instead: which answers make which downstream answers unreachable, and why. Then identify at least six regions of the space that no position occupies, and diagnose each as incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or merely unexplored, with a sentence of justification for each diagnosis.

**Inseparability.** Flag every pair of leaves that no answer pattern in your tree actually separates. For each pair, either state what further evidence would separate them or concede that the distinction is verbal as applied to this assemblage. Do not paper over these; if the tree turns out to sort respondents by temperament rather than by position along some axis, say so.

**Order-dependence.** State which nodes must precede which and why, and whether any permissible reordering would assign the same respondent to a different leaf. If your tree is order-sensitive, identify the sensitive pairs.

**Temperaments.** Close with three or four higher-order profiles of inferential style that cut across the leaves — for example, privileging form over context, requiring a functional explanation before a symbolic one, treating typologies as real, tolerating underdetermination — each with the tell-tale answer pattern that reveals it.

**Constraints.** Write in Markdown, using an indented outline for the tree and tables for the leaf summaries. No bibliography, no citations, and no naming of real sites, scholars, or schools; where you think a real debate is analogous, describe the reasoning rather than its pedigree. Aim for roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words. If, having built the instrument, you believe one leaf is in fact best supported by these observations, say so in a final paragraph and argue it briefly — but your verdict must not be inferable from the phrasing of any node, and the instrument itself must remain neutral.
