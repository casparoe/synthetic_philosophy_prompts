I run a small group working on diagnostic criteria and disease classification, and I read in the philosophy of science, though I am not a professional philosopher. I am drafting a paper and have hit a naming problem I cannot resolve on my own.

Over the past decade I have become preoccupied with a pattern that recurs in my field and, so far as I can tell, in every other classifying science. Stated plainly:

A kind is picked out by a criterion — a measured quantity above a threshold, a structural feature, an assay result. The criterion is then refined: the assay becomes more sensitive, the imaging resolves smaller lesions, a consensus panel lowers the cut point, or the criterion is replaced by something more mechanistic. The extension of the kind changes, sometimes abruptly and by a great deal. What interests me is not the change but what happens afterwards. The inferences the kind used to license — the prognosis attached to a diagnosis, the treatments shown to work, the aetiological story, the prevalence figures in textbooks — were established on the old extension, under the old criterion. After the change they continue to be applied at undiminished strength to a population that now includes a large number of members who would not have qualified before, and nothing is re-derived. When the transferred inferences fail, moreover, they fail at the margins, among precisely the newly admitted members; and the failure is then reported as evidence that the kind is heterogeneous, that there are subtypes, that severity gradients matter — never as evidence that the boundary was moved. The criterion can be revised again the following year, and the revision costs the kind nothing.

Two instances, from opposite directions. In the late 1990s the fasting-glucose cut point for diabetes was lowered from 140 to 126 mg/dL, and a band of prediabetes was later created below it; in 2017 the U.S. cardiology threshold for hypertension moved from 140/90 to 130/80, reclassifying on the order of thirty million adults at a stroke. In both cases the relation between the measured quantity and subsequent events was continuous, with no discontinuity at the new cut point, and yet the label, the guideline, the drug indication, and the identity of the patient category all moved with it. The trials that established the benefit of treatment were conducted largely under the older criterion; their results are now cited for the new population. In molecular biology, the number of human genes has been estimated at around a hundred thousand, then around twenty thousand, and then substantially upward again once non-coding transcripts were admitted: the count follows the definition. The inferential habits attached to the word were formed under the classical conception of a gene as a locus whose variants produce a trait, and they are now applied to whatever the current operationalisation yields, without re-examination.

Against these, a contrast case that I suspect is doing more work in my argument than I can justify. When it turned out that atomic weight did not individuate the chemical elements, the term was re-anchored to atomic number, and the periodic table together with all of chemistry's inductions survived intact. That re-anchoring seems to have cost nothing because the new criterion explained why the old one had worked as well as it did.

So, my questions.

1. Does an established concept exist — in the philosophy of science, in the philosophy of medicine, in the methodological vocabulary of epidemiology itself, or anywhere else — for this configuration as a whole? I know of two neighbours and I think neither is it. Clinicians speak of overdiagnosis and of diagnostic creep, but those concern the harm done to individuals who are labelled and would never have been symptomatic, not the epistemic bookkeeping. Philosophers discuss what a term refers to when its extension is revised, but that question is about what the word picks out, not about whether an inductive licence earned by the old extension may be spent on the new one.

2. If several candidates fit part of the description, rank them, and say for each what it captures and what it misses.

3. State the mechanism so as to separate three things that are usually run together: the change of extension, the transfer of warrant, and the absorption of failure. My working hypothesis is that the first is often harmless and that the second and third together constitute the real phenomenon; tell me if that decomposition is wrong.

4. Boundary cases. Is there a principled criterion distinguishing a re-anchoring of the kind the chemical element enjoyed from one of the kind I have been describing — something like whether the new criterion explains the success of the old? Or is the difference only a matter of whose interests are served, and of how much of the old warrant happens to survive by luck?

5. If there is no established term, coin one, define it tightly enough that a reader could decide whether a given case falls under it, and say what it excludes.

I am not asking for a bibliography and would prefer not to receive one; if a term is genuinely established, I want the term and the tradition it belongs to, and I will chase the references myself. Conversely, if you are not confident that a candidate term is established, please say so explicitly rather than offering something that merely sounds like a term of art — a plausible name would send me on a wasteful search. What I need is the carving: whether the thing I have described is one phenomenon or three that happen to co-occur, where its edges lie, and what it is not.
