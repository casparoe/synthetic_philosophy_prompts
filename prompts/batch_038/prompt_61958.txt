Last month my continuing-education reading group took up the second part of Franklin's *Autobiography*, and the discussion of his project for arriving at moral perfection was effectively closed by one member. He quoted the customer in the ax-grinding parable — "Yes, says the Man; but—I think I like a speckled Ax best" — and drew from it the conclusion that Franklin's own settled verdict was that the whole enterprise had been misconceived: that a mixed character is not a consolation prize but a preferable one, and that the ledger of thirteen virtues was itself the source of the trouble. I was unpersuaded but could not say why, and I would like to come to our next session with the objection properly articulated.

Could you set out, as a list, the distinct and precise claims that this line — and the nickname "the speckled ax" that has grown up around it — can be taken to make? For each reading, please give the thesis in a sentence or two, an assessment of whether it is truistic, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and a note on whether Franklin's text actually supports it. I would especially like the readings separated along these axes:

- **Whose voice.** The phrase occurs twice in the passage: once in the mouth of the ax-buyer, and once in Franklin's remark that many who lack his method have given up the struggle and "concluded that a speckled Ax was best." What is the difference between these two occurrences, and does either commit Franklin to the conclusion?
- **Report or endorsement.** Whether the line expresses a temptation Franklin entertains and reports — he introduces the surrounding considerations with "For something that pretended to be Reason," among them that "such extreme Nicety as I exacted of myself might be a kind of Foppery in Morals," and that a perfect character might be envied and hated while a benevolent man should "allow a few Faults in himself, to keep his Friends in Countenance" — or a considered judgment he adopts.
- **Scope.** Whether the concession concerns one virtue (he calls himself "incorrigible with respect to Order"), the weekly-examination method, or the ideal of a faultless character as such.
- **The sense of "best."** Prudential, social, aesthetic, or moral; and whether it is a claim about what is preferable or a rationalization formed under fatigue, in the manner of an adaptive preference.
- **Attainability versus worth.** How the claim that an ideal cannot be reached relates to the claim that pursuing it is worthwhile. His later statement that he was "by the Endeavor a better and a happier Man" plainly does not sit at the same level as the ax-buyer's preference, and I suspect the comparison he draws to learners imitating engraved copies is doing structural work here — work that the popular retelling drops. Please say what that analogy adds that a bare appeal to effort does not.

Close by telling me which of the readings, if any, would in fact license the conclusion my fellow member drew from the parable, and which reading is most likely intended by those who now use the phrase as a tagline for abandoning a self-improvement regime altogether.
