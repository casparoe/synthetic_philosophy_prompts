I'm doing a semester abroad in Leiden taking an EU law and politics course, and there's one thing about accession I can't stop chewing on. When a country joins, it has to take over the entire existing body of EU law — decades of treaties, regulations, decisions of the Court, the whole accumulated thing — chapter by chapter, and what's actually up for negotiation is basically the pace and some technical transitional arrangements. A candidate state cannot say "we'll take the single market and the competition rules but not the justice and home affairs stuff." It's presented, and I think genuinely understood by the people doing it, as non-negotiable: you accept the order as it stands or you don't come in. Meanwhile the countries already inside have picked up all sorts of carved-out exceptions over the years — Denmark kept an opt-out from the euro and a pretty rigid one from justice and home affairs (it voted in 2015 to keep that one rather than switch to a case-by-case opt-in, and only dropped its defence opt-out recently), Ireland stays outside Schengen, and Sweden has simply never entered the exchange rate mechanism, which means it can postpone euro adoption indefinitely even though the treaties treat adoption as an obligation for every member except Denmark. And there's an exit clause for leaving the whole thing, but no mechanism anywhere for a member to withdraw from one policy area. So the shape of the practice is: total acceptance on the way in, piecemeal exceptions once you're in, and all-or-nothing on the way out.

My flatmate says this is just bargaining leverage, the incumbents are protecting themselves, but that seems like an answer about why it happened rather than about what the practice assumes. I want the second kind of answer. What view of sovereignty does a rule like this presuppose — specifically, what does it assume about whether being sovereign means being the author of the rules you're subject to, or whether it might mean something more like the capacity to bind yourself to rules someone else wrote? Is the implicit picture that the EU is a treaty among states who consent, or a standing normative order that admits new participants on its own terms, closer to a club with existing bylaws or to a contract you can only sign as drafted? And what does the fact that exceptions exist for old members but not for new ones presuppose about legitimacy over time — is consent treated as a one-off act that keeps working forever afterwards, or as something that has to be renewed? I keep having the sense that the practice is quietly committed to some answer to the democratic deficit complaint, one that nobody ever states out loud.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and separate what the practice strictly needs from what merely fits it comfortably — I suspect there's more than one account of sovereignty that would license the accession rule, and I want to know which ones are actually ruled out rather than just unfashionable. Then tell me whether the three pieces are consistent with each other: does the no-exceptions-on-entry rule sit comfortably with the accumulated opt-outs for insiders and the all-or-nothing exit clause, or do those three presuppose rival theories of membership that the Union is living with rather than resolving? It would help if you said where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where the same institutional facts would look identical under two very different theories and we'd need extra evidence to tell them apart. And finally, suppose we replaced the implicit view with the one critics of the democratic deficit usually gesture at — that being subject to rules you had no ongoing hand in making is the actual problem, so legitimacy requires continued authorship and not just an initial yes. What would have to change institutionally for that to be real rather than decorative? Dont give me a list of treaty articles to read, I want the reasoning about what the practice commits us to.
