So our department runs this tiny internal scheme every autumn — £3,500 and one term of teaching buy-out, and you have to put two pages in front of a mixed panel. This year the panel is two decision theorists, a philosopher of probability, and (unluckily for me) a statistician who has already told me in the coffee room that the whole business is a mathematicians' parlour game with no consequences for anyone who ever has to make a choice. I want to use the money for a chapter's worth of work on the Pasadena game, and I need help turning my half-thoughts into an actual proposal rather than a summary of a debate.

The bit I already have clear in my head, so you can take it as given and don't need to re-explain it at length: fair coin tossed until heads first comes up; if heads lands on toss n you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and lose 2^n/n when n is even — so £2 up front, −£2, +£8/3, −£4, and on. Multiply by the probabilities and you get 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯, which is the alternating harmonic series, ln 2 if you add it up in the order the tosses happen. But it's only conditionally convergent, so by rearranging the terms you can make the sum come out as any real number you like, or diverge to ±∞. Hence the complaint that the game has no expectation at all, only an expectation-relative-to-an-ordering. And hence the usual moves: truncate at toss N and take the limit, or say flatly that the expectation fails to exist and that's an acceptable verdict, or abandon real-valued expectations for some comparative notion, or insist the game is too pathological to be a real objection. I'm not interested in re-running any of those.

Here's the angle I want the proposal built around, and it's the part I can't yet state crisply. Taken at face value, the rearrangement result doesn't make the game's value vague, it makes it maximally indeterminate: the set of values you can generate is the whole real line, which is not an informative answer to anything, and a treatment that hands back "any number whatsoever" looks like it has conceded the point rather than solved it. But that set is generated by allowing *arbitrary* orderings of the payoffs, and it isn't obvious that arbitrary orderings are the ones a decision theorist should be quantifying over. The coin tosses come in a temporal order; the game is *described* in that order; truncation respects it. So my half-formed thought is that the live question isn't "what number does this game deserve?" but "which orderings of a gamble's outcomes are admissible for the purposes of ranking it against other gambles, and can that notion of admissibility be cashed out without smuggling in a summation rule you were supposed to be deriving?" — with the corollary that what decision theory actually needs from these cases is a transitive, dominance-respecting ranking, and a ranking doesn't have to come from a real-valued expectation in the first place. I'd like the proposal to press both halves and to be honest about the worry that they're just the "no expectation" view wearing a new hat, and about the other worry, which is that admissibility can only be motivated by a story about what makes one ordering the real one.

Deliverable: the two-page proposal itself, in British English. I want the question stated so precisely that a reader could tell whether a given piece of work answers it or not; a short account of why the four moves above leave it open (positions and arguments only, no bibliography — I'll put the citations in myself, and please don't invent any); what I'd actually do with four months of buy-out, roughly month by month, including which toy cases I'd work through before touching the general question; what a positive result would look like on the page, i.e. what theorem-shaped or argument-shaped thing would make the panel think the money was well spent; and what would count as failure, with the specific failure modes named — the admissible set collapsing to a single value, the ranking turning out intransitive or dominance-violating, the whole thing collapsing back into "there is no expectation", and the circularity worry. Please also give me a paragraph aimed squarely at the statistician: why anyone should care about a ranking rule for games nobody can actually play. Then a candid note on scope — what I should cut if the panel signals the project is too big for one person in one term. Keep it under two pages of prose, no headings deeper than two levels.
