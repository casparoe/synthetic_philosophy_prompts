ok so i run an informal reading group, about 14 people, two hours on a sunday afternoon in a library room. mix of backgrounds: three or four are EA-adjacent and think in expected value, two are economists, a couple lean virtue ethics and are suspicious of the first group, rest are just interested. nobody's a specialist and i'm not their teacher, i'm the person who books the room. the two camps have had this fight before, roughly along the lines of shut-up-and-multiply vs some-things-dont-reduce-to-numbers, and both sides think they already won.

topic for next session is compromise between value systems — specifically the gains-from-trade move, the idea that two people who disagree about what's good can both come out ahead by trading on that disagreement instead of splitting the difference or fighting it out, and the reasons people give for refusing (integrity, the expressive content of a compromise, corruption of the thing traded, cheap talk about how strongly you feel).

design me something they *play*, not a discussion i lead. what i want them to walk away having felt, in this order:

1. that trading on moral disagreement is a real pareto improvement and refusing it has a cost you can see
2. that some trades nonetheless land as betrayal rather than as a win, and that this can't just be waved off as squeamishness
3. and the one i actually care about: that compromising changes what the compromiser cares about, so a trade that looked like a win ex ante may not be a win once you account for who you became by making it. i want them to hit 3 in play, by getting burned by it, with nobody saying the words out loud.

my half-baked idea is a rule where after each round the intesity values on your card drift a little toward the group average, but i don't know if that's the right mechanism — make it work properly or replace it with something better. cards do need some way to express how strongly a position cares about each issue, since claimed intensity is the obvious place for cheap talk to creep in, and i want that visible rather than solved away.

hard constraints: no lecture beforehand and no naming of terms until the debrief (no "gains from trade", no "endogenous preferences"); printable on one home printer in under 20 mins; no real money, tokens/beans/paper slips fine; scoring simple enough that i'm not doing arithmetic aloud; i should have almost nothing to say during play. don't bother with a bibliography or reading list, i'm not assigning anything, i just want the activity.

the value systems on the cards have to be positions actual people in that room could hold — no cartoon villainy — and at least one has to be something most of the room will find genuinely repulsive to trade on. if every value is comfortably tradeable it's just a bargaining game and nobody learns anything.

also: it should be possible in ordinary play, not rigged by me, for the game to end with several players worse off by their own lights than if they'd refused to trade at all. i want that reachable without anyone noticing the trap until it's sprung.

deliverables: setup and materials list; timeline in minutes; the actual printed text of whatever cards or handouts you want; full scoring rules including the drift mechanic; debrief questions mapped onto 1, 2 and 3 above with a note on where each term finally gets introduced; a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time i run this and what to do about it in the moment; and an honest paragraph on which feature of moral compromise the game structurally cannot reproduce and that i'll have to supply by talking.

then, as the very last line, exactly this: \answer{lands} or \answer{flops} — your honest call on whether the drift mechanic (or whatever you replaced it with) will actually produce discovery 3 in a room like mine, with one sentence on why.
