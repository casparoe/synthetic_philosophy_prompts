I am trained in political theory, not historiography, and I am preparing for a seminar in which a general-interest review essay on the humanities and post-truth has been assigned as background. One passage in that essay concerns the long-running dispute over objectivity in history. The passage runs as follows:

Modern history as a discipline begins with Ranke, who insisted that the historian's only business is to show how things really were: to let the documents speak, to extinguish every opinion of one's own, and to resist the temptation to measure the past against the standards of the present. It is a noble ideal and an incoherent one, as E. H. Carr demonstrated. Facts do not present themselves to the historian; the historian goes looking for them, and in settling which of the traces the past has left behind deserve to be counted as historical facts, he settles, in that same act, what the past consists of. Since those standards of significance shift from age to age, the past that survives into books is always the past the present has some use for. Hayden White completed the demolition by showing that historical writing is a literary artefact. Because the historian must shape real events into a story, and because stories have shapes — openings, reversals, closures — that no sequence of events possesses on its own, the account produced is not made true or false by the events it recounts; it stands to those events as a novel stands to the world it depicts. What divides one history of the French Revolution from another is therefore not the evidence but the kind of plot the historian has reached for, a choice that is aesthetic and moral at bottom rather than evidential. The upshot is that history has no firmer claim on truth than historical fiction, which may explain why the discipline has been so slow to repudiate distortions of the record that serve causes its members approve of.

My own inclination, for what it is worth, is that Carr's point about selection is true but unsurprising, and that White has been over-read by people who need him to be a relativist. I cannot tell whether that is a defensible place to land or merely a comfortable prejudice held by someone outside the field.

Could you audit the passage claim by claim? For each substantive assertion, tell me whether it is accurate, a fair simplification, a distortion of the position it describes, or simply false; and where it is a distortion, say what the figure in question actually maintained and what a careful defender of that figure would object to in the essayist's wording. Four points in particular I would like settled: whether the Ranke described here is the Ranke specialists write about or a stock character, and how much of the caricature is inherited from later polemic rather than invented by this author; whether Carr is being credited with a thesis about the constitution of the past that he would have endorsed, and whether demolished is the right word for his relation to Ranke, given what Carr took himself to be arguing against; whether White's argument is properly described as showing that historical narrative is not the sort of thing that can be true or false, or whether the passage collapses a distinction he was at pains to preserve, especially in light of how deliberately provocative his own formulations could be; and whether the final inference — from the premises about Carr and White to a claim about history's standing relative to historical fiction, and from there to an explanation of the discipline's alleged slowness to condemn useful distortions — is supported by anything in the accurate parts of the passage.

Beyond the errors, I would like you to identify the places where the essayist is doing something subtler than getting a claim wrong: where a modest thesis has been quietly strengthened, or where two scepticisms of quite different kinds have been stacked as though they were successive steps down a single slope. Where you think a philosopher of history and a practising historian would grade the same sentence differently, please say so and say why. Close with a short verdict on how much of the passage survives correction, and on whether my inclination about Carr and White is one I can defend in seminar or should abandon.
