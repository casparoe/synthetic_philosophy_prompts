I spent 34 years doing analytical chemistry, mostly trace metals in groundwater, and a good chunk of that was arguing with people about whether a signal was real or an artifact of how the method was set up. Since retiring I've been auditing a couple of intro philosophy courses and running an occassional reading group in my garage with four or five other retirees. Last month we read Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" (1982) and it split the group roughly down the middle. I came out firmly on one side, and before I keep defending it I want the position stress-tested properly rather than cheered on.

Here's what I actually believe, and please attack my version, not the more famous one. I don't think the problem with maximal moral goodness is that there are many legitimate ways to live and sainthood crowds out the aesthetically interesting ones — that's the pluralist route and I find it mushy. Mine is narrower and I'd like to think more testable: the moral saint is undesirable because the project is structurally self-undermining, not because it's merely one option among many. Roughly, moral commitment has returns that rise, flatten, and then go negative — and the negative stretch isn't only about the saint's own life getting worse, though I think it does, but about the moral output itself. The attentional and motivational resources that extreme moral effort draws on come out of the same pool that sustains the ordinary attachments — friendships, a hobby, work you care about for its own sake — which keep a person going and keep them honest about what is actually happening out there. So at the limit you get someone producing less good, slower, less reliably, and with worse calibration about where the good even is. If that's right, you don't need value pluralism to get the anti-saint conclusion, and a straight utilitarian should sign on too.

What would count against it? Sort it the way I'd sort evidence in the lab: what would actually kill the thesis versus what would just make it embarrassing. Specifically — (a) which findings, from psychology or wherever, would be decisive if they came out the other way, for instance if very high donors and very high-effort do-gooders just don't show the burnout, attrition and mis-calibration pattern I'm assuming, or if they do show it but only becuase of contingent features of how those communities happen to be organised; (b) which purely philosophical arguments bite without any empirical premise at all, including the worry that I've smuggled in the conclusion by building "sustained by ordinary attachments" into my account of what morality demands of a person; (c) whether some rival theory — one that says the saint's life is worse but that this is morally irrelevant, say, or one that denies the trade-offs exist in the first place — predicts something mine doesn't, and what that something would be; and (d) honestly, whether I've insulated myself. I suspect I have: I can always say the negative-return regime hasn't been reached yet, or that any counterexample you produce is a lucky saint rather than a typical one. Tell me whether those two moves are legitimate for a claim of this kind or whether they amount to unfalsifiable curve-fitting, and if they're a problem, sketch what the minimum-risk version of my thesis would look like. Finally, and this matters most to me: which of these would a friendly defender of my position concede as genuinely counting against them, and which would they wave off? I'd rather be handed the concessions than the heckles.
