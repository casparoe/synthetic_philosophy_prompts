Three years into a novel that's really nine linked stories about one rust-belt river town, and every story is supposed to be a different species of collective self-harm — the thing where everybody does the reasonable thing and the town ends up worse off than if they'd all done nothing. Problem is I can't tell my species apart anymore. So here's the list of situations in the book plus two I cut, with my gut verdict on each, and I want to know what principle, if any, I've been applying without knowing it. Because if there isn't one, I have to admit I wrote the same story nine times in different hats.

1. Two grocery families on the same street. Both stay shut Sundays for forty years. Then one opens, so the other has to. Within a decade nobody gets a day off and both make less than before. Trap, obviously — but the weakest kind in the book, becuase one phone call would have fixed it, and I'm still a little embarrassed to have it as my opener.

2. Every farm along the river starts pumping groundwater for a crop that pays triple. Water table drops. Each farmer's well is fine right up until the summer it isn't. Trap, and a real one.

3. Same valley, same crop, different reason: everyone switches because they simply want the money, and they'd want it even if the water never ran out. I don't want to call this a trap. It's people getting what they wanted, and the wanting doesn't change. But 2 and 3 are the same physical event and I've given them opposite verdicts, and all I can say for myself is that in 3 nobody is being held hostage by anybody.

4. Every contractor in town knows the cheap insulation is a fire risk. Say so and you lose every bid to the ones who don't. Nobody says. Trap, and worse than 2, because no handshake fixes this one — telling the truth is unilateral disarmament.

5. Every family pays for test prep. The ranking doesn't move. Every family is poorer. Nobody can stop, because stopping means your kid falls. Trap, and I think the most inescapable one in the book — and yet nobody in it is harmed in any way you could point at and measure. The harm is made entirely of positions.

6. The mill closes because of a price move in a market nobody in town touches. Everyone's worse off. Nobody defected from anything. Not a trap, just weather. I'm confident here and I don't expect to be argued out of it.

7. The two weekly papers both go free online to win readers, the ad money evaporates, and now neither can go back, because readers have learned it costs nothing. Trap. But in an earlier draft they did go back after two bad years and everybody shrugged and it was fine — and that version doesn't feel like a trap to me at all. Same setup, same choices, exit available. So apparently I think irreversibility is part of what makes the thing a trap, which I don't think I believed when I started.

8. The one I keep going back and forth on. The town solves 4 with gossip: everyone knows everyone's work, and shame keeps the insulation honest for a generation. Then the shame machinery keeps running after it's needed and starts getting used on things nobody meant it for, and no one can dismantle it without letting 4 come back. Is that one trap, two traps, or a trap that was never actually escaped? I've rewritten this chapter eleven times.

What I want:

- Is there a single principle — conditions stated tightly enough that I could run it on a situation I haven't thought of — that gets all eight, or nearly all? Give me the conditions one at a time and say which case drags each one into the account. I want to see the load-bearing parts.
- If two rival principles between them cover everything, give me both, say which you'd bet on, and name the case that would decide between them.
- Where a condition has to stay vague, say so and say what makes it vague. An honest "this is a judgment call" beats a fake threshold every time.
- Then tell me which of my verdicts it can't save. I expect 3 and 5 to be in trouble — 3 because the entire difference between it and 2 seems to live inside somebody's head, and 5 because I can't say what the loss is. If one of them has to go, say which and why and what the book loses. If the principle can keep them, tell me what it costs to keep them; I assume the price is complexity and I want to know how much.
- Run it on two situations I haven't listed, ideally ones that would wreck the book if they were real: one the principle calls a trap and my gut says isn't, one my gut calls a trap and the principle says isn't.
- And whatever it says about 8, say it plainly.

No bibliography and no reading list, please — I've read the essay everyone sends me and I'm not going to look anything up while I'm drafting. I want your reasoning, not your sources. If a term of art saves you a paragraph, use it and define it in a clause.
