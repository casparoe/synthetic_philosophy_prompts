I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on causation, and I've got a slightly weird request. Act as someone who knows the metaphysics of causation cold but also writes decent literary fiction — think a municipal bureaucracy rendered by Kafka with a law review at his elbow. First, spend a few lines brainstorming out loud how to stage each technical problem as an actual plot event rather than a lecture, then write me a short story, maybe 1200–1600 words.

Setting: an office that certifies causal claims so liability payouts can go out. The old procedure is Lewis-flavored — locate the nearest world where the defendant didn't act, see if the harm still lands. A new hire is trying to replace it with an interventionist protocol: no verdict until you've named a variable set and shown that an intervention on one variable wiggle the other.

The case that breaks them should do three things at once. It should be one where the old test denies causation even though the harm came right through the defendant's hands, because a backup would have finished the job anyway. It should also involve a double prevention — someone disabling a safeguard that would have stopped a harm that was still coming from elsewhere — where the old test cheerfully affirms causation and everyone in the room feels that's too cheap. And the kicker I really want: the same claim certified true relative to one variable set and false relative to a rival set that just adds a single variable, with the adjudicator having to decide whether that's a finding about the world or a confession that "cause" was never one relation.

Keep the jargon out of the dialogue — the reader should get the philosophy through the plot and the paperwork. Give me a twist in the last few paragraphs that forces a reread of who caused what.
