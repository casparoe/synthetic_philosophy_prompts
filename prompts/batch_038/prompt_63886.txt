I produce a monthly podcast on the philosophy of mind for listeners who have read a bit of the literature but aren't specialists, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has built a substantive metaphysical position out of Hofstadter's careenium imagery — the picture of a cranium full of chaotic, careening micro-events whose clustering yields symbol-like entities that collide, trigger one another, and set off cascades. She calls her view "symbolic traction," and it makes three claims that I want the interview to test rather than applaud. First, that in a mature symbol-using brain the symbol level is not a convenient redescription of the careening underneath: the counterfactuals we actually rely on when we predict and explain what a person will do next are licensed by symbol-level relations, and the neural description fails to license them under interventions that hold the symbol level fixed. Second, that this is compatible with the causal closure of the physics, because symbol-level and neural-level causation don't compete for the same work — the symbol-level relation is what makes one neural cause the difference-making cause it is, so the higher level selects among realizations rather than adding force to them. Third, a distinctive clause: a symbol has traction only insofar as it figures in the system's model of itself, which is why the same content can be causally potent in one brain and inert in another, and why she thinks self-representing loops are what convert mere correlation into control.

My worry is that the episode will turn into an appreciative tour of the imagery. She is charming and, when pressed, has two reliable retreats: backing down to the observation that of course everything is physics and that she has never denied it, and backing down to the claim that causation is level-relative and interest-relative, so the appearance of competition between levels is a category mistake. I'd like the question sequence to close off both retreats progressively rather than springing a gotcha at the end, and to close on falsifiability — what result would convince her that symbols don't push the substrate around after all. Please avoid anything generic about her intellectual formation, and avoid re-litigating the general question of whether consciousness is an illusion, which isn't what she writes about.

Draft ten to twelve interview questions, ordered, and for each one supply: a single line naming which of her three commitments it targets; the answer she is most likely to give; and a follow-up keyed to that specific answer, not to a strawman. Mark the three questions you consider load-bearing, and note which ones a non-specialist listener can follow unaided, providing one sentence of plain setup for the rest. At minimum the set should include: a question on the exclusion problem and its causal-drainage variant, pressing whether her talk of the symbol level "selecting" among realizations asserts anything more than the familiar proportionality response in terms of determinables and determinates, and if so, what the extra content is; a question on whether the intervention she appeals to is available at all, since if symbol identity is fixed functionally, any neural variation that preserves the symbol is by definition irrelevant, which would make her first claim true but empty — and a follow-up for her likely reply that she means something more empirical than that; a question on what her account predicts about cases where the symbol level seems causally silent or actively misleading, such as subliminal priming, blindsight, confabulated self-reports, and the intrusive symbolism of an epileptic aura or a drug state; a question on the converse direction, where many candidate symbols appear to share one distributed neural pattern, and whose traction is being exercised then; a question on the self-loop clause and what it implies for non-human animals, pre-linguistic infants, and beliefs the subject has never reflected on, including whether it commits her to introspective access being a measurable determinant of causal efficacy; a question on whether "symbolic traction" is distinguishable from standard functionalism or is functionalism plus a thesis about explanation, and what she would say to someone who insists the difference is merely rhetorical; and a closing question asking her to name the experiment or finding — in neuroscience, in comparative cognition, or in a machine learning system whose high-level organization can be ablated while the substrate is held intact — that would force her to abandon the view.
