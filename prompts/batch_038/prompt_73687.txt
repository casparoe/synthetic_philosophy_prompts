Forty-odd years in a crystal-growth lab, retired three years now, and my daughter keeps handing me philosophy books like its a prescription. Currently chewing on Hofstadter's old complaint about machine translation — the idea that a program that carries meaning across by swapping strings around is doing something fundamentally shallow, that a real translation requires actually understanding the situation being described. I follow the intuition, but the standard comeback always bugs me: "he was writing about tiny rule-based systems, feed it enough data and the problem evaporates." I don't buy that its just a data problem, but I can't pin down why.

So help me pin it down. Give me one concrete, spelled-out example of a sentence that a modern translation system — trained on more parallel text than a human could read in a hundred lifetimes — would still render wrong in precisely the way Hofstadter was worried about, where what's missing could not in principle be supplied by more text. Constraints, because I want the hard version: no proverbs, no idioms, no puns, none of the folklore chestnuts about vodka and rotten meat, and nothing that's merely rare or under-represented in the training data. The missing ingredient has to be something that never made it into any corpus at all, no matter how big the corpus gets.

Set it in a working lab if you can — a line out of a notebook, a note taped to a furnace, something a technician says to a colleague over a glovebox. I want the sentence, the fluent-but-wrong translation the system produces, the correct translation a human who has actually done the work would produce, and then a precise account of the gap: what does the human have that the system doesn't, and why is that thing not text?

Then the part I actually care about: give me your own criterion — something operational, the sort of thing I could in principle test rather than argue about — for where "it never saw that in training" stops being a legitimate excuse and starts being a claim about understanding. And tell me honestly whether your example survives your own criterion, or whether you ended up smuggling in something you can't defend. If it turns out no such example exists and Hofstadter was just wrong, say so and show me the reasoning instead.
