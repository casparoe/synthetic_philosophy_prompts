so I'm the ecology lead at a county land management agency — we handle about 1,400 hectares of nature reserves plus something like 60 road verge and rail-adjacent sites, and for the last two years our board has had a line in its strategic plan saying that when we manage habitat we'll "take account of the welfare of individual invertebrates so far as the evidence allows." Nobody told me what that means operationally and now it's my problem. Proposals come in maybe four or five times a year, and the ones that go to the commissioners get read out loud at public comment by people who either think we're being soft on bugs or think we're bug murderers, so whatever I hand my team has to survive being argued with by a lay audience.

Here's where I've got to. I've read enough of the insect-welfare stuff to know the basic shape of it: the numbers are stupidly large (order of 10^19 individual insects alive at any one time, apparently), the probability that any given one is sentient is low and contested, and the argument is that huge N times small p still comes out big. My draft procedure has three gates and I'm not sure it works. Gate one: a biologist scores p(sentience) for the taxa affected, 0 to 1, using a checklist of traits. Gate two: estimate the change in number of individuals. Gate three: estimate whether the average life gets better or worse. Then you multiply the three together, discount by p, and rank proposals. Two problems. First, the product is dominated by whichever of those numbers I'm least confident about, and I genuinely can't tell whether my p estimates are tracking something in the world or just reflecting which bugs I find sympathetic — I'd say 0.2 for adult bees and hoverflies, near zero for springtails, and I have no idea how to defend either. Second, if gate three ever comes back "the average insect life here is net negative," the arithmetic tells me to make the reserve worse for insects, and I'm not going to sign my name to that, which makes me suspect the metric rather than the finding. To stop that I've been floating something I'm calling the abundance-neutral rule: treat anything that increases total insect numbers as welfare-neutral unless we have positive evidence that lives in that habitat are net good, and score proposals only on changes to per-individual conditions. A colleague says that's just a bias toward doing nothing wearing a lab coat, and I can't tell if he's right.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run in about two hours per proposal — me and a policy officer, no ethicist on staff, no original research, whatever field data we already have. Give me the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops and returns a no, and where it hands the call to a person because it's a values question or a political one rather than a factual one. Tell me what to do when there's no usable number at all, and be straight with me about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I want the abundance-neutral rule dealt with explicitly — keep it, kill it, or repair it — and I want the p(sentience) discount handled in a way that doesn't quietly make our decisions hostage to a number nobody can defend. Then run it on three real proposals: (a) switching about 40 verge sites from two cuts a year to a single late-summer cut with a sown wildflower strip, which our own surveys say should raise invertebrate abundance a lot; (b) retrofitting the reserve car park and two access roads to narrow-spectrum amber LEDs on an 11pm-to-5am curfew; (c) releasing a parasitoid wasp as biocontrol against an invasive leaf-miner that's stripping a shrub we're trying to restore, where the hosts die slowly from the inside. Flag whichever of those the procedure turns down, because I already know (a) is the one my whole career says yes to and I'd rather hear it from the procedure than from a commissioner. Finally, tell me the limits — the kinds of proposal this thing will get wrong, and the questions it looks like it answers but doesn't.
