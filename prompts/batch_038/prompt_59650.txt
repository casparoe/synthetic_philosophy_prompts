I teach a senior seminar in the history of economic thought titled "Money Before Economics," and I am writing the final. I need one document-based essay question on Berkeley's monetary thinking — specifically on *The Querist*, the long sequence of rhetorical queries he published in three parts between 1735 and 1737, when he was Bishop of Cloyne and pressing the case for Irish economic development, including a national bank that would issue paper notes. Students will have the excerpt below in front of them in the exam room, will have forty-five minutes, may consult nothing else, and the question is worth 25 marks. Query numbers are those of the text I am using.

> 5. Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?
> 6. Whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money.
> 7. Whether the real end and aim of men be not power? And whether he who could have everything else at his wish or will would value money?
> 8. Whether the public aim in every well-govern'd State be not that each member, according to his just pretensions and industry, should have power?
> 9. Whether power be not referred to action; and whether action doth not follow appetite or will?
> 10. Whether fashion doth not create appetites; and whether the prevailing will of a nation is not the fashion?
> 13. Whether it may not concern the wisdom of the legislature to interpose in the making of fashions; and not leave an affair of so great influence to the management of women and fops, tailors and vintners?
> 20. Whether the creating of wants be not the likeliest way to produce industry in a people? And whether, if our peasants were accustomed to eat beef and wear shoes, they would not be more industrious?
> 21. Whether other things being given, as climate, soil, etc., the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?
> 22. Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?
> 23. Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?
> 25. Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?
> 26. Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?
> 32. Whether there be any vertue in gold or silver, other than as they set people at work, or create industry?

The question must make students confront what a ticket is a ticket *for*. Berkeley's official line, and the one they will recite, is that money has no intrinsic worth and is a token whose whole significance lies in what it does; query 6 goes so far as to concede that any other means of exciting and circulating industry would serve just as well. But a counter or ticket derives its worth from a claim enforceable against an issuer, and the queries never say who the issuer is or what obliges them. The excerpt offers at least three different candidates for whatever makes people take the token in exchange: power over the labor of others (queries 7–9), appetites and fashions deliberately shaped by the legislature (queries 10, 13, 20), and circulation itself, treated as a relation between quantity and speed (queries 21–22). The crux is which of these Berkeley can actually bear weight on, and what his account costs him once he leans on it — if acceptance is manufactured by state-shaped demand, for instance, what could ever make such a currency fail, and has he any explanation of depreciation to offer?

Please design the question so that it cannot be answered by (i) retelling Berkeley's immaterialism or his hostility to abstract ideas, (ii) repeating the analogy between money and the counters used at a card table that he works out in the seventh dialogue of *Alciphron*, which the students will also have read, or (iii) announcing that Berkeley anticipated some later theory of money. Students who reach for (ii) tend to answer a question about meaning when the question is about acceptance, and I want that to lose marks rather than pass for insight.

Give me three things: the question itself, in no more than 120 words; a model answer of no more than 600 words that a strong student could realistically produce in forty-five minutes; and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer must make and the marks each carries, states what separates an A from a B (a B should be able to locate the tension but not to say what Berkeley would have to add to close it), and lists at least four named mistakes that should cost marks, each with one line explaining why it is an error rather than a mere omission.

Two further requests. First, keep the settled distinct from the contested: whether Berkeley's remarks are best read as anticipating a state theory of money, or as a variant of quantity-and-velocity reasoning, or as something that does not map onto later categories at all, is not a settled matter among people who work on this material, and your marking scheme must not award marks only for endorsing one of those readings — say explicitly where in the model answer a claim is interpretive rather than textual. Second, keep the entire response under 1,000 words, and give me no bibliography, no suggestions for further reading, and no citations beyond the query numbers in the excerpt.
