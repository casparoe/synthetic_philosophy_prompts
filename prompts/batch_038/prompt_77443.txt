I write literary fiction — six novels out, all of them reviewed politely, none of them bestsellers. My philosophy education consists of one term of something called Ethics and Public Policy at uni, most of which I spent in the bar, plus whatever I've picked up from reading on trains. What I've been reading on trains lately is the pair of essays that both go under the title Moral Luck, the one by Bernard Williams and the one by Thomas Nagel — I understand Nagel's was written as a reply to Williams's, and I got maybe two thirds of the way through each before I had to stop, because I kept having this unnerving feeling that they were describing my day job. Nagel's four varieties, if I've got them right: who you are, what situation you land in, how things turn out, and then some deeper one about the will itself being caused. And underneath all of it the idea that we should only be judged for what was in our control — which seems to me to be the exact opposite of what a novel is for.

Here's the situation. The book I'm working on is built as a fork. The same middle section is told twice. In one strand a woman driving home after two glasses of wine at a launch party glances down at her phone for maybe a second and a half, and the boy who steps off the kerb at that moment dies; in the other strand she glances down at her phone for exactly as long, the pavement is empty, she gets home, makes toast, and the book runs on for another two hundred pages in which nothing whatsoever happens to her. Same woman, same wine, same glance, same second and a half — different weather, different child, different life. There's a second strand too, her brother, whose temperament she didnt choose and who's doing an enormous amount of unchosen causal work in everything he ends up blamed for; and a bit where a character's whole project only gets its verdict thirty years later, which reminded me of Williams's Gauguin case, the painter whose life is either justified or condemned retrospectively by whether the work comes off.

So this is what I want from you, and I want it done properly rather than diplomatically. Take that debate — the control principle, the four kinds of luck, Williams's resistance to the whole tidy morality-system picture — and restate it in the vocabulary I actually use. Not watered down for a layperson: genuinely translated into the working terms of a novelist. Point of view and who is doing the perceiving. Free indirect style and the blur between a character's judgement and the narration's. Dramatic irony, which is arguably just resultant luck with the reader let in on it. Scene and sequel. Characterisation versus backstory, and the difference between a motive and a plot function. The contract with the reader about what a book is allowed to make matter. What a first-person narrator can and cannot be trusted about. The fact that a novel is structurally a machine for making consequences land on someone. Go term by term, and then give me the residue report, because that's the part I actually care about: which bits of the philosophers' argument drop cleanly into craft language, which only fit if I bend them out of shape, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. Then tell me which side the failure sits on. My suspicion — test it, don't flatter it — is that my form is already an argument against Nagel's control condition, that a novel simply is a device for holding people answerable for outcomes they never intended, and that a reader who closes the book devastated about the dead boy is not committing an error but doing something the philosophy can't accommodate. But there's an unflattering alternative you should take seriously: that craft talk has no word for blame at all, only for sympathy and interest, and that I've been confusing being moved by a character with judging them. If that's the right answer, say it flatly. British English throughout please, and don't waste space summarising the two essays at the top — I've read them, more or less.
