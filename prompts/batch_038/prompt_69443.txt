I have arrived at a position in the theory of rational choice that I would describe as certainty-primacy, and I want a rigorous account of what would count against it.

The position has three components. First, the Allais common-consequence pattern is not a cognitive error but a correct deliverance of practical reason: an agent may rationally weight a branch that is certain differently from any merely probable one, even where the two prospects differ only in how an otherwise shared branch is filled in. Take a case of my own construction, structurally identical to the original. Option A1 preserves the patient's life with moderate but stable disability, with certainty. Option B1 offers a 0.10 chance of full recovery, a 0.89 chance of that same moderate disability, and a 0.01 chance of death. Option A2 offers an 0.11 chance of moderate disability and death otherwise; option B2 offers a 0.10 chance of full recovery and death otherwise. A1 and B1 share their 0.89 branch, and A2 and B2 are what remain of them when that shared branch is replaced by death in each. I hold that reason requires preferring A1 to B1 and B2 to A2 — requires it, not merely permits it. Second, I reject the standard accommodations (decision weights, rank-dependent or cumulative-prospect-theoretic weighting functions, regret-adjusted utility) as changing the subject: they model the intuition instead of vindicating it, and my claim is normative rather than descriptive. Third, I accept the consequences for dynamic choice: I would rather surrender dynamic consistency — accept that I may, at a later node, abandon a plan I now endorse and finish worse off than if I had held to it — than surrender the normative priority of the certain branch.

Given all that, map the position's exposure. Specifically:

- Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether it is formal, empirical, or intuition-based, and why that matters for a thesis about what reason requires rather than about what people do.
- Say which of these a committed certainty-primacist would concede as counting against them and which they would dismiss, and whether the dismissals are principled or ad hoc.
- Identify what the view predicts, in choice or in argument, that its rank-dependent rivals do not. If the honest answer is nothing, say so, and then assess whether a normative thesis is damaged by lacking distinctive empirical content or whether demanding such content is a category mistake.
- Determine whether the privileging of certainty has a principled stopping point: what must the view say about 0.99 against 0.999, about a certainty that is only described as such, and about cases where the certain branch is itself a bad outcome.
- State precisely what has to be assumed to derive the result that a sure-thing violator will pay to avoid information that is free and relevant, and whether my willingness to give up dynamic consistency blocks that derivation or merely relocates it.
- Judge whether the reported reversal of these choices under extended reflection, or under redescriptions of the very same options, counts against certainty-primacy, and what would have to be true for it to count.

I am familiar with the textbook list of objections and do not need it repeated. What would move me most is a case in which my two verdicts above cannot be held together without contradicting some further judgment I am unwilling to give up; tell me whether such a case exists, and conclude honestly whether the position has been qualified into invulnerability — and whether invulnerability of that sort is a defect here or simply the normal condition of an axiom.
