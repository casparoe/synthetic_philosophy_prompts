I write the math-and-ideas column for a weekly magazine — audience is basically people who were good at maths at school and haven't opened a textbook since. I've been sitting on the two-envelope problem for months now: two sealed envelopes, one holds twice what the other does, you take one, then you're offered the swap, and the expectation arithmetic says swap, because half of 2A plus half of A/2 comes out to 1.25A. I know the lineage roughly — the Belgian necktie puzzle from the 40s, the wallet version that got into the popular puzzle books, and that asymmetric variant where the second envelope is filled by a coin flip *after* the first one has been handed over. What's driving me nuts is that every source I read locates the mistake somewhere different, and I've started to think the disagreements aren't really about the arithmetic at all — they're about which thing each person is least willing to give up. So: build me a decision tree that sorts people by exactly that.

It should start from "I accept the 1.25A calculation exactly as written" and end at a named resolution, maybe seven leaves and six to eight nodes. Every node has to be a question a smart non-specialist can answer without knowing which camp it leads them into — no node may contain a word like "prior" or "improper" in the question itself, those belong in the leaf labels. Name the leaves by what they sacrifice, not by whoever's name is attached to them. I need the tree to reach, at minimum: the line that says no way of filling the envelopes can make the argument work at every amount you might open, so the setup as described is impossible; the line that says the A in the doubling branch and the A in the halving branch aren't the same A; the line that grants an unbounded prior but denies expectation is the right yardstick here; the threshold line, where with any ordinary distribution there's a figure above which you shouldn't swap and the always-swap result needs a prior that's blind to scale; the bounded-utility-of-money line; and the deflationary-in-reverse line that the coin-flip version is genuinely a case where value comes from nowhere. Then flag every pair of leaves your tree can't really separate — I already suspect the unbounded-prior and the infinite-variance people end up standing in the same spot, and I'd rather the tree admit it than paper over it.

Two of the nodes should be concrete cases, not abstractions, and here's two I've actually run on people so you can use them. First: my friend fills the envelopes, but she only has about $300 cash in the house, so there's a hard ceiling on what can be in play — you open yours and see $160; does the puzzle change, and if so where did it go? Second: you may pay a dollar to be told whether your envelope is above or below $50 before you decide whether to swap. The people who were most confident that the whole thing dissolves once you insist on some distribution got strangely quiet on that one and I want the tree to catch them. Constraints: my editor wants something a reader can do on a phone in five minutes, so nodes need to be answerable with a gut reaction plus one line of arithmetic, no notation past A and 2A. Also mark any cell of the logical space that comes out empty and tell me whether its empty because the view is incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or because nobody's bothered to look — I'd love to be able to put one of those in the piece as a position you could hold but nobody does. And please no bibliography or paper titles, I can't chase references on deadline and I'm not publishing anything I can't verify myself.
