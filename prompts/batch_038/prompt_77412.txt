I'm the head of evidence and data at a mid-sized international NGO — about 200 staff, we work in four countries and we're one of roughly twenty orgs in a loose consortium that overlap heavily in the same districts. Two or three times a month I have to make the same shape of decision: do we do X now, on our own authority, when the rest of the sector either hasn't agreed or simply can't move as fast as we can? X is usually one of three things — publishing something (a dataset, an evaluation, a security incident report), fielding something new (a method or a distribution channel that isn't in national guidelines yet), or handing something over to a government body. I chair a four-person committee that decides these, we get about 45 minutes per case, nobody on it has formal training in statistics or economics (I have a public policy degree), and anything with a big enough reputational hit has to go to the board's ethics subcommittee, which meets quarterly, so "escalate it" is usually not a real option. Right now we decide by vibes and whoever argues hardest.

The thing I keep coming back to is the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it: if a bunch of parties each form a noisy estimate of whether some initiative is net good, and the initiative goes ahead whenever *any one* of them judges it positive, then the set of initiatives that actually get done is skewed toward the cases where the most optimistic estimate was simply wrong. So the remedy isn't a blanket don't, it's demanding a bigger margin than your own honest assessment says you need, where the size of the margin depends on how many other parties were in a position to form a judgment and on how noisy everyone's judgments are. I believe the original write-up ends by proposing something called a principle of conformity and sketching a few models for implementing it, but that's pitched at whole communities and norm-setting, and I need something one comittee can actually run on a Tuesday afternoon. If any part of my summary above is wrong or overstated, say so before you build on it.

So: please write the decision procedure. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure just stops at "no," and the points where it hands the call to a named human because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. It has to be usable when we can't get numbers — tell me what to do instead of a count of how many other parties could have judged, and whether our internal log (we've been recording a confidence rating on roughly the last 40 of these decisions and I can go back and score them against how they turned out) can stand in for the error term. I also want you to deal with three wrinkles honestly. One: our estimates are *not* independent of our peers', because we all read the same grey literature, hire from the same talent pool and sit in the same coordination meetings — does that shared background shrink the correction, or is shared bias exactly the reason to be more scared? Two: our field director's standing objection is that the curse can't apply to us because we're not racing anybody, we're just the only org with the capacity to act in the window — I want the procedure to address that objection head on rather than dodge it, since I suspect he's half right. Three: "wait and see" isn't a neutral baseline in our line of work, so the procedure needs a way of deciding when inaction is itself the unilateral move that needs the margin applied to it. Then run it on three real-shaped cases: (a) we're about to publish a pre-registered evaluation showing that a very popular donor-funded intervention that we and six other consortium orgs deliver has essentially no effect on the outcome everyone claims for it, two peers have weaker internal data pointing the same way and have sat on it for years, and three partner country directors have asked us privately not to publish; (b) cholera season is six weeks out in a district where we're the only org with a cold chain and community health workers, and there's a promising water-treatment approach that won't clear the national guideline process for about five months, so nobody else can act inside the window even in principle; (c) a ministry is asking for our beneficiary location data to run a cash program, and three other orgs hold overlapping subsets that any of them could hand over independently. One of those three should come out against what I want, and I'd rather the procedure tell me now than in a year. Finally, mark which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, say plainly what the procedure can't settle, and keep any math to arithmetic I could do on a whiteboard in front of four tired people.
