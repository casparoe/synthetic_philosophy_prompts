Our city council votes next month on a pilot that would require adults receiving general assistance to perform twenty hours a week of municipal work — clearing invasive growth in the parks, sorting donations at the food bank, filing in the clerk's office — in exchange for a flat stipend added to their existing benefit rather than an hourly wage. The local paper endorsed the proposal in an editorial whose argument ran almost entirely on the claim that the program is a hand up, not a handout.

Two objections came up at the public hearing. A council member noted that a parks supervisor had conceded the department would not otherwise pay anyone to rake the same trails, so much of the work is being invented for the occasion. A legal aid attorney calculated that participants would earn well under the minimum wage per hour for tasks the city would otherwise have to pay market rates for. The editorial board answered both objections the following week by repeating the slogan, and added that the requirement would also serve as a check on the few people collecting benefits they are not entitled to — again, because it is a hand up rather than a handout. Meanwhile a letter on the other side of the debate used the very same phrase to argue for one-time unconditional grants, on the grounds that a sum a person can spend on a certification or a security deposit is a hand up, whereas the council's program is neither that nor a handout.

I am not trying to settle whether the pilot is good policy. I want to know what the phrase commits a person to. Please give me the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing — I suspect there is at least one about what work does for the person doing it, one about what recipients owe the community in exchange, one about the difference in kind between a wage and a grant, and one that is not a claim at all — and sort them by status: which are platitudes, which are empirical propositions that could turn out false, which are normative claims about desert or reciprocity, and which are empty. Then tell me, thesis by thesis, whether it would actually answer the make-work objection, the below-minimum-wage objection, or the point about catching people who are not entitled to benefits. Finish by identifying the reading the editorial most plausibly needs given how it deployed the phrase, and say whether that reading is consistent with the letter writer invoking it for the opposite proposal. I would like this as an ordered list I can bring to the next hearing.
