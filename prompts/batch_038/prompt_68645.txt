I host a small philosophy-of-mind podcast, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has just published a book defending the claim that dreams are not experiences. Her position is a contemporary version of the line running from Norman Malcolm's *Dreaming* (1959) through Dennett's confabulationism: nothing with genuine phenomenal character happens while we sleep, and what we call a dream is a narrative composed at or around the moment of waking. She is fluent in both sources, well read in the sleep literature, and skilled at absorbing objections by conceding something thin and keeping everything important. I do not want questions that let her restate the view.

Please draft ten interview questions, ordered so that the sequence builds pressure rather than jumping between unrelated topics. For each question, supply three items:

(a) the question as I would actually ask it aloud — self-contained enough that a listener who has never encountered the debate can follow what is at stake, but not padded;
(b) the answer she is most likely to give, in a sentence or two;
(c) a follow-up keyed specifically to that anticipated answer, not to a strawman version of it.

The ten questions must collectively cover the following ground, though you may combine or reorder where it helps:

1. **Verification and the reach of the argument.** Malcolm's insistence that a dream report cannot be checked against anything — whether this is a point specific to dreams or a general skepticism about first-person reports that would equally dispose of afterimages, pains, and mental imagery. What makes the dream case distinctive if the structure of the complaint is the same?
2. **Pre- versus post-experiential revision.** Dennett's distinction between Stalinesque and Orwellian accounts of how the brain handles content, and his observation that the two are agreed on the underlying processing and differ only in whether they deem that processing pre- or post-experiential. Which is her view? If Orwellian, she has conceded an experience that is later rewritten; if Stalinesque, what supplies the material out of which a coherent narrative is composed at waking? And if the two accounts are empirically indistinguishable, what exactly does her thesis rule out?
3. **Lucid dreaming as attempted verification.** The Stanford-line experiments in which trained lucid dreamers executed pre-arranged eye-movement signals during polysomnographically confirmed REM sleep, together with the later findings that time estimates, breath-holding, and some autonomic measures in the dream track their waking counterparts. Anticipate her likely reply — that a signal establishes motor control and a fragment of self-knowledge, not a visual field — and press her on what observation *would* establish a visual field.
4. **Stimulus incorporation.** The classic nineteenth-century anecdote of the sleeper who dreams an extended scene from the revolutionary period, culminating in the guillotine blade falling on his neck, and wakes to find a bed rail across his neck. Does this support wholesale construction at waking, or does it presuppose that most of the narrative was lived through in something like real time, with only the ending retrofitted?
5. **Confabulation with nothing to confabulate about.** In the paradigm cases — split-brain patients offering reasons for behavior they did not initiate — confabulation involves misreading cues that are genuinely present. Her view posits construction without source material. Ask what the mechanism is, and why its output is narrative, emotionally inflected, and stage-dependent rather than arbitrary.
6. **The contentless dream sense.** People frequently wake confident that they have dreamed yet unable to produce any content. On her account, was there a dream? If yes, the report is not constitutive of the dream; if no, what is the confident sense a sense of?
7. **Conceptual claim or empirical claim.** Is she analyzing the grammar of "dream," in Malcolm's ordinary-language manner, or making a claim about the sleeping brain? Push hard on the consequence either way: in the first case, why should the science of consciousness care; in the second, why does the thesis need conceptual argument rather than evidence, and what finding could defeat it?
8. **The fate of Cartesian dream skepticism.** The Meditations' dreaming doubt depends on dreams seeming qualitatively similar to waking perception. Her view appears to dissolve that dependence. Ask whether she accepts the dissolution, and how she then explains the *appearance* of indistinguishability without folding that appearance itself into the confabulation, which would leave the view unfalsifiable.
9. **Reports from outside REM.** Hypnagogic imagery and mentation reports collected from non-REM sleep. Does her view deny that these involve experience too, or does it concede a category of genuine sleep experience while reserving "dream" for something constructed? What principle draws the line?
10. **The closing evidential question.** What observation would she count as evidence against her view — and then put a concrete proposal to her: pre-registered awakenings at scheduled intervals, with dream content matched against signals agreed in advance and against physiological measures recorded in real time. Ask her to say in advance what pattern of results would force revision.

Two further constraints. Keep each spoken question to roughly three sentences and each follow-up to one or two. Where you refer to Malcolm, Dennett, or any experimental result, paraphrase rather than quote, and do not put anything in quotation marks unless you are certain of the exact wording; likewise do not have me ask her to recommend papers or books, since I want the exchange to stay on the content of her position.
