I am chairing and hosting a two-day workshop, and I have to deliver the opening welcome. Please write the speech for me.

**The workshop.** Its advertised title is *Do Models Have Representations?* — chosen eighteen months ago, before we understood how unevenly it would be read. The researchers who submitted papers read it as a question about our methods: whether the things our instruments report are features of the models or artefacts of the instruments. The fifteen officials from a national standards body who are attending as observers read it as a question about model psychology, and have come to find out whether interpretability evidence is the sort of thing one could require in a technical standard. Two journalists are in the room. The rest of the eighty seats are filled by interpretability researchers and by a dozen philosophers of science, who are here partly because we invited them and partly because they are curious what we think we are doing.

**The work.** For two years our group has been building what we call the atlas: a few thousand directions in a large model's activation space, each associated with a labelled concept, each of which lights up reliably in the presence of that concept, each of which can be strengthened or suppressed by intervention, and each of which is recovered, with minor drift, by the same procedure applied to related models trained on different data, with different seeds and different widths.

**The disagreement.** Two of my colleagues — call them Priya and Tomás; both are in the room, both are friends of mine, and neither will thank me for caricaturing them — draw opposite conclusions from this. Priya holds that the atlas entries are real structure in the model: that the atlas records a discovery, that its entries would be recovered by any sufficiently careful procedure, and that their stability across models is evidence that they track something method-independent. Tomás holds that the entries are a choice of basis: that many bases fit our intervention data equally well, that which one we get is a function of the priors we built into the procedure — sparsity, non-negativity, dimensionality — and that the only thing we are entitled to is a list of invariances, of the form *when this direction is strengthened, that behaviour changes*. On his view, asking whether the entries are real is a category error about a coordinate system. Both accept every number we have. They disagree about what the numbers are numbers of.

**The two anomalies.** These are why the workshop exists, and I want them on the table in the first ten minutes rather than left to emerge. First: roughly one atlas entry in twelve has no detectable behavioural effect when we suppress it. It is stable, interpretable, reliably responsive to its concept — and intervening on it changes nothing the model does. Second: we have three interventions that reliably change behaviour, one of which reverses a preference we care about a great deal, for which we cannot find any atlas entry, or combination of entries, that mediates the change. We can produce the effect; we cannot locate what we are producing it in. Priya says the first shows our labels are wrong and the second is a failure of search rather than of ontology. Tomás says the first is exactly what you should expect from a basis fitted to predict activations rather than behaviour, and that the second proves his point: the interventions work, and the ontology is surplus.

One of the philosophers has already told me she will open the afternoon with a criterion from the realism literature about theoretical entities — roughly, that we are most entitled to believe in the things we can use to bring about effects, and that a posit which we can neither manipulate nor feel the manipulation of is at best idle. That criterion appears to decide the first anomaly in Tomás's favour and the second in Priya's, though she will resist the second half of that, and the resistance is one of the things I would like the room to be expecting to work through.

**What I need the speech to do:**

1. Run to about 1,100 words, roughly nine minutes at speaking pace.
2. Contain three sentences — unmarked in the text — that both Priya and Tomás would say aloud happily and mean differently. After the speech, in no more than 150 words, tell me which three they are and what each side hears in them.
3. Take hold of the title's ambiguity rather than apologising for it. The welcome should make the room aware that the officials' question and the researchers' question are not the same question, without letting anyone feel they have come to the wrong building, and without resolving the ambiguity in favour of either reading.
4. Be deliberate about intentional language. The officials will quote the speech back to us, so it must nowhere say that a model believes, wants, understands, or intends anything; but stripping out all representational idiom would beg the question against Priya, and one of the philosophers will say so from the floor. Decide where the speech does and does not use such talk, and let the pattern be regular enough that a philosopher can see it is a decision rather than an oversight.
5. Include one moment of humour in the first ninety seconds that lands for the engineers and the philosophers simultaneously, depends on nothing the officials have not already been told, and is not at the officials' expense.
6. Say something to the observers about what this work can and cannot be asked to deliver in a standard — neither promising them assurance nor telling them to expect nothing, and without sounding like a disclaimer read aloud by counsel.
7. Disclose that the atlas is my own group's work and that I am not neutral, and say something about what a host owes a room when the host has a stake in it. For what it is worth I lean towards Priya, and I have never been able to say what would count as evidence against her; the speech may admit that, but it must not turn into an argument for her.
8. Cite nothing. No papers, no named philosophers, no appeals to the literature, no reading list at the end. Anything that needs saying should be said in terms the room already has.
9. End by handing over to the first talk, which is Tomás's, and whose opening slide is the second anomaly. The last thing I say should leave the room waiting for that slide.

Please do not have me settle the dispute, predict what the two days will conclude, or propose a third position that reconciles them; the welcome's job is to make the disagreement precise enough that the workshop has something to attack.
