I sit on a small governance-design working group attached to a mid-sized research institute, and for the better part of a year we have been trying to decide whether to pilot something futarchy-like — not a national government, obviously, but a conditional prediction market over a discretionary grant budget, in which a committee would vote on the objective (expected publications, say, or some composite of researcher-reported usefulness and cost-effectiveness) and traders would bid on which of several proposed allocation rules would best serve it. We got as far as drafting resolution rules and then stalled, and the stall turns out not to be about our institute at all. It is about futarchy itself: the proposal Hanson put forward in his 2000 manifesto and refined in his 2013 Journal of Political Philosophy paper, and which Buterin adapted to blockchain governance in 2014. Reading the exchanges around it, and listening to our own, I keep getting the sense that the participants are not so much disagreeing as failing to intersect.

Three positions keep recurring. Call them the impossibilists, the engineers, and the deflationists. The impossibilists say the mechanism requires a price for welfare conditional on a given policy actually being adopted, and that no such price can be a genuine market price: only one branch is ever chosen, so the counterfactual branch never resolves, never pays out, and can never be scored; traders face a question with no possible feedback, and whatever number they post is unconstrained by evidence rather than merely noisy. Since the conditioning event — the council adopts X — has probability near zero or near one by the time anyone would want to trade on it, they conclude the conditional is unbuildable in principle, not merely unprofitable. The engineers reply that this conflates a semantic worry with a design problem. You can void or refund the unchosen branch, appoint a subsidized market maker obliged to quote on both, cap position sizes, delay resolution, or run the market over a sequence of similar decisions so the same conditioning question recurs and a track record accumulates. On their view the live difficulties are the ordinary ones — thin liquidity, manipulation by large bettors, a metric that can be gamed — all matters of degree with known mitigations, and Hanson's own refinements (statistical tests for whether a price is significantly better than its alternative, agenda auctions, veto mechanisms) are patches of exactly this kind. The deflationists think both sides are arguing at the wrong layer: the architecture assumes a vote can fix the welfare measure and that everything downstream is then a purely factual question, but choosing the measure, choosing which conditional to consult, and choosing the resolution rules are themselves normative acts that carry the entire political weight, so futarchy either smuggles in a technocracy wearing a market's clothing or is unstable, because the demos will not accept outcomes it cannot contest on the grounds it takes to be relevant.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept, is that the three camps are answering different questions under a single heading, and that the heading is the question of what a conditional price is. For the engineer it is an incentive object, a number traders can be induced to post and be scored on. For the impossibilist it is an epistemic object, a number that would have to be answerable to how things would have gone, which they think no number can be. For the deflationist it is a political object, a number whose selection allocates authority. If that is right, the apparent contradictions dissolve: the engineer can concede that counterfactual branches are unscoreable and still be right that traders will post useful numbers, while the impossibilist can concede that a workable market can be built and still be right that it will not measure what futarchy claims to measure. But I am not confident. There may be a real empirical question buried underneath — whether prices on branches that never resolve are in fact less accurate than prices on branches that do, which one might probe by running parallel conditional markets and scoring chosen and unchosen branches alike against later evidence — or a real formal question about manipulability and identifiability, in which case the disagreement is substantive after all and could be settled by a theorem or a trial.

So: diagnose the stall. For each pair of camps, tell me whether the disagreement is verbal, whether it survives a stipulative fixing of terms, and whether it is really about forecast accuracy, about incentives, about the semantics of counterfactual conditionals, or about who gets to decide. Say where, if anywhere, a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work, and be concrete about what would count as answering it — what a pilot like ours would have to measure, and what a result would have to establish, before anyone could rationally change their mind. One symptom in particular I want accounted for: whenever a camp proposes a fix, the objection is not answered but relocates to a new layer of the design, and the same three positions reappear one level down, which is why we have spent a year going in circles. If that relocation is exactly what the structure of the dispute predicts, say so and explain why; if it instead means one camp is right and the others are rationalizing, say that and argue for it. Please do not survey the futarchy literature or the general track record of prediction markets, and do not give me a reading list — I want the structure of the disagreement, with reasons, and an honest verdict on whether progress is available here or whether this is one of those debates that simply runs down.
