I have spent the last three years running translation assays in a molecular biology department, and I have been talked into contributing a short essay to our university's interdisciplinary seminar on biological information, where the audience is roughly half philosophers and half working biologists. My first draft argued the familiar deflationary line — that DNA is not really a code, that the language of programs and instructions is a metaphor run amok — and a philosopher colleague pointed out, fairly, that this is the position everyone in the room already expects to be attacked, so attacking it would be a waste of forty minutes. I have accordingly rewritten the piece around a criterion of my own devising, and then had the disconcerting experience of watching the criterion break against the recoding literature halfway through. The draft below is what I now have. My co-author thinks paragraph 6 hands the whole argument to the opposition and wants me to cut it; I think the concession is the most interesting thing in the piece and should stay. I would like a second opinion before I commit either way.

Here is the draft, with paragraph numbers as they will appear in the final version:

1. Molecular biologists say that DNA is a code, and unlike most of their metaphors this one has a precise cash value: there is a table of sixty-four entries pairing nucleotide triplets with amino acids and termination signals, and that table is close to identical in nearly every organism. The pairings are very nearly arbitrary — nothing in the chemistry of UUU singles it out for phenylalanine — and they were settled early in the history of life, after which any reassignment would have scrambled every protein in the cell at once, which is why the table has been shared ever since.

2. I want to distinguish two things that get called a code. In the table sense, a code is a fixed correspondence between two alphabets, and DNA plainly qualifies. In the interpreter sense, a code is a system of symbols whose meaning depends on a reader who consults the correspondence and could consult it wrongly. Source code is a code in the second sense: a compiler can be buggy, a build can be misconfigured, a programmer can misread the intent.

3. My claim is that the translation machinery is not a code in the interpreter sense, and that biologists slide into that sense without noticing. The ribosome does not consult the table; the ribosome, together with the population of charged tRNAs, is the table. Codon–anticodon pairing and the specificities of the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases are physical dispositions, not rules applied by something over and above them. So I propose a test, which I shall call the fallible-decoder criterion: a symbol system is a code in the interpreter sense only if there is a gap between the mapping and its implementation wide enough for the implementation to violate the mapping while remaining the same implementation. A person decoding a cipher can misapply it. The ribosome cannot misapply the genetic code, because there is nothing there that could be doing the applying.

4. The corollary, which my colleagues resist, is that on the table sense alone the claim that the genome is a program is not loose but actively misleading, since a program presupposes something that might execute it incorrectly and no such thing exists in the cell. The metaphor smuggles in a semantics the wetware does not supply.

5. Honesty requires giving the exceptions their full weight, and they are worse for my case than I expected. In vertebrate mitochondria UGA specifies tryptophan rather than terminating, so a single human cell runs two tables simultaneously on two separate translation systems. Worse: UGA is read as selenocysteine when a SECIS element is present in the message, and in a selenium-starved cell the very same codon in the very same message terminates translation instead, yielding a truncated and useless enzyme. Programmed frameshifting behaves similarly, one message giving two proteins depending on where the ribosome slips. In these cases the gap the criterion demands seems to reappear: same symbols, same machinery, different outcome, and a failure that is not a change to the mapping.

6. I therefore concede that the genetic code is a code in the interpreter sense after all, but locally and conditionally. This produces a conclusion I am not certain I want to defend: the genome counts as a program in proportion to how much of it is recoded, so the metaphor is licensed precisely where the biology is least typical, and is least licensed where it is most often invoked.

Please write in British English and give me exactly five numbered comments, each one or two sentences long, ordered from most to most damaging, with no introduction, no summary and no sign-off. Every comment must name the paragraph it concerns and must end in a recommendation — cut, qualify, or defend — rather than simply registering disagreement. At least two of the five should be about whether I have got the biology right or have overstated it, and should say plainly where; at least one should tell me whether the fallible-decoder criterion in paragraph 3 earns its keep or is merely my original intuition wearing a lab coat; and at least one should adjudicate the dispute with my co-author by saying whether paragraph 6 destroys the thesis or is the only reason the essay is worth giving. Do not rewrite the essay for me, and do not send references, citations, or a reading list: I am finishing this on a train with no access to the literature, and I want your judgement rather than a bibliography I cannot check.
