I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am two-thirds of the way through a piece on expert disagreement — specifically on the formal question of when two well-informed parties who share an evidential base are entitled to go on disagreeing. I have been reading the Bayesian literature on that question: Aumann's agreement theorem, the common prior assumption that does the work in it, and the long-running argument over whether that assumption has any empirical content at all or is only a modelling convenience of the sort Harsanyi defended. I do not want a survey of that literature; I can write the survey myself. What I want is one worked case, and I have settled on the National Intelligence Estimate of October 2002, Iraq's Continuing Programs for Weapons of Mass Destruction — the coordinated estimate produced for Congress in the run-up to the authorization vote, whose Key Judgments were declassified and published in July 2003, after the fact. Analyze that episode through the common prior assumption and the agreement theorem, in both directions: what the framework says the intelligence community faced, and what the episode in turn does to the framework.

So that you need not reconstruct the record, here are the facts I will be relying on. The opening Key Judgment ran: "We judge that Iraq has continued its weapons of mass destruction (WMD) programs in defiance of UN resolutions and restrictions. Baghdad has chemical and biological weapons as well as missiles with ranges in excess of UN restrictions; if left unchecked, it probably will have a nuclear weapon during this decade." Fastened to that judgment was a pointer — "(See INR alternative view at the end of these Key Judgments.)" — because the State Department's Bureau of Intelligence and Research declined to go along with the finding that Baghdad was reconstituting a nuclear program. On the high-strength aluminum tubes, which were the single most consequential piece of technical evidence in the estimate, the Key Judgments recorded a second and narrower split: "(DOE agrees that reconstitution of the nuclear program is underway but assesses that the tubes probably are not part of the program.)" The document was thus coordinated across the community and issued as a collective product, yet it carried two attributed departures from the collective view inside its own text, where any reader with the patience to reach the annex would find them. The Senate intelligence committee's 2004 report subsequently found the Key Judgments unsupported by the underlying reporting; the IAEA's assessment of the Niger documents in March 2003 took out another leg of the case; and on the nuclear question the dissenting offices were substantially right while the consensus was substantially wrong. Everyone appears to have known everyone else's position before the ink was dry, and the disagreement did not converge.

What I need from you is the mapping discipline, and then the honest reckoning. Set out the structure as the theorem would require it: who the agents are (the coordinating agencies, and separately the policymakers who consumed the product), what the state space is (the propositions actually in dispute — reconstitution, the intended use of the tubes, the reliability of the sourcing behind each), what each office's information partition plausibly looked like, and which of these were matters of common knowledge as opposed to private or semi-private information. Then tell me whether the agreement theorem bites at all, and if it does not, which condition fails first: the common prior, the common-knowledge clause, or the idealization of the agents themselves. I want a straight answer on a question I keep circling — whether this was a disagreement about priors or a disagreement about likelihoods, about how much evidential weight to give a single uncorroborated human source and a single ambiguous procurement pattern — and what turns on getting that distinction right, since the two diagnoses imply very different repairs. I have also been carrying around a coinage of my own that I want you to test rather than flatter: a distinction between a community's paper prior (the shared tradecraft standards and coordination procedures all signatories nominally subscribe to) and its shop priors (the dispositions each office brings in from its institutional mission, so that a diplomatic-intelligence office and a counterproliferation office do not start in the same place even when handed the same cable). Say whether that distinction earns its keep here or is only dressing on the ordinary point that different agencies weight evidence differently. Relatedly, assess a hypothesis I intend to argue for in the piece and would like attacked: that an estimate of this kind is a bargaining process wearing the clothes of an updating process, and that the annexed dissent is the residue the bargaining could not absorb.

Finally, mark the places where the framework simply has nothing to say, rather than conscripting it: the aggregation question (if there is no common prior, is there any fact of the matter about what the community's posterior is, and what kind of act is it to publish one anyway); the design question (a formal dissent mechanism looks like an institution that deliberately refuses a common prior — is that a defect or a virtue on the theorem's own terms?); the audience question (policymakers and press received a single sentence and an optional annex, so common knowledge of the disagreement was engineered downward rather than achieved); and the fact that the episode was eventually resolved by the arrival of new evidence and by inspection, not by further deliberation among peers. End with a verdict I can put in the piece: does the October 2002 estimate confirm the common prior assumption, constitute an anomaly for it, or fall into a category the assumption was never built to handle? Aim for something between 1,300 and 1,800 words, written for an intelligent reader who has never seen a type space but will not tolerate vagueness. No bibliography, no list of further reading, and no citations — I will do the sourcing and the fact-checking myself. And please do not retell the political narrative around the war; my readers know it, and it is not what the piece is about.
