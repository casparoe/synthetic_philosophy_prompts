I am a junior at a public high school, and I co-run a twelve-person philosophy-of-mind club that meets Thursday mornings before first period. Our faculty sponsor has asked us to submit a piece to a regional student research symposium in the spring, and the two members who have dominated our discussions all year have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration instead of staging another debate, on the condition that I draft the joint statement they could both put their names to. One of them, a senior, defends the language of thought hypothesis in roughly its classical form: he holds that thinking is a matter of tokening expressions in an inner medium with a combinatorial syntax and a compositional semantics, so that anything capable of genuinely entertaining thoughts must have something with that structure, and that natural languages are learned precisely because a prior representational system makes learning them possible. The other, a junior who has taught himself a fair amount about neural networks, rejects this: he holds that cognition is best described as pattern-sensitive activity distributed over many simple units, that terms like symbol, rule, and representation do no explanatory work in that description, and that positing an inner language purchases its appearance of systematicity at the cost of an implausibly rich stock of innate concepts and a little reader inside the head.

Their argument has stalled on one exchange that they have repeated in different words roughly six times. The senior's line is: if someone can think that dogs chase cats, they can think that cats chase dogs, and the only explanation is that those thoughts have constituents that get recombined, so a system that acquired one thought without the other has not acquired a thought at all but merely two unconnected habits. The junior's reply is: that pairing is a fact about the world rather than about the vehicle, since the situations in question really are built from the same ingredients, so a learner exposed to enough of them will recombine for free; what the senior is doing, he says, is reading structure out of the training distribution and then announcing that he has discovered syntax. I suspect both of them are onto something and that a real portion of the dispute concerns what counts as following a rule, but I would rather the document establish that than my saying so at a meeting.

Please write that joint statement, and write it to a standard where each party would sign it without footnotes of protest. It should contain, in order: a numbered section of claims both can actually sign — about non-human animals and prelinguistic infants solving problems without a natural language, about what neural networks have demonstrably accomplished and where they demonstrably fail, about the distinction between the content of a state and the physical vehicle that carries it, and about what any adequate theory of cognition owes — phrased so that neither side's proprietary vocabulary does the work, and with a note wherever an apparently shared claim is weaker than it looks because the two of them are hearing different things in a common word. Then a disagreements table, with one column stating each contested question in neutral terms and two further columns giving the formulation each side would accept as its own, so that a reader can see where the wording itself is doing the arguing. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, ranked by how much they would move the debate. For each crux I want a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both would agree bears on it — for instance a compositional generalization task in which a network is trained on structured commands with certain combinations systematically withheld and then tested on the withheld ones, or a human artificial-language learning study comparing a compositional rule structure against a holistic one — together with each side's prediction written down in advance and each side's account of which result would count against its own position. Finish with a section identifying which of their disagreements turn out to be merely verbal and why, and a short list of the claims each agrees to stop attributing to the other, since I would like the document to end with the caricatures formally retired.
