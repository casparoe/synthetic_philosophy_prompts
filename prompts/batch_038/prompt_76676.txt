I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since before it had a name, and if there's one theorem I've watched get abused in comment threads more reliably than any other, it's the Condorcet jury theorem. It gets cited as though it settled the question of whether to trust crowds: add more voters, get more truth, take the limit and the majority becomes an oracle. What almost never survives the trip into a forum post is that the result is a knife-edge rather than a slope. It says: give me a binary question with a fact of the matter, voters who answer it correctly more often than not, and votes that are independent of one another, and the majority's accuracy climbs toward certainty as the body grows. Reverse the second condition and the same proof runs the other way, delivering a majority that is confidently, overwhelmingly, asymptotically certain of a falsehood — and nothing about how the vote feels from inside distinguishes the two regimes. Independence is the assumption everyone forgets. A secret ballot does not make voters independent if they were all trained on the same commentary, and a body that deliberates before voting has spent the morning manufacturing correlation and then congratulated itself on the resulting unanimity.

I want a story that inhabits this structure rather than arguing about it.

Write a short story of 1,800–2,200 words, in close third person and past tense, set in a harbor city built on a tidal shelf inside the flooded caldera of a sleeping volcano. Once a generation the city convenes the Assize: several hundred readers drawn by lot from the guilds — clerks, masons, pilots, midwives, well-diggers — who hear the evidence about the mountain and vote, yes or no, on whether the shelf will hold for the next twenty years. In living memory the Assize has never been wrong, and its majorities have grown steadily larger. Your protagonist is the Assize's record-keeper, who has spent thirty years tallying past verdicts against what actually happened, in a pre-statistical idiom: marks in a ledger, counts of marks, no vocabulary for what he is doing. During his tenure the city has drained the aquifer and bored new vents of its own, and — this is the hinge — the signs the readers rely on have stopped tracking the mountain, so that the evidence now before the Assize is no better than a coin flip dressed up as omen. The record-keeper is the only person positioned to suspect this, and he cannot prove it: his own ledger's sample is far too small to separate luck from skill, and the margin of the coming vote will be enormous.

Requirements:

1. Never name the theorem, its author, or any piece of modern statistics. No character utters "probability," "independence," "correlation," or "sample size." The mathematics must be legible from events and from the record-keeper's arithmetic with tallies and counts, which a reader should be able to follow and check.

2. Include a scene in which the Assize deliberates before voting, and in which a reader can watch the members become alike — and watch them take that likeness for agreement with the truth. Present it as warm, collegial, and good. Do not comment on it.

3. Include a charter rule that no question may be put to the body unless it admits exactly two answers, and have a young reader propose a three-way question instead: raise the seawall, abandon the lower terraces, or do nothing. Let the record-keeper defend the rule in practical, in-world terms that satisfy the room. A specialist should recognize precisely what the rule is protecting the city from, though the story never says so.

4. Include one reader who votes against his own reading of the signs, or abstains, on the reasoning that his vote can only matter in the contingency where most of the others got it wrong — and therefore that the pivotal case is exactly the case in which his own sign is least to be trusted. Let this be shrewd rather than cowardly, and let the story refuse to adjudicate whether it saved anyone.

5. On the crucial vote, the accessible evidence must be genuinely uninformative, and the reader must be unable to determine from inside the story whether the body is above or below the edge. The near-unanimity should be the tell, and everyone in the chamber should read it as reassurance.

6. Do not end with the lone dissenter vindicated by superior insight. If he is right, let it be luck, or something external arriving after the verdict has been recorded and its authority spent — or let it never arrive at all. I would rather the story close on the record-keeper making an entry he knows he will not live to check.

Keep the prose plain and physical: brine on the stone, the smell of gas in the well water, the sound the shelf makes at low tide. No narrator with a thesis, no essayistic interpolation. The point is not that crowds are stupid — the machinery genuinely worked for two centuries, and the tragedy is that its working cannot be told apart from its failing. Success, for me, is a story where a reader who knows the literature can point to each broken assumption in a scene rather than in a sentence, and a reader who has never heard of any of it still gets a story about a man who suspects that his city's oracle has quietly stopped being one and has no way to prove it before the vote.
