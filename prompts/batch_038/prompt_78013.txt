I am a data-standards architect in central government. My working vocabulary consists of canonical reference models, shared schemas and ontologies, conformance profiles, versioning, deprecation and backward compatibility, migration costs, and the governance machinery by which a dozen agencies are brought to adopt a single common representation. I am fluent in that vocabulary and have no philosophical training, though I have read enough Carnap to be struck by how much of his project sounds like my job. Restate it in my terms as faithfully as you can: rational reconstruction and explication, the Aufbau's attempt to derive a common language for the sciences from a chosen basis, the principle of tolerance, the distinction between questions internal to a framework and questions about whether to adopt one, and the claim that metaphysical disputes are pseudo-problems. Treat Carnap's own biography as a clue rather than an aside — the teenage Esperantist and Independent Social Democrat who co-authored the Vienna Circle's manifesto, and who regarded a deliberately constructed common framework as an instrument of social planning rather than a neutral convenience. Then give me the residue report. Say which claims translate cleanly, which translate only by smuggling in assumptions my discipline does not share (about reversibility, about who bears the cost of migration, about whether a choice between frameworks is merely a question of expediency), and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all — above all the conviction that conceptual clarity is itself emancipatory political work. For each piece of residue, judge whether the loss exposes a poverty in engineering's vocabulary or an indeterminacy in the philosophical claim. Please write in British English.
