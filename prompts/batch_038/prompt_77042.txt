I'm putting together the take-home for my honours seminar on probability and induction — mostly fourth-years plus two taught-masters students. They've had the usual week on single-case probability (Venn's consumptive Englishman wondering whether he'll make it to sixty-one, Reichenbach on incorporating an event into a reference class) and a week on self-locating belief, but I dont want this question to turn on exegesis: no name-dropping, nothing that depends on remembering who said what, and no sources for the students to look up or for you to cite.

One question, 25 marks, about 1500 words from the student. The failure mode I'm trying to kill is the student who can recite the shape of the reference class problem beautifully and never once has to pick a number. So the question hands them a case with actual frequencies plus a rule that a fictional regulator proposes to apply to it, and makes them break the rule.

Here's the case — keep the numbers exactly as they are, tighten the prose if you like:

Kestrel is a utility's autonomous transmission-line inspection programme. Eight flights so far, three crashes, all three before the software was upgraded to release v3 after flight 5; the three v3 flights are all clean. Industry-wide there were 611 autonomous line-inspection flights between 2016 and 2025, with 118 crashes. Of those, 240 were flown in sustained winds above 25 knots, with 74 crashes. 143 industry flights ran v3 or later, with 19 crashes. Two of Kestrel's three v3 flights were on the North Ridge corridor with the same two-person crew. The regulator's table does not break v3 flights down by wind speed. Flight 9 is a v3 aircraft, North Ridge, same crew, forecast sustained wind 30 knots. An internal engineering log says the v3 fix addressed a sensor-calibration defect that only showed up in gusts; an independent audit says two of Kestrel's three early crashes would probably have happened anyway.

And the rule the fictional Bureau of Infrastructure Risk proposes:

Sequential Narrowing Rule. Begin with the broadest class for which the Bureau has reliable data. Add the case's known properties one at a time, ordering them by the size of the frequency shift each one produces. Stop when either the class has fewer than 30 members, or the next property would move the relative frequency by less than two percentage points. Report the frequency of the class where you stopped.

The question is in three parts. (1) 8 marks: run the rule twice — narrowing on wind first, then on software, and then the other way round — say what number the Bureau ends up publishing in each run, and identify which clause of the rule is doing the damage each time. (2) 9 marks: can the rule be repaired by adding conditions, and what must any repaired version presuppose? Is the most specific class the case actually describes — v3, North Ridge, that crew, above 25 knots — one the Bureau could ever have data for, and what follows if it isn't? (3) 8 marks: assess the objection that reference classes are a crutch, that a Bayesian who conditions on the total evidence needs no reference class at all. Kestrel's flights are not obviously interchangeable, since each is flown by people who rewrote their procedures after the last crash; say what that does to the objection. And say whether the asymmetry in the Bureau's costs — a crash can kill a line worker, grounding the fleet costs ratepayers — changes which number is correct, or only which number to act on.

Deliverables: the question exactly as it would appear on the sheet, formatted, with the marks shown; a model answer of roughly 1100 words that a strong student could plausibly have written in the time; and a marking scheme that names the specific moves earning each chunk of marks, says what separates a first from a solid 2:1 (at least four named moves an A must contain and a B typically misses), and lists the mistakes that should cost marks — for instance treating the empty or near-empty narrow classes as merely a small-sample worry, or accepting condition-on-everything as a complete answer without saying where the likelihood came from.

Two things at the end, please. First, sanity-check the case: if any of the numbers don't actually generate the tension I think they do, or if a student could legitimately read the wind data and the software data as independent when I intended them to interact, tell me and propose the fix rather than quietly rewriting it. Second, tell me where your model answer's verdicts are most likely to be wrong. In particular: what argument would make you flip part (3) and hold that the Bayesian objection does succeed, or hold that the Sequential Narrowing Rule is salvageable once properly patched? And if a genuinely strong student could reach the opposite verdict on any part and still deserve full marks, say which part that is and how I'd have to reword the marking scheme to allow it.
