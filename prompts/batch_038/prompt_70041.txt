I am helping to draft the framing document for a small interdisciplinary program on suffering-focused ethics, and before we solicit proposals I want the specification settled: what a theory in the family described below has to be able to do before it counts as an adequate candidate at all. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, for names, or for references — I want the requirements themselves, each with the case or consideration that motivates it.

Fix the target family as follows. Call a view a *threshold-lexical negative utilitarianism* if it holds: (a) hedonic states admit of intensity along a single signed scale, pains negative and pleasures positive; (b) there is a threshold τ on that scale such that any episode of suffering whose intensity exceeds τ is lexically prior — no finite number of sub-threshold episodes of suffering, and no quantity of pleasure however great, outweighs a single above-threshold episode; (c) below τ, intensities are cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable, and are aggregated additively; (d) the view is impartial across persons and does not discount for temporal or modal distance.

Write the desiderata such a theory must meet. For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration, and mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of a particular camp that has been smuggling itself in as a requirement. At minimum, the list should tell me how the theory is to handle the following — but I expect you to add cases of your own, and the ones you add matter more than the ones I supply:

1. **Peak versus integral.** One second marginally above τ, against fifty years marginally below it. Does the lexical clause look at instantaneous peak intensity, at the intensity-time integral, or at something else, and can it say so without quietly giving up (b)?
2. **Comparisons above the threshold.** Clause (b) blocks trades against sub-threshold quantities, yet two lives each containing above-threshold suffering still have to be ranked against one another if the theory is to guide anything. What residual cardinal machinery is required there, and does it reintroduce precisely the commensurability the lexical move was meant to escape?
3. **Vagueness.** τ is not phenomenologically sharp. If a case is borderline as to whether it exceeds τ, is the lexical claim itself borderline? Does the view need a second-order threshold, and where does that regress terminate?
4. **Locating τ.** What evidence could fix τ across individuals, across species, and for subjects who cannot report — infants, nonhuman animals, patients under anaesthesia depth monitoring? Is a τ that could not in principle be located disqualifying, or merely inconvenient? Be explicit about which of those two verdicts you are giving and why.
5. **Commensurability.** Clause (a) assumes pleasure and pain share a scale. If they are incommensurable — two scales with no exchange rate — is (b) a substantive claim or a trivial one? What would the view have to add to keep (b) from collapsing into vacuity?
6. **Emptiness and collapse.** Any candidate τ can be set so high that nothing ever crosses it, or so low that the lexical structure does no work and the view becomes ordinary negative utilitarianism. What principle is supposed to discipline the choice, and is that principle part of the theory or an external constraint on it?
7. **The extinction inference.** Does this family imply that painlessly ending all sentient life is required, or does the lexical structure block that inference? State what would have to be true for it to block it.

Then tell me which subset of the desiderata you believe is jointly unsatisfiable — this is the part I most want, and if the answer is that none is, argue for that rather than asserting it. My own hunch is that (b), a cardinal sub-threshold aggregation, and a τ fixed by phenomenology alone cannot all stand together, but treat that as a conjecture to be tested, not a premise. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate views, and — in one paragraph, no citations — sort the standard escape routes (weakening the lexical clause to a very strong weight, denying (a), relativizing τ to the subject, and any others you think of) by which requirement each one abandons.

Several of these requirements turn on contested positions about measurement and vagueness, so I want your confidence stated rather than assumed: how confident are you in the list overall, and, where they differ, in the individual unsatisfiability claims? Then single out the point you are least sure of — the place where a competent philosopher working in this area would most likely say I have misdescribed the issue or manufactured a requirement out of a confusion.
