I work on a tiny model-monitoring team and I've been trying to spec out an anomaly-detection setup that's supposed to flag when a redeployed checkpoint has changed *how* it does something rather than just *what* it outputs. The idea is that you'd snapshot the internal story of a model doing some task, then after each round of finetuning compare the new model against the snapshot and raise a flag if the internals have drifted even when the benchmark numbers haven't. My colleague thinks this is overengineering: his line is that behavior is all there is, so if eval accuracy is within noise and the outputs on a held-out set are basically the same, then whatever you want to call "the mechanism" is the same by fiat, and the flag will just fire on irrelevant reshufflings. I partly buy it and partly don't, and to figure out where I stand I've been reading mech interp stuff alongside some philosophy of science on mechanisms, which turns out to be a rabbit hole about identity conditions.

So here's what I want: compare two candidate criteria for "same mechanism." The first is causal/interventionist — roughly the criterion philosophers of neuroscience lean on (Craver's mutual manipulability, as far as I've got it: a component belongs to the mechanism for some phenomenon if intervening on the component can change the phenomenon, and intervening on the phenomenon can change the component), transplanted into interp where the interventions are ablations and activation patching. On this view two checkpoints run the same mechanism if the same interventions on the same sites reproduce the same pattern of behavioral differences. The second is structural/implementation: same features in the same places, same circuit graph, same weights carrying out the same computation. Compare these seriously — where they come apart in practice and in which direction, whether either one is even well-defined for a network that has been retrained rather than surgically edited (features move around, superposition means a unit isn't a component in any clean sense, and "the same site" after finetuning may not mean anything), and which one an anomaly detector actually needs given that it has to be cheap, automated, and have a low false-alarm rate. And is there a third criterion — say, sameness at the level of the algorithm or policy the model implements, regardless of which neurons realize it — that just dissolves the fight between the other two, or does it make the problem worse?

Concretely, two cases I want you to run both criteria through. Case one: accuracy is flat across the two checkpoints, but on a subset of items the later model has stopped using what looks like a direct fact-lookup feature and instead does a little multi-step computation, and patching the old site no longer flips those answers. Case two: the features and the patching signature look unchanged, but the later model systematically differs on a narrow class of edge-case inputs nobody thought to benchmark. For each, tell me plainly whether the two criteria give the same verdict, and if they diverge which one I should trust for the purpose of catching a model that's quietly changed strategies. Please commit to a view rather than hedging into mush — but also tell me how confident you actually are, which step in your reasoning is most likely to be wrong (my suspicion is that it's the move of treating "component" as meaningful at all for a trained network), and what argument or empirical finding would change your mind about which criterion deserves to be the target of the detector.
