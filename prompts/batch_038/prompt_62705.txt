Last night I spent close to three hours arguing with a friend — she is finishing a clinical psychology doctorate, I am a year into a philosophy MA — about the multiple personality epidemic in North America, and we got nowhere, so I want the case laid out by someone with no stake in it. The sequence as I have it, roughly: after the 1957 book and film about Eve, and far more influentially after the 1973 book Sybil and its 1976 television adaptation, multiple personality became a widely available cultural script; DSM-III in 1980 listed multiple personality disorder as a diagnosis; reported cases then climbed steeply through the 1980s, from a couple of hundred to tens of thousands by the early 1990s, with the typical number of alters reported per patient inflating along the way until some clinicians were describing patients with more than a hundred personality states; treatment came to depend heavily on hypnosis, age regression, and the recovery of memories of abuse, frequently of organized ritual abuse, and it overlapped almost perfectly with the satanic panic, whose allegations investigators were eventually unable to substantiate; then patients began retracting, some sued their therapists and won, the experimental literature on suggestibility and memory distortion landed, the diagnosis was renamed dissociative identity disorder in DSM-IV in 1994, and the epidemic receded. The category itself, though, never disappeared: it is still in the current manual, an organized clinical community continued to defend a trauma-based account against a sociocognitive one, and in the last several years a new cohort of adolescents has arrived at clinics self-diagnosing after short-form videos about alters. If any of that is materially wrong or misleading, correct me before you build on it.

What I want is a disciplined case analysis, not a summary of either side. First, do the mapping properly: who the players were — patients, therapists, the DSM committees, book publishers and television, trial lawyers and insurers, experimental memory researchers, the press, and later the patient communities themselves — what each of them was responding to, and precisely where the classification entered each of their decision problems. Then run Hacking's account over it. Looping effects are supposed to be a feature of interactive kinds: classify people, and the classified change in ways that feed back into the classification and into the evidence for it. I want to know which features of this episode that mechanism actually predicts, which it merely accommodates after the fact, and which it leaves untouched — for instance, why the loop ran hardest in North America, why it ran through a specific therapeutic technology rather than through the diagnosis alone, and why the alter counts rose even as the diagnosis grew more respectable. My own contribution to the argument, which I want you to stress-test rather than adopt, is a three-rung decomposition of what "looping" can mean. On the first rung, purely descriptive, the label supplies vocabulary: people come to narrate distress in its terms, and only the reporting changes. On the second, behavioral, the label changes conduct and self-interpretation: people start genuinely having the experiences, alters multiply inside the therapy hour because the therapeutic situation calls for alters. On the third, evidential, the label changes the classifiers' thresholds and the archive they draw on: the case series that confirm the kind are partly manufactured by the search for it. My worry is that the rungs slide. Rung one masquerades as rung three, because if descriptions change, the record changes even when nothing else does; and rung two looks exactly like rung three to a skeptic who has already decided the phenomenon is enacted. Only rung three is supposed to threaten realism about the kind, and only rung two is supposed to make the classification a genuine causal power. Tell me whether the multiple personality episode can actually be sorted along those rungs, or whether the sort collapses on contact with the historical record — and if it collapses, say which rung does the real work in my friend's argument and in mine.

Three further things. Second, the dynamics: a looping explanation of an epidemic needs positive feedback — media to patients to clinicians to journals to media — but epidemics also end, and the theory as usually stated says nothing about damping. Work through the candidate dampers in this case (retractions, lawsuits, the suggestibility literature, the collapse of reimbursement for years-long alter-integration work, professional embarrassment, the renaming) and say for each whether it is an output of the loop itself or an external shock. A looping account that needs external shocks to explain the decline looks a lot weaker than one that does not, and I want to know which we have here. Third, and connected: the puzzle I could not solve last night is that the diagnosis survived the discrediting of its epidemic. The 1980s loop burned out, the flagship case was exposed as substantially constructed, and yet the category persisted, regained prevalence claims, and then started looping again on a new medium. Does looping theory anticipate that a kind can outlive the refutation of its first loop, and what does that fact say about whether the loops were ever evidence about the kind's reality at all? Which brings me to the disagreement itself. My friend reads Hacking as an anti-realist who showed multiple personality to be a cultural artifact; I argued that he was careful to insist that interactive kinds can be looping and real at once, and that her reading turns a point about classification into a debunking he did not commit to. Adjudicate that between us as best you can on the conceptual level, without needing to settle what he actually wrote: does the distinction between "this kind loops" and "this kind is unreal" have any purchase on this particular episode, or is it idle here? Relatedly, give me a counterfactual test — what the history would have to look like for this not to be a looping effect, and whether such a description is available at all, or whether the hypothesis simply redescribes anything culturally contagious. Finally, as a check on whether your account generalizes, close with a single paragraph saying what the same framework predicts about the expansion of the autism spectrum from the 1990s onward, where the classified organized and rewrote the category from below — the diagnostic label that the community itself repudiated was removed from the manual, and prevalence estimates climbed by orders of magnitude — and whether that episode is the same phenomenon, its mirror image, or evidence that the framework is being stretched past its useful range.

Two notes on how I want this handled. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I have the primary material and I want the analysis. But I do want you to mark your confidence as you go: distinguish the steps where you are relying on settled facts from those where you are reconstructing or extrapolating, and flag any place you suspect I have misstated the history, since my recollection is secondhand in spots. And please push back where pushback is due. If you think the framing rests on a confused premise — if the three rungs are not really three, or if this episode is a poor test of looping effects because the historical record is too contested on both sides to support the mapping, or if the whole thing is better described by some mechanism that has nothing to do with classification — say so plainly at the outset rather than complying politely and quietly working around it. I would much rather be told that the question is malformed than be given a well-written answer to a bad one.
