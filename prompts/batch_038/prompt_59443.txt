I'm refereeing a chapter for a volume on the architecture of autonomous systems, and one of its two load-bearing arguments has me stuck. The chapter's thesis is that restricting an agent's inputs is never a safety measure: any design that withholds available observations from a decision-making component is, on the authors' account, the deliberate installation of a defect. Their argument runs as follows.

(1) Good's theorem: if an agent ranks acts by expected utility relative to its own credences, then for any costless signal, the policy of waiting, observing the signal, and afterwards choosing whichever act maximizes expected utility conditional on it has expected utility at least as great as choosing an act now, in ignorance of the signal.
(2) The proof is two lines of convexity: the expectation of a maximum is never less than the maximum of an expectation. Nothing in it turns on the content of the signal — not on how surprising the signal is, not on whether the agent would welcome what it says, not on what it is about.
(3) So the theorem ranges over every signal there could be: signals about states the agent cannot influence, signals about the agent's own later choices, signals the agent would pay not to receive.
(4) For such an agent, rationality is nothing over and above selecting the act with the highest expected utility by its own lights.
(5) Refusing a costless signal therefore selects a policy that is never strictly better, and is sometimes strictly worse, than accepting it. There is no signal for which refusing beats accepting. So a rational agent refuses no free signal: not the distressing one, not the irrelevant one, not the one about itself.
(6) The reasoning transfers intact to whoever builds the agent and configures its inputs. Blinding a component, filtering its observations, or putting it on a need-to-know footing imposes the never-better and sometimes-worse policy on it from outside. That is a defect in the design and is not redeemed by the designer's intentions, however cautious.
(7) Hence exhaustive information-seeking is a requirement of rationality rather than one disposition among others; what we call strategic ignorance is the name we give to irrationality we have decided to tolerate; and no principled limit exists on what an agent may be permitted to observe.

I cannot accept (7), and I do not think the authors can either, since three of their own worked examples depend on blinding a subsystem. These are the cases I keep reaching for. (a) A dose-finding trial in which neither the patient nor the clinician knows which arm the patient has been assigned to, and where the chapter concedes that the evidential value of the result depends on that ignorance holding. (b) A patient offered a test for a progressive condition with no available treatment, who declines, saying he would rather have thirty years than thirty years spent waiting. (c) A negotiator who has the map of the contested valley destroyed before the talks begin, because he knows that once he can see the pass he will be unable to let it go, and because the other side knows this as well as he does. (d) A desk head who instructs his broker to withhold intraday prices, having read his own blotter and knowing what he does with them. (e) A subject handed a sealed note which a well-calibrated predictor claims states which of two boxes he will take tomorrow, and who leaves it unopened.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to rank my attachments, I would rather keep (2) than anything else in the chain — the convexity argument is the one part of the chapter I am confident is correct — and I would like to keep (4) as well. I should add that I do not assume all five of my cases are counterexamples; part of what I want from you is the sorting. Tell me which of them genuinely threaten the argument, which are perfectly consistent with it once described precisely, and what separates the two groups. If the trouble is that (5) and (6) extract more than (1) licenses, then state exactly what the theorem does license, what conditions its proof quietly relies on, and what would be needed to get from the weakened result to something a designer could act on — because otherwise I expect a deflated version of (7) to survive, and I want to know how deflated it can get before the chapter's thesis stops being interesting. And if you think (7) does follow after all, tell me which of my five cases I have misdescribed and how.
