ok so context — I'm a senior and I run my school's "infinity club" (about ten of us, we are absolutely the kind of people who watch youtube videos about the continuum hypothesis for fun). Our teacher is letting each of us write one question for the end-of-year challenge exam the club sits. I got the actual-infinity one: 40 minutes, 25 marks total, one essay question, blank scratch paper allowed, no notes.

What they already know: everyone has seen the Hilbert's hotel thing and can recite it (guest in room n moves to room 2n, boom, all the odd rooms are free for the infinite bus). They know Cantor's diagonal argument informally. They've heard the phrase "actual vs potential infinity" and know that Aristotle and finitists have a problem with completed infinities. So a question that just asks them to explain the hotel is worthless as a discriminator — they'd all nail it and I'd learn nothing.

Here's the raw material I want the question built out of, which I'm pretty sure none of them have seen. A club member (call him Devin) said the hotel trick is basically cheating because it lets you move people arbitrarily far. So he proposed a rule: every existing guest has to move at most k rooms, where k is some fixed number decided in advance. Under that rule he claims you can never free up more than k rooms, so you could never fit the infinite bus in, not even close. I checked k=1 by hand and I think hes right — the best you can do is shove everyone up one, which frees exactly room 1. For general k I think you can count it out: the guests from rooms 1 through N can only end up in rooms 1 through N+k, so of the first N+k rooms at least N are still occupied, which leaves at most k empty. Something like that, anyway.

Then Devin draws a big conclusion from it. If you can only ever free k rooms when nobody gets shoved too far, then the whole "just as many naturals as evens" business isn't tracking size at all — it's an artifact of a rule that lets you do unlimited amounts of moving. Which he thinks means Euclid was right that a whole is always bigger than any of its parts, and Cantor is just refusing to admit it.

So: write me the exam question around this. I want part (a) to be them establishing the at-most-k result properly (my counting sketch above is hand-wavy and I want the model answer to do it cleanly), and part (b) to be them evaluating Devin's leap from that result to the Euclid point. The crux I care about is whether (b) is answerable by someone who just sides with Devin because the math in (a) felt finitist-friendly. I want a top answer to notice that (a) is a theorem about bijections that respect a bound on distance, and to say clearly whether that is actually evidence about how many elements a set has — either direction is fine, but it has to be argued, not asserted. If a good answer can partly concede Devin's point somewhere, I want it to say where and why.

One more thing before you write anything: tell me if my math is even right, because if the at-most-k claim is false the whole question collapses and I will look like an idiot in front of ten people.

Format please, in this order:

- **Math check**: ~100 words — is the claim true as I stated it, and is my counting argument right or does it need fixing
- **The question**: as it would appear on the paper, with (a) and (b) and the marks split, under 200 words, readable by a high schooler in one pass
- **Model answer**: ~750 words — what the best kid in the club writes in 40 minutes, not a journal article
- **Marking scheme**: itemised to 25 marks, and be specific about which moves seperate an A from a B, because I want to hold it next to a script and know within a minute which pile it goes in
- **Where they'll go wrong**: five or six mistakes I should expect, a line each, plus how many marks each one costs

No reading list or bibliography — they can't look anything up and neither can I.
