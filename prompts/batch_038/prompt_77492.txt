Our infrastructure team runs an informal reading group, and last week it drifted out of a paper on model-generated imagery into an argument about forgeries that I still haven't settled in my own head. One of my colleagues — a strong engineer, the person who designed our whole artifact repository — offered this analogy, and it landed well with the room: a forgery that is visually indistinguishable from the original is like two files with identical bytes but different git histories. The content hash is the same, so for every purpose that could possibly matter the artifact is the same object; the only difference is metadata about who committed it and when. On his account, paying more for the original is like paying more for a build because of the author field in the commit record — the premium on provenance is a caching error, a stale belief about the object that the available evidence no longer supports. He then extended it: the same reasoning disposes of objections to AI-generated images, since a generated image that is perceptually identical to a photograph or a painting is in the relevant sense the same file, and the complaint is only about metadata.

I want to push on this, but I lack the vocabulary to say why. Two things bother me. First, the analogy seems to fail inside our own domain. When someone reimplements a hard algorithm and the result is byte-identical, we do not treat the two implementations as interchangeable. A clean-room reimplementation and a copy of the original both give you the same output, yet we call one engineering and the other copying, and we credit the original author with having solved the problem first even though the diff against their version is empty. If identical output really did collapse the distinction, that practice would be incoherent, and I don't think it is. Second, an art historian friend raised the case of the Dutch forger who fabricated supposedly newly discovered Vermeers in the 1930s — the most famous being an invented composition that Vermeer had never painted, purchased by a Rotterdam museum in 1937 and authenticated by leading scholars of the period. Her point was that after the confession, critics who had praised that canvas began reporting that it looked to them like a strained, dated pastiche. That is awkward for my colleague's view: the bytes did not change, and yet the perceptual experience apparently did. Either the analogy misidentifies what the object of appreciation is, or the critics were confabulating, and I can't tell which.

So please audit the analogy. Start with an explicit correspondence table: what in the painting case plays the role of the file content, the content hash, the commit history, the author field, the build process, the runtime behavior, the license. Then give me the places where the mapping breaks, but separate the disanalogies that actually threaten his conclusion from the ones that are noise — for instance, I don't think the fact that a file copies in milliseconds while a canvas takes months does any work against him. Tell me what the analogy is quietly importing, especially any assumption that the value of a thing is a function of its output alone, and whether it also assumes that perceptual indistinguishability is as stable and testable as byte-identity is. Then judge the two conclusions separately: does the metadata-only view survive for human forgeries of paintings, and does the extended version survive for model-generated images, where the "implementation" was itself fitted to millions of other people's outputs? If the analogy has to be scrapped, propose a better one — from software or anywhere else — and state precisely what that better analogy does and does not license. I have no formal background in philosophy, so define any term of art you use, and don't send me off to sources or further reading; I want the reasoning laid out, not a bibliography.
