I've been crashing my partner's history-of-science reading group for years (I do software, they tolerate me for the coffee runs). Last week they were re-drafting the intro survey and two of the grad students, Dana and Ravi, got into it over whether to keep a unit titled "The Scientific Revolution." It went like this:

(1) Dana: cut it. Nobody living through the thing called it that; the label got fixed afterwards, by people writing a triumphal arc from Copernicus to Newton. There's no non-arbitrary start or end either — 1543, 1687, the 1660s, take your pick — so the phrase names a story we used to tell about ourselves, not a period.

(2) Ravi: arbitrary boundaries don't prove there's nothing there. "Bald" has no non-arbitrary cutoff either and it's a perfectly real property. If vagueness disqualifies a term you'd have to drop "the Bronze Age," "the Enlightenment," and "adult" too.

(3) Dana: bad analogy. "Bald" picks out something real about heads. This term was introduced as part of a story whose content was mostly false — a small set of Europeans in a century and a half inventing the modern world, everything before it superstition, everything after it consequence. When a term is introduced by a theory that turns out false, reference doesn't automatically survive; sometimes it shifts, sometimes it just fails.

(4) Ravi: then use the ordinary fix — what pins down the reference isn't the introducers' beliefs but whatever in the world best explains why they said what they said and got some of it right. And there's a real candidate: a shift in how claims about nature got warranted. Instruments, repeatable controlled experiments, journals and correspondence networks, mathematized idealization, institutions able to enforce credibility. That cluster coheres, and it changed what counted as evidence.

(5) Dana: you saved the word by redefining it, and what you've defined isn't what the fight was about. If the phrase now just means "the shift in evidential norms," the actually contested questions — was it one thing, was it a revolution, was it European — got settled by stipulation. Also half your cluster has precursors: medieval Paris and Oxford, Islamic astronomy, Jesuit mathematics in China. So your candidate isn't obviously more natural than a rival one covering a longer, wider stretch.

(6) Ravi: fine — but if two candidate properties are both decent and neither uniquely wins, the right verdict is that the term is indeterminate, not that there's no fact of the matter. And indeterminate terms stay worth using when the disambiguations agree on most of what matters.

(7) Dana: they don't agree on most of what matters, that's exactly the part that was contested. In a required survey an indeterminate label isn't neutral convenience, it's a hidden argument — students silently get whichever disambiguation the textbook happens to carry.

(8) Ravi: then keep the unit and make the dispute over the label the unit. You can't teach students that the periodization is contested if the contested label isn't on the syllabus.
