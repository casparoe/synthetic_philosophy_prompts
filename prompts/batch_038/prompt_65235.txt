I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I've gotten myself stuck on something that started as a work thing and turned out to be a philosophy problem. I want to dump my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and have someone tell me what principle they add up to, if there is one.

Context: I work on a service that other companies' systems call into. Normally nothing life-or-death, though we do run payroll for a couple of clients, which is closer to real life than it sounds. I've been collecting cases, some from work and some made up but realistic, and my intuitions refuse to line up.

1. A bad deploy goes out with a bug that silently fails about 1% of transactions. I see it on the dashboard and don't roll back because it's Friday and I want to go home. Customers lose money. → Not fine. I clearly did something wrong.
2. Exact same bug ships, but nobody notices for a week. Same total harm. → Less bad, but not innocent-feeling either. Like the team is still on the hook somehow.
3. We have an automated watchdog that restarts a service if it hangs. I disable it for a load test and forget to re-enable it. The service hangs, a payroll run stalls, four thousand people don't get paid on the 1st, some of them eat overdraft fees and one gets an eviction notice. → This feels worse than case 1 to me, and I can't explain why. I didn't cause the hang. The hang was going to happen either way.
4. Same payroll client, same hang, same damage — but the watchdog never existed, because nobody ever built one. → Feels clearly less bad than 3. Almost nobody's fault. But the world from the moment of the hang onward is identical in 3 and 4, and that's the pair I can't reconcile. The only difference is what was sitting there beforehand.
5. A rate limiter is wrongly dropping legitimate traffic from one region. I raise the limit. A fraudster floods us and drains customer accounts. → I feel bad, but I don't feel like I harmed those customers in the same way I would have if I'd…
6. …written a script that moved their money out myself. Same number of victims, same dollars. → Obviously vastly worse.
7. On-call, I get paged at 3am, I look at it, decide it's a known non-issue, go back to sleep. It wasn't a known non-issue. → Bad, but I'd file that under bad judgment, not under "I did it."
8. Same pager, but instead I permanently mute that alert so it stops waking the whole team up. Months later it masks a real outage. → This feels like *doing* something, and worse than 7, even though 7 and 8 are both just "you ignored a signal."
9. I ship a feature that's genuinely good for people, with a side effect I foresaw and accepted anyway — a ranking tweak that boosts engagement and makes a chunk of users feel awful about their lives. → I feel worse about this than about 5, which is strange, since 5 was also a deliberate action.

I know the trolley problem and this isn't that. There's no lever and no five-versus-one. What I seem to be tracking is something dumb like "did I move or did I not move" — except 3 and 8 break it, because in both I definitely moved, I typed a command, and the harm came from something I stopped from happening rather than something I started.

So: what's the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that gets the most of these right? I'd like it stated precisely, clause by clause, with which of my cases each clause is there to handle — I don't want a slogan, I want something I could almost check against. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle can't save and I should just give up on. And tell me what it says about a few cases I haven't listed: deleting a cron job that was doing nightly backups (I assume that's 3 again?), leaving a known memory leak unfixed in a repo you're handing to another team, and shipping code you know another team will misuse because it's their call, not yours.

One more thing that bugs me and that I'd like an answer on. In our infra the "default" state of the world is just whatever the last person committed. Nothing happens unless some code says so, and every line of code someone else wrote is already acting on the world continuously, whether or not I'm around. So it feels like there's no neutral baseline for me to be non-intervening *from*. If that's right, does the whole doing-versus-allowing framework even apply to software, or does it only work for bodies in rooms where you can point at what would have happened if you'd stood still?
