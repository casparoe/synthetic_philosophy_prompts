Elizabeth Anscombe died in January 2001 and so never witnessed the subsequent two decades of warfare, but her 1958 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree" — written in protest of Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary doctorate — leaves behind a fairly determinate framework. Her charge was that Truman was a murderer, because, as she put it, "choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder," and the inhabitants of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were by and large noncombatants. She also thought the appearance of necessity was manufactured by the Allies' own insistence on unconditional surrender. Two further features of her position matter for any extrapolation: she was emphatically not a pacifist, holding that war can be legitimate and that killing enemy combatants can be permissible; and her objection ran through the philosophy of action rather than through a calculus of outcomes, since what mattered to her was what was chosen, under what description, and by whom.

Predict, with argument, how Anscombe would assess each of the following developments, all of which postdate her:

1. Remotely piloted strike programs in which targets are pre-approved together with a numeric threshold of anticipated civilian deaths, above which authority escalates and below which the strike proceeds — and in which the operator's own account is that the civilian deaths were foreseen but not intended. Does her analysis of intention permit that self-description, or does it collapse?

2. The casualty-accounting practice, widely reported in the press, of counting every military-age male in a strike zone as a combatant unless specific intelligence establishes otherwise, so that a strike's civilian tally can be zero as a matter of bookkeeping. Note that this changes the description of the act rather than the act; consider what her account of act descriptions implies about that.

3. The debate over lethal autonomous weapons systems and the demand for "meaningful human control" as the condition of legitimacy. Can there be a violation of the prohibition she treats as exceptionless when no agent's intention is directed at the deaths? Is the human-control requirement, on her way of thinking, a genuine moral constraint or a way of locating someone to hold responsible after the fact?

4. Revisionist just war theory as developed since 2000 by philosophers such as Jeff McMahan: the rejection of the moral equality of combatants, the claim that liability to be killed tracks individual moral responsibility rather than uniform status, and the consequences that follow — that some civilians may be liable to attack while many conscripted or otherwise non-culpable soldiers may not be.

5. Nuclear deterrence as it is now practiced, in which a state maintains a declared readiness to destroy cities in retaliation. Treat the deterrent intention as conditional and ask whether, on her account, a conditional intention to do what may never be done is itself a choice.

For each of the five, I want the following: the verdict you predict she would reach; the argument she would actually give, articulated in her own conceptual vocabulary — intention as distinct from foresight, the direction of intention, the description under which an act is chosen, the exceptionlessness of the prohibition on murdering the innocent — rather than in the generic vocabulary of contemporary applied ethics; and an explicit statement of where her published framework runs out and she would have to build something new rather than apply something already there.

In addition, identify at least one point on which she would find herself agreeing with people whose views she would otherwise reject, and at least one on which she would oppose people whose general sympathies she would share; the interesting cases are the ones that cut against her expected political and confessional alignment. Flag at least two ways a careless answer would mispredict her — for instance by assuming that a traditional Catholic must therefore accept the doctrine of double effect as military lawyers deploy it, or by assuming that a critic of consequentialism must therefore be a pacifist. Give each prediction a confidence rating, and for each say what single further fact about her thinking would most change your prediction.

Write roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words in American English. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and do not put any words in quotation marks as hers: paraphrase her reasoning instead.
