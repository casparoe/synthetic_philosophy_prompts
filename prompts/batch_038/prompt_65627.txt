I help edit a small public-philosophy zine — think 40 pages, printed on cheap stock, distributed mostly at bookstores and a couple of campuses — and the next issue's theme is "contact." My co-editor wants the issue to open with a story instead of an essay, because we both know that any essay on Benacerraf's dilemma ends up being the same three paragraphs restated. So here's what I want to commission from you: a short story, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, that dramatizes the dilemma from Benacerraf's 1973 paper *Mathematical Truth* without ever stating it. She was an algebraic geometer before she got into editing, so she will absolutely notice if the philosophy is fudged, and I don't want to be the one who has to explain it to her.

Here's the seed I've been sitting on, feel free to change names and furniture but keep the shape. A human delegation has made contact with a species whose mathematicians have literal, physical contact with their numbers — the objects of arithmetic are things you can pick up, trade, wear down, and point an instrument at, and their proofs are demonstrations rather than arguments. Their arithmetic never goes wrong. But the delegation gradually notices two things. First, the aliens have no word for what we'd call mathematical truth holding independently of anyone: because their numbers are reachable, different clans work with different progressions — different objects playing the role of zero, of one, of three — and every clan's arithmetic works perfectly and none of them can say why theirs is *the* numbers rather than a numbering. Second, when the human protagonist explains that her own arithmetic has exactly the same content but that no one can say how she knows any of it, the aliens can't parse the problem, and the reason they can't parse it is that they take it for granted that knowing something requires being causally hooked up to it — an assumption she realizes she has been making too, and that her whole predicament depends on. The plot turn I'd like: the delegation is offered the chance to be hooked up the same way, there's a deadline and something like a signature, and she has to decide whether a mathematics you can explain is worth more than a mathematics that's about something. The ending is yours.

Ground rules. No character says the words Platonism, abstract object, epistemology, or Benacerraf, and nobody explains the dilemma out loud over dinner — it has to be carried by what happens and what she notices. I'd like it to be recognizable as the real thing to someone who's read the paper: one horn where arithmetic is about objects outside space and time that our beliefs could never be causally responsive to, so knowledge of them looks impossible on any ordinary causal account of knowledge; the other horn where you make the objects reachable and get an explicable knowledge at the cost of there no longer being a determinate fact of the matter. A little texture from the 1965 *What Numbers Could Not Be* is welcome — the arbitrariness of which objects get to be the numbers — and it'd be great if the story left a crack open for the thought that the causal account of knowledge is itself an assumption rather than a law of nature, without turning that into a sermon. Put the story first, then one section titled "What's doing the work" — call it 200 to 300 words — mapping the story's pieces onto the two horns, saying where you stretched or simplified the argument to make it narratable, and naming the one thing you deliberately left unresolved.
