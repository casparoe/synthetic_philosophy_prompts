I am a novelist, and I have reached a scene I cannot write. The narrator's older brother — a structural engineer who signs off on bridges, and who has, since their parents' deaths, effectively been the one who raised her — is giving the toast at her wedding. He is a man who has absorbed the argument sometimes called pragmatic encroachment without ever reading it: he holds that whether someone knows a thing depends partly on what would be lost by being wrong, and that the very evidence which licenses a stranger's confidence does not license his. Because more rides on this marriage for him than for anyone else under that roof, he finds that he cannot honestly claim to know how it will go. Nor can he retreat into "I don't know," which a hundred and twenty guests — her aunts, his rugby teammates — would hear as a warning issued from the head table. The bride is herself an epistemologist, and three of her colleagues are seated at her table; she will know precisely what her brother is doing.

Please write the toast as it is delivered aloud, around 450 words. It must be built out of speech acts that are not knowledge claims — things a man in his position can honestly *do* rather than predict — so that the family hears blessing and assurance exactly where the philosophers hear a deliberately chosen substitute for assertion. No names of philosophers, no technical vocabulary, no explanation of the idea, and no winking at the head table. The parents may appear only obliquely. Include one line the family would repeat for years without ever realizing it was philosophy, and one moment of real laughter that costs the bride nothing.
