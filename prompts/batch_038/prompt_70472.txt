I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British current-affairs magazine, and I am in the middle of reporting a long piece about how disasters get explained — not the seismology or the engineering failures, but the stories we tell afterwards about why people behaved as they did. The spine of the piece is the aftermath of the Great East Japan Earthquake of 11 March 2011. For the better part of a fortnight the international press could hardly describe the event without describing Japanese character: the queues for water and petrol that nobody jumped, the cash and wallets handed in to police boxes, the absence of any looting on a meaningful scale, the evacuees who thanked rescuers, the workers who stayed at the Fukushima Daiichi plant after the meltdowns began. Commentators reached, more or less explicitly, for a national-character explanation — shame, group loyalty, a Confucian inheritance, the famous Japanese word for endurance — and a good many of them reached for the same vocabulary that has been in circulation since Ruth Benedict compiled her study of Japan for the US Office of War Information in the mid-1940s, at a distance, from documents and interviews with Japanese-Americans, having never been to the country, and which popularised the contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures. That book was translated into Japanese in 1948, sold enormously there, and is usually credited with seeding four decades of domestic essays on Japaneseness — a case, as several Japanese scholars complained at the time, of mistaking a society in acute dislocation for a normal condition, and an extraordinary historical moment for a fixed norm of behaviour. I want to know whether the 2011 coverage was doing the same thing.

Since you cannot look any of this up, here are the facts I am treating as settled, so that the argument does not have to rest on my reporting. On 11 March 2011 a megathrust earthquake of magnitude 9.0 to 9.1 struck off the Oshika Peninsula; the tsunami killed something under 20,000 people and left a couple of thousand missing; three reactors at Fukushima Daiichi melted down; hundreds of thousands were evacuated from the surrounding zones, and more than two hundred thousand were still living away from home four years later. As a control case from further back in the same country: on 1 September 1923 the Great Kanto Earthquake killed somewhere between 105,000 and 140,000 people, martial law was declared, police and officials circulated false rumours that ethnic Koreans were poisoning wells and planning arson, and vigilante bands, together with soldiers and police, killed an estimated 6,000 people, mostly Koreans but also Chinese and Japanese who were misidentified or who held the wrong politics. Japan still marks 1 September as Disaster Prevention Day. As a control case from another country: after Hurricane Katrina in New Orleans in 2005, coverage was dominated by looting, sniper fire and bodies in the Superdome, much of which was later shown to be wildly exaggerated or simply invented, while most of the rescues were in fact carried out by neighbours in boats; Rebecca Solnit's 2009 book on disaster, drawing on a strand of disaster sociology going back to Charles Fritz, argues that mass panic is rare and mutual aid typical, and her cases are San Francisco in 1906, Halifax in 1917, Mexico City in 1985, New York after 11 September and New Orleans — a species-level claim, not a national one. There were also, in 2011 and after, widely reported instances of evacuees from Fukushima being shunned or their children bullied at school; I flag that as the weakest item in my list and would not want the analysis to bear much weight on it.

What I want from you is a case analysis that runs in both directions, and I am not after a reading list or a set of references — I know the literature well enough; I need the reasoning. First, the mapping, and please be disciplined about it. State precisely what the explanandum is: which behaviours, over what window, in which places, and observed by whom, since orderly queues are also simply very photographable. Then state what the national-character thesis actually claims when used this way — that a durable, shared, internally coherent set of dispositions carried by a people explains the aggregate behaviour — and line up the rival explanantia against it: state capacity and a century of drills, dense civic and neighbourhood organisation, low inequality, the visibility of conduct under conditions where everybody is watching everybody, the reputational stakes of being seen to jump a queue, the structure of the event itself, and the possibility, following Solnit and Fritz, that solidarity is the human default in catastrophe so that nothing specifically Japanese needs explaining at all. Say which of these can absorb how much of the 2011 record, and be explicit about where the character hypothesis is silent rather than forcing it to speak.

Second, put the thesis under pressure with the cases I have given you, and tell me honestly what survives. A single national character has to produce both the queue and the lynching mob of 1923 — can it, and if it can, what does the concept now cost you in explanatory content? Consider the unfalsifiability worry, of which the cleanest instance is that group harmony was offered as the cause of Japan's postwar economic miracle and then, from the 1990s onwards, the same disposition was offered as the cause of stagnation, redescribed as excessive conformity. Consider the reflexivity problem, which I think is the most interesting thing about the Benedict material: a character description that becomes widely believed by the people it describes may start to cause the very conduct it purports to explain, which makes the description true and empty at once — tell me whether that is a reason to distrust it or a reason to take it seriously as a social mechanism. And consider what the Japan–New Orleans comparison does to the whole enterprise: if two populations behaved in broadly similar ways and the two press corps told opposite stories, then perhaps national character was never a property of the observed but of the observers, in which case the thing that needs explaining in 2011 is Western journalism, not Japan.

Third, and this is the part my editor will interrogate, the normative half. Given everything above, is there a form of generalisation about peoples that survives — statistical, revisable, explicitly about distributions rather than essences, disciplined about base rates and about the difference between what a group does and what a group is — and if so, does the 2011 case meet its conditions? Then tell me whether the constraint on such generalisations is epistemic, moral, or both, and whether they come apart: if a claim is epistemically licensed but foreseeably feeds a discourse that has been used to justify internment and massacre, does that settle whether I may print it, or only how I must frame it? I would like a clear answer to a concrete question — may I write the sentence about the queues, and in what form — plus a statement of what the episode ultimately shows about national character as a concept: whether it is confirmed here, refuted here, or simply doing no work that something else was not already doing. Please also tell me where you think my summary of the facts is wrong or where your confidence runs out, rather than filling the gap with invention.
