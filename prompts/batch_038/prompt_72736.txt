I am seventeen and in my final year of secondary school. Two years ago I founded a forecasting society here; we are eleven members, we post questions on a shared spreadsheet, and we score one another with Brier scores at the end of each term. Our first-year reading was Tetlock's book on forecasting, which is where I picked up the habit of starting from how often a thing has happened rather than from the story of the particular case.

Over the summer I built a dataset for the long-run questions the club keeps asking. I hand-coded forty-one polities, from the Neo-Assyrian Empire to the present, that I would count as great powers, and recorded how long each held that status. I am now writing the methods chapter of the club's handbook, in which I want to state plainly what I am committed to when I forecast about the deep future. Before it is printed, I would like a consistency audit. My commitments, stated as I would state them in public:

1. Base rate first. For any question about the future I begin from how often that kind of thing has happened before, and the resulting number is the anchor. Adjustments away from the anchor are permitted, but each one owes a justification; the anchor owes none.

2. Reference classes must be fixed before you look at outcomes. I know that most published lists of great powers are assembled after the fact, so I wrote down three membership criteria in advance — the polity could project force well beyond its home region, its contemporaries treated it as one of the leading powers, and it controlled a substantial share of world population — and only then coded durations. I would not accept a base rate from anyone who did it the other way round.

3. I also hold that there is no determinate fact about how long many of these polities lasted. Whether Rome's status ends in 395, 476, 1204, or 1453 is a choice rather than a discovery, and for perhaps a third of my list the choice moves the duration by a century or more.

4. My unit of analysis is the polity, not the year; I count polities. In September's newsletter I put fifteen percent on a currently dominant power losing great-power status within the next fifty years, on the grounds that seven of my forty-one polities lost the status within fifty years of attaining it. Three of the forty-one still hold it, so their rows record the age they have reached rather than an end date.

5. I will not attach numbers to questions that resolve after I expect to be dead. Scoring is the whole discipline; a number that no one will ever be scored on is a performance of rigor rather than rigor. The handbook will say this explicitly.

6. A base rate from forty-one cases is statistically close to worthless — the uncertainty on it is enormous. It is nonetheless far better than my unaided intuition about the same question, and I would take it every time.

7. Narrative history is nearly useless for prediction, because it is written by people who already know how the story ends. But my own coding of which polities belong in the class depended on my narrative understanding of what each of them was, and I see no problem in that.

8. The modern world is unprecedented in ways that matter — industrial growth, nuclear weapons, integrated finance — so pre-industrial polities cannot straightforwardly tell me anything about what comes next. I use them anyway, and I think that is defensible.

9. The point of the whole project is practical: I want my club, and eventually the commentators my country takes seriously, to stop reasoning about the future from edifying stories about the past and start reasoning from what actually happened to things of that kind.

Please go through these and tell me which pull against one another and how badly. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; for each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me. Separately, tell me which pairs only look like tensions, and why the appearance arises — I expect I will be accused of some contradictions that are not there. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I deliberately have not said which of these I am most attached to, because I suspect that would steer you toward preserving it; treat my attachment as unknown.

Two things about your own confidence. For each tension you identify, say how sure you are that it is a genuine problem with the package rather than an artifact of how I described it. And close by telling me what would change your mind: what argument or consideration would lead you to conclude that this set of commitments does hang together after all, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail — in particular, whether you are holding a hand-coded spreadsheet assembled by a schoolboy to standards borrowed from statistics that it cannot meet and does not need to meet.
