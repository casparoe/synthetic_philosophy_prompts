I am three-quarters through a novel whose engine is indecision, and for two years I have sent each finished chapter to a friend who teaches philosophy at a small college. She has no professional stake in the book; she reads it as a reader and then, unasked, rules on whether my characters have actually chosen anything. Her rulings have become the private case law of the manuscript. She has more than once observed that my protagonist is a scholastic donkey with a mortgage. She is away for the term, and the last chapter must exist before she reads it, so I should like to know how it will be decided — because the ending is a different ending under each decision.

Her verdicts so far:

(i) A man offered two posts in two cities, identical in pay, standing, and distance from everyone he loves, who cannot decide and lets the committee's deadline decide, taking the one still open: not a choice. "The committee chose," she wrote. "He was merely present."

(ii) The same man in an earlier draft, who takes the northern post because he once spent a happy fortnight there as a boy: a choice, unambiguously, though she thought the reason thin.

(iii) A woman in a shop facing two grey sweaters she cannot distinguish on any ground she can state, who reaches for one: a choice, but grudgingly conceded. In the margin she noted that the woman had chosen with her hand rather than with her judgment, and that she was not certain the distinction would hold up under pressure.

(iv) A man who, unable to rank two options, walks out and takes the colour of the next passing truck as his sign, and obeys it: not a choice. Superstition, she said, is an abdication of the question rather than an answer to it.

(v) A man who, knowing in advance that he will be unable to rank two options, adopts a rule while still calm — if the light is red at the corner, he turns left — and follows it when the light goes red: a choice, and she added that it was a better one than I appeared to think, but declined to explain.

The unwritten chapter ends as follows. Seven months before the decisive scene, in a mood of unusual self-command, my protagonist writes himself a sealed instruction, to be opened only if he should ever find two courses of action genuinely equal; it names one of them. In the scene he finds them equal, opens the letter, and obeys. He has no preference at that moment — none whatever — and he finds the handwriting and the reasoning of the letter faintly absurd, the artefact of a stranger's better mood. He obeys nonetheless, and precisely because it is his own: he holds that a man who will not honour such an instruction has no self left to honour. The chapter closes on him walking into the outcome the letter specifies.

Will she call that a choice? I should like four things, in this order. First, set out the candidate rules that accommodate verdicts (i) through (v); more than one does, and I suspect they part company exactly here — note that (iv) and (v) are near-twins in structure, an indifferent man letting an external event settle it, and she ruled against one and for the other, so any candidate rule owes me an account of that split. Second, say what each candidate delivers on the sealed letter. Third, tell me which one she will in fact apply, and why it fits her case law better than its rivals — attend especially to the weight of (iii) and of her unexplained remark about (v). Fourth, name the further scene, as concretely specified as the five above, that would settle which rule she is running, and explain why the ones I already have fail to settle it.

And one question of craft, since the meaning of the chapter turns on her answer. If the letter chose, I have written a scene about a man who has arranged in advance to be absent from his own life; if he chose, I have written a scene about a man who is present only by proxy, which is a colder and I think a better thing. Say which of these readings each rival rule commits me to, and whether her case law permits a third that I have not thought of — for instance that the letter and the hand that opened it are not the same agent, so that the scene is about something other than either of my two options.
