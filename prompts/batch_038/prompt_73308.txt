I cover neuroscience and the philosophy of mind for a national science desk, and I am reporting a long feature on whether integrated information theory is empirically accountable. Rather than stage another exchange of open letters, I have persuaded two people to undertake a good-faith adversarial collaboration and to sign a single joint statement. One is a working IIT theorist who accepts the axioms-to-postulates method and treats integrated information as a quantity that can in principle be computed for a specified substrate. The other is a methodologist who presses what is sometimes called the unfolding argument: the claim that a theory constructed by mapping first-person axioms onto physical postulates can always be re-unfolded to accommodate whatever the data turn out to be, so that the theory is empirically unconstrained rather than merely difficult to test. Both regard the other as reasonable and both have agreed in advance not to relitigate the framing once the document is drafted.

Please draft that joint statement. It should contain four parts.

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign: what each accepts about the relevant empirical record, about what IIT does and does not assert, and about what the unfolding critique does and does not establish. These must be substantive rather than diplomatic filler; a claim belongs on the list only if a hostile reader from either camp could not deny it without misrepresenting their own side.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the issue; the IIT theorist's formulation, in wording they would accept as a statement of their own view; the methodologist's formulation, likewise; whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, and if verbal, which term is doing the work (candidates include testable, falsifiable, prediction, and post hoc); whether it is a crux, meaning that settling it would move at least one side; the test or argument both accept as bearing on it; and each side's prediction, recorded in advance.

3. For the predictions column, both parties have pre-committed to one shared protocol so that they are not forecasting abstractions. Two artificial circuits of 64 binary units each are fully specified at the level of individual units, with identical total connection counts and an identical input–output function; both are matched at d-prime of 2.0 on the same two-alternative forced-choice discrimination. Circuit F is strictly feedforward; circuit R is densely recurrent. Because the architectures are given, integrated information can be computed exactly at the unit grain, with no coarse-graining choice left open, and both camps have agreed in advance on the same computation and the same grain. State what each side predicts about the ordering of the two values, and, more importantly, what result involving these circuits each would accept as counting against their own position.

4. A closing section in which each side names one finding or argument that would constitute genuine defeat for them, distinguished from one that would merely prompt revision of an auxiliary assumption or of the measurement procedure; plus at least one crux on which the agreed test is an argument rather than an experiment, such as whether the inference from axioms to postulates is deductive or an inference to the best explanation. If you conclude that some portion of the dispute is verbal, say which portion and why, in language neither party would disown.
