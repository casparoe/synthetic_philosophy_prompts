Our department has two people, R and D, who have been going back and forth about Boltzmann brains on and off for about two years, and I've finally talked them into co-writing something instead of shouting across a seminar table. I want you to draft the document they should have produced.

Their positions, in their own framing:

R: the BB argument is a genuine reductio. Take any cosmology with an eternal de Sitter-like late phase — nonzero Lambda, eternal inflation, doesn't matter which. Thermal fluctuations at the horizon temperature produce freak observers at a Boltzmann-suppressed but nonzero rate, so over an infinite future the expected count diverges, so almost all observers are freaks, so a typical observer is a freak. You're not one, so the cosmology is wrong, and you have to be willing to pay: set Lambda to zero, or throw out the measure that did the counting, or drop the whole move of running statistical mechanics inside a horizon spacetime.

D: no cosmology has ever been refuted by an argument of that shape. D's line is that the argument crushes three different questions into one word — how many such things there are, where I am given that count, and whether the person doing the counting can be trusted — and that a hypothesis which entails that the reasoner deploying it is a random fluctuation doesn't get empirically refuted, it self-undermines, which is a different species of failure that R keeps treating like its the same one.

To stop it being about slogans they've both agreed to argue inside one toy. Toy T: an expanding spacetime whose late phase is de Sitter with horizon entropy S = 10^122, containing N = 10^22 ordinary observers, all of whom form in the first 10^11 years. Fluctuation-observer nucleation runs at a rate e^-B per horizon volume per Hubble time, B a free dial. Setting 1: B = 30 and the de Sitter phase ends after 10^40 Hubble times, so the freak count is finite — around 10^27 — and still bigger than N. Setting 2: B is set to whatever both of them take the honest estimate for a brain-sized fluctuation to be (they agree it's enormous, they don't agree on the number) and the de Sitter phase lasts forever, so the count diverges. I know B = 30 is not a remotely realistic suppression for anything brain-shaped; that's deliberate, they agreed the toy isn't about magnitudes. Neither disputes the arithmetic of either setting. They dispute what the settings license.

Draft the joint statement. I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both can actually sign. Include claims about the toy, about what we observe of Lambda, and about what each concedes the other's best argument establishes. Scrub the vocabulary — if a sentence contains a word only one of them would say out loud, rewrite it.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the question in dispute; which of D's three questions it falls under (counting, self-location, credibility) or whether it falls under none of them; R's version of the disagreement in R's preferred phrasing; D's version in D's; a verdict on whether it's substantive, verbal, or two questions wearing one label; the calculation, case, or argument both accept bears on it; R's prediction about that, written down now; D's prediction, written down now.

3. Two cases that have to show up in the table. First, the Told-Brain case: an agent is informed by an oracle it has independent reason to trust that it is a fluctuation observer and that its memories of an ordinary past were deposited by the fluctuation. What should it now believe about the external world, and about the reliability of the reasoning it is doing at this moment? I expect them to diverge here and I expect the divergence to be the real crux, so make them commit rather than hedge. Second, the cheapest-fluctuation case: is the lowest-entropy fluctuation compatible with my present experience a lone brain, a brain plus a thin fake surroundings, or an entire ordinary history? Have each say what they'd need to see actually calculated before they'd move.

4. What each lists as evidence or argument that would move them against their own position — not against the other guy's. Then: is there anything both agree would settle one of the cruxes? If there isn't, say so flatly and say what that implies about whether this collaboration was worth the two years.

5. A short closing section on which parts of the argument were verbal and why, phrased in terms of the specific words that were doing the work.

Don't adjudicate, I'm not asking who's right. I want something both would put their names to, with the residual difference sharp enough that an outsider could say what would change each of their minds. And please don't let it collapse into "they're just using observer differently," which is what everybody says about this debate and which I think is true of only part of it.
