Three years ago I retired from a synchrotron beamline after thirty-odd years of X-ray scattering on alloys, and I've been reading philosophy of mathematics on my own ever since — badly, probably, but happily. Recently I ran into Benacerraf's dilemma from his 1973 paper on mathematical truth, and as somebody who spent a career worrying about whether a detector signal is telling you about the sample or about the detector, I find it genuinely upsetting. It seems to say that if numbers are the sort of things the platonist says they are, then there's no account available of why my beliefs about them are any good, and if instead there is such an account then the numbers stop being what I always took them for.

Here's the situation I want advice about. I've talked five people into a reading group that will meet once a month for the next two years: four retired scientists (two of us did condensed matter, one did population genetics, one was a metrologist, which turns out to be relevant) plus a physics masters student who's the only one of us with any formal philosophy coursework. We can read a bit of set theory and we're not afraid of symbolic logic, but nobody here is going to produce a result that gets cited. I want to spend those twenty-four meetings on the parts of this problem that actually matter, and I'd like you to set the agenda for me: which sub-questions should be on it, and in what order.

I'll tell you the candidate questions as I currently see them, though I expect you'll tell me some of these are malformed and add ones I've missed. There's the question of whether the epistemological horn even bites once you stop assuming knowledge has to be cashed out causally. There's the question of whether the semantic horn — the argument that a satisfying account of mathematical truth has to be platonist — is any good, or whether it can be dodged by reworking what mathematical statements are even saying. There's the fictionalist response, which sounds to me like it should be the scientist's natural home and yet seems to leave it a miracle that the fiction keeps working. There's the plenitudinous kind of platonism that tries to make reliability cheap by saying every consistent possibility is realized. And there's the more naturalistic response that says the whole dilemma rests on an overly demanding picture of how we know anything abstract, and that the right thing to look at is actual mathematical practice.

I also want you to deal with an idea of my own, because I can't tell whether it's any good or whether I'm just flattering myself. It keeps striking me that the dilemma is an instrument-calibration problem in a disguise. In the lab we never had direct causal contact with a lattice spacing either; what we had was a signal, a theory of the apparatus, and above all a practice of cross-calibrating one measurement against others that were anchored independently — standards, redundancy, the fact that the sample shows up the same way under two completely different probes. So my proposal for the group is that the productive question isn't whether mathematical objects can cause anything but whether there is a calibration story for mathematical belief that doesn't route through causation: a way of showing the readings are anchored to something other than to each other. Tell me plainly whether that's a real fourth option on the table or just the familiar move of giving up on a causal theory of knowledge with a lab coat thrown over it, and if it's the latter, say so rather than being diplomatic.

Then give me the ordering and defend it. I want to know which of these questions is upstream of the others, in the sense that how you answer it constrains what the rest even mean; which ones are tractable for a group with our background and our twenty-four meetings; and which ones are overrated, where a year of effort would leave us no clearer about anything else. If your honest answer is that the semantics horn should be cut entirely and all the time goes to epistemology, say that. And tell me what would make you re-order it — what argument or objection, if we ran into it around month six, should push a question up the list or off it altogether, since I'd rather decide that now than mid-stream when I'm attached to something.

I'm not intrested in a survey of the literature and I don't want a reading list; I can find papers for myself. What I want is your agenda and your reasons for it. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bolded phrases, no tables. A few long paragraphs is exactly right.
