I'm teaching a senior seminar in moral psychology and I have to write the in-class final: two hours, closed book, one essay question worth 25 marks, they'll write maybe 1,200 words. The term has been guilt vs. shame — the old shame-culture/guilt-culture split from mid-century Hellenist scholarship, Williams's line that shame reaches the whole agent while guilt is tethered to the act and to making it up to someone, Nietzsche's story about bad conscience as cruelty turned back on itself, and then the psychology, where the finding that shame-proneness tracks depression and externalised blame while guilt-proneness tracks reparative behaviour runs into the cross-cultural pushback that this is a WEIRD result and shame does pro-social work in honor societies.

I want a question they can't answer by reciting any of that, so I've made up a case and I want the question built around it. Two communities, described to the students in the exam paper itself:

**Ridge.** After a wrongdoing, people report everything the standard criteria put in guilt: the object is the act, there's a felt demand to make repair, they go and find the person they harmed, they talk in terms of what is owed. But the language has no word that translates as shame and they find the idea of being lowered in someone's estimation unintelligible. And when repair is ruled out — the person died, or left for good and can't be reached — the feeling doesn't deepen or turn inward. It just stops. They say the matter is finished because there's nothing to be done, and no trace of self-assessment survives it.

**Harbour.** People report everything the standard criteria put in shame except the social part: it's about what they are rather than what they did, they want to hide or disappear, they describe being exposed. But it never has an audience — not present, not imagined, not internalised. Asked whose eyes they are in they say nobody's, and insist that being seen would change nothing; they'd feel exactly the same under a guarantee of total secrecy, and the same if everyone found out and didn't mind. There's no pull to repair, only to conceal themselves, and they treat this as a standing fact about themselves rather than a response to any particular act.

The question has to ask them, in this order: whether either description is coherent and which community has which emotion, or whether the case shows the taxonomy picking up vocabularies rather than emotions; what would have to be true of an emotion for something like *requires an audience* or *takes the self as its object* to be constitutive of it instead of merely typical; and then, on the strength of that answer, whether a community could set out to become the Harbour kind, and what such a choice would actually consist in given that whoever is doing the choosing already has some repertoire. Don't let them get away with describing that last part — I want them committed.

Deliverables: (1) the question exactly as students will see it, case included; (2) a model answer of 700–800 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 25 organised around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth, plus a seperate paragraph saying what divides an A from a B — and I want that stated as specific moves present or absent, not as "the A is more nuanced"; (4) four or five concrete mistakes that should cost marks, each with a line on what it shows the student doesn't understand. Assume the two most likely bad answers are "Ridge has guilt, Harbour has shame, done" and "Harbour is impossible because shame is essentially social," and make sure the scheme actually sorts those out.

No citations, footnotes or reading list in the model answer — gesture at Williams and the psychology by idea if you need to, but the answer shouldn't depend on anyone remembering who said what.
