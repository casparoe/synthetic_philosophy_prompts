Hi — I'm setting the final for my upper-level philosophy of biology seminar (14 students, mix of grad and strong undergrads; they've read the Gould–Lewontin spandrels paper, the Mayr and Maynard Smith replies, and some stuff on exaptation) and I want one long essay question. 50 minutes of writing time, one question, 30 marks.

My problem is that every question I can think of just lets them recite the debate back at me — "is the adaptationist programme testable?", "distinguish a spandrel from an exaptation", that sort of thing. I want a question that hinges on a case where the labels genuinely run out, so knowing the controversy is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. So please build the question around this vignette. I made it up, but keep it biologically concrete and don't wink at the students about its being fictional:

A genus of rock-dwelling lizards on a small archipelago (call it the Tarnean rock lizards). Most species have a hollow bony tube opening just behind the jaw joint, which produces an audible tone when the animal exhales forcefully.

- 24 species in the clade; the tube is present in 19.
- Across all 19, tube length tracks skull length very tightly (r² ≈ 0.96) — including 12 species that never produce an audible tone at all (burrowing, nocturnal, or living in habitat where airborne sound carries badly).
- In embryos the tube forms where a cranial nerve branch and a blood vessel exit the skull; the outgrowth appears before any cartilage that could plausibly matter for resonance, and in the tubeless species the same exit point carries a shallow groove instead.
- A distant outgroup has the groove, no tube, and no sound production.
- Exactly one species (the "piper") uses the tube as a social signal. In the piper the tube runs about 40% longer than the clade-wide allometric line predicts.
- Field experiment on the piper: wax plugs in the tube cut territory-holding success and mating success by roughly 30% over a season. Plugging has no detectable effect on thermoregulation, bite force, or hearing.
- The tube is metabolically cheap, and no species that has it appears to have lost it.

Then I want a short second part that drops this on them, because I think it's the part that actually seperates the students who understand the paper from the ones who memorised the word "spandrel": there's a live dispute about the basilica itself. One commentator argues the surfaces in question are pendentives, not spandrels, and that the difference matters; a philosopher argues the mosaics were part of the design brief from the outset, so those surfaces are adaptations chosen for largely aesthetic reasons rather than leftover space that clever mosaicists exploited; and a structural engineer argues back that the geometry of setting a dome on four arches forces surfaces of that shape into being regardless of what anyone intended.

What I want from you:

1. The question exactly as students will see it — stem, vignette, parts, with marks allocated per part and a sense of how long each part should take. Don't ask them for citations, a bibliography, or page references; anything they need has to be in the stem or in what we covered.
2. A model answer, about 800 words, that actually reaches a view on the lizards rather than hovering.
3. A marking scheme worth 30 that names the moves an A answer has to make and a B one doesn't, plus a list of the specific wrong turns and what each costs.

Among the moves I want the scheme to police, in case it's useful: that the plugging experiment shows current utility and is simply silent on origin; that calling something a spandrel requires naming the developmental or architectural feature it's a byproduct of, not just failing to think of a good story; that a trait can be a byproduct across the clade and a shaped adaptation in one species without any contradiction, and what the piper's excess over the allometric line does and doesn't establish; and that a non-adaptive explanation needs positive evidence, not just the collapse of the adaptive one. Add whatever else you think belongs, and tell me if you reckon any of mine are misjudged for 50 minutes.

One thing I don't want: a question whose correct answer is shaped like "both sides have a point." If the honest verdict is that the trait is a co-opted byproduct, say so and defend it.
