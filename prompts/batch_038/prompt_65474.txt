I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine and just filed a feature on biological individuality — two weeks sitting in labs where people grow slime molds, biofilms and siphonophores, and the one thing every researcher did, without fail, was hedge about whether the thing in the dish is one organism or a crowd of them. My editor wants an interactive sidebar to run with it: a sorting quiz, the kind that tells readers what they actually believe instead of asking them to pick a label they've heard of. The catch is that the feature is sneakily about the special composition question, and I want the quiz to deposit readers onto real positions in that debate — but nothing on the reader-facing side is allowed to sound like metaphysics. I took exactly one metaphysics course in college and mostly used it to get out of a language requirement, so assume I need things spelled out.

Two deliverables. First, the space before the questions: lay out the axes that generate it — does composition stand in need of an explanation at all; can the right answer be trivially "always" or trivially "never"; if it's restricted, what sort of restriction (contact or cohesion, fastening, function or design, the activities of the parts constituting a life, an agent's or a community's interests); whether the quantifier itself is domain-relative; whether composition is identity. Then drop the actual positions into those cells — van Inwagen's life-only answer, unrestricted composition, the brute answer, the conservative/common-sense answer, quantifier variance or deflationism, composition as identity, and anything else that belongs on the map — and mark which cells are occupied. For every empty cell I want one line saying whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because no one has simply thought to stand in it.

Second, the instrument: nine items, three options max (that's all our interactive team can render), each a concrete case a reader can answer cold, with zero philosophy in the wording — if an option reads like a thesis statement, rewrite it as a claim about the world, e.g. "there's one thing here now" versus "there are still two things, touching" versus "it was always just particles, 'organism' is a way of talking." At least six items should come off my beat, and I'd like you to use these specifically: the slime mold that is a single cell with thousands of nuclei and gets cut in half with a scalpel, both halves regrowing; the Utah aspen clone where forty-odd thousand trunks share one root system; the Portuguese man o' war, whose zooids are so specialized that none of them can survive alone; a biofilm of unrelated bacterial species sharing nutrients and signals in a self-made slime matrix; a lichen whose fungal and algal partners can be separated in the lab and reassembled; and a bone-marrow transplant patient who now carries two distinct genomes in one body. Two or three items should be non-biological — my left shoe and a rock on Titan, considered as one object; two coins nudged a nanometer closer together at a time until they touch; the same grains of sand as a loose heap versus as a mortared wall — because otherwise every position gets to hide behind biology. Work in at least two consistency traps, where the point is that a reader who answers item A and item B differently is letting something non-obvious do the work: one I'd suggest is pairing the biofilm with a crowded subway car full of people exchanging heat, CO2 and moisture, which is qualitatively the same story with a different cast. Give me a private one-line note on each item saying which axis it's probing, since that won't be printed.

Then the scoring key: for each leaf, the technical name, a reader-facing nickname that isn't a technical name, the answer pattern that sorts into it, and one sentence on the most awkward thing that view has to swallow. Finish by telling me which pairs of leaves the quiz cannot actually separate — I'd bet nihilism and quantifier variance come out identical on every one of my cases, and I suspect universalism and composition as identity do too, but you may disagree with me — plus what you'd say about a reader whose answers land in a pattern the key doesn't cover. No reading list and no citations, I just need names where names exist. Whole thing should take a reader about four minutes on a Sunday, and I have to hand it to the desk Thursday so thier designer has time.
