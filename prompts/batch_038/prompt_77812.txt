Six weeks ago I got handed the scoring model for a mid-sized foundation's grants program. Mostly global health, but last cycle we opened it up to long-horizon work — climate adaptation, nuclear waste stewardship, stuff where the benefits land in 2300. Our spreadsheet basically does people affected × welfare gain × probability, which I now realize quietly commits us to the total view, and a board member who actually reads philosophy tore into it: she pointed out our own rubric would rank a future with zillions of people whose lives are barely worth living above a smaller but flourishing civilization. So we've commited to the repugnant conclusion without ever voting on it, and now fixing it before the next round is my problem.

Write a decision procedure our three-person panel (me, a nurse, a lawyer — nobody has any philosophy training) can run on a pair of competing proposals. About 40 applications a cycle, roughly two minutes of panel time per pair, ~$2M riding on each, and whatever we write has to survive the board. I want ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and clear exit points. Specifically it needs to tell me when the choice between total, average, critical-level, and person-affecting views actually flips the ranking versus when it's moot, what to do when it does flip, and how to handle proposals where the winner changes who exists — so the better-off people wouldn't otherwise have existed at all. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say plainly what the thing can't settle.

Then run it on two pairs: (a) cutting chronic pain substantially for 40,000 people alive now versus very slightly improving life for an enormous number of people over ten millennia, and (b) a modest wellbeing gain for 2 million existing households versus adding 5 million new people at a decent but lower welfare level with nobody else made worse off. Give me one answer I'm going to hate — probably it'll be about last cycle's highest-scoring long-horizon application.
