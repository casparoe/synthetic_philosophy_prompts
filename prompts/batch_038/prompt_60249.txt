I'm the volunteer coordinator for a river-watch group in my town (about 4,000 people, one main road that floods). Every autumn we have to decide whether to put the demountable barriers up on Mill Road ahead of a storm. Putting them up costs a whole weekend of volunteer labour plus roughly $9k in equipment rental and road-closure permits, and the town council is getting irritated about doing it for nothing — we've deployed four times in the last six years and only one of those actually flooded. Not deploying when it does flood is obviously much worse. We have three inputs: the national river model, which spits out a percentage chance of the water overtopping the road; an old guy named Dale who's fished this stretch for fifty years and gives a gut call; and a WhatsApp group of farmers upstream reporting creek levels and how fast they're rising. My current process is that I force all three into a percentage, average them, and if the average clears 30% we deploy. It's crude, but it stops us argueing until 2am.

Here's what bugs me though. Two situations can give me the exact same 30% and feel completely different. If the model, Dale and the farmers all point the same way, I sleep fine. If it's just one thin source — say the model alone, which nobody on the crew actually understands and which has been recalibrated twice in three years — I feel like I know basically nothing, even though the number on the sheet is identical. The averaging seems to throw that difference away. And I'm not even sure Dale's judgement and the model's output are the same *kind* of thing that can be averaged at all. Dale can't tell you 25% versus 30%; he can tell you "it'll go over" or "not this time." I've been squeezing that into a decimal so it fits my spreadsheet and I worry its a bit of a lie. Reading around this I keep landing on Keynes — not the famous economics, an earlier book he wrote about probability when he was young. Two things I took from it, tell me if I'm mangling them: first, that he thought there's a second dimension alongside the probability itself, something like how much relevant evidence is actually sitting behind a judgement, which can grow or shrink without the probability budging at all. Second, that he didn't assume every pair of uncertain claims can be ranked or laid out on one numerical scale — that some of them just aren't comparable. That second one sounds exactly like my Dale problem.

So what I want is: what do you think are the most important implications of those two ideas for how a group like mine should actually run this decision? Not a general talk about living with uncertainty — the concrete consequences. Does the averaging have to go, and if so what replaces it that we can genuinely execute at 9pm in a firehall with wet tired people shouting over each other? Can a low-number-but-lots-of-evidence situation ever outrank a high-number-but-thin-evidence one, or am I mixing up two different questions? What should we hand the council — they want a single figure and I'm tempted to give them two, but I'm worried two numbers just means they pick whichever one suits them that week. And is there a risk this whole way of thinking turns into a permanent excuse not to act, since you can always say the evidence isn't heavy enough yet? My sister thinks I'm overthinking it and that we should just deploy whenever the model says over 25% and stop pretending the rest of it is science. She might be right. But I'd like to understand what I'd be giving up if she is.
