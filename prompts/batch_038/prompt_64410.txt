Our trust's clinical ethics committee asked me for a short paper on donation after circulatory death, and in trying to defend the programme I've walked myself into an argument I can't get out of. A colleague and I built it together and every step looked unimpeachable at the time; the conclusion is that nobody in DCD is actually dead when we take the organs. Help.

(1) Whether a patient is dead is a fact about the patient's body, not a fact about our intentions or our protocols. Deciding something doesn't make it so.

(2) Death requires the loss of function to be irreversible, and irreversibility is always irreversibility *relative to some set of interventions*: to say a function is irreversibly lost is just to say that no intervention in that set would bring it back.

(3) In controlled DCD we withdraw life-sustaining treatment, wait for asystole, observe a stand-off period (ours is three minutes) to rule out auto-resuscitation, and then declare death on the ground that circulation and respiration have ceased irreversibly.

(4) At three minutes circulation could plainly be restarted — compressions, ECMO, the retreival team's own circuit. The only reason it won't be restarted is that everyone in the room has agreed not to try. So the set of interventions relative to which the arrest is irreversible is a set we narrowed by decision.

(5) By (2), then, the patient at three minutes isn't dead, only dead-relative-to-our-decision; and by (1) that is no death at all.

(6) The obvious repair is to switch to brain criteria and declare death on the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness rather than on circulation. But the version of retrieval doing most of the work now uses normothermic regional perfusion: after the stand-off the team clamps or ligates the arch vessels, runs an oxygenated circuit through the abdomen and thorax, assesses the organs, and only then retrieves.

(7) What makes the loss of brain function irreversible in that sequence is the clamping. Leave the arch vessels open and the same circuit that perfuses the kidneys perfuses the brainstem and cortex too.

(8) So on the brain criterion as well, the state we cite as constituting death is brought about by something the team does. An act of ours, not a fact about the patient, is doing the work.

(9) Therefore either DCD retrieval kills the donor, or the declaration of death is really a record of a decision we've taken — in which case (1) is false and "dead" doesn't mean what I thought it meant.

I'm most attached to (1) and (2), which seem like the only way to keep the word meaning anything, and to (4) and (7) as straight descriptions of what happens in theatres. But the committe will not accept (9), and I don't want to just say the argument is valid and shrug.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what do you pay for rejecting it? And does the same machinery bite elsewhere — a patient cooled to 18 degrees for aortic arch surgery spends forty minutes with no circulation and a flat EEG, and we cheerfully say they survived. If irreversibility is intervention-relative there too, either they died and came back or (4) is doing something I haven't spotted. Tell me which, and whether the difference between that case and the DCD one is principled or just a question of who's standing at the end of the bed.
