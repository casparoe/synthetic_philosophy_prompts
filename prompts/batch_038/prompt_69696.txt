I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; what follows comes out of an ethics reading group I've been attending for six months, mostly as a listener. I need a diagnosis of an argument I wrote, because a colleague has told me it proves too much and I cannot see where.

The case that generated it. My team builds the control software for a fleet of autonomous forklifts in a distribution warehouse: roughly forty vehicles, a central scheduler that assigns aisles and routes, and a maintenance subsystem that can place individual vehicles into reduced-capability modes. Last year a pedestrian was seriously injured. The reconstruction is unambiguous and, from an engineering standpoint, unremarkable: the scheduler routed two vehicles into the same aisle at overlapping times, which it is permitted to do when one of them is flagged as slow-moving; one of those vehicles was in a maintenance mode that disabled its speed governor; and the aisle's occupancy sensor had been degraded for nine days without raising an alert, because the alert threshold had been tuned for an earlier warehouse layout. Each of the three components was behaving within its own specification. There is no line of code anyone wrote that caused this. The incident review assigned the entire finding to the technician who had put the vehicle into maintenance mode and not taken it out, and that assignment is what pushed me to write the argument, because I believe she is the least responsible person in the story and I wanted to show why in a general way.

1. A person is morally responsible for a harm only if that person could have prevented it.
2. Preventing this harm required changing something about the system: the scheduler's routing rule, the maintenance-mode policy, the sensor threshold, or the monitoring process that let a degraded sensor run for nine days.
3. In a system built and maintained by hundreds of people over years, where the harmful behavior arises from the interaction of components that no single person fully understands, no individual could have made any of those changes on their own; each would have had to persuade or coordinate with others, which is not something any individual could have counted on.
4. Therefore no individual is morally responsible for the harm.
5. Responsibility for a harm does not simply vanish: if it is not borne by the individuals, it must be borne by the collective — the company, the team, or the owners of the system.
6. But a collective's responsibility, in the only sense capable of bearing moral weight, is the responsibility of the individuals composing it, aggregated; anything else is blaming a legal fiction.
7. Therefore no one — neither any individual nor any collective — is morally responsible for the harm.
8. Therefore, as systems grow larger, longer-lived, and more internally opaque, the moral responsibility attaching to their harms shrinks toward zero, whether or not those systems are autonomous.

The objection I cannot answer. My colleague says (8) is absurd, and that the argument is not really about autonomy at all: the same reasoning would show that no one was responsible for a fatal bug in a two-hundred-person codebase with no learning component anywhere in it, and it would imply that a team can always reduce its total responsibility by hiring more careful people, since each additional person dilutes every individual's capacity to prevent. She also points out that my argument leaves the technician's assignment looking merely unfortunate rather than wrong, since on (7) there is nothing to have been wrongly assigned. I have no reply to any of that, and I suspect she is right that something has gone wrong, but I do not know which premise to abandon.

What I am attached to is the case-level intuition that this technician is absorbing blame that belongs elsewhere, and step 3, which I think is an honest description of how large systems actually get built. I am least attached to step 5, which I included only to make the argument go through.

So: tell me which step fails. I want a committed answer rather than a survey. If you think more than one step is defective, tell me which one fails first and why the others only look defective once you see that one; if you think the argument is valid and the question is which plausible premise is cheapest to surrender, say so and name it. Then explain why the failing step looked unassailable to me, since it plainly did, and price your repair — I assume that giving up step 1 or step 3 has consequences I am not equipped to foresee, and I would rather hear them stated than discover them later. Finally, and this is the part I actually care about: once the argument is repaired, is anything left of the thought that autonomous systems create a special problem for responsibility, or does that worry dissolve entirely? Give me a verdict on that question with your reasons, not a list of the positions available.
