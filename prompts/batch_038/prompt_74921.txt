I cover science and ideas for a British broadsheet — climate research, research integrity, the occasional AI ethics row. I'm wrestling with a piece and I want help taking a proverb apart before it goes anywhere near print.

The situation, lightly fictionalised so nobody gets identified, but structurally exactly what I'm dealing with. Call him A: a climate scientist who has spent twenty years telling governments that cutting frequent flying is the single highest-impact change a professional can make. A leaked expense record turns up roughly 40 long-haul business class trips over three years. The head of his institute — call her B — signs a public statement saying his conduct was incompatible with the standards he had publicly advocated, and that the institute takes the criticism of him seriously. A's reply is that the facts are right but the criticism is not B's to make, because B flies about twice as much he does. Then there's C: a transport economist I know, completely upfront that he flies a lot and thinks it's fine for him, who has never claimed anything about his own conduct, and who says exactly the same critical thing about A on the record. And D: someone who did what A did, got caught four years ago, admitted it, hasn't flown since, and has now said the same thing about A.

The proverb being thrown around by literally every party to this — A's defenders in my inbox, B's comms office, and my own readers in the comments — is "people who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones". Occasionally with the bit about casting the first stone substituted in.

I want you to treat that one sentence as a bundle of candidate claims and tell me which of them are actually true. I'm reasonably well read but not a philosopher, and I suspect it's doing at least four different jobs in my story, some of them harmless and at least one of them flatly false.

Work as a moral philosopher who specialises in blame, standing and moral address, but who has done enough media work to know that a sub-editor will cut anything hedged. Do the thinking before the writing: rough out the candidate readings as notes first, try to break each one against A, B, C and D, merge any two that turn out to be the same claim wearing different clothes, and only then order them weakest to strongest and write it up. Where you think the proverb is being used dishonestly — to deflect from a criticism that's simply true — say so plainly rather than diplomatically.

Give me these sections, in this order:

**Bottom line** — four to six sentences. What the proverb can and cannot do here, and which of B, C and D, if any, loses their footing.

**The readings** — the meat, 900 to 1,200 words. Numbered, each with a short name, the claim stated precisely enough that I could tell you whether you'd got it wrong, a verdict tag (platitude / substantive and defensible / substantive and contested / false), and one line saying which of my four characters it bites on.

**The parameters** — a compact table. The proverb is vague about a lot of things: whose conduct counts, whether the wrong has to be identical or merely similar, whether the blamer's own record has to be worse or just as bad, whether it has to be current or can be in the past, whether it has to be concealed, and who's allowed to invoke it in the first place — the accused himself, a bystander, a journalist. One row per parameter, one column for whether varying it changes which readings survive.

**What it can never do** — about 200 words. The uses that fail on every reading, so I know what to strike when a source says this to me.

**The line I could actually print** — three candidate sentences precise enough to survive a legal read, plus one you'd tell me not to use and why.

He's openly a non-complier and never pretended otherwise, so if the proverb has no purchase on him, that tells me its not actually about consistency — which would reshape the whole piece. Second: does anything in the proverb bear on whether B's criticism is *true*, as opposed to whether she's the right person to make it?
