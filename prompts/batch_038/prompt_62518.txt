I convene a third-year undergraduate module, Well-Being, Measurement, and Public Policy, and I am serving as second marker on this year's two-hour closed-book examination. I should be grateful for an independent assessment of one script, reproduced below together with the question and the single page of materials candidates were permitted to have in front of them. I have formed no view of the script, and the first marker's grade has been withheld from me as well; please do not attempt to guess it, and give me your own mark and your own reasons.

**Examination question (40 marks; suggested 45 minutes; maximum 1,200 words)**

National league tables of subjective well-being — above all the annual ranking of some 140 countries by average Cantril ladder score — are cited both as measures of how happy countries are and as targets for governments that have adopted well-being policy.

(a) Explain what the ladder question requires a respondent to do, and identify and discuss at least two respects in which national averages of these responses may fail to measure what the ranking is said to measure.
(b) Evaluate the claim that a government which raises its country's mean ladder score has thereby made its citizens happier.
(c) A critic of such league tables argues that, because a ranking is an ordering, no country can rise in it unless another falls, and that a government's pursuit of a higher position is therefore purely positional. Assess this argument.

**Materials sheet issued to all candidates**

1. The ranking rests on one question, administered by Gallup in nationally representative samples since 2005, in which respondents are asked to picture a ladder whose top step, 10, stands for the best possible life for them and whose bottom step, 0, stands for the worst possible life for them, and to say which step they feel they personally stand on at the present time.
2. A country's score is the average of these responses, pooled over three consecutive survey years; approximately 1,000 respondents per country per year, some 140 countries.
3. Separately, the volume reports for each country six variables — income per head, social support (whether the respondent has someone to count on in trouble), healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption — together with a residual. These figure in a regression whose aim is to explain differences between countries' average ladder scores; the comparison benchmark is a hypothetical country made up of the lowest national values of each of the six. The published order of the ranking, however, follows the ladder averages themselves.
4. The same survey organisation also collects reports of the emotions experienced during the previous day. These are reported in the volume but are not what the headline ranking orders.
5. The report's authors argue that life evaluations vary more between countries than reports of daily emotion do, and are better explained by differences in people's life circumstances.

**The script** (roughly 650 words of the permitted 1,200; complete as submitted)

(a) The ladder question does not ask people how they feel. It asks them to place their life as a whole on a scale whose endpoints they supply themselves: the top step is not a fixed description of a good life but whatever the respondent takes the best possible life for them to be. The measure is therefore a life evaluation rather than a report of affect, and the materials sheet confirms that the previous-day emotion items are collected separately and are not what the ranking orders. This is at once the instrument's strength and its weakness. Its strength is that it imposes no account of what makes a life good; its weakness is that the same numeral can mean different things in different places, since a respondent in a poor country and one in a rich country are not holding the same ladder, and nothing fixes the endpoints by a common description. Translatability helps only partly, because what translates is the word, not the standard.

Two failures follow. First, the ranking reports a national mean, and a mean of evaluations is not the evaluation of a mean life: no respondent occupies it, and it can rise while the worst-placed tenth of the population falls. Second, and more seriously, the score published for each country is an aggregate of the six variables on the sheet — income, social support, life expectancy, freedom, generosity, corruption — with the ladder average retained as a check that the index behaves sensibly. If that is so, the ranking is not a subjective measure in any straightforward sense, and the much-noted strength of the correlation between it and national income ceases to be surprising: the ranking is partly made of income. That is also why the ranking and income per head tend to order countries in much the same way.

(b) A government that raises the mean has not thereby made its citizens happier, for at least three reasons. Averaging, as above, lets gains at the top mask losses at the bottom. Adaptation means that evaluations drift back towards a baseline after improvements in circumstance, so a policy that genuinely removes a burden — adequate pain relief, say, or a shorter wait for treatment — may leave the ladder score where it was; Sen's argument about adaptive preferences makes the stronger point that reports of satisfaction cannot enter into an assessment of justice at all, since the deprived adapt until their reports cease to be evidence of anything. Third, unlike reports of yesterday's emotions, a life evaluation is a considered judgement and is not moved by the respondent's mood on the day of the interview, by where in the questionnaire the item sits, or by the weather; it can, however, be moved by a change in the standard against which the respondent judges, so a rise in the mean may record not better lives but lowered expectations. In that case the government's achievement is real and the measurement is silent about it.

(c) The critic's premise is that a ranking is an ordering and an ordering is zero-sum: a country can rise only if another falls. That is true of positions, and it does not follow from it that the pursuit of a higher position is purely positional. But the ladder is self-anchoring: what sits at the top of it is set by what a comparison group takes to be achievable, so when one country's circumstances improve the standard rises for respondents elsewhere as well, and the scores themselves, not merely the ranks, are zero-sum. On that reading the critic's conclusion holds, though for a reason the critic does not offer. The weightier objection to targeting the ranking is another one: once a measure becomes a target it stops measuring, and a government attentive to the ladder will become attentive to whatever moves it.
