I am working through the Preface to Franz Boas's *Handbook of American Indian Languages*, Part 1 (Smithsonian Institution, Bureau of American Ethnology, Bulletin 40, 1911), as background for a graduate seminar on the history of the language-and-thought debate, and I have stalled on a cluster of sentences. My copy is an old scan with some damage, and the antecedent of "the latter" in the first line is illegible:

> "…knowledge of the latter has been obscured by the innumerable attempts to represent the grammars of Indian languages in a form analogous to that of the European grammars. It was originally intended to give a somewhat elaborate introduction, setting forth the essential psychological characteristics of American languages; but with the development of the plan of work it was found necessary to relegate this discussion to the end of the whole work, because without a somewhat detailed discussion of the various languages the essential points can not be substantiated by reliable evidence. I have not attempted to give either exhaustive grammars or exhaustive discussions of phonetics, because the object of the whole work has been to describe as clearly as possible those psychological principles of each language which may be isolated by an analysis of grammatical forms. […] I desire to express my sincere thanks to the collaborators who have contributed to the volume, and who have willingly adopted the general plan of presentation of grammar outlined by the editor."

Four things I cannot resolve.

1. What is a "psychological principle of a language," as distinct from a grammatical category or a rule of the grammar? Why would isolating such principles be the *object* of a descriptive grammar rather than a by-product of one?

2. When he condemns describing these languages "in a form analogous to that of the European grammars," is the fault in the borrowed descriptive vocabulary (case, tense, gender, part of speech), or in the prior assumption that the corresponding distinctions are there to be found? Are those two separable, and if so, is there a test for telling which one a given descriptive decision has gone wrong about?

3. He also says the general discussion of psychological characteristics had to be postponed until after the individual sketches, since otherwise the points "can not be substantiated by reliable evidence." What kind of epistemic constraint is that — a claim about comparative evidence in general, or about a specific error he thinks a priori comparison commits?

4. The final sentence says every contributor worked to one common plan of presentation outlined by the editor. Does a single template imposed on all the sketches not do precisely what the first quoted sentence condemns? State what distinction would have to hold for Boas to be consistent, and say whether you think it does hold.

Finally: everything in this passage appears to concern how the linguist should describe, not what speakers think. Is there any claim here at all about the minds of speakers, and what exactly would have to be added to these sentences before they amount to a thesis about language shaping habitual thought? Please work from the quoted phrases themselves, and wherever the answer turns on an ambiguity in Boas's wording, flag the ambiguity and give both readings rather than choosing silently.
