I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, and our formal epistemology seminar has just spent three sessions on how an agent should revise her credences when what she takes on board is a conditional rather than a plain proposition — van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin case and the industry of revision rules that grew up around it. A friend of mine is separately building a system that ingests conditional reports from human operators, so the question is not purely academic for either of us, and it is the reason I want a diagnosis of the discussion rather than another tour of the proposals. The standoff, as far as I can reconstruct it, is this. The toy case is meant to show that learning "if A, C" is not learning that some event in the algebra has occurred, so neither ordinary conditionalization nor Jeffrey conditionalization applies in any straightforward way: there is no partition whose new probabilities have been fixed. And yet the agent has plainly learned something — her conditional credence in C given A has gone up and her disposition to act has changed. Everyone in the seminar grants that much, and everyone grants the two desiderata the case is supposed to pin down, namely that the update should drive P(C|A) to 1 and should disturb as little else as possible. What nobody can agree on is what "as little else as possible" means, and the rules actually on offer — Lewis's proposal, Douven's evidential-support rule, imaging-style rules, and the conditional-event algebra approaches — return different numbers for the same input, with no shared argument for preferring one to another. Meanwhile the theorists who deny that indicative conditionals express propositions at all (the suppositional line descending from Ramsey and Adams, together with the triviality results that constrain any attempt to give conditionals truth conditions while preserving Adams's thesis) say the whole exercise is misconceived: nothing propositional was learned, so no revision rule is owed, and the numerical questions are artifacts of forcing a conditional into an event-shaped slot. Neither camp is embarrassed, nobody has moved, and my impression is that the literature has been in this condition for decades.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as something I want echoed back at me, is that the disagreement is not about which rule is correct but about what kind of state accepting a conditional is. One camp treats acceptance as a change in a probability function, a species of belief revision with a unique right answer; the other treats it as adopting a suppositional policy or a constraint on future reasoning, which on that picture has no unique numerical signature at all. If that is correct, the divergent posteriors are symptoms of two different objects of study masquerading as rival answers to one question. A second and related hunch is that the case only looks like a learning case because of how heavily it is idealized. Real headquarters messages arrive embedded in background information about the sender's evidence, aims, and reliability; once you model the sender as an agent who has learned something categorical and has chosen to transmit it in conditional form, the bare conditional seems to dissolve into an ordinary testimony problem, and the special-purpose rules lose their purchase. If that is right, the debate is partly about how much idealization is legitimate, and the substantive question becomes whether any of the rules survives de-idealization or whether they all collapse into arbitrariness once the sender is modeled.

So I would like to know what kind of disagreement this really is: substantive (a single well-posed question with rival answers), verbal (turning on what "learn" or "minimal change" is being made to carry), a disagreement about standards (about which intuitions count as data and how much idealization a toy case may absorb), two groups answering different questions under a shared label, or evidence that the question is ill-posed because no agent ever receives a bare conditional in the first place. Please test my two hunches rather than restate them, and say plainly where they fail. Ground the diagnosis in what each position actually commits to — what a revision rule owes, what a suppositional account denies, where exactly the triviality results bite — but I do not want a catalogue of proposals. I also do not want a bibliography or a reading list; name authors and positions where that is the clearest way to pick out a view, and do not supply dates, volume numbers, or page references, since I have no way to check them and would rather have none.
