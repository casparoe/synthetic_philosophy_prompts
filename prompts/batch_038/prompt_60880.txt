Two graduate students in my reading group — Dana and Emre — spent our last session arguing about what Aumann's 1976 result on agreeing to disagree actually establishes, and I was asked to referee and could not. Neither of them disputed the setup: a finite space of possible worlds carrying a single probability assignment; two agents, each of whose evidence picks out the cell of a fixed partition containing the actual world, and each of whom conditionalizes the assignment on that cell to obtain a posterior. The theorem says that if, for some event, the two agents' posterior probabilities together with the fact that each holds them are common knowledge in the fully iterated sense, then those two numbers must coincide. Both accept the mathematics. Their fight is entirely about what it amounts to. Here is the exchange, as close to the order it happened in as I can render it:

(1) Dana: the theorem shows that settled, informed disagreement is a symptom rather than a standing condition. Once two people who are both reasoning correctly genuinely know each other's conclusions — know them, not merely suspect them — they cannot differ. So when an argument like ours runs for an hour without movement, the correct conclusion is that at least one of us is failing to conditionalize properly or is not actually attending to what the other has said. The theorem converts a social stalemate into a diagnosis of faulty reasoning.

(2) Emre: it does nothing of the kind, because the knowledge hypothesis is never satisfied off a blackboard. I would have to know your posterior, know that you know mine, know that you know that I know, and so on without end. In practice I am rarely certain what you believe, and less certain that you are certain what I believe. A theorem whose antecedent fails in every real case is consistent with any behavior whatever, and therefore cannot be evidence about ours.

(3) Dana: that objection is available against essentially every idealized result in the discipline, and if it defeats this one it defeats all of them. Besides, the interesting hypothesis is not the knowledge condition but the shared probability assignment, and that one is meant as an idealization about how rational agents ought to be built, not as a report about how we happen to be built. Read that way the theorem is not empty: disagreement that survives full disclosure of each party's conclusions marks a failure of the idealization somewhere in one of us.

(4) Emre: watch what happens when you try to use it. Suppose we do achieve the disclosure you want and our numbers still differ. The theorem then tells us that the shared-prior hypothesis is false, and that is all it tells us. It does not say whose prior is defective, how either of us should revise, or even that one of us must revise rather than both. As an instrument for reaching agreement it fires only in the case where there is nothing left to do.

(5) Dana: not quite, and there are two replies. First, on the normative reading the prior is the credence function an agent with no evidence at all should hold, and there is a fact of the matter about what that is; two agents who learn that they differ in it have learned that at least one of them was mis-constructed, even if no repair procedure is supplied. Second, there are weakened versions of the result in which literal identity of priors is dropped — priors that are merely close, or common only approximately — and agreement is recovered approximately. So the theorem is not confined to the exact case.

(6) Emre: you cannot have both. If the shared prior is a requirement of rationality, the content of the theorem is that irrational agents can disagree, which nobody needed a partition argument to learn. If it is instead a description of us, it is false, and a theorem with a false hypothesis licenses no conclusion about us. Pick one; either way the interesting claim evaporates.

(7) Dana: by that standard no idealization in the field says anything, since each is either normative and hence about a fiction or descriptive and hence false. I would like to know what would count, for you, as a theorem being applicable at all.

(8) Emre: here is what would. I think the result does real work, but in the direction opposite to yours. It is an impossibility theorem functioning as a map of where to look for trouble: any disagreement that survives mutual disclosure must be explained by one of a short list of suspects — priors that are not shared, knowledge that is not common, or conditionalization that has broken down — and its value is that it exhausts the list. Add to that the fact that in markets and auctions prices converge and do something like the theorem's work without anyone needing common knowledge of anyone's beliefs. That is the interesting application, not us.

(9) Dana: then we agree on the mathematics and you are making a claim about usefulness rather than content. And I still maintain that the first thing a rational agent should conclude from a disagreement that survives full disclosure is that one party is reasoning incorrectly. That is the plain moral of the theorem, and it is not a trivial one.

Neither budged. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the nine numbered claims, say whether it is clearly wrong, partly right, or right, and justify each rating by reference to what the theorem actually says rather than to which speaker you find more sympathetic. Then answer three further questions. (a) Is Emre's dilemma at (6) genuinely forcing, or does Dana have a coherent third option — and if so, state it precisely enough that Emre could not simply re-run the dilemma against it? (b) When Dana asserts at (5) that approximate common priors yield approximate agreement, is that a real feature of this body of work or an overreach, and how much weight can Dana's argument bear if the assertion fails? (c) Is there any reading on which Dana's conclusion at (9) survives — that is, on which the theorem does license an agent to infer faulty reasoning in a persistent dispute — and if so, what extra premises does that inference require? Close by saying what both of them missed.
