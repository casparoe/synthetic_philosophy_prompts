The assignment was a take-home short-answer question worth 20 marks, with a 400-word limit. Roughly forty scripts were submitted; I am marking them blind over the weekend and want a second reader's judgment on this one.

The question was:

"State the Repugnant Conclusion. Then explain Parfit's mere addition paradox, identifying which premises do the work in carrying us from the first outcome to the last. Finally, say whether average utilitarianism avoids the Repugnant Conclusion, and what it costs."

The student's answer:

"The Repugnant Conclusion is the claim that, for any large population whose members all enjoy a very high quality of life, there is a much larger imaginable population whose lives are worth living but of far lower quality, and which would be better. It is repugnant because it implies that a world of ten billion people with wonderful lives is worse than a world of hundreds of billions scraping by. On the total view it follows immediately: what matters is the sum of welfare, so enough lives of low positive quality must eventually outweigh a smaller number of excellent ones.

Parfit's mere addition paradox is meant to show that the conclusion is hard to avoid even for those who reject the total view. Begin with A: ten billion very well-off people. Now add ten billion more whose lives are worth living though less well, and who are divided from the first group so that no one is affected; call this A+. Since this is a mere addition, A+ is not worse than A. Next, holding the population fixed, redistribute, so that everyone enjoys a high quality of life, slightly below that of the best-off group in A+ but far above that of the added group; call this B. B is not worse than A+, since it is more equal and the worst off are much better off. Repeating these two steps as often as we like, every step is either a mere addition or a change that makes things no worse, so by the transitivity of the not-worse-than relation we reach Z, a vast population whose lives are worth living but very poor, and Z is better than A.

Average utilitarianism avoids this, since it tells us to maximize welfare per person rather than in total, and Z's average is far below A's. Its cost is that it can never recommend creating anyone: an extra person almost always drags the average down, so the view tells us to stop having children even when their lives would be splendid. This is why Parfit thinks the non-identity problem is the same difficulty under another name. Nobody in Z is harmed by being created, since otherwise they would not have existed, so the objection to Z cannot be that its inhabitants have been wronged; and any principle that refuses to count impersonal losses will end up at Z anyway."
