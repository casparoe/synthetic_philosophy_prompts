You are a professional philosopher of science with working competence in both economic methodology and the philosophy of biology and mind. Please think this through slowly rather than producing a summary. Before drafting your answer, spend one short paragraph brainstorming the space of relations the two positions could stand in — identical, one entails the other, they are contraries about the same object, they are about different kinds of objects, one is a special case of the other, and so on — and then commit to the relation you find most defensible and argue for it.

I have read a version of the analogy I am about to describe made glibly on forums for years, always at the level of slogan and never adjudicated. I want it adjudicated.

First position. Milton Friedman's 1953 methodological essay holds that the significance of a positive theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions for the class of phenomena it is intended to cover, and that a theory should be assessed as a body of propositions whose assumptions need not be descriptively true of the world. The provocative corollary is his claim that the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions, on the grounds that significance goes together with a high degree of abstraction and simplification. The stock illustration is the expert billiard player, who behaves as though he could solve the differential equations that yield optimal shots. Paul Samuelson later dubbed the move the F-twist and charged it with affirming the consequent: the empirical adequacy of a fragment of a theory's consequences licenses no conclusion about the adequacy of the fragment of the assumptions that generated them. Dan Hausman pressed a related point as an invalid inference, from "a good hypothesis predicts well" to "predicting well is the only test of a good hypothesis," and illustrated it with a used-car analogy: a good used car drives safely and economically, but nobody concludes that the road test is the only relevant check, because looking under the hood tells you about reliability in conditions you have not yet driven in. Stanley Wong objected differently again, that both Friedman and Samuelson leave explanatory content out of the picture entirely.

Second position. Daniel Dennett's 1995 book distinguishes a skyhook, an alleged source of lift in the design process that is not itself grounded in the base-level processes it is supposed to explain, from a crane, a subprocess that genuinely accelerates design work but is itself built out of and explicable by the slower base level. Greedy reductionism, on his account, is the error of trying to skip levels, of claiming more lift than the base can supply; good reductionism insists that every crane be cashed out in terms of mechanisms that are in principle specifiable. Two further Dennett commitments matter here: substrate neutrality, the idea that an algorithm's logic is independent of its physical realization, and the intentional stance, on which we attribute beliefs and desires to a system insofar as doing so buys predictive success, even when nothing in the system corresponds literally to the attributed states.

The puzzle I want you to work on. The two positions look like mirror images and, I suspect, are not. Friedman says predictive success licenses assumptions we know to be false, indeed that false assumptions are a mark of strength. Dennett says success does not license a missing mechanism, that getting the right output without an earnable process underneath is precisely the defect, not the virtue. But Dennett's own intentional stance attributes states as if the system were rational, and cashes the attribution in predictive success rather than in any inner correspondence — which reads like an F-twist applied to minds. And Dennett's critics have said exactly that: on their reading the intentional stance is itself a skyhook, because it borrows the rationality of the attributer and smuggles it into the system attributed to. Meanwhile substrate neutrality looks like a further Friedmanite move, since it says the algorithmic level is what carries the explanatory weight regardless of how unrealistic the hardware description is.

So: (a) State what the two positions are actually claims about, and identify the crux on which their apparent symmetry turns. (b) Answer directly whether Dennett can consistently endorse the intentional stance while condemning skyhooks, or whether one of the two has to give. (c) Propose a diagnostic — a set of conditions under which an unrealistic assumption or an as-if attribution earns its keep, and conditions under which it is a skyhook — and then test it on three cases: Friedman's billiard player, the attribution of beliefs to a thermostat, and profit maximization as an assumption about firms. Say for each whether the diagnostic licenses it, and whether your diagnostic is doing real work or merely relabeling your prior verdicts. (d) End with a single sentence stating which of the two positions, if either, has the better of the exchange, and name the one consideration that decided it.

Throughout, keep a sharp line between what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material. The realist-versus-instrumentalist reading of Dennett's own position is itself disputed; if you adopt the instrumentalist reading to make my puzzle bite, say that you are doing so and that a realist reading of him would change the shape of the answer. Do not present your preferred resolution as the consensus view in either literature, and flag any place where you are advancing an interpretation that would strike specialists as a minority one.
