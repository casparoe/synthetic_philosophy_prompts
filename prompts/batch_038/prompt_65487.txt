I'm TA-ing a political philosophy seminar next term and I want to run one session on where the "democracies are more peaceful" intuition actually comes from in Kant — specifically the first definitive article of Perpetual Peace and his reasoning that a republican constitution makes war less likely because the people whose consent is needed are the same people who'll have to fight it, pay for it out of their own pockets, rebuild afterwards, and carry the debt. Treat yourself as a historian of political thought who also does analytic political philosophy, and please think carefully before writing anything. I've been calling the failure mode I want to dramatise the liability franchise: the gap between who gets a vote and who actually bleeds and pays. So: build me a thought experiment in which a broad-suffrage commercial republic comes out clearly more eager for aggressive war than the enlightened, decidedly non-republican monarchy next door, such that Kant's cost-incidence mechanism is inverted rather than merely absent. Constraints, since the constraints are the whole exercise — start with a short brainstorm: sketch three or four candidate institutional mechanisms in a line or two each, say which you're going with and why, and only then write the scenario. The scenario itself should be one tight paragraph, call it 150 words max, with two fictional but historically plausible states and invented names. Don't set it in a real country; I want my students arguing about the structure, not about whether Prussia really did X. No sci-fi, no exotic technology. And crucially the republic's beligerence must not come from the voters being bloodthirsty, ignorant, or worked up by demagogues, and it must not come from the republic keeping a standing army or hiring mercenaries — Kant already rules those out in the preliminary articles, so if that's your engine you've just rediscovered a preliminary-article violation instead of designing a case. Same for war debt as such. The engine should sit inside the republic's own franchise and property rules: who counts as a citizen with a vote, who doesn't, and how that lines up with who fights and who holds the paper. It also shouldn't collapse into "greedy merchants want war," because Kant's claim about the commercial spirit points the other way, so a pure commercial-greed story just contradicts a different part of the essay rather than generating anything new. For what it's worth I tried a version of this myself where the electorate is all shipowners who profit from privateering, and my supervisor said it was just special-interest capture dressed up in a flag, which is fair, so please don't hand me that back.

What I want after the scenario, in this order: (1) a compliance check, one line per constraint, saying explicitly how the case satisfies it — if one of them isn't really satisfied, say so instead of papering over it; (2) the Kant-side walkthrough: what the first definitive article predicts about these two states, what actually happens in your case, and exactly where the cost-incidence reasoning breaks down; (3) name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, then run the removal test — extend the franchise to the people who actually fight, or whatever the load-bearing feature turns out to be — and tell me whether the case collapses back into Kant's prediction or whether something else keeps it inverted; (4) the strongest reply a Kantian has using only resources already in the essay, which I'd guess involves the preliminary articles, the cosmopolitan-right article about hospitality, or the federation of free states, plus your verdict on whether that reply rescues the thesis, relocates it somewhere weaker, or concedes the mechanism entirely; (5) a short honest note on how a contemporary democratic-peace defender would attack the case, e.g. by arguing your republic isn't really a republic on Kant's own criteria, or that the outcome is a selection effect rather than a causal one.

No bibliography, no footnotes, and no page numbers please — nobody in the room can check them and I don't want invented ones. Assume I know the essay reasonably well, so don't burn words summarising it; I care about whether the case is engineered, not about whether you've read it.
