I'm a staff engineer on the data platform team at a mid-sized private foundation (roughly $900M in endowment, making governance, anti-corruption, and basic-services grants in about thirty countries), and last spring I was seconded onto our Grants Policy Panel — seven members, mostly program officers plus two trustees, with me on it because I built and maintain the internal allocation model that the panel votes on. I have no formal training in any of the social science involved; what I know I picked up from a methodology appendix I read on a flight, and my partner, who teaches sociology, told me afterwards that I was "asking a measurement question and calling it a policy question," which I'm still turning over. The panel meets on the fourteenth, and the item I need help with is a proposal from a senior program officer to fold a commercial "state fragility score" into our annual country-allocation formula as a negative weight: a higher forecast instability for a country would lower its tractability multiplier and therefore shrink its envelope. The score comes out of the cliodynamics tradition — a structural-demographic model whose driver variables are, roughly, stagnating real wages for ordinary workers; an oversupply of credentialed graduates relative to the number of positions of status and power available to absorb them, which that literature calls elite overproduction; visible conflict among established elites; and rising public debt. Its target is a forecast probability of political instability in a given country over the following decade.

The panel is split in a way I can't resolve on my own. Two members favor adoption, and their reasons are not frivolous: the research lineage behind the product includes a forecast published in a major journal in 2010, in advance, that instability in the United States and western Europe would climb steeply around 2020, and the 2020s have broadly borne that out; and our current allocation process is staff judgment plus wherever our program officers happen to have worked previously, which they argue is worse than a crude explicit model. On the other side, when I tried to reproduce the score internally I found several things that I don't know how to characterize. The vendor's current release is refit on data through 2023, so the celebrated 2010 forecast now sits inside the fitting window rather than outside it. The target index is assembled by counting reported events — riots and demonstrations, strikes, terrorist attacks, political assassinations, attempted overthrows of government — which means a country with a free press and a digitized newspaper archive registers as more unstable than a closed country with identical underlying conditions, and the news corpus the vendor draws on expands sharply around 1995, putting a structural break through the middle of the training series. The degree-count proxy for elite overproduction was reclassified twice over the period. And when I reran the published specification on country subsets, two of the four driver coefficients changed sign, while the drivers themselves correlate with each other at roughly 0.8. Separately, one of the trustees objects on grounds I can't quite formalize: the score would direct money away from precisely the places our mission statement says we exist to work in, and the fund would be publishing a forecast about countries where we then act on it, which she suspects changes what the forecast can even be checked against.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate to the panel before the fourteenth: adopt, adopt with conditions, or reject, with a clear bottom line and reasoning a non-technical trustee can follow without being condescended to. If the recommendation is conditional, the conditions need to be specific enough that I could actually implement them — what we would have to establish about the instability index before it is permitted to enter a formula at all; whether a probability is the right output shape for a decision about how much to spend in a country, or whether the score should enter as a ranking, a flag, or a trigger for human review; how to handle the reporting-capacity confound and the 1995 break if we keep the index; and whether we should require the vendor to hold out the post-2010 period and show us the 2010 forecast scored against it. Treat the case for adoption at full strength rather than as a straw man, since I suspect it is the better argument in some respects, and say plainly what the panel gives up if it rejects: what we would be relying on instead, and why that is or isn't defensible. I'd also like the memo to name, in ordinary language, the two or three questions here that are not really about the vendor's code quality — the ones about what the index is supposed to be measuring, and about whether a published and acted-upon social forecast can still be validated the way an unpublished one can — because I expect those are where the panel will talk past each other.
