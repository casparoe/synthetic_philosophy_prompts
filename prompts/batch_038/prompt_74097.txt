So I'm in a small Stoicism reading group, six of us, and this month we did The Prince because someone insisted Machiavelli is the great anti-Stoic and I wanted to check that for myself. Honestly? Reading it with the dichotomy of control in hand, I think it's the opposite — it maps almost too cleanly. Fortuna is just the not-up-to-us column: other people's armies, the pope's mood, the weather, whether the times happen to suit your temperament. Virtù is the up-to-us column: your own deliberation, your resolve, your willingness to act. And everything he actually wants that isn't literally in your control — glory, a durable state, the good opinion of the crowd — is basically preferred indifferents, which would explain why chapter 18 tells the prince to work on seeming merciful and faithful rather than on being so. Appearances are indifferent; character is what's up to you.

That leaves me with two questions I can't settle.

First: on that reading, does the flood-and-dykes business in the fortune chapter still have any content, or have I explained it away? My gut is that its just a picturesque way of saying prepare your judgments during the calm, which any Stoic says anyway — but two people in the group insist that if my reading makes a famous passage redundant, that's evidence the reading is wrong, and neither of them can say what's left over. Is something left over? I keep coming back to the fact that the dykes have to be built before you know whether the river will rise, which doesn't feel like advice about judgments at all, but I can't make that into an argument.

Second, and this is where I actually got stuck: if virtù is the up-to-us column, it ought to be sufficient for doing well no matter what fortuna throws at you — that's the whole Stoic deal. But Machiavelli seems perfectly happy saying a man can be impetuous and successful for years and then be destroyed when the times turn cautious, precisely because he can't change his nature. A Stoic isn't allowed to say that; the sage adapts. Am I missing a way to reconcile those two, or is that the crack in the mapping? And if it is the crack, how deep does it go — does the rest of the book survive it, or does the whole virtù/fortuna pairing stop being about inner states once you pull that thread?
