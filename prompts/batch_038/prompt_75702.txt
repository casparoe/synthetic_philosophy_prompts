In a seminar on Hegel's philosophy of right, a classmate answered the charge that Hegel glorifies war by leaning on an image Hegel himself offers in the lecture additions to his discussion of war: as the blowing of the winds keeps the sea from the foulness that a prolonged calm would produce, so war preserves a people from the stagnation that permanent peace would bring. From this the classmate argued that Hegel praises war no more than a sailor praises storms — he is registering that their absence has a cost, and registering a cost is not endorsing the thing. I doubt the analogy can bear that much weight. Please set out the mapping explicitly: what answers to the sea, to the calm, to the foulness, to the wind, and to the sailors exposed to it. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on this deflationary use from those that are beside the point. In particular I want pressure on three questions. Is the wind external to the sea in a way that war is not external to the states that wage it? Does the analogy's harmlessness — wind destroys no sailors, and the sea is the same sea afterwards — survive contact with a remedy consisting in the deaths of the very individuals whose indifference to their own finitude it is meant to dispel? And does the image settle whether the benefit accrues to the whole or to its parts, and whether what has value for Hegel is actual fighting or the standing readiness for it? Finally, tell me what is left of the classmate's conclusion once these are on the table, and whether the analogy was quietly supplying the premise that the calm is already a corruption — the premise the charge against Hegel most needs to deny.
