Some background, because I haven't got a philosophy degree — I do backend engineering, and anything I know about this stuff I've picked up from blog posts and one or two extremely long podcasts.

About eighteen months ago I started keeping a slightly odd spreadsheet. For claims about my own code that I can't cheaply settle — does this function halt on every input, can these two services deadlock under load, does this regex ever match a string we'd realistically see, is this refactor behaviour-preserving — I write down a confidence number, and then score myself when the answer finally comes out. Once a month I play my own bookie: if I can spot a cheap mechanical rule someone could have used to reliably take money off me (always buy "it deadlocks" from me on a Friday, or fade me whenever I say something is definately fine), I reprice the whole book to shut that hole. Doing this for a while has pushed me into a position I now hold fairly strongly, and I want to know where it's exposed.

The position: uncertainty about logical and computational facts isn't some extra special flavour of uncertainty sitting alongside empirical uncertainty. It's just ordinary probabilistic reasoning that happens to be pointed at stuff I haven't finished computing, and the only non-arbitrary standard for it is the no-exploitation one. Your beliefs about unrun programs should be readable as prices in a market that no cheap, mechanically describable trader can milk for unbounded profit; if they aren't, they're simply wrong. I gather this is close to the logical induction picture, but please treat it as my claim, not as a book report — I want it attacked on its merits.

So: what would count against it? Give me the defeaters ranked from genuinely fatal to merely embarrassing. Things I'd like you to take a proper swing at:

- I suspect the limit-based criterion says nothing about how I should price anything today — that I could prepend eighteen months of garbage beliefs and still technically qualify. If that's right, does the whole view just evaporate, or is there a fix?
- The guarantee is relative to a complexity class, so it seems to deliver "not obviously exploitable by a cheap script" rather than "correct". How much of a problem is that, and is it a problem a defender would concede?
- Does this collapse into plain Bayesianism about physical facts? If my uncertainty about whether some loop halts is really just uncertainty about what a long computation will output, is the market framing doing any work at all, or is it decoration?
- What does my view predict that its rivals don't, and where could I go and look for it? I'm thinking about actual bounded reasoners — trained models, humans doing mental arithmetic — that get systematically fleeced by trivially cheap traders and yet still make excellent decisions. If that's the normal case, does it undercut the claim that non-exploitability is the standard?
- Self-reference: my book contains claims about my own future beliefs and about my own reasoning. Does that break the setup in a way it doesn't break the empirical analogue?
- What about sentences that are true but never settleable in practice, or cases where I'm unsure which formal system I'm even reasoning in?
- And an honest verdict on the big one: have I insulated the position by leaning on "cheap" and "in the limit" until it can't be contradicted by anything? If so, is that actually a vice for a view of this kind, or is that just what asymptotic standards look like?

What would move me most is a concrete little trading strategy that beats a book like mine, or an argument that any such book has to be exploitable. What moves me least is being told ideal rationality is unattainable — I know, that's rather the point of the exercise.
