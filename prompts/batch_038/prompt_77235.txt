I sit on the trustees' sub-committee of a volunteer mountain rescue team in the UK — twelve of us, all volunteers, meeting once a month — and at our last two meetings the same phrase, "above and beyond the call of duty", did decisive work in two arguments that struck me as quite unlike each other. Our next meeting is in a fortnight, and I want to arrive able to say what the phrase actually commits a person to.

First use. A proposal was tabled to add a line to the team's code of conduct requiring members who are signed on to the callout rota to make themselves available for at least one midweek callout a month where their employment permits. A long-serving member objected that this would convert what has always been above and beyond the call of duty into duty proper, and — the part that carried the room — that once the behaviour is written into the code, nobody can any longer be praised for it, so the team would be destroying the very thing it most values in its members in order to guarantee it. The proposal was withdrawn.

Second use. A member who took three weeks of unpaid leave from his job to remain on standby through a severe weather period asked the team for an ex gratia contribution towards his lost earnings. The treasurer refused on the grounds that the member had acted above and beyond the call of duty, and therefore that there was nothing the team owed him.

My suspicion is that these are not the same claim, and that at least one of them is being asked to bear more weight than the phrase can carry, but I cannot articulate where the difference lies. Could you lay out, as a numbered menu I can circulate to the committee beforehand, the distinct precise theses that this one phrase might be expressing? I would expect the menu to include at least candidates along these lines: that the act was not obligatory, so that its omission was permissible; that the act was better than whatever was obligatory; that the act lies outside the obligations of the member's role rather than outside the obligations of morality; that no one has standing to demand it, or to complain of its omission, and if so, who would; that the beneficiary acquires no claim, debt, or entitlement from having received it; that requiring it would diminish or destroy its praiseworthiness; and that duty has a boundary determinate enough for an act to sit intelligibly on the far side of it. For each reading, please state what exactly a speaker is committed to, whether it is a platitude on which nothing could turn, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false or incoherent; and identify the points at which two readings that are usually run together come apart in practice.

Then please turn the menu onto my two cases: which reading or readings would actually support the long-serving member's objection to codification, which would support the treasurer's refusal, and whether any single reading supports both moves simultaneously. I would particularly like to know whether the objection to codification depends on the thought that praiseworthiness requires non-obligation — and, if it does, what explains why an act that is the best thing available to an agent should escape being required at all, rather than the phrase merely papering over an asymmetry between praise and blame that nobody has argued for. If it turns out that the objection only succeeds on a reading that almost nobody in the room would knowingly accept, please say so plainly and say what the committee ought to have replied instead.

Please write in British English. I am not after references, a reading list, or a survey of who has held which position — argue the analysis from the case itself, and assume your reader is intelligent but has no philosophy beyond A level.
