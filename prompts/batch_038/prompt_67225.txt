I am at work on my fourth novel, which follows the delegates to a constitutional convention in a small country emerging from two decades of civil conflict; the country is invented, and I would rather you did not go hunting for its real-world model. Two of the delegates have been antagonists since they were graduate students together, and their dispute is the spine of the middle section of the book. One — I think of her, only half in jest, as belonging to the Mast Party — holds that a founding generation can and should substantively bind its successors: an entrenched bill of rights, amendment rules so demanding that they function as side constraints, an independent central bank whose mandate is placed beyond ordinary politics. She defends these on the ground that a polity, like a sailor lashed to the mast, can know in a lucid hour what it will be unable to resist at a worse one. The other holds that any such device is legitimate at most procedurally — it may make change slow, costly, and deliberate — but never substantively, because once a competent and informed present majority repudiates the bond, the earlier decision has no claim on it; on his view the Odysseus analogy fails at the first step, since there were sailors to enforce the order and a polity has no equivalent, and no institution speaks for the bound self except the very majority now chafing against the ropes. My editor has suggested that the book carry, as an appendix, the document these two should have written instead of arguing for eleven years.

What I want is that document — not a dialogue, not a mediation, and not a ruling on which of them is right. Please draft the joint statement that a committed defender of substantive entrenchment and a committed opponent of it could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin with a numbered section of claims both can accept, and push this well past the trivial: I want to see what they agree on about the intrapersonal cases — saving for retirement, addiction, psychiatric advance directives that override a present refusal of treatment — about the difference between a device that removes an option and one that merely changes its price, and about how far the intrapersonal analogy can be stretched before the differences in identity, and in who pays the cost of the binding, begin to do real work. Follow this with a disagreements table in which each row states the surviving disagreement in wording that each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view. Then set out the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of the two. For each crux, give me a concrete case, historical comparison, or piece of evidence that both would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance — for instance, whether rigidly entrenched rights provisions do in fact survive the emergencies they were written to weather or merely relocate the fight into the courts, and whether sunset clauses and supermajority requirements change what legislatures decide or only when they decide it. If part of the quarrel turns out to be verbal — I suspect this is true of the question whether the people is a single trans-temporal agent capable of promising itself something, or a succession of agents none of whom is bound by the last one's word — say which part and why, rather than dressing it up as substantive. Close by telling me where the statement papers something over: which residual disagreement resisted neutral wording, and what each side had to swallow in order to sign.

Three constraints, and then a request about how you handle uncertainty. No bibliography, no reading list, no quotation — my characters are not scholars on the page and I would rather have the ideas than the apparatus, so paraphrase any classic scene or familiar argument you invoke. Keep the institutions ordinary and recognizable rather than science-fictional. And do not smuggle in a verdict by rigging the table: if one camp is right that some consideration defeats the other, that belongs among the cruxes, not among the agreed claims. As to the request: throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this material from what is contested among them, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. Within the agreed-claims section in particular, distinguish claims that represent real points of agreement in the field from concessions extracted for the sake of the exercise — I need to know which of my two delegates is being generous and which is merely being polite.
