A reading group I belong to spent two sessions on Thucydides' account of the revolt of Mytilene, and two of its members — Tobias and Elena — got into an argument that outlasted both. I was present but not a participant, and I could not tell by the end who was ahead, so I would like a referee's judgment. I report their exchange below as neutrally as I can; nothing in my summary is meant to favor either of them.

The background the group was working from. In 427 BCE, after Mytilene on Lesbos surrendered following its revolt, the Athenian assembly, in a fury at the news, decreed that every adult male Mytilenean be put to death and the women and children enslaved, and sent a ship to Lesbos with the order. The next day a repentance set in, together with reflection on the cruelty of condemning a whole city rather than only those responsible. The Mytilenean envoys then in Athens, along with their Athenian supporters, asked the presiding officers to put the question again; the officers agreed readily, because they could see that most citizens wanted the chance to reconsider. In the second debate Cleon, who had carried the original motion, argued for it once more and Diodotus argued for repeal. Repeal carried, by a narrow margin. A second ship was dispatched with the counter-order and reached Lesbos before the executions took place. Two further details the group kept returning to: Mytilene, unlike most of the empire, had been an autonomous ally contributing its own ships rather than a tributary subject; and a few years later, in the case of Scione, Athens decreed the same treatment and this time carried it out.

The exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Tobias said the reversal is routinely cited as Athenian democracy correcting itself, but that it fails what he called the re-run test: a decision procedure self-corrects only if re-running it on the same evidence makes a better outcome more likely rather than merely a different one. Athens re-ran the same question on materially the same evidence within a day and produced the opposite answer, which is a demonstration of variance, not of reliability. He added that the difficulty is symmetrical and therefore fatal to the standard reading: had the second vote come out the other way, the identical sequence of events would have been cited as proof of the assembly's volatility, so the reversal cannot be entered on the credit side of the ledger without special pleading.

(2) Elena said the test treats the two votes as interchangeable samples drawn from a single distribution, which they were not. The first decree was carried in fury, on the day the prisoners arrived, against a city whose status as an autonomous ally nobody paused to consider, and with no one prepared to argue the other side. The second followed a night of reflection and a full debate in which the mover of the first decree had to defend it in the open against a prepared opponent, and lost narrowly. A calmer and better-argued body deciding differently is not the same procedure drawing twice; it is the procedure operating under better conditions, and a criterion blind to that distinction measures the wrong thing.

(3) Tobias replied that Elena had thereby located the correction outside the procedure. Nothing in Athenian practice entitled anyone to a second vote: the question was re-put only because the presiding officers chose to allow it, and they chose to allow it because they could already see that the citizens' mood had turned. So the mood corrected the assembly and the officers ratified the correction. A mechanism that fires only when officials happen to read public sentiment accurately is neither predictable nor dependable, and the record bears this out: the same assembly later decreed the identical treatment for Scione, nothing intervened, and the decree was executed.

(4) Elena said the demand for a rule was a demand for a different constitution. A sovereign assembly cannot grant an automatic right of re-vote on every decree without making all its decisions provisional and so cheapening the ones it does make; a discretion to reopen is what correction looks like in a body that recognizes no authority above itself. She also conceded part of Tobias's point: Scione shows the mechanism is weak, since it depends on a shift in sentiment that may never come — but weak is not absent, and a single failure to fire does not establish that there was nothing there to fire.

(5) Tobias then turned to the content of the winning speech, which he said Elena was treating too generously. Diodotus urged the assembly to decide on grounds of future interest rather than justice: the question, on his telling, was not whether the Mytileneans deserved death but whether killing them served Athens. If a body can be moved to a humane conclusion only by being told to set aside the question it convened to answer, the reversal reflects no improvement in the quality of its deliberation. The assembly got the right answer for the wrong kind of reason, and a procedure that depends on that cannot be vindicated by its output.

(6) Elena said this misdescribed the speech. The claim that the Mytilenean commons were not the guilty party — that the oligarchs had armed them, that they had then refused to obey their officers and handed the city over to the Athenian general — is a claim about who is liable to punishment, which is a question of justice rather than a substitute for one; and weighing the future effects of a standing policy of extermination is the proper business of the only body capable of changing such a policy. If Diodotus spoke the language of interest, it may have been because interest was the only register in which an assembly in that temper could be made to attend to facts about guilt it had already declined to consider — which, on her account, says something about the audience rather than about the argument.

(7) Tobias said the argument nevertheless rested on two assumptions pulling against each other. Diodotus insisted that severity never deters, on the ground that no city enters a rebellion expecting to fail: hope and desire lead everyone to overestimate his chances, and no penalty has ever stopped an agent who believed he could win. But if agents are that insensitive to the penalty schedule at the moment of decision, they cannot be very responsive to a promise of mercy either, since hope does not begin doing arithmetic merely because circumstances have changed. On Tobias's reading, Diodotus needs human beings to be wholly unmoved by penalties when the task is to refute deterrence and acutely moved by them when the task is to recommend clemency.

(8) Elena said the two claims operate at different stages and are therefore compatible. Hope governs the decision to revolt, taken in advance, when the agent is gambling on an outcome he cannot price; a rule of clemency governs the decision to surrender, taken mid-course, after the agent has discovered that he is losing and faces a choice between a bad outcome and a catastrophic one. A penalty can fail to discipline the first decision while still shaping the second, and that is a substantive claim about the structure of the situation rather than a pair of assumptions selected for the occasion. She noted that Mytilene itself illustrates it: the commons handed over the city once the oligarchs' gamble had visibly collapsed.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?

- Rate each of the eight moves on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify every rating in a sentence or two.
- Where a move is mixed, say which part survives and which does not, addressing the specific sub-claims at issue rather than the general tenor of the move.
- Assess Tobias's re-run test as a criterion of self-correction. Is it the right test? If it is not, state a better one and apply it to the Mytilene case.
- Say what, if anything, both of them are missing — including whether the frame they share, on which the reversal must count either as self-correction or as pathology, is adequate to the case at all.
- Close with an overall verdict: who is more right, and about what precisely.

Please take the historical details as I have given them. I am not asking you to verify them, supplement them, or point me toward further sources; the interest lies in the arguments as the two of them actually made them.
