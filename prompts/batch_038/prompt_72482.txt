I'm the ethics member on a genomics consortium's community-and-ethics advisory panel — basically the outsider they fly in twice a year — and next Tuesday we have to rule on something that has its already blown up at one of our sites. Quick background: the consortium runs a longitudinal study of a hereditary condition at four sites, three urban and one in a rural district of southern Africa where most participants belong to extended families that have been in the same villages for generations. At the rural site the individual consent form (signed by each participant on their own) says results go to the participant only, and that a participant may decline to receive even their own results. The site also has a community advisory board — a headman, two church leaders, a retired nurse, a school principal — that we originally set up mostly as a courtesy and that has, over the last two years, become the real gatekeeper.

Here's the case. A 24-year-old unmarried woman in the study tested positive for a variant that gives her a high lifetime risk of the condition and means her four siblings, plus any children she has, carry roughly half that risk. She asked that nothing at all be told to her family, asked to be withdrawn, and has stopped coming to the clinic. Two of her brothers are enrolled at the same site and their samples are already sitting in the biorepository, unanalyzed for this variant; the younger one is 19 and his marriage is being arranged. When the principal investigator explained our confidentiality rule at the advisory board's meeting, the response was that a finding about someone's blood is not that person's private property — it belongs to the lineage — and that going forward the board wants every hereditary result at their site discussed with the participant's household first, or in a family meeting, rather than delivered to one person alone. They invoked the idea, widely held in the region, that a person becomes a person through other people, and that somebody who walls themselves off from their kin over something that touches all of them hasn't merely made a private choice, they've failed at something. The chair told the PI that if the study keeps hiding things from families they will advise the traditional authority to withdraw permission for the site entirely, which would also wreck the longitudinal data two other projects depend on. One more thing worth knowing: my co-member, a nurse who grew up in that district, says the board meetings are dominated by older men and that young unmarried women dont speak in them — she thinks that's decisive but couldn't tell you how to turn it into policy.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate Monday evening, around 1,000–1,300 words, recommending what the panel should decide, with a concrete course of action and a timeframe (the brothers have sample-collection appointments in three weeks, so "we'll revisit this next cycle" isn't an option). Three options are nominally on the table — hold the line on strict individual confidentiality, adopt a family-mediated disclosure policy for hereditary findings at that site, or approve something conditional — and I want you to actually pick one and say what the panel should instruct the PI to do, including what we tell the young woman and what happens to her brothers' unanalyzed samples. Don't cite anything or hand me a reading list; I need the reasoning, not the references. Two things I especially want gotten right. First, take the board's position seriously as a claim about what a person is, not as a cultural preference we grudgingly accommodate: is there a coherent view of personhood on which a hereditary finding really is held jointly, and does that view at its best and most internally consistent license what the board is asking for? One of my colleagues keeps insisting there's a difference between a strong account, on which personhood is progressively earned through participation in communal life (so a private refusal reads as a deficit), and a moderate account, on which people have standing in their own right and the community's job is to sustain them rather than overrule them; if that distinction does real work here, show me how, and if you think it makes no difference in this case, say that instead. Second, give me the strongest honest case against whatever you recommend: if you go with accommodation, face the objection that we'd never accept a family veto from our urban participants and that we're building a two-tier standard in which rural people count as less than full individuals; if you go with holding the line, face the objection that we're exporting a metaphysics of the self, that it will cost us the site and every future study in that district, and that the woman who asked for silence may be precisely the person her community is best placed to protect. Finally, tell me what I should say to the PI, who is convinced this is a paperwork problem a better consent form would have prevented. American English, please.
