here's my situation. I write nonfiction. A colleague of mine died in the spring — a scientist, a famously generous teacher, the sort of man a town builds a story about. First: the story everybody tells about him, that as a young man he gave up his place on an expedition to a friend who needed the credit more. I chased it down and the friend told me it was invented thirty years ago to explain why he never went. It never happened. Second: a box of letters showing my colleague was, over decades, quietly ruinous to students who disappointed him, and that at least one of them left the field entirely because of it. My editor's advice is to keep the expedition story and drop the letters, on the grounds that the piece exists to give people something to imitate, and that a truth which only makes readers sadder about a dead man isn't doing that work.

I want you as the other side of this, and I'm going to come at you with your own words. In the Alexander you write: "For it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives; and in the most illustrious deeds there is not always a manifestation of virtue or vice, nay, a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles where thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities." Fine — but notice that's a rule about which true things to pick out, not a licence to add things that aren't so. The indulgence you ask for is from readers who don't know whether the tale is true. I do know.
