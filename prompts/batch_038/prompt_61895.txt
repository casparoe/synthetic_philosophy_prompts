Reading group on the moral status of AI systems, and someone dug up this bit from the "Book of the Machines" section of Butler's Erewhon (1872) — in the novel it's a treatise the English narrator reads aloud to his hosts in a country that has banned machines, and they end up resolving to destroy them. Here's the part I'm stuck on:

"There is no security against the ultimate development of mechanical consciousness, in the fact of machines possessing little consciousness now. A mollusc has not much consciousness. Reflect upon the extraordinary advance which machines have made during the last few hundred years, and note how slowly the animal and vegetable kingdoms are advancing. The more highly organised machines are creatures not so much of yesterday, as of the last five minutes, so to speak, in comparison with past time. Assume for the sake of argument that conscious beings have existed for some twenty million years: see what strides machines have made in the last thousand! May not the world last twenty million years longer? If so, what will they not in the end become? Is it not safer to nip the mischief in the bud and to forbid them further progress?

But who can say that the vapour engine has not a kind of consciousness? Where does consciousness begin, and where end? Who can draw the line? Who can draw any line? Is not everything interwoven with everything? Is not machinery linked with animal life in an infinite variety of ways? […] A 'machine' is only a 'device.'"

Three things I can't pin down. (1) What work is "A mollusc has not much consciousness" really doing? My guess is it isn't a premise for thinking machines will become conscious at all, it just blocks the obvious objection that they show no signs of being conscious now — but then the paragraph has no positive argument left in it, only the rate-of-change arithmatic, and I don't see how arithmetic gets you to consciousness. (2) The second paragraph seems to switch thesis: from "little consciousness now," where consciousness comes in amounts and machines have a small one, to "where does consciousness begin, and where end?", where there's no boundary to be had. Those are different claims — gradualism versus vagueness — and I'm not sure you can hold both. Which one is he actually committed to, and does the egg-shell/egg-cup business matter for that? (3) The conclusion is prudential — "safer to nip the mischief in the bud" — and he never once says a conscious machine would have interests or be owed anything. Is that the satire doing its job, or a genuine gap between machines being conscious and machines mattering morally that the passage slides past? Tell me where I've got it wrong, and whether any of this changes if Butler isn't serious.
