I'm a staff engineer at a consumer fintech company — budgeting, saving, money transfers, about 4M users. For the past year I've been on our internal Product Impact Panel, which is six people (two PMs, me, our general counsel, a designer, and the customer support lead) and it has to sign off before anything that changes user-facing behavior goes to 100% of users. I have no philosophy background at all, so I'm probably going to describe this badly. I need the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting.

The feature is called Second Look internally. It scores every outgoing transaction for "probability this user will later wish they hadn't done it." Three tiers: nothing, a dismissable banner, and a firm hold — the money just doesn't move for 12 hours and there is no override button in that tier, on purpose. The training signal is a question we ask 30 days after any nudge or hold ("looking back, was that the right call?"), shown next to a running total of what they saved that month. Important detail: the model isn't trained on the individual user's own answers, it's trained on everyone's pooled answers and then applied to each person.

Six-week pilot, 41,000 users, roughly 2,300 firm holds. Of the people who answered the 30-day question, 78% said yes it was the right call, 11% said no, and the rest never answered. A recurring theme in the free-text of the 78% was some version of "I hated it in the moment and it was still right." Two complaints got escalated to support leadership: a woman who tried to send $900 to her brother at 11pm so he could get his car fixed and keep his shift, got a firm hold, and he was written up at work — her ticket said the app decided what she'd want better than she did. And a freelancer who sends his mother $200 in another country on the 3rd of every month, held twice, because irregular income plus recurring outbound looks like a pattern to the model.

The feature team's best argument, which I think is actually strong and which the memo needs to take seriously rather than swat: people's in-the-moment selves reliably sabotage the things their calmer selves care about; we already ship a savings-goals feature and almost nobody sets it up, and the users who'd benefit most are exactly the ones who wouldn't opt in; an override in the top band would gut the feature in precisely the cases where it does any good; and 78% is not nothing — those are the same people, later, saying it was right.

The thing I can't shake, and I don't know if its a philosophy problem or a data-quality problem or both: we've built a system that votes on behalf of a hypothetical better version of the user, using a model of that better version learned from other people. And now that we're intervening, every future 30-day answer comes from somebody who's been living inside our interventions, so the number we're using to justify the feature is partly manufactured by the feature.

What I need from you: a memo of about 800 words, two pages max, that the panel could actually act on. Bottom line up front — ship to 100%, ship with conditions, or kill it — and if it's conditions, say exactly which ones and by when, with a checkpoint date. Give reasons the feature team can argue back against, not vibes. Say what would have to be true for their case to go through, and whether it is true here. Name the strongest consideration on the side you're *not* recommending, honestly, because counsel will find it anyway. Please don't just say "get better informed consent" — we have a consent screen and everybody taps through it. And end with about five lines giving the panel a reusable rule of thumb, because there's a shopping-app version of this in the pipeline and a "we'll hold your order until morning" version, and I don't want to relitigate from scratch each time.

Keep it plain enough that the support lead and the PMs can read it without any jargon. No citations or reading list or "further resources" section — nobody on this panel reads that, they read the memo.
