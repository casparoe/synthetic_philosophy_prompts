Six weeks ago I became the duty officer at a regional flood forecasting centre. I sign the public warning messages that go out to about 300,000 people, and through the winter there's roughly one event a month where I have to decide, inside about fifteen minutes, whether we say a thing flat out or whether we hedge it — our director only sees the severe tier, everything below that is mine. Two messages last season blew up, and they blew up in opposite directions. In November a colleague wrote 'the Cale will overtop the weir at Tefford and flood the lower village', no qualifier, because two gauges and the model all pointed that way; it didn't happen, the crest came eleven centimetres short, and the parish council wrote to the director saying we'd cried wolf and they'd take no notice of the next one. In February someone else, terrified of repeating that, wrote 'there is a possibility of significant flooding in the Teff area' at a moment when we were as confident as we ever get, and only two households put their boards up; nine were flooded. I've now been told to write a one-page standing rule that every officer on the rota can run before a message goes out, and I'm stuck, because the obvious rule — only say it if you know it's true — seems to forbid every forecast we issue, since strictly speaking nobody knows the future.

There's got to be a usable version of that argument somewere. I did a philosophy course years ago and I remember people fighting about whether the standard for stating something flat-out is truth, or knowledge, or just reasonable belief, and that the way you settle it is by looking at the cases where those come apart: the lottery ticket you end up right about, the stopped clock that reads the correct time, the rumour that turns out to be true. Please turn that into a procedure my rota can actually follow — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a settled answer to each, where the message must be flat, where it has to carry a number or an attribution to a source, and the point where the procedure stops being about evidence and hands the decision to a person because it's really about how much risk you're allowed to impose on people who can't opt out of it. It has to be runnable in ten minutes by someone who has been awake since 3am and has never heard the word epistemology, it should say explicitly what to do when we can't get a number out of the model, and it should be honest about which of its steps are just my own judgement dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three messages so I can see whether it behaves: (a) the February one, where we were as sure as we ever get but the wording said only 'a possibility of'; (b) a case where the flat statement came out exactly right but we got there by trusting an upstream gauge that we later found had been logging garbage — right answer, rotten route; (c) a warning we've been asked to issue off a 1-in-200-year projection for a reservoir nobody locally believes will go, where the cost of a false alarm is evacuating a town of four thousand for two days. I already suspect the procedure is going to tell me (b) was improper even though nothing bad happened and the officer was completely vindicated, and if that's the answer I want it stated plainly rather than softened, because it's exactly what my colleagues will refuse to accept and I need to know whether they're right to. Last thing: no bibliography, no reading list, no name-dropping — I can't check any of it and my staff definitely won't. Just the reasoning, plus a clear account of what the procedure can't settle and shouldn't pretend to.
