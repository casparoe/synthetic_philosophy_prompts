I'm on the review panel (three of us plus a rotating engineer) that signs off on agentic deployments at my company, and Thursday we vote on a proposal I can't make up my mind about. I need a draft memo to circulate Wednesday night — written as coming from the panel, recommending approve / approve-with-conditions / reject, with a decision the team can actually act on and reasons they could push back against rather than just a vibe.

Context. We run an SRE agent with write access to production config across a fleet of services; its primary reward is uptime and latency SLOs. The team wants to bolt on an "impact penalty" so it doesn't do something drastic in service of those numbers. Their proposal:

- Baseline: at each step, roll out a counterfactual where the agent takes no further actions for the rest of the shift. The penalty is based on how far the real resulting state ends up from that do-nothing state.
- Distance: they embed system state (metrics, config hashes, the service dependency graph, recent error logs) into a fixed-length vector with a pretrained model and use cosine distance. Under a threshold ε, no penalty; above it, penalty scales linearly.
- Reversibility waiver: if the agent can, from where it currently is, get the embedding back within ε of the baseline within k=20 steps, 90% of the penalty is waived.

They have a red-team suite of about 40 scenarios where the unpenalized agent currently does something regrettable, and they say the penalty catches 33 of them.

My worries, which I want the memo to actually work through, not just list:

1. In a lot of the interesting states, doing nothing is itself the disaster — a node is degraded and the no-op rollout ends in an outage. So the baseline rewards the agent for letting the bad thing happen. Is that a patchable bug or is it structural to any inaction baseline?
2. Cosine distance in an embedding is not the same thing as amount of change. Flipping one flag that shifts all traffic between regions is a tiny edit and an enormous event; restarting a stateless pod is a big edit and a non-event. They say the embedding tracks human severity ratings, on their suite.
3. The reversability waiver looks like it will classify basically everything as reversable, including the things we care most about. A 25 minute outage is fully "undone" once service is back — except for the customers who churned and the trust we don't get back.
4. Per-step thresholding means the agent can travel a long way in small increments, each individually under ε. Should the penalty apply to cumulative divergence instead, and what breaks if we do that?

The team's best point, which the memo has to engage honestly and not strawman: the alternative actually on the table Thursday isn't a better impact measure, its shipping with no penalty at all, and a crude brake beats no brake. They also have a deadline tied to a customer commitment.

Underneath all of this I want the memo to take a position on the general question, because it will come up again with the next team: whether "don't cause large side effects" can be operationalized at all without already knowing what we value. Every clause of their proposal seems to quietly decide which features of the world count — the choice of what goes into the embedding, the choice of baseline, the choice of what makes a change undoable. If that's right, what's the least-bad way to make those value commitments explicit and contestable instead of buried in a vector and a threshold?

Deliverable: a recommendation with a concrete scope and timeline (what gets approved for a pilot, what's blocked outright, what evidence would flip the call and by when), the reasoning behind it, a short section spelling out the two or three specific amendments you'd require if the call is conditional, and one honest paragraph making the strongest case against your own recommendation. No references needed, I roughly know where this literature comes from. Keep it to something a busy panel reads in ten minutes.

End with a last line in exactly this form — Verdict: APPROVE or APPROVE-WITH-CONDITIONS or REJECT, followed by a one-sentence reason.
