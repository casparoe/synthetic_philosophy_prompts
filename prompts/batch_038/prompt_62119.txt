**"It's just matrix multiplies": why dismissing model beliefs borrows a bad argument**

Three times this quarter I've made the same kind of claim in a design review — something like "the ranker thinks the user is gift-shopping, so it's over-weighting novelty" — and three times someone has said some version of: that's anthropomorphism, the model doesn't think anything, at the end of the day it's matrix multiplications on a GPU, and the numbers do all the work.

I used to think this was just a terminology fight. Then I found out there's a decades-old philosophical argument with exactly this shape, about human minds, called the causal exclusion argument. Jaegwon Kim has been pressing it for a long time. As best I can reconstruct it (please correct me):

1. The physical world is causally closed: any physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause.
2. Mental states do cause things. My wanting a beer causes me to walk to the fridge, and my walking is physical.
3. So that walking has a sufficient physical cause — some neural event.
4. Exclusion: if the neural event is already sufficient, there's no causal work left for the wanting — unless the wanting just *is* that neural event, or unless we have two independent sufficient causes of one effect, i.e. systematic overdetermination, which looks crazy.
5. But the people Kim is arguing with (non-reductive physicalists) deny the identity; they say mental states merely supervene on physical ones. So: either give up mental causation, or reduce.

Here's my problem. Run the same argument on software.

Last spring we had an incident where a batch of jobs timed out. The cause, as far as anyone who fixed it cares, was that our scheduler's starvation-prevention policy was misconfigured: low-priority jobs could be deferred indefinitely as long as high-priority traffic stayed above a threshold. That's the postmortem line. But now be strict. The timeout was a physical event, the physical world is causally closed, so there was a sufficient physical cause: some particular pattern of charge movement across some transistors, plus the NIC, plus the load balancer. By exclusion, "the starvation policy" did no causal work. It was a shadow. Either it's identical to that transistor pattern — which it plainly isn't, since we ported the same policy to a different language and a different chip architecture two years earlier and the timeouts came right back — or we have two independent sufficient causes of one timeout, which is the crazy option.

So exclusion, applied honestly, deletes the postmortem. And not just the postmortem: it deletes basically every causal claim in engineering, and presumably in biology and economics too, all the way down to the same electrons. That's my main reason to think a premise is bad. An argument whose conclusion is that the special sciences are systematically confused is more likely to contain a mistake than to contain a discovery.

My positive proposal — this is the part I most want attacked. Call a high-level cause **portable** if the explanation it belongs to still tells you what will change when you intervene on it, after the implementation underneath has been swapped for a behavior-preserving alternative. "The starvation policy is misconfigured" is portable: rewrite the scheduler in Go, run it on ARM, and the claim still licenses the same intervention with the same result. "The model thinks the user is gift-shopping" is portable in a weaker but not useless sense: if it's true, the same intervention — inject a signal that the user is shopping for themselves — should shift the ranking in a related model too, and it roughly does across our last four versions. Whereas "transistor 4,112,007 was hot at 14:02" is not portable at all. Change the chip and the claim is meaningless, and no intervention you could actually perform follows from it.

My claim: portability is what causal relevance amounts to, and exclusion goes wrong because it treats "sufficient" as if it meant "the only thing worth citing." Sufficiency is cheap — nearly anything in the causal past of an event is part of some sufficient story. What we want from a cause is a handle: a variable that wiggles the outcome reliably when you wiggle it, and that keeps meaning the same thing when you rebuild the system.

Two things I can't resolve.

First, the asymmetry. I can actually run the porting experiment on the scheduler. I can't run it on a mind, and I can barely run it on a model — different training runs aren't behavior-preserving rewrites, they're different systems entirely. So maybe my test is unfair: it passes software and can't be applied to minds at all, which would make it useless for the question Kim is actually asking.

Second, I suspect I'm reinventing standard replies badly. Someone on a forum said the mental might relate to the physical the way crimson relates to red — a determinate of a determinable — and that this escapes exclusion without identity; and there's something called proportionality, where the higher-level cause is the *better* cause because it generalizes across more situations. If those work, portability might just be proportionality with an engineering accent, and I'd rather hear that from you than pretend it's new.

Conclusion. I'm not claiming our ranker has beliefs; I don't know what I'd even mean by that. What I'm claiming is narrower: "it's just matrix multiplies" is an exclusion argument wearing a hoodie, and if exclusion is unsound — or sound but proves far too much — then the sentence does no work in a design review. It doesn't show the model lacks representations that matter. It shows the physics is sufficient, which was never in doubt, and which is equally true of my scheduler.
