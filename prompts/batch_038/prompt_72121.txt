I was listening to a podcast where the host did a segment on money-pump arguments and I'd like to know how much of it to trust. Here's roughly what he said:

"Say you prefer the blue mug to the red one, the red one to the green one, and the green one to the blue. Now somebody who knows this comes up to you — you're holding the blue mug — and offers to trade you the red one for a dollar. You take it, red beats blue. Then he offers the green one for your red one, another dollar, sure. Then he offers your own blue mug back for the green one, another dollar, sure. And now you're exactly where you started, minus three dollars. Notice what just happened there: that wasn't philosophy, that was arithmetic. If your preferences go in a circle, someone can charge you for walking in place. Which is why I've got no patience for the they're-just-hard-to-measure defense when studies show people flipping their choices depending on how the options get worded, or wanting the cake now and the gym later. Real people have cycles in their heads, cycles cost money, and that's the end of it: intransitive preferences aren't some interesting rival to a ranking, they're a defect that gets exploited."

So — where's he right, and where's he sliding? I half bought the arithmetic line while I was listening but now I'm suspicious: the pump seems to need the situation set up in a particular way to work, and I can't say what that setup is doing for him. Does the argument really establish that going in a circle is irrational, or only something narrower about people who accept a run of trades like that? And does the last part, about worded options and cake versus gym, follow from the mug story at all, or is it a different phenomenon he's folded in? Tell me what a decision theorist would nod along to, what they'd push back on hard, and whether anybody who actually wants to defend intransitive preferences has a decent reply left. Please also say how confident you are in the pieces you're asserting and single out the one you're least sure about.
