For thirty-eight years I worked in applied geophysics, the last twenty of them on seismic tomography: constructing images of the Earth's interior from travel-time data, and spending most of my working life on ill-posedness, non-uniqueness, resolution kernels, null spaces, and the choice of regularization. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in a somewhat desultory manner — first epistemology, which felt like home territory, and more recently social choice theory, by way of an undergraduate text a former colleague lent me. I have got as far as Arrow's theorem and the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result, and I find that I cannot read either except through the only conceptual apparatus I properly command. My present summary of the second is this: for a deterministic rule that takes a profile of voters' strict preference orderings over at least three alternatives and returns one of them, if every alternative lies in the rule's range and no voter can ever obtain an outcome he prefers by misreporting, whatever the others report, then the rule must be dictatorial — its output is fixed by a single voter's report alone. I gather that the restriction to strict orderings is doing real work and that admitting indifference costs something; if so, I should like to know what.

What I want from you is a translation, and then an honest account of what the translation fails to carry. Restate the theorem and the notion of strategy-proofness in my vocabulary — forward and inverse problems, identifiability, the null space and resolution, exogenous noise as against adversarial or biased data, regularization and the bias–variance trade-off, worst-case as against mean-square error guarantees, degrees of freedom in the model — condition by condition, as faithfully as you can manage. I read mathematics comfortably but have almost no game theory; I could not prove the theorem, and my idea of a dominant strategy amounts to one paragraph's worth of reading. So please do not simply relabel the game-theoretic concepts in estimation jargon and enter that as a success. For each condition of the theorem, tell me whether it maps cleanly, whether it maps only with distortion, and if the latter, what the distortion is and in which direction it runs; and then tell me which elements of the result have no counterpart whatever in the language of inverse problems. I am especially puzzled about the status of the voter's private information. In my field the data are noisy measurements of a single shared world, and the error model is a property of the instruments rather than a choice made by an agent who is trying to steer my reconstruction toward an outcome he likes. Whether that asymmetry is the whole of the difficulty, or only part of it, I cannot tell from where I sit.

Then give me your judgment on where the residue belongs. Is it a limitation of my framework — inverse theory simply lacking the resources to describe agents who select their own errors — or is it a sign that the economists' claim is less perspicuous than it appears, so that some of the work being done by talk of manipulation and sincerity rests on assumptions a measurement theorist would state explicitly and they leave tacit? If my summary above is wrong in any respect that would change the translation, correct it before you begin rather than faithfully translating a misstatement.

Throughout, I should like you to mark your confidence: say plainly how sure you are of each of the principal mappings, and single out the one step in your translation that you trust least — the place where you suspect you have quietly imported an assumption from one side and presented it to me as a finding about the other.
