I am a high school senior writing the final paper for my school's philosophy of mind seminar. My topic is a reading of Pixar's Inside Out, with some use of Inside Out 2, against Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model. What I want is criticism rather than a summary of either the films or the theory, and I would rather be told where my own reading goes wrong than be reassured.

I have the obvious points already, and I would like to be pushed past them: Headquarters is a room with a screen on which the world is presented to five small spectators, which looks like precisely the homunculus arrangement Dennett thinks no coherent theory can keep; the memory orbs are finished recordings, filed on shelves and replayed intact, which looks like the single canonical stream the multiple drafts model denies. What interests me is where the films argue against their own set design.

Could you take up the following?

First, the two films treat the record in opposite ways. When Sadness touches a stored memory and turns it blue, and when the hockey memory Joy finds in the dump — the one she had always experienced as a triumph — replays from its beginning as a lost game and only then as comfort, is that Orwellian, meaning the event really happened one way and the stored version is revised afterward, or is it a case of there having been several drafts of the same event all along, none of which was the memory? In the sequel, by contrast, Joy's catapult flings unwanted memories to the back of the mind before they can enter the Sense of Self, and those exiled memories later flood back and regenerate it. That reads to me as pre-emptive editing that fails because a draft can be displaced but never deleted. Is that a point the film is genuinely entitled to make, or am I over-reading a plot device?

Second, Dream Productions is a crew composing a dream backstage while Riley sleeps: content assembled before anyone experiences it, with no fact about what she would have seen unedited. That struck me as the only genuinely Stalinesque object in either film, and yet the finished dream is still shown on a screen. Does the film know it is contradicting itself there, and does the contradiction cost it anything?

Third, the train of thought is the films' one literally sequential stream, and it stops running when Riley falls asleep; meanwhile the personality islands rise, collapse, and are rebuilt as emphasis shifts. Are the islands a dramatization of the self as a center of narrative gravity, or does the film commit itself to something more substantial than Dennett can allow, since in the film the islands seem to sustain behavior rather than to summarize it? And should the train be read as the stream the multiple drafts model says does not exist, or as merely one probe among others, alongside the console and the family dinner scene, where three Headquarters negotiate a single conversation and what Riley reports to her parents diverges from what her own console is doing? That scene also stacks Headquarters inside Headquarters — Dad's emotions watching Riley on their screen while she watches them on hers — which is either the film's clearest regress or its clearest joke about one, and I cannot tell which.

Fourth, my teacher's objection is that any film with characters inside a head cannot escape the homunculus problem: if the emotions watch a screen, we owe either an infinite series of inner watchers or an admission that Riley herself is nowhere in particular. I want to know whether the films can answer this with their own resources — the first film's ending, where the console has become a wide mixer that no single emotion operates, and the sequel's Sense of Self, a structure built out of accumulated beliefs that nothing appears to watch — or whether the honest verdict is that they relocate the theater rather than abolish it, leaving the audience in the cinema as the last homunculus.

Please be fair in both directions: do not reduce the films to an illustration of Dennett's position, and do not bend the position to make the films come out right. Where a film resists the reading, say so plainly, and tell me which of my four claims you think is weakest. I would like you to close by saying what, if anything, the films add to the debate that the arguments do not — my suspicion is that it has to do with what it is like to have your own past recolored by a mood you did not choose, but I have not been able to state that rigorously. I do not need references or a bibliography; I need the reading itself.
