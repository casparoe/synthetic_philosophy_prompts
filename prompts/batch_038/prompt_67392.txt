I have spent twenty years earning a living from sentences: three published novels, a fourth in draft, and a working fluency in the vocabulary my trade uses on itself — focalization and whose eyes the story is seen through, free indirect style, scene set against summary, the reliable and unreliable narrator, the distinction between a detail that is merely vivid and one that is representative, the minor character who ends up carrying more weight than the protagonist. I read history for pleasure, and I have run into a quarrel that I cannot locate in that vocabulary, although it looks as though it ought to be there.

The quarrel runs roughly as follows. On one side stand the historians of structure and the longue durée — Braudel's Mediterranean, the price series, the demographic aggregate, the state and its bureaucracies — who hold that the forces which actually shaped the past operated at scales no single life can register, and that a case study is at best an illustration. On the other side stand the microhistorians and the practitioners of history from below: Ginzburg's miller before the Inquisition, Zemon Davis's returned husband, Darnton's Parisian apprentices and their cat massacre, Thompson's artisans and weavers. These argue that one densely documented life, read at close range, discloses a social world the aggregates flatten out of existence, and that the great men and great institutions were in any case only ever a thin slice of what happened. Suspended between the two camps is an accusation that keeps recurring: that the microhistorian's case is precisely the case that got written down because something went wrong, so that the inquisition file, the lawsuit and the poor-relief record preserve the exception rather than the norm.

What I want is a translation, not a summary. Restate the disagreement as faithfully as you can in the terms I work in — as a dispute about scale of narration, about point of view, about what a minor character can be made to carry, about whether a single scene can stand for a condition. Where a historian's claim has a genuine counterpart in craft, name the counterpart and say how exact the fit is.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims translate cleanly. Which translate only by being bent, and in which direction you bent them. And which have no expression whatsoever in the vocabulary of fiction — I suspect the aggregate, the price series and the archive's own mechanism of selection are candidates, but I may be wrong. For each item in that last category, take a position: is the residue a defect in my vocabulary, something fiction simply has no way of saying, or a sign that the historians' disagreement is less substantive than it appears — an argument about narrative scale dressed up as an argument about truth?

One caution. My vocabulary carries its own commitments: that everything must be seen from inside someone, that a detail earns its place by being telling. Do not allow those commitments to settle the historians' question by fiat, and where a craft term you use already contains a verdict, mark it as such.

Test the translation against my own case if that helps. The fourth novel is built on a woman who surfaces in four documents across thirty years and never in her own voice: as a defendant's wife, as a complainant's neighbour, as a name in a parish register, as the recipient of a small bequest in a will. I have invented her inner life, which the historians may not do. I should like to know whether the difference between what I did with her and what Ginzburg did with his miller is a difference of kind, or only of how much rope each of us allows himself.
