I write literary fiction, and there's a scene I can't crack, so bear with me. In the book, a government is about to formally apologize for something it did to a minority population roughly three generations back — land confiscated, children taken into state institutions, that kind of thing — and my narrator is the speechwriter who has to draft the thing. I've been reading the actual argument about whether state apologies for historical wrongs even hold together, and it might as well be written in Akkadian. But I do speak one language fluently, which is craft: POV, scene versus summary, free indirect style, the unreliable narrator, the obligatory scene, dramatic irony, denouement, want versus need, whose story it is, who the implied reader is.

So translate that debate into my language as straight as you're able. Go claim by claim:

- the speech-act one: an apology has conditions, it has to come from the right mouth to the right ears, and it has to be recieved to count (the word I kept running into was uptake) — plus the adjacent worry that an apology with nothing attached to it is just theatre, or worse, a cheap way of closing the file;
- the standing one: nobody alive did any of it, so what entitles today's citizens to apologize at all, unless you treat the state as some kind of continuing corporate agent that outlives its members;
- the addressee one: the people actually wronged are dead and their descendants weren't the ones harmed, so who is being apologized to, and who could possibly accept it;
- and the lawyers' one: officials say regret instead of apology because apology concedes liability, and whether a hedged, counsel-approved statement is still an apology.

For each, give me the clean craft counterpart, what you had to bend to get it there, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary whatsoever. I want the residue to be the main event, not a courteous footnote at the end, and don't flatter me: tell me straight whether my toolkit is quietly converting a question about justice into a question about whether an audience gets a satisfying scene — like, does craft only have words for making people feel that something has been resolved? Then the last bit. Is what's left over a hole in my framework, or evidence that some of what these people are arguing about is fuzzier than it sounds from the outside? I ask because I suspect my subplot feels hollow because I'm writing the apology as a climax when the thing it's supposed to repair is unrepairable, and I can't tell if that's a structural problem I should fix or actually the point I should be leaning into.
