I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, but for the past six years I have also run our newsroom's experimentation program, and that second hat is the one I want to write from. I am fluent — not casually familiar — in the vocabulary of online controlled experiments: randomization unit and exposure allocation, power analyses and minimum detectable effects, guardrail metrics versus the overall evaluation criterion, pre-registration of the hypothesis and the primary metric before assignment, peeking and sequential-testing corrections, novelty and primacy effects, interference and violations of the no-spillover assumption in two-sided settings, holdback and long-term holdout experiments, proxy metrics and their drift, surrogate endpoints, and the whole apparatus of deciding whether a change is real and whether it is good by the criterion we committed to in advance. A colleague of mine, after reading some Dewey at my insistence, remarked that Dewey was essentially a product manager avant la lettre and that a self-governing community on his account is just an organization doing continuous deployment with a fast feedback loop. I suspect that flattens him, but I cannot yet say where or why, and I want the translation done properly before I decide.

So: take Dewey's experimentalism about inquiry and democracy and restate it in the vocabulary I actually use at work, as faithfully as you can, claim by claim. The claims I have in mind are these — that inquiry begins in a genuinely indeterminate situation and ends in a resolved one, so that the unit of analysis is the whole episode rather than the belief; that logical forms and epistemic norms are instruments that arise within inquiry and are answerable to how well inquiry goes, not mirrors of an antecedent structure; that the right characterization of a successful conclusion is warranted assertibility rather than correspondence with a ready-made reality; that ends-in-view are themselves revised in the course of inquiry rather than fixed prior to it and merely pursued; that a public comes into existence around the indirect consequences of associated activity, and the state is that public organizing itself to regulate those consequences; and that democracy is therefore not an aggregation device for pre-existing preferences but the social counterpart of experimental method — the arrangement under which a society can run inquiry on itself and revise its own criteria. Include, if it earns its place, his running disagreement with Lippmann: that the trouble with modern publics is not that ordinary people cannot follow expert findings, so that the remedy is a better-insulated expert apparatus, but that the public cannot locate itself because the consequences of what people do to one another have not been made perceptible. For each claim, give me one of three verdicts: it translates cleanly into experimentation-platform terms (and here is the translation); it translates only with distortion (and here is precisely what gets bent); or it has no counterpart in the target vocabulary at all.

Two things I want held to strictly. First, do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call the result a translation. The experimentation vocabulary gets several things for free that Dewey's view refuses to grant: a success metric that is exogenously given and frozen at pre-registration, a well-defined and stable randomization unit, a single authority empowered to ship or roll back, a population that does not reconstitute itself in response to being measured, and a clean separation between choosing means and specifying ends. Wherever a mapping only works because one of those has been assumed rather than argued, say so explicitly and label it a disagreement rather than a rendering. Second, give me the residue report, and take both horns of it seriously. Where something fails to translate, tell me whether that failure indicts my vocabulary — for instance, whether a framework whose primary metric must be fixed before assignment has simply no way to represent the revision of the criterion itself, no way to represent the measured population as also the deciding subject, and no way to express that who sets the evaluation criterion, and on whose behalf, is a political question rather than a technical configuration choice — or whether it instead suggests Dewey's claims are less determinate than they appear, with inquiry and democracy stretched until they cover everything and a normative conclusion tucked inside what is presented as a method. Use a concrete case from my side to test which horn applies: our headline and format tests optimize proxies for attention that everyone concedes have drifted from what we think the journalism is for, and when we proposed a long-term holdback to check whether a change to the criterion itself had been a good idea, we discovered there is no agreed procedure for adjudicating that question — the criterion gets changed in a meeting and defended rhetorically. Say whether that gap is what Dewey is pointing at or whether I am overreading him into an ordinary governance problem. Finally, tell me what a genuinely Deweyan experimentation program would have to give up in rigor, comparability, and accountability to accommodate the residue, because if the answer is that it would have to give up everything that makes an experiment an experiment, I want to know that too. No reading lists, no citations, no gestures to secondary literature — I can't check them and I don't want them; I want the translation and the accounting of what it lost.
