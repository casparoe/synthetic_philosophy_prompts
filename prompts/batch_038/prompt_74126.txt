I'm in a decision theory seminar this term and we just finished the week on infinite expectations — St. Petersburg first, then the Pasadena game (the Nover–Hájek one in Mind, 2004 I think). Quick version of the setup as our TA wrote it on the board: a fair coin gets flipped until it lands heads for the first time, and if that first heads comes on toss n you receive (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars. So +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, −16/3, and so on. Since the chance of first heads at n is 2^-n, each outcome contributes ±2/n to the expectation, and the whole thing is 2(1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …) = 2 ln 2 ≈ 1.39. Two people in my section, Ruth and Owen, went at it for like forty minutes after class and I genuinely could not tell who was landing anything. Roughly how it went:

(1) Ruth said the game shows expected-value theory is broken, not just strained. The series only converges conditionally — take absolute values and you get the harmonic series, which diverges — so by rearranging the pay-off table you can make the "expected value" come out to any number you want, including ±∞. Same game throughout, any value you like, therefore there is no value.

(2) Owen said that's a stunt with notation, not a fact about the gamble. The payoffs are pinned to outcomes by the rules, so there's a perfectly natural ordering — by n — and in that ordering the sum converges to 2 ln 2. The mere possibility of writing the terms in a different sequence doesn't give anyone license to reorder the outcomes.

(3) Ruth said "natural ordering" is doing all the work and is arbitrary. An expectation over a countably infinite outcome space isn't defined as the limit of one particular sequence of partial sums; it's a sum over outcomes, which requires unconditional convergence, and unconditional convergence requires absolute convergence. Here the positive part and the negative part are each infinite on their own, so the expectation fails to exist as a matter of definition, not convention.

(4) Owen conceded the math but said it's beside the point for deciding anything, because nobody ever actually faces the infinite game. Every real house has a finite bankroll, so what you face is truncated, and every truncation has a perfectly well-defined expectation: chop it off at toss N and you get twice the Nth alternating-harmonic partial sum, which is within 2/(N+1) of 2 ln 2 and converges to it. So the paradox dissolves once you take finitely rich agents seriously.

(5) Ruth said that's exactly the sleight of hand: truncating at toss N is not what a bankroll does. A bankroll caps the size of the pay-off, and that's a different operation with different results. Suppose the house can pay out at most a billion but can only collect up to your net worth, say $8,000 — you've clipped the huge negative tail while leaving most of the positive tail intact, and the truncated game's expectation is well above 2 ln 2. Cap only one side and leave the other running and the expectation is literally ±∞, since each half-series diverges. So there's no such thing as the value of the Pasadena game, only the value of a game relative to a pair of balance sheets. Owen said fine, but that's a feature and not a bug — how much a bet is worth to you obviously depends on your balance sheet — and he added that unless the two caps differ by orders of magnitude, the truncated value sits within a few cents of 2 ln 2, so Ruth's "it's all relative to the bankrolls" is technically true but practically empty.

(6) Then Ruth tried a seperate attack: consider the mirror game where every pay-off is negated, so it's worse than Pasadena in every possible state of the world, heads-on-toss-one costing you $2 instead of winning you $2. Strictly dominated, obviously. But if neither game has an expectation, EV theory can't rank them — the theory goes silent exactly where it's most embarrassing. Owen said that redounds to his credit: dominance is a seperate principle and it delivers the right verdict with no expectations involved, so all the case shows is that expectationalism isn't the whole of decision theory. He then suggested you could also rescue a determinate expectation by Abel summation — multiply the toss-n term by r^n, sum, let r → 1 — and claimed that gives a different answer from the plain sum, which would prove the plain sum isn't privileged after all. Ruth said that's just false, Abel summation gives the same 2 ln 2 here, and anyway once you start shopping around for summation methods you've re-licensed the rearrangement problem in a new vocabulary.
