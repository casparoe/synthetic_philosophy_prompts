I am assembling a small anthology of deadpan bureaucratic satire aimed at formal philosophy, and I need one piece on two-dimensional semantics.

**The form.** An official guidance note issued by an invented regulator — something like the Authority for Reference and Designation — instructing firms how to determine what the words on their labels, packaging, and standard contracts actually refer to, now that the jurisdiction has adopted two-dimensional semantics as its official theory of meaning. Roughly 700–900 words. Give it authentic front matter (status of this note, who it applies to, what it replaces), numbered sections to two decimal places, and a closing appendix of three "worked determinations" — one to three sentences each — drawn from complaints the Authority has adjudicated. Every firm, official, complaint, and case file is invented; do not quote any real text and do not attribute any line to any real philosopher.

**The doctrine, applied correctly and without apology.** Expressions carry a primary intension, fixed by a reference-fixing description and evaluated over scenarios — centered worlds, since the Authority's forms must be filed here, now, and by you — and a secondary intension, evaluated over worlds considered as counterfactual with the actual world's reference-fixing facts held fixed. The note should genuinely need both dimensions and deploy them for different purposes: primary intensions for questions about what a reasonable consumer could know a priori from the face of the label, secondary intensions for enforcement, product safety, and anything that turns on how the world is. At least one worked determination should involve a beverage whose lakes and rain are filled by something other than H₂O, and at least one should turn on a necessary identity that nobody could have known a priori (a double-billing dispute is the obvious shape). Somewhere in the procedure, include a requirement that is knowable a priori by every filer and yet contingent — the regulator's own equivalent of an "I am here now" clause.

**Where the comedy comes from.** The Authority is competent, sincere, and perfectly consistent; the absurdity is what happens when this semantics gets operationalized rather than any stupidity on the regulator's part. Build in a licensing or prior-notification regime for rigidifying devices (the operator actually, the demonstrative that, a dthat-style rigidifier), and a final provision specifying which intension the guidance note itself is to be read under — then let that provision do whatever it does. The objections a specialist will recognize should be enacted by the Authority's procedures and never stated as objections: that a reference-fixing description must itself be interpreted, that primary intensions look uncomfortably speaker-relative, that the second dimension may not be doing any semantic work, that a firm cannot tell which world is actual and therefore cannot tell what its own labels mean. Include one consultation response, quoted at length from an invented trade body, holding that all of this is simply one-dimensional possible-worlds semantics with centered worlds and an extra step; let the Authority dispose of it in a single sentence of bureaucratic contempt.

**Two further requirements.** First, keep it legible: a reader with no background in philosophy of language should still enjoy it as a piece of regulatory absurdism, without needing the appendix explained. Second, make definite choices rather than offering me options — commit to the centered-world, primary/secondary intension version of the framework, write as though it were settled law, and do not hedge inside the fiction.

After the guidance note, add one paragraph of at most 120 words, outside the fiction, telling me where you took liberties: which contested claims the satire leans on, which of the objections you think are actually fatal rather than funny, and any simplification a specialist would flag.
