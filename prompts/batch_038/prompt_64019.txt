Last autumn I settled my grandfather's estate in the district where he farmed, a place that went through a stretch in the 1990s when the national currency stopped being worth the trouble of counting. Among his papers I found, pressed into the back of a ledger, a bundle of narrow paper strips he had printed himself and used to pay his harvest labourers. They were unbacked by anything: no metal, no warehouse receipt, no promise of convertibility. What made them spendable was the reverse of each strip, which carried a running column of names — every person who had accepted the strip in payment added their own name in their own hand beneath the last. A strip with forty names on it was treated as sounder than a fresh one, and when the season closed the strips were brought back to my grandfather, exchanged for work already done on his land, and torn up. I have spent the winter with Berkeley's Querist, which he wrote to promote an Irish national bank, and two of its queries describe those strips better than anything I have read before or since: whether money "be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry," and whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"

I am now the unpaid treasurer of a workers' cooperative, and we are seriously debating issuing notes on the same principle. Several members of the board object that paper which is not backed by anything real is not money but a confidence trick, and that my grandfather's strips worked only because he personally owned the land at the end of them. I do not think an argument will move them. I think a story might.

Please write a short story of about 1,500 to 2,000 words set in a single small community that has begun issuing notes of exactly this kind: transferable, unbacked by metal or stores, each bearing on its reverse the signatures of everyone who has taken it, so that a heavily signed note commands labour more readily than a fresh one, and each surrendered at intervals to the body that issued it and cancelled against work performed. The specifications I would ask you to meet are these.

First, give the community one member who cannot accept that the names are what do the work and who insists the notes need something real standing behind them. Put the strongest available case in this character's mouth, not a straw one; they should turn out to be right about something that matters, and the story should show what.

Second, build a middle section in which someone begins holding back the oldest, most heavily signed notes rather than spending them, for reasons that make sense to that person, and show what happens to work undertaken, to what things cost, and to how people speak to one another once the best notes stop moving.

Third, introduce an outsider who knows none of the names, or someone who must leave the community for good, and let the notes meet that case.

Fourth, the Berkeleyan content — that money's whole business is to set industry in motion, that a community can be poor in coin and rich in money or the reverse, that hoarding is a kind of dereliction rather than thrift — must emerge from events, from the mechanics of the strips, and from what people say to each other in their own idiom. No exposition, no epigraphs, no character naming Berkeley, and no use of terms such as fiat, nominalism, seigniorage, or store of value.

Fifth, keep the prose plain and concrete, in close third person or the first person, and end on a particular image involving a note being cancelled or reissued rather than on a verdict about who was right.

After the story, add a single paragraph — no references, no suggestions for further reading — saying which of Berkeley's commitments about money each principal character embodies, and naming the point at which the story presses hardest on his account: the thing about these notes that his theory does not obviously explain.
