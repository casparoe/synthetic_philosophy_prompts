**Role.** You are a literary science-fiction writer who also holds graduate training in decision theory and moral psychology: someone equally at home in a literary quarterly and in a behavioral-economics seminar, known for embedding rigorous technical structure in fiction without ever explaining that structure on the page. Take the commission seriously and work through it in order; the planning stage matters as much as the prose, and a thinner, internally consistent story beats a richer, incoherent one.

---

**Part One — Planning notes (about 300 words, clearly labeled, not part of the story).**

Before writing any fiction, settle the following:

1. **Her discount function.** Give your protagonist a present-bias parameter and a long-run rate. Choose one concrete good she wants — a finished manuscript, a sum of money, a signed contract — and state its subjective value to her at delays of one hour, one month, and one year. Then identify two specific moments at which her ranking of options reverses: the reversal that leads her to begin something she had judged she should not begin, and the reversal that leads her to abandon something she had judged she should finish. Show the arithmetic that produces each.
2. **Her self-knowledge.** Locate her on the spectrum between the agent who is wholly unaware that her future self will defect and the agent who predicts that defection with complete accuracy. Most real people are neither. Specify the direction and rough magnitude of her error, and name one thing that error causes her to do which neither a wholly unaware nor a wholly clear-eyed person would do.
3. **The two readings the story must hold open.** On the first, she acts against a judgment she genuinely still holds at the moment of acting — weakness of will in the strict sense. On the second, she holds no such judgment at that moment, because near-term magnitudes simply present themselves to her as larger than they are: an error of perception or measurement rather than of will. State which reading the closing scene will tip toward, and identify the single image that will do the tipping.

---

**Part Two — The story (1,800–2,400 words).**

Write a story built on this situation. You may change names and surface details, but keep the load-bearing machinery:

- A clinic in a near-future city offers a one-time, irreversible procedure called **calibration**. It does not make a patient more disciplined. It makes her time-consistent: after calibration she no longer discounts the near future more steeply than the far, so her preferences no longer reverse as a deadline approaches. She wants now what she will want later. Waiting lists are long.
- Your protagonist has, over several years, installed three commitment devices aimed at forcing her to finish the work she keeps failing to finish. Two are already defeated; dramatize one of those defeats concretely somewhere in the story's past.
- The third is a contract that pays a substantial sum to an organization she finds morally repugnant unless she completes a specified act by a specified date. It contains a **void clause**: she may cancel at any time by paying a fee. The fee was set by her own earlier, clearer self at precisely the amount she would be willing to pay at every distance from the deadline except one. The story turns on whether she reaches that exception, and what she finds when she does.
- Include two foils. One is a person who is entirely accurate about her own future defections and has therefore stopped making plans at all. The other is a calibrated convert. Show the convert doing one thing that a present-biased person could never manage, and failing at one thing a present-biased person finds easy; let neither fact be narrated as a moral.

**Constraints.**

- No named philosophers, economists, studies, or institutions; no epigraphs; no equations, formulas, or Greek letters anywhere in the story itself. The technical content belongs in the planning notes and must be carried in the fiction by objects and procedures — a fee schedule taped inside a cupboard door, a stair, a jar, a countdown display, a form with a box that must be initialed.
- At least one scene must be told entirely from inside the ninety seconds preceding a choice, at a scale where the reader can feel the discounting happening.
- Include one short exchange in which the protagonist is accused of weakness of will and replies with something that is true and completely unhelpful.
- Do not resolve whether she is weak-willed or merely mistaken about magnitudes. Construct the events so that an attentive reader can argue either side from the same evidence.
- End on an action or on a sentence of physical description — never on reflection, summary, or a stated theme.

**Avoid.** Addiction framed as an inner monster or a voice; the word *addiction* at all; dream sequences and abrupt wakings; a reveal that the clinic has been running an experiment on her; any character who delivers the theme aloud.

**Final check before you submit.** Reread the story against your planning notes and confirm that every choice your protagonist makes is implied by the discount structure and by the size of her self-knowledge error as you specified them. Where a scene contradicts them, revise the scene or amend the notes — but do not deliver the two in conflict, and do not paper over a gap with interior monologue.
