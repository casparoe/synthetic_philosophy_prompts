I am a novelist at work on a book whose hinge is a disputed document, and I want the conceptual machinery underneath it to be sound rather than merely atmospheric. I would like a case analysis, running in both directions: a real episode examined through a theory of letterforms, and the episode turned around to test the theory.

The episode is the one that came to be called Fontgate. After the Panama Papers leak, a joint investigation team in Pakistan examined documents submitted by Maryam Nawaz concerning her family's London properties. One of them, a trust deed purportedly signed in February 2006, was found to have been typed in Calibri. Calibri was designed by Lucas de Groot, developed between 2002 and 2005, first shown in a 2005 beta of Windows Vista, first made usable with a beta of Office 2007 in May 2006, and released to the general public in January 2007. De Groot, asked about the deed, said there was "really zero chance" that it was genuine. The same reasoning had already disposed of a 2009 letter attributed to the sprinter Bob Hayes, who had died in 2002: it was typed in Calibri. There is a further wrinkle I want you to take seriously. In 2013 Google released Carlito, a freely licensed face built to be metrically compatible with Calibri, with glyph shapes derived from the open-source Lato. A document set in Carlito reflows, line for line, exactly as it would in Calibri.

The theory is the Letter Spirit work Hofstadter pursued with his research group in the 1990s, as I understand it — and if I have garbled it, correct me before you use it. The claim, roughly, is that the identity of a letter is not a checklist of features that an instance must possess, but a style or spirit that persists across radical variation: an 'a' drawn on a small grid, slanted, broken, ornamented, or barely legible is still an 'a', and no fixed list of strokes and counters will capture why. The project tried to model this by generating fonts on a coarse grid and by tasks such as deriving a whole alphabet from variations on a single capital. Its foil is the view that a letterform has an essence expressible as a template against which instances are matched.

What I want from you:

First, state precisely what the font-dating inference claims and what picture of typeface identity it presupposes. Is the argument about letters at all? Or is it about a typeface as a dated artifact — a manufactured thing with a release history — rather than about a category whose instances we recognize? If those are different questions, say which one the experts were actually answering, and whether the public discussion conflated them.

Second, map the inference honestly: what it establishes, and where it leaks. Consider the gap between the May 2006 beta and the January 2007 general release, the possibility of an authentic 2006 original retyped later, the difference between reading glyphs off a scan and reading a font name out of a file's metadata, and the fact that identification of a face is itself a judgment that can be wrong.

Third, do the Carlito case. If a face can be replaced by one with different outlines such that every line break and column width is preserved, what has survived the substitution? Does this vindicate the Letter Spirit claim that a spirit can outlive its body — with metrics and rhythm playing the role of spirit — or does it show that what survives is not style but a table of numbers, which is closer to the template view the theory rejects?

Fourth, and most important, tell me where the theory is silent rather than making it speak. If letter recognition is a matter of spirit and not of criteria, what does that imply about the weight of expert testimony on which face a document was set in? Does Fontgate confirm Letter Spirit, stress it, or fall outside the range of cases it was ever built to handle?

Write in American English, roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, with a few section headings if that helps. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; work from what you already know, and say plainly when you are uncertain. Close with two or three sentences of advice to me as a novelist: which of the two pictures of a letterform I should give my document examiner, and what that choice will cost her when she has to testify.
