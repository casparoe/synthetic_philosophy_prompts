I sit in on a little metaphysics reading group, and last week two of the regulars — call them Nadia and Owen — got into a long argument about Leibnitz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, specifically about Max Black's old thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, some fixed distance apart, same size, same stuff, same temperature, everything the same. I took notes while it was happening and honestly couldn't referee it myself, so I'd like someone else to.

The exchange, as near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Nadia: the case kills PII, full stop. It's perfectly coherent to suppose a universe like that, and if it's possible then two distinct things can share all their properties, so PII is false — or at least not a necessary truth. Nothing in the scenario tells the spheres apart.

(2) Owen: the thought experiment begs the question. To even set it up you have to say "two spheres," and PII is precisely the thesis that you can't describe such a situation without smuggling in a difference between them. So the case assumes numerical diversity comes for free, which is what's actually in dispute.

(3) Nadia: that's just refusing to engage. Conceivability arguments are allowed to stipulate a scenario and then ask whether the principle survives it. The spheres are given as two by the setup, sure, but the interesting question is whether anything *in* the scenario discerns them, and the answer is no.

(4) Owen: something does. They stand in a relation to each other. Each is that distance from the other, and "is that distance from" holds between the two spheres but never of a sphere with itself — it's irreflexive. Standing in an irreflexive relation is enough to ground non-identity. And you can state it without naming either sphere: you don't say "a is that far from b," you say "these two bear R to each other." So the circularity charge you're about to make doesn't get a grip. There's a label for this — weak discernibility, as opposed to discerning them by a property one has and the other lacks.

(5) Nadia: two problems. First, weak discernibility is only a *criterion* for non-identity once you already know the objects aren't identical; it doesn't explain why there are two spheres rather than one, and in a perfectly symmetrical case it can't be doing any work, since nothing picks out which is which. Second, notice what you've conceded: they do share every qualitative monadic property. If PII is read as a claim about qualitative properties — the only interesting reading — then weak discernibility doesn't rescue it, it just swaps in a weaker principle and calls it a victory.

(6) Owen: fine, bite the other bullet. If they're genuinely indiscernible, consider the supposed possibility that the two spheres switch places. That's not a real possibility distinct from the way things are in that universe; there's no state of affairs it corresponds to. Which suggests the "two spheres" description is defective rather than PII being false — there's no fact about which sphere is which, so we're not really describing two individuals at all. And if you insist there are two, you owe us primitive thisness: a bare numerical difference with no qualitative ground, undetectable, and unstatable.

(7) Nadia: primitive thisness is a cheap commitment, not a scandal — we believe in plenty of undetectable things. But your switching point cuts against you. If the switching scenario describes nothing, that's a reason to think the case contains *fewer* discernible differences than you claim, not a reason to think there's only one sphere. You can't get from "no qualitative difference" to "no numerical difference" without already assuming PII. And thisness isn't an idle wheel: we need something like it to talk about the very same objects across time, or about possibilities in which these particular things are arranged differently than they are.

(8) Owen: one last thing, and it's against my own view as much as yours. Weak discernibility is fragile. Irreflexivity isn't a purely logical feature of "is that distance from" — it depends on the geometry of the space the spheres are in. Suppose the space were closed in a way that let a single sphere stand in that distance relation to itself, going the long way round. Then the criterion would report two spheres in a one-sphere universe. So it's not a general account of numerical diversity, it's a lucky fact about our spatial assumptions.

Nadia said something dismissive at that point and they went to get food.

So, could you go through this and (a) rate each of the eight moves — clearly right, partly right, or mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification each; (b) tell me specifically who wins on the circularity charge in (2)–(3), who wins on whether weak discernibility rescues PII in (4)–(5), and who wins on the switching scenario in (6)–(7); (c) say what both of them are missing, since I suspect there's something neither of them raised; and (d) actually settle it — does the two-sphere universe refute the identity of indiscernibles, and if it only refutes some formulations, say which formulation is worth holding and whether you think that one is true. Don't just split the difference at the end, I want a verdict.
