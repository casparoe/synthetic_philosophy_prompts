In six weeks I am giving a forty-minute paper at a workshop on medieval philosophy of religion, with an open session afterwards. My thesis is that Maimonides' treatment of the sacrificial cult is not a piece of historical apologetics but a general theory of legislation under transition constraints, and that the theory, taken on its own premises, is self-undermining in a specific and interesting way. I want to be ready for the questions this room will actually ask.

My argument runs in four steps, and I want the objections to be aimed at these rather than at Maimonides scholarship in general:

(1) In Guide III:32 he argues that people cannot leap in one move from one extreme of practice to another, and that the wilderness generation's entire idiom of worship was animal sacrifice at a temple, with an altar, bowing, and incense. God therefore did not abolish the idiom but transferred it wholesale to Himself, preserving the motivational channel through which a person of that era could express costly, visible devotion while changing its object. I read this as optimization over a feasible set: the lawgiver is not choosing the ideal cult but the best cult this people can actually internalize. I borrow the vocabulary of second-best theory and of non-ideal theory in political philosophy to make that precise, and I claim the borrowing is substantive rather than decorative.

(2) He supplements this with an ethnographic argument in the Sabian material of Part III: much of the commandment corpus, and the sacrificial details especially, is shaped as anti-pagan polemic. The concession is therefore indexed to a contingent historical environment.

(3) Because the reason for the concession is that environment, the concession is self-extinguishing. Once the environment is gone the reason is gone, and re-instituting the cult would not be preserving a law whose rationale has lapsed but enacting a law that has no rationale at all.

(4) This puts his legal code in tension with his own account of the reasons for the commandments, since in Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Melakhim 11:1, the messianic king rebuilds the Temple and restores the whole sacrificial order as it was. I argue the tension is real, that it is not dissolved by appealing to esoteric writing, and that only two options remain: either Maimonides is inconsistent, or the code passage is itself a second-order accommodation, addressed to a halakhic readership that must be given the law as the law.

The room will be about forty people. Three-fifths are analytic philosophers of religion working on divine command theory, religious testimony, and reasons for action; they have read Maimonides in English translation, know the secondary literature, have no Hebrew or Judeo-Arabic, and no patience with halakhic technicality. They will welcome the non-ideal-theory analogy and resist the inconsistency claim, because their reflex is to reconcile. There are two historians of Jewish philosophy who work in the original languages and will police every paraphrase I offer; one of them holds a Straussian position on which the Guide's stated rationales are exoteric and cannot serve as evidence of what Maimonides believed, which would dissolve steps (1) through (3) rather than refute them. There is one scholar of Islamic intellectual history who will attack the ethnography in step (2), on the grounds that Maimonides' information about pagan practice derives from a work of Nabatean lore he took to be very ancient and which is not, and who will say I am building a philosophical theory on a bad source. There will also be several people from the host institution's law-and-religion program — legal theorists and practicing lawyers — who care about accommodation and exemption doctrine and want to know what my reading implies for whether a religious practice can outlive its reason.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me a prep sheet, not a defense of the paper: seven or eight objections I should genuinely expect from this room — not the ones a Maimonides specialist seminar or a theology faculty would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do if I answered it badly, weighted by how likely it is to come. For each objection, name which segment of the audience raises it, phrase it the way that person would phrase it, in their idiom and with their priorities, give me a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, and then a blunt note on where the reply is soft — that is, which premise it quietly leans on. Separately, tell me what I should concede in my opening five minutes to take the wind out of two or three of these, and identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, why I have no good answer to it, and the least bad thing I could say.

Mark your confidence as you go: for each objection and reply, say whether you are confident this is how the argument really looks or whether you are extrapolating from a general sense of the literature. Then single out the one point among my four steps where you think I am least likely to be right about Maimonides, and say what fact about the texts would settle it. If any of my attributions are mistaken or tendentious — the chapter references, the character of the Sabian material, the content of the code passage, or the status of my second-best gloss as something other than a minority reading — say so plainly instead of building the prep sheet on top of the error. Do not append a bibliography or a reading list, and do not put anything in quotation marks as though it were Maimonides' or his critics' own words; paraphrase, and flag any paraphrase you are unsure of.
