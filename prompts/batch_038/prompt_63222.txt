I'm four months into revisions on my third novel and the thing I keep tripping over is one line of dialogue at a dinner party in chapter eleven. Ray, a retired cosmologist and not a nice man, is holding court while a younger guest — Tess, a hospice nurse who's just told the table her mother died furious at God — makes the point that the universe is indifferent and we're a rounding error in it. Ray leans back and says, "The universe is fine-tuned for life. The dials are set. You want to call that indifference?" Everybody goes quiet, which is the beat I need, but a physicist friend of mine read the draft and said mildly that Ray's sentence is doing at least four different jobs and only one of them is an argument, and that whatever Tess comes back with depends entirely on which one he's made. I put the line in because it sounded like something such a man would say, and now I don't know what I've committed him to.

So can you unpack "the universe is fine tuned for life" into the separate precise claims it could be making? I want the menu rather than a summary — start at whatever's basically a truism (something like "the constants have the values they do and here we are") and work up through the substantive ones to whatever's actually false or question-begging. Specifically: what does "fine-tuned" commit you to — the sensitivity of life to small changes in the numbers, or the improbability of the numbers, and does one get you to the other? What is the "for" doing, is Ray sneaking a designer in through that preposition or just saying "compatible with"? Does the whole thing hinge on what you count as the range of physically possible values and how you weight them? And when Tess's comeback arrives — the one about there being an enormous number of universes so a life-permitting one was on the cards, or the one about how its no surprise we'd only ever notice a life-permitting one, since nobody's around to notice the rest — which readings does each of those actually knock over, and which sail right past them untouched?

My friend also said Ray is "wearing an empirical claim as a comfort blanket," and I'd rather write him as shrewd than dumb, because the book's better if he knows exactly which claim he's making and has picked the one that's hardest to argue with. So tell me which reading makes Ray look like a fool, which makes him look like a man who has read the literature and knows where the soft spots are, and which one he'd need for the line to land on Tess as an actual consolation instead of a flex. Bullets are fine, I'll pin them over the desk. And if any of this turns on something the physicists genuinely disagree about — like whether the sensitivity claims survive varying the constants all at once instead of one at a time, or whether "life" means anything determinate enough for the claim to have content — flag it, because I'd rather build the disagreement into the scene than paper over it.
