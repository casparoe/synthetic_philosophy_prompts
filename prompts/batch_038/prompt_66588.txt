The cases I care about at minimum: the impairment ones, where you get credible news that your reasoning is compromised by something like a drug or altitude or fatigue while your evidence about the first-order question is unchanged (including the version where the news is misleading and nothing is actually wrong with you); the peer-disagreement ones; the iterated ones, where you get higher-order evidence about your higher-order evidence, and whether the theory has to keep that from running away; and ones where the thing you're told to distrust is a competence you already have a lot of independent reason to trust, like arithmetic or reading a gauge you use every day. The distinctions I want respected: evidence about the world versus evidence about your evidence; actually having a defeater versus merely having reason to think you have one; being rational versus being rational-by-your-own-lights; "I can't tell whether I'm impaired" versus "I'm impaired"; and whatever a theory says has to be consistent with the fact that you can't generally be in a position to know that your own cognitive states are obtaining, so no theory gets to assume you always have transparent access to your own reliability. Also worth saying explicitly which candidate requirements are doing no real work — like demands that a theory explain every intuitive verdict about every case, since half the verdicts are what's contested.
