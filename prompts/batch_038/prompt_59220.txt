I have spent the last two years defending a fairly strong claim inside a working group of otherwise sensible people — actuaries, a couple of risk engineers, one philosopher of science who mostly keeps quiet — namely that coherence is not a convenient idealisation we may adopt for modelling purposes but a genuine requirement of rationality, and that the Dutch book theorems are what establish it. Concretely, the position I hold commits me to four things: that an agent whose degrees of belief cannot be represented by a single probability function is, as a matter of necessity rather than luck, exposed to a package of wagers they would individually accept and that jointly guarantee a loss; that this exposure is not an incidental fact about gambling appetites but reveals a defect in the beliefs themselves, which is why the same theorem is supposed to tell us something normative rather than something about betting dispositions; that the converse holds too, so that coherence buys immunity and thereby earns its claim to be the standard; and that the diachronic variants extend the result to revision, so that an agent who is certain they will update by conditionalising on whatever evidence arrives is again exploitable unless they do. I realise I have been treating the betting setup as a harmless measuring device, like a thermometer, and I suspect that is where the position is most vulnerable, but I would rather be told precisely than left to guess.

What I want is a map of the view's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, sorted by how decisive each item would be — fatal, seriously damaging, merely awkward — rather than presented as a flat list of objections. For each candidate defeater, say what the world or the formal landscape would have to look like, whether it is live or only logically possible, and, importantly, whether a committed defender of the position would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it as changing the subject; I am much more interested in the concessions the view's own proponents should make than in what its critics say. I would also like the mirror-image question answered honestly: what does the coherence view predict or explain that its rivals — views on which incoherence is permissible in some cases, or on which rational belief is simply not point-valued — fail to explain, and is there any observation that would favour the coherence view over a rival, or is the dispute purely a matter of which idealisation we prefer?

Please work through, in particular, four situations I have been unable to resolve with my colleagues, and tell me for each whether it is a genuine counterexample, a limitation that the view can absorb, or no threat at all. First: an agent who is flagrantly incoherent — say, degrees of belief that blatantly violate the probability axioms across a handful of propositions nobody else cares about — but who has no disposition to accept wagers, or who lives in an environment where no bookie ever appears; is the notion of immunity-in-principle doing all the work here, and does it still do normative work? Second: an agent whose beliefs are deliberately imprecise, represented by a set of probability functions rather than one, and who cannot be booked because their acceptance of a bet depends on how it interacts with the others they hold; if such an agent is immune, does coherence lose its exclusive claim to be the standard, or is this agent covertly coherent after all? Third: a bookie who knows far more than the agent does, and who exploits the agent precisely because the agent's beliefs are coherent, stable, and legible — does this show the connection between coherence and immunity holds only under informational parity, and how much of the argument survives that restriction? Fourth: a coherent agent who is nevertheless reliably money-pumped over time because a predictor can anticipate how their evidence will arrive. Finally, and I want a straight answer rather than a diplomatic one: has the position been insulated by qualification — retreat from what agents will actually accept to what they would accept under idealised conditions, from single bets to packages, from actual to merely possible bookies — and if it has, is that insulation a defect given that the view was supposed to be a substantive claim about rationality, or is it simply what a claim of that kind must look like? If the honest verdict is that the view risks very little and survives almost everything, say so and explain why it is still worth holding; if the honest verdict is that one or two of these situations are genuinely fatal, say that too.
