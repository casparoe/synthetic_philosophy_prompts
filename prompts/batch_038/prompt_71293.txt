Three weeks out from my quals oral and the committee wants a one page list of what my project actually commits me to, so they can come at it. The project is roughly "what complexity theory does and doesn't show about prediction and explanation in a deterministic world." Here's the draft list. I'd rather you tell me which of these can't all be true than reassure me, and I'm deliberately not saying which ones I'd be willing to drop — that's supposed to be your job.

1. Determinism is an ontological thesis: the complete microphysical state at a time plus the laws fixes the state at every other time. I'm a physicalist about that, and I don't think anything below threatens it.
2. But there's no Laplace demon. I think "computable in principle" does dishonest work in a lot of this literature, and that some systems have futures that are fixed yet not predictable by anything that could physically exist. I want this to be an objective, world-side fact, not a report on the limitations of human heads.
3. Separately, I take there to be a diagonal argument that no device inside the universe can be an infallible universal predictor whatever its resources: wire in a switch that inverts its own output and you're done. I treat this as independent of complexity theory entirely.
4. Quantum computers will be built and they kill the extended Church–Turing thesis — not Church–Turing proper, I'm careful about that distinction. I'd put 95% on one-way functions existing; honestly that feels like the least controversial item on the whole list.
5. P ≠ NP, and necessary in the strong sense: there's no possible world, under any laws, whose computers crack NP-complete problems in polynomial time.
6. And I expect the eventual proof to go via circuit lower bounds in the standard, clean way: name a property of truth tables that you can check quickly and that most functions have, then show no polynomial-size circuit for an NP-hard function has it.
7. The case I lean on in the chapter: a driven double pendulum on a lab rig, chaotic, so that predicting where the arm is 60 seconds out is intractible in the complexity-theoretic sense even though the trajectory is fully determined. Same case, second half: the complete microphysical description of the rig at t0 "contains" the answer, and I want to say that description just isn't an explanation of it — full stop, not "isn't an explanation for creatures like us."
8. None of this gets you the old Gödel-sentence argument against mechanism. Incompleteness is the wrong tool there and intractability is the right one, and I don't think mathematical insight outruns what an algorithm can do.

Audit it. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, say explicitly which pairings only look like tensions and why they dissolve, and for each real one give me the cheapest revision plus what that revision costs me elsewhere on the list. Don't flag everything to be safe — I need to know which of these claims is load-bearing. Then, at the end, tell me what would change your mind about your own ranking: what consideration would make you re-order these, and which of your diagnoses you think is most likely to be wrong — including whether I've mis-stated one of the technical bits in a way that quietly makes a tension disappear.
