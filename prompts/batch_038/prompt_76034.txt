I run a little self-study reading group (three of us, one's a postdoc in neuroscience) and last month we got stuck on epiphenomenalism, specifically the thing Chalmers calls the paradox of phenomenal judgment. My summary of it, tell me if I've mangled it: if experience really has no causal effects, then my typing out "the afterimage looks greenish-blue to me" was caused entirely by neurons, none of which were themselves caused by the experience. So either the physical machinery would have spat out the same words with no experience present at all — in which case it's a standing miracle that my reports keep coming out right — or the reports aren't actually right. And unlike most debunkings, the epiphenomenalist can't grab the second horn, since the whole case for taking experience seriously in the first place leans on how trustworthy those reports seem.

Then one of us said "wait, that's literally Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma with qualia swapped in for values" — evolution shaped our evaluative judgments for reproductive usefulness rather than truth, so a realist who insists the moral facts played no part in shaping them is left with the same inexplicable-correlation problem, and in both cases the defender's move is to point at correlation instead of causation: something else (neural states / fitness-relevant third factors) does the tracking. I've started calling that reply borrowed tracking, because the view keeps accurate reports by leaning on extra machinery that isn't the facts in question — for Chalmers, the psychophysical laws pairing experiences with brain states. So are these actually the same argument form, or is the resemblance superficial? My suspicion is there's a real asymmetry hiding underneath. The value realist's opponent is entitled to conclude "so our moral judgments are probably way off" — that's the debunking upshot, no self-refutation involved. But the epiphenomenalist is not entitled to conclude "so my reports about my own experience are probably way off," because that saws off the branch. There's also a granularity difference: I can apparently report a distinction inside a color gradient I was never trained on, which feels like far too much accuracy to be luck, whereas nobody thinks our moral intuitions are accurate at anything like that resolution. And borrowed tracking looks available in one case but not the other — Chalmers can invoke laws that reliably hook experience up to neural states, but what is the value realist's equivalent? There's no moral-psychophysical law, and if they posit a faculty that tracks moral truth they're back to saying the facts shaped the judgments, which is precisely what the evolutionary story denies.

So: compare the two properly. Lay out whatever structure they genuinely share, then say exactly where the analogy breaks, and give me a verdict on which is the more damaging problem for its target, with reasons — I don't want a hedge. I'd especially like you to push on the borrowed tracking point: does invoking psychophysical laws actually dissolve the paradox of phenomenal judgmenmt, or does it just relocate the coincidence one level up, since we still have to ask why the laws would be arranged so that the experiences they produce line up with the reports a causally closed brain produces, when the experiences do nothing at all? And spell out whether the same maneuver is open to the value realist, and if not, what that tells us about whether the two arguments belong in the same family. Please skip the general tours of either debate — I've read the intros, it's the structural comparison I'm after.
