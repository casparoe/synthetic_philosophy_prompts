Retired three years ago after forty years of experimental physics — mostly beamline work, measuring things until they stopped being ambiguous. Since then I've been reading philosophy properly, slowly, and rather badly, and I've landed on Anscombe. Its one episode I want to run past you; I'm after an analysis, not a summary.

The episode: in 1956 Oxford proposed an honorary doctorate for Harry Truman. Anscombe opposed it, in person and in a privately printed pamphlet called Mr. Truman's Degree. Her ground, roughly: Truman had chosen to kill the innocent as a means to his ends, and choosing that is always murder, so the honour was unfitting and conferring it implicated the university. She lost, and the degree went ahead.

Facts I'll supply so you don't have to guess: the two bombs killed on the order of two hundred thousand people between them, mostly noncombatants; in a radio address a few days after Hiroshima, Truman described the city as a military base and said the aim had been to avoid killing civilians so far as possible; and the firebombing of Tokyo in March 1945 had already killed something like a hundred thousand people in a single night without any unusual weapon being involved.

I want the analysis to run both directions — the theory on the case, and the case back on the theory.

1. Through her lens, what exactly does the honorific get wrong? She herself points out that an honorary degree isn't a reward for merit but a mark of being distinguished. So is the vote even the sort of act that can carry the moral charge she loads onto it? Treat the degree like an instrument: what is it calibrated to register, and is her complaint that the instrument is miscalibrated, or that its reading is being used for something it was never designed to do?

2. The signature. One objection she takes seriously is that Truman killed nobody, he merely signed a piece of paper. I've had my name on detector papers with several thousand authors where my own contribution was approving a budget line, so I'm hardly innocent here. Does her account of intention and of how an action gets described give a principled way to locate Truman on that chain — or does it simply assume that the intention lives at the top?

3. The control case. If the prohibition is absolute, Tokyo in March 1945 ought to look like Hiroshima in August, arguably worse, since no new instrument was required and the deaths were just as intended as a means to ending the war. Yet the moral attention went overwhelmingly to the atom bombs. Does her theory explain that, predict it, or is it embarrassed by it? And is there any room in the framework for the thought that Truman's real fault was less the killing than the fact that he never seems to have thought of it as murder at all?

4. What the episode does to the theory. She lost the vote, and as far as I can tell lost the wider argument for decades afterwards. Does that count against the view, or does it show the view was aimed at something a vote can't settle — a missing piece of moral vocabulary rather than a disputed conclusion?

Be honest about where the theory simply has nothing to say. I'd much rather you point at the gaps than fill them with plausible noise. And no bibliography please, I'm not going to chase refrences and I know you can't either.
