I have been going back to the two-envelope paradox on and off for fifteen years, mostly in comment threads rather than in journals, and I have come to think that the interesting thing about it is not the paradox but a shape that recurs throughout the arguments about it — and in a number of unrelated places — for which I do not have a name.

Here is the shape as I see it. Someone writes a probability statement that is perfectly correct relative to one state of information. Some lines later, a second probability statement appears, written in exactly the same words, that is correct relative to a different state of information — different because something has been learned in between, or because a choice has been made, or because the question has shifted. Nothing in the notation marks the difference: same symbol, same sentence, and the derivation reads as valid. Once you pin down which state of information each statement belongs to, the two come apart and the puzzle evaporates. The error was never an arithmetical one.

The two-envelope case is the cleanest instance I know. The switcher's calculation rests on a line to the effect that 'the other envelope is as likely to contain twice what is in mine as half'. Relative to someone who has opened nothing, that can be true. Relative to someone who has opened one envelope and seen a definite amount, its truth depends entirely on the distribution over pairs of amounts — for many such distributions it is false, and for some it is false whatever number turns up. The argument goes through only because the same string of words has been doing duty for two different quantities.

I keep meeting the same defect elsewhere. In the Monty Hall problem, a large share of the wrong answers seem to come from writing a probability for where the car is with no mention anywhere of which door the host opened. In forecast evaluation, a pool of predictions can look badly calibrated purely because it mixes numbers formed before some item of news with numbers formed after it, when each was respectable relative to its own information. And I suspect something of the kind is at work in at least part of the argument over the sleeping beauty problem, where a credence formed before the experiment begins and a credence formed on waking are both reported as 'the probability of heads' — though I grant that last one is contested and you may want to strike it.

So: does this have a name? Not a name for the two-envelope paradox, and not the general thesis that probability is relative to a body of evidence, which I take for granted. I mean the specific defect — an argument that fails only because a probability is stated without the information state it is indexed to, so that two individually correct quantities get silently treated as one.

Several things sit nearby, and I want them separated rather than listed: plain equivocation, where a word changes sense mid-argument; the prosecutor's fallacy and base-rate neglect, where the direction of conditioning is transposed; Bertrand's chord problem, where what the setup omits is a method of randomization rather than an information state; and the general difficulty of settling on a reference class. Which of these, if any, actually covers what I have described? If none does, coin a term. I would rather have a usable name with a definition and two or three boundary cases than no name at all, but the label is not the point — I want the phenomenon carved sharply enough that I could recognize a fresh instance of it in the wild, and I want to be told which of my examples you would remove from the list and why.

Two constraints on the answer. I have no way of checking anything, so please give me no reading list, no citations, and no attributions of positions to named authors unless you are confident they are right; where you think a term exists in the literature but are not certain, flag that uncertainty explicitly rather than presenting it as settled. And tell me what would change your mind: what consideration would persuade you that this is not one phenomenon but a loose bundle of unrelated errors, or that one of the nearby concepts does after all cover it — and where in your own carving you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
