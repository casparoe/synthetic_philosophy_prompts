I chair the council of a learned society of some four thousand members. Last September, nine days after passages from a laureate's undergraduate writings of thirty years ago began circulating online, the council voted 7–4 to rescind the society's principal book prize, awarded to her eleven months earlier, and to call in the medal. She had not been invited to respond, and no member proposed that she be.

A motion to reinstate now stands for the meeting in nine days. Our standing orders permit a decision to be revisited within the same calendar year only on the basis of material facts not before the council when the original decision was taken. Three of the seven have switched, for reasons that are not the same reason. One says the volume of member correspondence made it impossible to judge calmly in September and that he can judge calmly now. One says she has since read the writings complete and in context and weighs them differently, though nothing new has come to light. One has received a written opinion from the society's solicitor, which nobody had thought to obtain before, advising that the September procedure leaves us exposed to a challenge we would more likely than not lose. In the interval the laureate has published a statement declining to apologize for work she no longer endorses but will not disown, and two fellows have said they will resign whichever way we vote.

A colleague has circulated a note invoking Thucydides' second debate over Mytilene, urging that the Athenians reversed themselves the following morning with no new facts and were right to do so; a second colleague has replied that Cleon's case against a body that changes its mind under pressure describes our situation exactly.

Draft the memorandum I will circulate to the council in advance. It should recommend one course — reinstate, uphold, or a third option you judge better — and set out how the meeting ought to proceed, including whether to decide that day or defer, and on what timetable if we defer. It should take the standing-orders question head-on rather than voting around it, stating which of the three switches, if any, meets the test and why the other two do or do not. Give the case for holding fast its full weight: that a council which reverses whenever the temperature drops teaches its members that its decisions belong to whoever campaigns louder, and that reinstating now may read as a second capitulation rather than a correction. And tell me, at the end, whether the Mytilenean parallel does any work here beyond decoration — what in the Athenian reversal genuinely maps onto ours and what does not.
