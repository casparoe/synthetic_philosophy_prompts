Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning, beginning from what should have been a trivial transaction, and I would like an outside assessment of the exchange — partly to learn what I should do next, and partly because I can no longer tell whether either of us ever made a real point.

Dev and I each hold 500 shares in a private company we both used to work for. The last funding round put them at roughly $30 a share, though nothing has actually changed hands at that price in two years. Dev worked on the finance side before he left; I was on the product side and have not been inside the company for eight months. A fortnight ago he emailed offering to buy my 500 at $40 a share, unconditionally, cash within a week. I declined. My reasoning, which I did not give him at the time, was that he sits far closer to the books than I do, and that a man who volunteers a third above the last round price probably knows the shares are worth considerably more than $40.

When I finally said this aloud, his first response was that my reasoning proves too much. If a willingness to buy is always evidence that the buyer knows something the seller does not, then no one could ever sell to anyone better informed; and by the same logic no one could ever make an offer of any kind, since every offer would be received as a confession. He said I had reasoned my way into a rule under which voluntary exchange is impossible, and that the actual reason I refused was that I did not want to be the one who came out worse.

His second claim was that his reasons had nothing to do with what the company is worth. He is forty-one, owns his home outright, and will not need cash for a decade; I am twenty-nine and may want a deposit next year. A stake he can hold indefinitely without inconvenience is worth more to him than the identical stake is worth to me, he said, and he would happily pay $40 for something worth $60 in his hands. I objected that $60 is a claim about the company's prospects wearing a costume: if he really appraises the shares at $60, then my original inference was sound and he has just confirmed it out of his own mouth. He answered that the $60 is not a forecast of any exit price but a statement about what he can afford to wait for, and that the same shares are worth perhaps $35 to me once my timetable is factored in — so we can both be perfectly correct about the company and still both gain from the swap.

I said this was an escape hatch: any offer and any refusal can be retrofitted with an innocent motive, so my inference could never be tested and his account could never be falsified. He said then test it, and offered to sell me a further 500 shares at the same $40 — same price, both directions, take either side or neither. I said that a man willing to sell at $40 cannot believe the shares are worth much above $40, which empties his original offer of the meaning I had given it and therefore refutes me. He said no: it only shows he is not betting in either direction, and that someone quoting one price both ways is still the one who gets hurt when the person across from him knows more than he does. I did not follow that, and I suspect he had not either.

I then mirrored him and offered to buy *his* 500 at $40. He refused, explaining that he wanted to stay above the holding threshold at which the company sends him financials, and that halving his stake would cost him that. I pointed out that this is structurally the same kind of explanation I had just attacked, and that he was using the escape hatch within thirty seconds of denying it existed. He said the difference was that his motive was checkable against the shareholders' agreement and mine was not.

He also mentioned — I still cannot decide whether this was scrupulous or tactical — that he had made the identical $40 offer to the three other former colleagues who hold shares. Owen accepted at once; Owen was a designer who never opened a financial email in his life. Nadia accepted as well; Nadia worked in finance alongside Dev and is getting married in June. Priya has not replied.

What I would like from you:

1. Was I wrong to decline, and does his "proves too much" charge genuinely defeat my reasoning, or does that charge itself prove too much?
2. Does quoting one price in both directions really cancel the inference I drew from his offer, or is there something substantive in his remark about still being the one who loses when the other party knows more?
3. Does his refusal to sell to me at $40 restore my original reasoning, given that the excuse he offered is of exactly the type I had just called unfalsifiable — and is "checkable against the agreement" a real distinction?
4. Do Owen's and Nadia's acceptances carry any weight for me, and does their weight depend on facts about them that I cannot verify?
5. Is there a workable rule for deciding when an offer from someone better placed to know should move my estimate of what a thing is worth, or is every such offer always explicable innocently — in which case two hours of our argument was about nothing?
6. Were Dev and I even disagreeing, or were we using "worth" in two incompatible senses the whole time?
7. Concretely: should I now accept, counter, or let the matter drop, and what would I need to learn first to decide?
