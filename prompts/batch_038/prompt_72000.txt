I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting turns on a decision theorist who defends risk-weighted expected utility: the view that an agent's probabilities enter a gamble not at face value but through a risk function applied to the act as a whole, so that the weight attaching to an outcome depends on where it falls in the ranking of outcomes that act produces. Once per manuscript I record a single uncut conversation with such a theorist, purely to keep my ear honest, and mine is coming up in three weeks. She is not a public figure and will not appear under her own name, but she is a serious and fluent defender of the position.

Draft eight to ten questions I can put to her, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want them to bite rather than to invite a survey. At minimum they should cover: whether applying the risk function globally is a principled commitment or a device for evading the dynamic-choice objections — the money pumps and diachronic Dutch books — and what her agent must do about sticking to a plan once each local step has become attractive; whether the theory is genuinely novel or a redescription of rank-dependent utility in decision-theoretic costume; whether Rabin's calibration result, so often cited as the motive for abandoning expected utility, is escaped or merely pushed from small stakes to large ones; whether the theory is sensitive to how finely an agent carves up her own state space, given that it is permitted to violate eventwise dominance and the sure-thing principle; and what it says about a risk attitude the agent cannot endorse on reflection, or about wielding someone else's risk function in a decision made on their behalf.

Two conditions. Keep each question short enough to be spoken aloud and hard enough that a deflection would be visible; and give me nothing to look up — no citations, no bibliography, no titles — because I will bring no books into that room and neither will she.

Then tell me how confident you are that these are the places she would genuinely feel pressure, and single out the one question whose characterization of the view you are least sure of. A mischaracterization would cost me a scene I have already written.
