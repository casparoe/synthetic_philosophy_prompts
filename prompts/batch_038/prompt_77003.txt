I am a third-year philosophy PhD student (background: an undergraduate degree in computer science, and I have spent the last two years informally auditing a machine-learning reading group) applying for my department's interdisciplinary seed grant, which funds four months of protected research time and requires a proposal of roughly 1,100 words plus a one-page work plan. The panel is two philosophers and one ML researcher who has never taken a philosophy course, so the question has to be intelligible to that third reader without being watered down. I would like you to write the proposal, and the half-formed idea I want sharpened is this. Both the idealized-desire tradition in theories of well-being and the coherent extrapolated volition family of proposals in AI alignment assume that there is a single thing, the idealized version of an agent's actual preferences, which the theory then consults. But the idealizations being bundled together are of at least two distinct kinds: epistemic ones (more information, fewer false beliefs, better instrumental reasoning, no cognitive bias) and character ones (more impartial, more sympathetic, less akratic, better able to regulate emotion). Standard treatments treat these as components of one package, and the packages are usually described as limits — take the process far enough and you get the fully idealized agent. My suspicion is that the two operators do not commute, and that the endpoint is genuinely path-dependent, because information-seeking is itself value-guided: which facts an agent acquires, and which reasoning habits she cultivates, are selected by what she already cares about, so idealizing cognition first lets a defective set of values shape the evidence base that the later character idealization has to work with. Consider the person nursing a grievance against a former business partner: informed first, she learns enough about the betrayal and about his vulnerabilities to make her resentment coherent, precise, and actionable, and the character idealization then has to undo a well-supported structure; made impartial first, she acquires a quite different body of information and arrives somewhere else. Or the procrastinating would-be pianist: disciplined first, she practices long enough to learn her real ceiling and gives music up for good reasons; informed first, the knowledge of that ceiling lands on an undisciplined temperament and hardens into a defeatist settled preference that survives the later acquisition of discipline. If this is right, then anyone who extrapolates volitions — a theory of well-being, or a system designed to act on humanity's behalf — has to choose an extrapolation path, and that choice is a substantive normative decision that currently gets smuggled in as an unexamined technical parameter. There is also a bootstrapping worry I have not solved: the standard used to pick the path is applied from the un-idealized standpoint, and a friend who is a decision theorist tells me the whole thing is a pseudo-problem because any sensible idealization is a single limit and limits commute.
