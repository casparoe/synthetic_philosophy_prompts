I'm in a reading group on philosophy of AI and last week a guy who works on self-modifying systems gave a talk that leaned hard on Hofstadter's Typogenetics — the GEB toy where a strand is just a string over four bases, pairs of letters get read off through the typogenetic code into command-like "amino acids," those fold into an enzyme, and enzymes then cut, delete from, or copy strands. His claim was that this code, which turns a strand into the very machinery that operates on strands, is basically Gödel numbering; that a self-replicating strand is basically a Gödel sentence; and so that *any* system able to build a copy of itself from a description of itself inherits the incompleteness result — a self-rewriting agent can always "state" things about itself that it can't settle from inside. From which he concluded you can never get a formal guarantee about such an agent, so verification-style safety is dead on arrival and everyone should go straight to interpretability.

Half the room pushed back, tho nobody could say cleanly why. Can you audit the analogy for me? Give me the correspondence table explicitly — bases, duples, enzymes, the code, whoever plays the ribosome, copy mode, the complementary-strand twist — against whatever each is supposed to be on the logic side. Then seperate the disanalogies into the ones that actually sink his inference and the ones that are just decorative. I especially want: what does real arithmetization require that a strand-chopping system flatly lacks; is the self-reference in a self-replicating strand the same species of thing as diagonalization; and would his no-guarantees conclusion survive if it were rebuilt on a different theorem instead (Rice's, the halting problem, something about self-description)? Also tell me what you'd have to bolt onto Typogenetics to make his claim actually true, and flag anything I've misdescribed about the system itself. If there's a better analogy for what he wants to claim, name it and say what it costs.
