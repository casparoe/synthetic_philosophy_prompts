I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level epistemology seminar — 14 students, all of whom have had at least one course with Bayes in it and most of whom are pretty smug about it. I want an in-class betting game that walks them into the problem of logical omniscience without me saying the phrase once until the debrief.

The thing I want them to feel: the coherence norms they think they've already bought — no Dutch books, credence 1 in anything logically true, credences that line up with what they entail — are norms that no finite thinker can actually meet, and that this isn't a matter of just trying harder. It's built into what a credence function is supposed to be.

So design the game for me. I need rules, materials that fit on one printable sheet, a payoff scheme using points or candy or something (no real money, and it has to actually feel like losing something), and a timeline that fits 75 minutes. My instinct is a market where each student posts prices on a set of sentences and I, as market-maker, go hunting for combinations of their own posted prices that lose chips no matter how the world turns out — with some of the sentences secretly equivalent, some secretly entailing one another, and at least one long disguised tautology in the pile. But I'm not married to that, use whatever mechanism genuinely works. What I do insist on: the logical relations shouldn't be visible on the surface; checking them has to cost the students something real (time, tokens, a turn, whatever); there has to be plainly not enough of that resource to check them all; and nobody can escape by confuring with a neighbor or the whole thing turns into a group project. It would also be good if I get arbitraged at some point too.

Then a debrief that maps the actual moments onto the pieces of the concept — the first student who gets book-beaten, the tautology everyone underpriced, the one kid who blew their whole budget computing — and pushes them to the question of what a norm of rationality is worth if only an idealized agent can satisfy it. I'd like them to run into the awkward consequence as well: if you weaken the norm to whatever the agent can actually compute, what exactly have you given up, and can you still call anyone irrational?

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I'm mostly worried about the math-y student who quietly works out every relation and then finds the game trivial, and about my own bookkeeping — constructing the winning combinations on the fly in front of 14 people sounds like a nightmare, so build in whatever prep or lookup table I need.

And be straight with me about which feature of the problem the game can't reproduce, so I know what I'll have to just tell them at the end.
