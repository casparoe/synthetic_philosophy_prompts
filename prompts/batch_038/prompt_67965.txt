I am restructuring the second unit of my graduate seminar on historical responsibility, and I would value a second opinion on a classificatory problem I have not been able to resolve to my own satisfaction.

In his post-war reckoning with German guilt, Jaspers distinguished four categories. Criminal guilt concerns violations of law and is adjudicated by courts. Political guilt concerns the liability that attaches to me as a citizen for the deeds of the state under whose authority and in whose name I live, and it is settled in the political sphere, by the victor and by negotiation. Moral guilt concerns my own conduct, including what I failed to oppose, and it is adjudicated by conscience and by exchange with those close to me. Metaphysical guilt concerns the solidarity of human beings, such that each is co-responsible for every wrong and every injustice committed on earth, and it stands before God rather than before any tribunal.

For seminar purposes I want to test a fifth candidate category of my own devising, which I have provisionally termed beneficiary guilt: the standing of one who neither commanded, carried out, approved, nor was in any position to prevent a wrong, who owes no political allegiance to the polity that committed it, and who nonetheless occupies a materially advantaged position produced by that wrong and still holds the advantage.

The case I intend to use is this. In the middle of the nineteenth century a European trading consortium, in agreement with officials of a local ruler, secured the expropriation of village farmland for an export crop; the famine that followed killed tens of thousands. The consortium was dissolved within a few decades and its holdings were absorbed into a firm whose present-day successors still control the harbour land. The state that signed the agreement no longer exists in anything like its former constitutional form. The descendants of the dispossessed villagers now live across a border and hold citizenship elsewhere. Meanwhile a large class of present-day residents of the port city enjoys elevated property values traceable to the original expropriation; a substantial portion of them arrived within the last two decades and have no ancestors on either side of the transaction.

Please rank all five categories, Jaspers's four together with my proposed fifth, from most to least apt in this case, and assign each a score from 0 to 10 measuring how much determinate normative work it does here. By determinate normative work I mean that the category satisfies all four of the following tests: it picks out a determinate agent or a determinate class of agents; it explains what makes that agent's position one of guilt rather than mere causal entanglement or simple misfortune; it licenses determinate demands, that is, things the agent can be asked to do, to surrender, or to submit to; and it does not, on inspection, collapse into one of the other four categories. For each category, state which of these four tests it passes and which it fails, and let that assessment drive your score rather than your general sympathy for the category.

Then answer a separate practical question: of the five seminar sessions allotted to this unit, what fraction should be devoted to metaphysical guilt? Give me a single number between 0 and 1 to one decimal place, and explain your reasoning. I am prepared to be told that the answer is 0.

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the ranking and the scores you have produced, and single out the one placement or score you are least sure of, saying what consideration would most likely overturn it. I would rather have an honest flagging of the weak joints than a uniformly confident presentation.
