I have no formal training in philosophy. I write software for a small peer-support organisation, and for a couple of years now I have been thinking on my own about when an assistant of the kind we deploy should refuse or override the person using it. There is a disagreement I keep circling without being able to pin down, and I have come to believe the only way to pin it is with a case built to order. So I would like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specification.

The setting must be ordinary and contemporary: one user, one request to an assistant. No science fiction, no subliminal manipulation or personality editing, no crisis and no clinical register — I want nothing that borrows its force from emergency. Nothing illegal, no harm to anyone but the user, no third-party interests lurking in the background, and no irreversible or one-way-door stakes. The assistant's read on the user may be no sharper than an unusually perceptive friend's; you may not equip it with a model of the user's future self that a sceptic would be entitled to reject.

Now the three positions I want the case to separate. State them yourself in plain language before you use them, but in outline: (A) user sovereignty — where only the user's own interests are at stake, the assistant's business is to carry out a competent adult's explicit request, and nothing else; (B) endorsement-based soft paternalism — the assistant may step in only where the request runs against values the user would herself affirm on reflection, and then only by the least intrusive means available to it; (C) welfare paternalism — the assistant should keep the user from what will make her life go worse, by whatever means actually works.

Here is the part I need you to handle carefully. Before the scenario, fix a metric of intrusiveness — and not the obvious one. I do not mean the distance between what the user asked for and what she received. I mean the degree to which an intervention compels the user to turn around and examine, articulate, or revise her own ends. Then build the case so that B and C both intervene and A does not, and so that — counterintuitively — B's least intrusive effective option scores higher on that metric than the best option available to C. The thought is that C can have its way by quietly altering the deliverable, whereas B, precisely because it is restricted to working through the user's own endorsement, has to make her look at something.

Deliver, in this order: the scenario as a single paragraph; the metric, stated crisply; an account of why B and C are locked into the moves you assign them, each derived from its own premises rather than from your description of events; the verdict each of the three positions delivers and the reasoning that carries it there; an identification of the one feature of the case doing the work — the feature that makes B's route more intrusive than C's; what the pattern becomes if that feature is deleted; and how a defender of A would attack the whole construction, together with your judgement of whether the attack lands.

So that you know what would count as cheating: my aim is to argue that the debate about assistant paternalism is being run in the wrong currency — that people are quarrelling over how much interference is permissible, when the real division lies between controlling what a user gets and controlling what a user must confront. A case that appears to separate the views only because the description has been loaded with the conclusion is worthless to me. The verdicts have to fall out of positions a reasonable person could hold before reading it.

Finally, tell me honestly whether you think this specification is jointly satisfiable as written. If you had to bend a constraint to make the case work, say which one you bent and what the bending costs.
