I'm in a medieval philosophy seminar and I've been put down to lead the discussion on Avicenna's flying man argument, and honestly I'm stuck on the passage itself rather than the background. Here's the bit I have to work with — an English translation of the floating/flying man passage from his psychology (it's in al-Shifa and shows up again in shorter form in al-Najat):

> One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence.

I don't want a biography of Ibn Sina or a history of the arguement, and please don't give me citations, page numbers or a reading list — I can't check any of it, so I'd much rather you flag where you're unsure than gesture at a source. What I need is help with these five things, in plain prose:

1. Lay it out as an argument. What's a premise, what's the conclusion, and where's the load-bearing gap? My instinct is that the gap sits between "he can conceive of himself without conceiving any bodily part" and "so he isn't a body" — that looks like a conceivability-to-separability move and I can't find the bridge principle anywhere in the passage. Am I right, or am I reading a Cartesian-style modal claim into it?

2. "not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth" — two different conclusions seem to be in play here: (i) the self is not spatially extended, and (ii) the self is not identical to the body. Those aren't the same thing. You could hold that the soul is the body's form: not extended, but not a separate substance either. Which one does this passage actually establish, if either?

3. Why all the stipulations about the limbs being seperated and out of contact with each other? If they're only there to block the concept of extension, then is the argument really about the self at all, or is it about extension? And what work is that last sentence doing — imagining a hand and not imagining it as part of himself or a condition of his existence? Which objection is it preempting?

4. This is the part that genuinely confuses me. He's created at a stroke with zero sensory input, ever. But on Avicenna's own account concepts get abstracted from images delivered by the internal senses, so where does the flying man get a concept of a self to affirm anything with? Is self-awareness supposed to be the one exception to his empiricism — immediate, not through any form or organ? And if it is an exception, what does that cost the rest of the psychology?

5. My tutor keeps calling it a proto-cogito and I want to push back but I don't have the ammunition. Give me the two or three places where the Descartes comparison actually breaks down, and be specific about the difference between systematically doubting everything and merely being cut off from sensation.

One more thing: a friend says the whole argument is circular, because the setup already grants that the guy is self-aware, so the conclusion is smuggled into the stipulation. Is that fair, or does the stipulation only cover sensory access? And relatedly, how much weight can "affirm" bear here — does it have to be a judgement he forms, or is it closer to his just being aware of himself? I suspect a lot hangs on that translation choice and I can't tell which way it cuts.
