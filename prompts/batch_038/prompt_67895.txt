Our little translation group ran the Marot exercise last month — you know the one, that 28-line scrap by Clément Marot that Hofstadter built a whole book around: three syllables a line, nothing but rhyming couplets, the stress always on the last syllable, the formal vous sliding into the familiar tu halfway down, the first line coming back verbatim as the last line, and the poet shoving his own first name into the middle of it. Everyone had to hand in a version. Two of the regulars, Marion and Kwame, got into a genuine arguement over Marion's, and I honestly couldn't tell who was landing blows. Can you referee it?

(1) Kwame opened by saying her version wasn't a translation of the poem but a new poem wearing its clothes. To make a rhyme land inside three syllables she'd swapped Marot's teasing threat — the warning about staying in bed eating preserves till she goes pale and loses her figure — for something about losing her ear for music. He said if you're free to change what the poem says, then the honest credit line is "after Marot," a variation, not a translation.

(2) Marion said by that standard the only translation of a poem is a crib. Hofstadter prints a plain unrhymed gloss of his own, sure, but as scaffolding, not as the poem; drop the chain of three-syllable couplets and you've carried the message across while leaving the artifact behind. Hers keeps the thing that makes it a poem, so on her accounting its more faithful, not less.

(3) Kwame said fine — then faithfulness gets scored constraint by constraint, and she doesn't get the top score either. He pointed out she'd dropped the sneakiest one: in the original the units of sense run out of phase with the rhyme pairs, so every unit of meaning straddles two different rhymes instead of closing neatly with one. Nobody reading her English can hear that, and she'd traded it away so her own couplets would snap shut.

(4) Marion said a constraint no reader can perceive isnt part of the poem's effect, it's part of the poet's process, and she wasnt embarrassed about the trade — no English version honors all the constraints, so translation is triage whether you admit it or not.

(5) Kwame said that's precisely his complaint: if it's triage, she hasn't handed him the translation, she's handed him one weighting out of dozens, and she owes a reason her weighting beats the others. She said the weighting is set by what an English reader can actually hear, and then she played her name card — Marot signs himself in the middle as the guy ordering this woman out of bed, and she'd put her own name in that slot, arguing the constraint is "the poet signs the poem," which for a translator means the translator signs; otherwise the English reader is left wondering who Clément is and why he's giving orders. Kwame called that the worst single substitution in the exercise: it converts a historical signature into the translator's vanity, and a signature is content, not a blank space for whoever happens to hold the pen.

Somewhere in there he also said she should have marked the vous-to-tu drop with you/thou; she said thou reads as more formal and more archaic to a modern ear, so it points backwards, and he half-conceded that but said she could have signalled the loosening other ways — ditching the honorific, moving to a nickname, slackening the syntax — rather than just letting the shift evaporate.

Go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step, and who's more right overall. Then tell me what both of them are missing — I have a feeling they're arguing past each other about what a translation is even supposed to be. And settle one thing for me directly: is Kwame correct that the out-of-phase constraint doesn't matter because no reader can hear it, or is he wrong about its being inaudible?
