I've spent a lot of years in the comment threads of the recurring EA Forum arguments about falling birth rates, and I'm now trying to turn one point from those arguments into a long-form essay. The standard presentation of population ethics to policy-minded readers runs: classical totalism implies the Repugnant Conclusion; average utilitarianism and critical-level utilitarianism are the two stock repairs; both repairs pay for their escape with the Sadistic Conclusion, meaning that each is committed to its being sometimes bad to create people whose lives are worth living. My contention is that this framing hides something far more decision-relevant. The two repairs are not two versions of the same compromise; they are different theories that disagree with each other, and the region in which they disagree is precisely where a state raising pronatalist subsidies, or a foundation deciding whether to fund a large cohort of additional lives, actually operates. To make that argument I need a worked case, and I would rather have one built to specification than the first construction that occurs to me, because the obvious ones all smuggle something in.

So: design a single decision problem — one choice, not a sequence, and please do not reuse Parfit's A / A+ / B / C apparatus or the structure of the Mere Addition Paradox — satisfying all of the following.

- **Setting.** Contemporary and institutional: an actual government or an actual foundation could face it within twenty years. No space colonization, no artificial gestation, no embryo-selection technology beyond what exists, no reliable predictors of the far future.
- **Strictly an addition.** One option leaves an existing population exactly as it is; the other leaves that population exactly as it is and adds a further group of people. In the base case nobody already alive is made better or worse off in any respect they would care about, and there is no leakage through funding: if the programme consumes resources, arrange things so those resources would otherwise have generated no welfare at all — an idle endowment, say — and say explicitly that you have done so.
- **Quality of the added lives.** Every added person has a life that is clearly worth living and clearly better than the average life in the status quo population.
- **Required verdict pattern.** Total utilitarianism says the addition is better; average utilitarianism says the addition is better; critical-level utilitarianism says the addition is worse; a narrow person-affecting view says it is neither better nor worse. The divergence I want is *between the two repairs*, not between totalism and everyone else.
- **No verdict by fiat.** State what you are assuming about the critical level relative both to the status quo average and to the added group's welfare, and argue that this is an assignment a critical-level theorist could genuinely endorse rather than a number picked to make my thesis come out. If you judge the assignment strained, say so in terms; I would much rather learn that now than build the essay on top of it. You may find the ordering easier to motivate if the status quo population is not an affluent one — but if you take that route, tell me what it costs the case in generality.
- **Arithmetic and generality.** Choose explicit cardinal numbers for population sizes and welfare levels and show the computations, but also state, in general form, the inequality each theory's verdict depends on, so I can see that the pattern is not an artifact of your figures.
- **Plain language.** Report verdicts as better, worse, on a par, or incomparable, and do not discharge any step of the reasoning by attaching a name to a paradox.

After the construction, run one stress test and then do the honest accounting. The stress test: introduce a modest, clearly specified benefit to the existing population — the programme also leaves behind infrastructure they use — and report which of the four verdicts move, which do not, and what the pattern reveals about the person-affecting view's initial indifference. Then tell me how the theories that come out badly would attack the case: what a critical-level utilitarian says in reply (in particular whether they can simply deny your ordering of the critical level, and what that denial costs them elsewhere in population ethics), and whether an average utilitarian or a person-affecting theorist would say you have mis-stated their view rather than embarrassed it. Be candid about which single feature of the case is doing the work and what the four verdicts become if that feature is removed.

Please answer in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

1. **Bottom line** (~80 words): whether the specification is satisfiable as written, and the most fragile assumption in the case.
2. **The scenario** (300–350 words): the decision, in prose, with enough institutional texture that a reader believes it.
3. **The parameters and the arithmetic** (~250 words; a table is welcome): population sizes, welfare levels, the critical level, and the four computations.
4. **The general inequalities** (~200 words): for each theory, the condition under which its verdict would flip, in symbols rather than numbers.
5. **Verdicts and derivations** (~450 words): one paragraph per theory, deriving its ranking from the constraints rather than asserting it.
6. **What does the work** (~200 words): the load-bearing feature, and the counterfactual if it is removed.
7. **The stress test** (~250 words): the infrastructure variant.
8. **Objections from the losers** (~300 words).
9. **Reservations** (~150 words): what this case does and does not license me to claim in the essay.
