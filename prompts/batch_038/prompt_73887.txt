I'm a software engineer — I build order-matching and settlement infrastructure for a mid-sized exchange, so my working life consists of trade lifecycles, position limits, and clearing rules. I have no formal philosophy training. What I know of ethics comes from a reading group two colleagues run; we spent one session on moral uncertainty (what you should do when you aren't sure which moral theory is right) and on the related suggestion that people who disagree morally might trade with one another — buying and selling abstentions, permissions, and offsets, roughly the way Coasean bargaining is supposed to resolve disputes over property rights — instead of voting, shouting, or fighting. That idea has not left me alone, mostly because it maps so cleanly onto machinery I already understand, and I suspect the place where the mapping fits is exactly the place where it goes wrong.

For our company's internal "weird ideas" night I'd like to read aloud a piece of satire in the form of an in-universe participant rulebook and help-center FAQ for a fully operational moral trade market. Please write it: roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, in the bloodless register of exchange rulebooks and broker onboarding packets — numbered rules and sub-rules, defined terms introduced in small caps, a short statement of participant obligations, a "frequently asked questions" block, and one or two house-style footnotes. The venue is fictional and everything in it should be invented: call it the Meridian Moral Exchange, clearing through the Impartial Spectator Clearing Corporation, supervised by a regulator you may name. For texture, here are seeds you are welcome to rearrange, extend, or discard. Participants must register their internal parliament on Form MP-1, declaring seats allocated to each moral theory in proportion to current credence, and must mark that allocation to market quarterly. Cross-theory settlements are priced off a Borda-linked reference rate, on the exchange's stated rationale that the theories share no unit of account. Tradable instruments include abstention futures, conviction swaps, moral offset certificates, and deontological credit default swaps written against one's own conscience defaulting. Know Your Conscience onboarding requires source-of-credence documentation. There was an incident, referred to only obliquely and with visible institutional embarrassment, in which one participant accumulated a controlling stake in a rule-consequentialist position and delisted it. And Rule 12(c) voids any trade in which an identifiable non-signatory bears the loss — a rule the exchange's own commentary concedes has never once been satisfied in practice.

The humor should come from the theory rather than from wackiness, and it should reward a reader who knows the moral-uncertainty literature: the difficulty of comparing choice-worthiness across theories that disagree about scale, the gap between revealed credence and sincere credence, whether a moral commitment can be alienated at all, who the victim of a wrong is and why that person is conspicuously absent from every trade ticket, the way wealth rather than moral insight ends up setting the clearing price, and the inevitable comparison to the sale of indulgences, which the exchange's public-relations section should deny with a straight face and in bad faith. Please don't state the objections; let them surface as operational friction — margin calls, litigation reserves, adverse-selection warnings from the market-monitoring desk, an eligibility rule so strict that it quietly excludes most of what anyone wanted to trade. Keep it deadpan and legible: the reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without any nudging from the author. Satirize the practice and the position, never a named living person; every participant, economist, and quoted spokesperson should be invented. Make it readable for a smart person who has never opened a philosophy book — defined terms and in-fiction glosses can carry that load — but do not simplify the ideas themselves, since I would rather it work on two levels. Write in American English, and please do not append a bibliography, reading list, or citations: I want a self-contained performance text, and I have no way to check references in any case.

After the piece, add an author's note of about 200 words in your own voice rather than the exchange's. I'd like it to do two things. First, tell me where the piece takes liberties: which of its premises are exaggerations or strawmen rather than real features of the moral-trade proposal, and where it leans on a contested reading of the moral-uncertainty material — I'll be reading this to two people who know far more philosophy than I do, and I want to know in advance which lines they will wince at. Second, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you think the market-based approach to moral disagreement is right, or at least considerably better than I am implying, and where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail — for example, if the satire only works because you have assumed the market version collapses in places where a non-market version of the same idea would survive intact.
