A proposal now before the System Board of the national health service on which I serve as an independent lay member would change how we allocate elective surgical capacity across our fourteen regional trusts. The Board votes on Thursday, it has been deadlocked in all but name for four months, and I have undertaken to circulate a written opinion in advance of the meeting. I would like you to draft it for me.

The current arrangement. Theatre hours, critical-care beds, and the staff time attached to them are distributed by bilateral negotiation among the trusts, priced according to an internal tariff that was set administratively eleven years ago and adjusted only sporadically since. Nobody on the Board thinks it works well. The disputes are about what would work better and about what could work at all.

The proposal. The Director of Analytics would replace negotiation with what he calls a rolling tâtonnement. Each trust reports weekly cost functions and demand forecasts to a central engine; the engine computes provisional shadow prices for each capacity input; trusts revise their reported supply and demand in light of those prices; the engine re-solves until reported excess demand clears within a stated tolerance; the resulting prices govern the following week's allocations. His case is that the standing objection to central calculation — that no board can assemble or solve for the information a market assembles implicitly — was at bottom an objection about communication and computation, and that both have been transformed since the argument was first had. He emphasises that the engine's inputs are supplied by the trusts themselves, so nothing is being extracted from local actors over their heads.

The opposition. Two members, one of them a former regional chief executive, hold that this reproduces an old error in new clothes. They advance three strands, which they run together and which I want separated and assessed on their own merits: first, that the reported cost functions are abstractions from a great deal of unrecorded local knowledge — how a particular list actually runs, which combinations of cases a given team can safely take, what a ward can absorb in a specific week — and that the requirement to report flattens precisely the information the engine needs; second, that reporting is strategic, and the trust covering our largest rural catchment has told us privately that it will not report true marginal costs because it expects a clearing engine to starve low-volume services; third, that the inputs which matter most have no shadow price at all — staff goodwill, informal cross-cover, a senior clinician's willingness to rearrange a list — and that an allocation which consumes them will look efficient on the ledger while destroying them in fact.

Three further matters that the Board is actively contesting, and which your opinion should treat as part of the problem rather than as background. The status quo tariff is itself an administrative construction that has never cleared anything, so neither side can claim to be defending a market and neither can claim to be escaping from one. A narrower pilot run two years ago on ambulance diversion is cited by the Director as showing an eleven per cent reduction in idle theatre time and by his critics as showing nothing, because the audit found the comparison period atypical and the participating trust's case mix had shifted over it; both readings are in the papers before us. And the engine requires a single scalar objective, while the Board cannot agree what weight to give equitable access across regions as against total throughput; the Director's answer is that this is a matter of fixing weights annually, and that the engine is neutral once they are fixed.

What I need is a memo of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words that the Board could act on. It should open with a plain recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt subject to conditions — and if it is conditional, the conditions must be specific enough that someone could later be held to them: what the pilot covers, which trusts are in it, how long it runs, what is measured and against what comparator, what would trigger suspension, and what decision rule governs the renewal vote. On that last point, say explicitly whether the trust that has refused to report honestly should be inside or outside the pilot, since a test that excludes the region with the strongest epistemic complaint about it may not be a test of anything.

The memo should also state and defend a diagnosis of what the disagreement actually turns on: whether it is a question of computational tractability, a question of mechanism design and the incentives reporting creates, or a question about whether certain kinds of knowledge can be put into reportable form at all. Distinguishing these matters practically, so identify which of them a time-limited pilot could settle, which it could not settle whatever its results, and what would change your own assessment at a midpoint review.

Address the objective-function point head-on rather than as a technicality; I think it is where the proposal's real difficulties are concentrated, and I suspect the claim of neutrality once weights are fixed is doing more work than the Director can support, though I want that argued rather than asserted.

Finally, give the strongest case against your recommendation in terms its proponents would recognise, not a reduced version, and say either why the recommendation survives it or what concession it forces. Do not write me a history of the calculation debate — several of us have read it, and our problem is to decide, not to summarise positions. Where you rely on either camp's reasoning, attribute it to the position rather than to any text, and assume your reader has no references to hand. Assume also that the memo will be read by people who have already made up their minds on both sides, and that anything appearing to flatter one camp will simply be discounted by the other.
