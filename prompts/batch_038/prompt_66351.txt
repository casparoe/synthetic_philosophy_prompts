I direct the evidence review group at a national health technology assessment body. Twice a month a sponsor dossier arrives — a diagnostic, a digital therapeutic, a biomarker-guided intervention — and we must produce a coverage recommendation within six weeks. The recommendation is signed off by a committee of clinicians, a health economist, and a patient representative, and the analyst who drafts it has roughly three working hours for the initial screen. Our assessment template asks that analyst to confirm that the sponsor's central claim is scientifically grounded and testable, and that single phrase is where our reports come apart. Half the group reads it as "has been tested, and the tests came out positive"; the other half reads it as "could in principle have been refuted, whether or not it has been." Neither reading is defensible alone. We have issued recommendations attacked for credulity and others attacked for rigidity, occasionally by the same stakeholder groups in the same year.

I want a screening procedure — a document to be printed and followed, not an essay about demarcation — that an analyst can run on a dossier: the questions to answer in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which a dossier is set aside as unevaluable rather than merely unproven, and the points at which the procedure must stop and hand the decision up to the committee because what remains is a question of values (magnitude of harm, who bears it, what is displaced) rather than of fact. Where a step looks mechanical but is in truth a judgment call wearing a procedural costume, the document should say so in the margin, so that our reviewers stop reporting those steps as though they settled something.

Three requirements shape it.

First, it must survive the objection that no single study can refute a whole claim, since a failed prediction can always be redirected at the population enrolled, the comparator chosen, the adherence achieved, the assay used, or some piece of background apparatus. The analyst needs a way to distinguish, within one dossier, a legitimate refinement of the claim from a rescue that leaves the claim incapable of failing — and the procedure should say how, without pretending the boundary is sharp.

Second, it must assess a claim's history, not only its current formulation. A sponsor whose claim has absorbed three apparent refutations may be doing what good research does, or may have built something that can no longer lose. The procedure should tell the analyst what to look for in the sequence of revisions: whether each was fixed in advance of the data it explains, whether it carries consequences that could have come out otherwise, whether the claim has been generating findings it did not previously accommodate or only re-describing old ones at greater expense.

Third, it must treat measurement honestly. Many dossiers define the very quantity they claim to detect by means of the model that detects it, so that instrument and claim cannot fail each other. I want a step that asks what it would have taken for this sponsor to report a negative result, and whether anyone in a position to know would have let them.

Then run the procedure on three dossiers, sketched here; keep each run compact, because I want to see the procedure work rather than read three full reviews.

(a) A digital therapeutic for adolescent anxiety. Four open-label trials, all positive; one randomized trial negative, after which the sponsor narrowed the indicated population to patients with high baseline attentional bias — a subgroup identified by post-hoc analysis of the trial that failed. The mechanistic account is elegant and our clinical team is attached to it.

(b) A breath-based volatile organic compound test for early-stage pancreatic cancer. The proposed mechanism is frankly speculative, the write-up is unpolished, and an early validation cohort failed and was reported as failed. The sponsor then revised the analyte panel, registered the revision, and stated new sensitivity and specificity thresholds before unblinding.

(c) A blood-derived index of immune age built by a proprietary model. The index predicted two clinical outcomes that no prior measure predicted, and both predictions were recorded before the data were collected. But the index is defined by the model, no independent assay reproduces it, and the sponsor cannot state what value of the index would count against the underlying account.

For each, report what the procedure concludes and where it declines to conclude. I expect at least one verdict to be one I will not like, and I would far rather discover that here than in a published recommendation.

Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which fields it systematically penalizes and which it flatters, how a sophisticated sponsor could game it, and what the analyst should do when she suspects she is being gamed. Any term of art that survives into the document gets a one-line working definition at first use; our analysts are epidemiologists and clinicians, they will not look anything up, and the reasoning has to live inside the steps rather than in a list of places to read about it.
