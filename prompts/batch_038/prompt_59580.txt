I coordinate the duty rota at a volcano observatory that watches a single andesitic stratovolcano standing above a city of about four hundred thousand people. Our alert scheme has four rungs and moving between them is a public act: the level goes out on the radio, schools adjust, the civil defence agency pre-positions buses. Two years ago we replaced our acquisition software, and the new system no longer reports each monitoring channel as a number. For each of our three principal channels — broadband tremor amplitude, sulphur dioxide flux, and the change in a GPS baseline running across the edifice — it emits a three-way soft classification over the bins we call quiet, elevated and high, for instance (0.15, 0.55, 0.30). The classifier is trained on features nobody here fully understands and sometimes returns something close to a coin flip, like (0.42, 0.39, 0.19). At present the analysts handle this informally: most take whichever bin is largest and treat the channel as having been *observed* to be in that bin, then update everything downstream on that hardened statement. Last winter a reading of (0.42, 0.39, 0.19) on tremor was hardened to quiet, the alert stayed where it was for another nine hours, and a small phreatic explosion at the summit — nobody was hurt, but a car park was wrecked — became a parliamentary question about whether we know how to read our own instruments. A colleague in the seismology group says what we actually need is something she called probability kinematics, or updating on uncertain evidence rather than on observations, but she couldn't tell me how to hand that to a technician at six in the morning.

There is a second worry I want taken seriously. Tremor amplitude and gas flux are driven by overlapping parts of the same plumbing, so a shift in one and a shift in the other may not be two independent pieces of news; when both move on the same shift I suspect our current habit of processing them one after the other double-counts something, and I also suspect the reverse happens when they move in opposite directions. And a third: a channel that has failed and is reporting nothing is not the same as a channel reporting a confident middle bin, though our spreadsheet currently treats them identically because a dead sensor defaults to (0.00, 1.00, 0.00).

So: write me the procedure. It should tell a duty analyst, step by step, how to move our working probability that the volcano produces an eruption above a stated size within the next thirty days — one number, tracked on the duty board, currently sitting at 0.14 — on the basis of the soft vectors the software hands over, inside the twenty minutes of a shift handover. It must be runnable with spreadsheet arithmetic by staff whose only formal training was a two-day internal course, so no measure theory and no notation that has to be explained before it can be used; where the honest justification for a step is a technical assumption, state that assumption in one plain sentence and then give the analyst the test to run. It must specify what gets written down at each step and to how many decimals, what to do with a near-tie between bins, what to do with a dead sensor, in what order to process several channels that moved on the same shift, and — explicitly — where the standard assumption that lets you propagate a changed probability through the rest of your beliefs is likely to break down in our setting, together with what to do instead when it breaks. Mark every step that is really a judgment call wearing the costume of arithmetic; I would rather see them labelled than smoothed over. Then run the procedure end to end on three cases and show the numbers: (i) tremor (0.10, 0.60, 0.30) with gas and GPS both firmly quiet; (ii) tremor (0.20, 0.50, 0.30) and gas (0.15, 0.45, 0.40) arriving on the same shift, with GPS down for maintenance; (iii) the case I most need handled, where the deputy director has already been on the radio at eleven saying we expect to raise the alert at noon, and the midday run produces a *lower* board number than the morning one — tell me what the analyst does, what she writes in the log, and who she telephones. Finally, say plainly what this procedure cannot deliver, where it will systematically err, and at which points the arithmetic has to stop and a signature has to take over.
