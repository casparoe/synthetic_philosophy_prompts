I help out with the production side of a small cognitive psychology journal — mostly chasing reviewers, nothing intellectual — and last week two of the associate editors had a huge argument in the WhatsApp group about the current wave of submissions. Over the last eighteen months we've gone from getting maybe two papers a year using this new implicit-measure paradigm to getting about twenty-five, and nearly all of them cite the same three founding papers. Here's roughly how it went (I've compressed it and taken out the bits where they were being rude to each other):

1. Rosie says it's a textbook information cascade. Each new author saw other people publishing with the paradigm, inferred it must work, and piled in, regardless of what their own data or instincts said. So the volume of submissions tells us nothing about whether the paradigm is any good — after those first three papers, people's actions stopped carrying information.

2. Dev says she's misusing the model. Herding can be perfectly rational and perfectly Bayesian; nobody in the cascade is being stupid, that's the whole point of it. And in this case the founding papers had genuinely strong effects, so the followers were responding to real evidence rather than to each other. A cascade is supposed to involve people overriding their own information, and if their private information was weak or noisy, then going along with the crowd is just correct updating — so calling it a cascade is a slur dressed up as maths.

3. Rosie says that's exactly the mechanism: rational agents rationally discount their private signal once the public history gets long enough, which is why cascades can settle on something false and stay there. And she says it can't be genuine convergence on evidence anyway, because something like a dozen of the authors have told her seperately, off the record, that they personally had doubts about the paradigm before they adopted it. That's proof they were ignoring what they knew.

4. Dev says a doubt mentioned casually to an editor isn't a signal precise enough to outweigh a public record of twenty successful papers, and that several of the same people are now enthusiastic about the paradigm having actually used it — which is evidence they updated on their own experience, not on fashion. He adds that the thing which would settle any of this, a proper multi-lab replication, hasn't been published yet, and until it has, "cascade" is just his word for "lots of people agree and Rosie doesn't".

5. Rosie says fragility is the diagnostic, and that a cascade looks exactly like a well-supported consensus from the inside — which is why you can't wait for the inside view. The tell is that a cascade collapses the moment a contrary public signal turns up, whereas a real consensus absorbs it. So the fact that the whole field is holding its breath until the replication lands is itself the giveaway.

6. Dev says that's unfalsifiable: she's counting a future collapse as present evidence, and if the replication confirms the paradigm she'll call it a "correct cascade" and win either way. He then says she's smuggling in a different diagnosis, because what she described in (3) — people privately doubting but publicly going along — is conformity or pluralistic ignorance, not a cascade. In a cascade people aren't being pressured, they're inferring. And the two have opposite cures: a cascade is broken by new public information, whereas conformity is broken by lowering the social cost of defecting.

7. Rosie says fine, but the policy upshot is the same either way: the editors shouldn't give the submission count any weight when deciding whether to run a special issue on the paradigm, because whether it's inference or pressure, that number isn't independent evidence.

8. Dev says the policies differ completely. If authors are responding to real evidence, ignoring the submissions throws information away. If it's conformity, a special issue makes things worse by raising the cost of dissent. If it's a genuine cascade, the single most valuable thing the editors could do is commission and fast-track the replication. So the diagnosis matters and you can't wave it off.
