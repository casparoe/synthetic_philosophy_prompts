I'm sitting in on a moral psychology seminar this term and next week we're doing the bystander effect, which of course means the Kitty Genovese killing in Queens in 1964 — the woman attacked twice over something like half an hour while neighbors supposedly listened, the piece the New York Times ran a couple of weeks later about the thirty-eight people who saw a murder and never phoned the police, and the whole diffusion-of-responsibility research program that grew out of it. What nobody in the seminar seems interested in is that the record got badly revised in the 2000s; I think there was an article in American Psychologist around 2007 by Manning, Levine and Collins arguing that the 38-witness parable isn't supported by the police and trial files, that a lot of the people counted as witnesses saw nothing at all or heard something they read as a street quarrel or a drunk, that at least one neighbor shouted out a window and the attacker broke off the first time, that a friend did eventually call the cops, and that one woman came downstairs and held her while she died. Her own brother made a documentary about the mess years later. So the case that founded a research paradigm is partly a newspaper artifact, and its the corrected version that interests me more.

Here's what I actually want. Take the episode — both the 1964 version everybody believed and the corrected version — and run it through Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in the Theory of Moral Sentiments. Do the mapping carefully before evaluating anything. For Smith sympathy isn't catching a feeling, it's an act of imagination: I can only feel what I would feel if I were in your situation, so I have to be able to conceive that situation vividly in the first place. What does that machinery predict about people in dark apartments at three in the morning hearing something they can't see? My hunch, which may well be wrong, is that Smith is starved of exactly the input his mechanism runs on — you can't change places with a scream. There's a second thing though: his account of mutual sympathy, where we pitch our own feelings down to the level the spectators around us can go along with, looks to me like it might actually anticipate pluralistic ignorance, each neighbor reading the composure of the others and calming himself to match. Is that right or am I over-reading Smith into a 1960s social-psychology finding? And bring in the thought experiment he runs in Part III, where a humane European hears that all of China has been swallowed by an earthquake, feels real sorrow, makes some melancholy reflections on the precariousness of human life, and then goes to sleep and eats his dinner as usual, whereas losing his own little finger tomorrow would keep him up all night. That's the closest thing Smith has to a theory of why proximity and vividness dominate, and it isn't about crowds at all, so tell me whether it does any work here.

Then flip it around. The place Smith looks like he has no resources is the crowd itself: the impartial spectator is individuated, its the man within each of us, so there's no obvious mechanism in his system by which thirty-seven other spectators make the thirty-eighth's inner judge go quiet. Is that a genuine gap, or does his apparatus of general rules of morality — the rules we build up from watching which conduct gets approved, which conscience then applies precisely when spontaneous sympathy fails — carry the load? If Smith's honest answer is that nobody needed to feel anything, that the sense of duty should have done it, then the episode stops being an anomaly for him and becomes something he predicted, which is a very different verdict from the one the psychologists drew. Finally, what does the revision do to Smith rather than to Latane? He thinks general rules of morality get built out of particular cases we've observed and judged, and here an entire moral rule about urban apathy was built out of a particular case that was substantially misreported and then circulated for decades. Say whether Smith's spectator can survive being fed bad testimony — does he have any notion of the spectator's own epistemic position, or is the theory just silent there?
