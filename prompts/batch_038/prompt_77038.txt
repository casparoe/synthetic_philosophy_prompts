I'm writing a chapter on decisions where the expected value doesn't just blow up to infinity but fails to exist at all, and before I wade into the proposed fixes I want the spec. So: what does a decision theory have to deliver to count as an adequate account of gambles with undefined expected value? Give me each requirement with the case that motivates it, and mark which are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are really just the priors of one camp (does the value even have to be a real number? does the ordering have to be total?).

Three cases I cooked up that I want the list to cover, all Pasadena-style:

1. Mirror game. Flip a fair coin. Heads: a coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if that happens on flip n you recieve (-1)^(n+1) 2^n/n dollars. Tails: same thing, every sign flipped. Symmetry screams the package is worth exactly zero, but the positive and negative parts of the distribution both diverge, so there's no fact to appeal to. Grouping the states by the outer coin gives 0. Other groupings don't behave.

2. Mirror-plus-one. Identical, except every payoff in every state has $1 added. This dominates case 1 state for state, so it has to come out strictly better — and the theory owes me an account of how "better" gets registered when neither value exists.

3. Truncation. If the answer is "cut the series off at N and let N grow with your patience," someone who knows your N can walk you through a run of individually acceptable trades that jointly drains you.

It also has to cover the boring end: no messing up ordinary finite-EV gambles, and no collapsing into "everything unbounded is incomparable." And it has to tell a bounded agent what to actually do.

If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say which — my hunch is bounded utility does most of the damage. Then a short scorecard of relative expected utility, truncation views, the boundedness move, and outright incomparability. No citations, I'll find the papers myself.
