I am presenting a paper in three weeks to a panel at an interdisciplinary conference on the limits of markets. The room will be mostly moral psychologists and experimental philosophers who study disgust and moral judgment: people who run vignette studies on visceral "yuck" responses, who treat repugnance as a bias to be corrected rather than as evidence, and who assume that disgust of this kind has no legitimate place in arguments about law. Two of the five panelists are market-design economists in Alvin Roth's tradition, for whom repugnance is real but relevant only as a feasibility constraint that clever design can sometimes dissolve. Nobody in the room is a Kantian, a theological ethicist, or a Marxist, so I will not be getting objections from those directions and I do not want rehearsal for them.

The paper's thesis is that repugnance toward markets in kidneys, votes, and gestational surrogacy is neither a moral intuition to be weighed against the gains from trade nor a squeamishness to be engineered around. It is an alarm about institutional decay: a signal that the practice in question is held together by a non-market institution — a norm of gift, of civic duty, of bodily integrity — which pricing would erode, and which we depend on in ways nobody can articulate at the moment the price appears. The normative upshot is that the real question is not whether the feeling is justified but whether the institution the feeling warns about can be rebuilt inside the market design itself. I develop this with a kidney-market proposal in which the donor's gift is publicly named, the payment is directed to a charity of the donor's choosing rather than to the donor, and the transfer is decoupled in time from retrieval.

The argument sketch they will be attacking has three steps: first, that repugnance concentrates on precisely those goods whose supply depends on norms known to be crowded out by payment, for which I offer the nineteenth-century resistance to life insurance and the dispute over paying blood donors in the 1960s as worked cases; second, that where the underlying institution was preserved or rebuilt, the repugnance faded, which suggests the alarm tracks the institution rather than the price tag; third, that a market's acceptability therefore turns on design and not on the intrinsic character of the good.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me exactly five numbered entries, ordered from the objection that would do most damage to the paper down to the one I can absorb, and mark which of the five is the question I am hoping nobody asks. Each entry should contain the objection phrased the way this particular audience would phrase it, in their vocabulary and citing the kind of evidence they actually cite; two or three sentences of reply stating plainly what I should concede and what I should hold; and a closing clause identifying where that reply is softest. Keep each entry under roughly one hundred words. No preamble, no summary of my position, no closing remarks — five entries only.
