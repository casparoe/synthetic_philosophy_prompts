I run a small internal estimation group at a mid-sized logistics company — eleven analysts. Twice a quarter we produce numbers the exec team actually acts on: how much warehouse capacity to lease, when a competitor opens a lane, what to assume about fuel next year. The process right now is that I post the question in a shared sheet, people answer one at a time in an order I set, and everybody can see the answers already in the sheet before they type theirs. I do it that way on purpose, because our senior analyst's reasoning is genuinely useful to the juniors and I don't want to throw that away.

The problem: when the sheet fills up with basically the same number eleven times, I can't tell whether I've got eleven people converging on the truth or one person's guess laundered ten times. I know the rough shape of the cascade argument — private signals, everyone watching what earlier people did, and it being perfectly rational from the fifth person onward to discount what you know. But I can't turn that into something I can run. My people are good in a spreadsheet, one has a stats minor, nobody's read the formal models, and my sponsor wants a single number by friday and won't accept "the range is wide" as an answer. I sign off personally.

So: write me a triage procedure I can run in about twenty minutes on any consensus number before it goes upstairs. Questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the point where it stops and tells me not to send this up at all, and the points where it's really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume.

Three things I want it to deal with explicitly:

- I've been toying with a metric I call the cascade gap: after the sheet is full, ask everyone for a fresh private answer and use the size of the drift as a measure of how cascaded the number was. Does that measure anything? If yes, build it in. If not, say why and what to do instead.
- We have a standing rule that the last three to submit have to come from different teams. I assumed that broke the chain.
- A pilot where I hid the first six answers: the final numbers moved about 4% on average, but the spread tripled. Does a bigger spread mean less cascade, or just more noise? I've been reading a tight sheet as a good sign and I suspect that's backwards.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A sheet where eleven people landed within a day of each other on a competitor's launch date, and the first two answers both trace back to the same trade-press article. (2) A sheet that's a mess — huge spread, two camps — leadership wants one number tomorrow and there's no time to rerun it. (3) A sheet that looks structurally clean by every rule I have: simultaneous private entry, teams mixed, no one saw anyone else's number — but all eleven started from the same third-party vendor model of regional freight volumes. I'm pretty sure (3) is fine and I'd like the procedure to tell me so; if it can't, tell me what it can't do and why no amount of process design fixes it.
