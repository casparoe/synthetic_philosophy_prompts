So I've been going down a rabbit hole on insect welfare and I want somebody to argue the side nobody in those spaces wants to argue. Let me set it up with a made-up framework first, because otherwise the conversation just turns into people trading intuitions about whether a beetle matters.

Call it the per-hectare ledger. Take one hectare of cropland, one growing season, one pest-management strategy S. Score it as two sums. First sum: over every individual harmed by S, p × w × d × i, where p is your credence the individual is sentient, w is moral weight per unit of experience, d is duration, i is intensity (negative when it's suffering). Second sum: over every individual who would have existed in the counterfactual world where you picked a different S, same formula, with the sign coming from whether their life was worth starting. Call the second one the population term.

The version of this argument I keep seeing online basically sets the population term to zero. You get p tiny, w tiny, but N absurdly huge, and out comes "never spray anything broad-spectrum, and if you must, pick whichever mode of action kills fastest." The population term gets hand-waved with either "insect lives are probably net negative anyway" or "we're not doing population ethics here, sorry."

Here's what I want you to argue: the population term is the one that actually dominates the ledger, and taking it seriously flips the recommendation toward much more aggressive suppression of insect populations than the cautious, targeted, swap-in-a-humane-insecticide approach. I want the strongest version — the one that survives a genuinely hostile and well-informed reader — not a strawman I can knock over in a sentence.

Please engage with all of the following rather than picking the easy ones:

- what has to be true about the sign of an average insect life for this to go through, and whether that's the kind of claim anyone could actually have evidence for, versus it just being a prior wearing a lab coat
- the asymmetry between killing and preventing existence, and whether the usual person-affecting escape hatch is even available when the beings in question aren't people and may not have the kind of ongoing psychology the objection leans on
- how much of the argument survives if you swap total utilitarianism for average util, or for a person-affecting view, or for something that cares about flourishing rather than hedonic tone
- the ecology objection, which I think is the real problem: a suppressed population gets replaced. Pest resurgence, secondary pests filling the vacated niche, the loss of the pollinators and predators and the whole food web that was riding on it. So you might not be removing suffering at all, just moving it around and adding variance
- whether there's a formulation that's robust across several of these at once, or whether every version only wins under one specific stack of controversial premises, which would be its own kind of answer

I don't want a bibliography or a reading list — I can't look anything up and I'd much rather you reason than name-drop. But I do want you to label things as you go: which parts of what you're saying are more or less settled among people who actually work on this, and which parts are live disputes where you're just picking a side and could plausibly be wrong. Please don't let a contested position sneak in dressed as consensus. And at the end, tell me where you think the argument you just made is weakest, and what would make you drop it.
