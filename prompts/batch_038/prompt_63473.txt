Last night I spent three hours arguing with a colleague who serves with me on our state fisheries board's Scientific Advisory Committee, and we ended exactly where we started, so I want the argument written down properly before the board has to vote. The situation is this. Two of the board's standing panels were asked the same question: the probability that the spawning stock of the estuary herring run falls below its recruitment threshold within twenty years under the quota proposal now before us. The stock assessment panel reported 0.18; the ecosystem effects panel reported 0.51. Both received the same data packet, the same modeling budget, and the same deadline; they exchanged drafts twice and sat through a joint session, after which the numbers moved to 0.20 and 0.48 and not an inch further. Our staff's own analysis localizes nearly all of the residual gap to two inputs: how much weight to give a single small-sample but directly relevant tagging study funded by the fishing cooperative, and what prior to use for natural mortality in an unfished analogue stock. The ecosystem panel's prior was elicited from three of its members' combined fifty-odd years of fieldwork in an adjacent system that the other panel regards as not analogous in any relevant respect. Both reports describe their own method as Bayesian updating, and both chairs are, by any measure I can apply, competent and honest.

My colleague has circulated a motion, and it is his justification I care about. He proposes that the board publish no range at all. Instead, it would mandate a formal reconciliation protocol — six rounds of anonymous posterior exchange run by an outside facilitator — and if the panels have not come within ten percentage points by the end of the sixth round, the board would declare the exercise a failure and suspend the quota decision. His stated ground is Aumann's agreement theorem: rational agents who share a common prior and know one another's posteriors cannot disagree, so a disagreement that survives a full exchange of evidence is not a difference of opinion but a symptom of somebody's irrationality, and an official range would lend the board's credibility to what is, on his telling, defective reasoning dressed as uncertainty. My reply last night was that the hypotheses are doing all the work and none of them hold here: the panels plainly do not share a prior; reading one another's reports aloud in a courteous room is not the same as common knowledge of posteriors, let alone common knowledge of rationality; and the theorem is a static consistency result that licenses no claim whatsoever about whether a facilitated Delphi sequence produces genuine agreement or merely exhaustion. He came back at the common prior assumption itself, which is where I think I was slippery: on one reading, differences in priors are really differences in information, in which case fifty years of fieldwork in a non-analogous system is smuggled data and the theorem does bite; on the other, a prior is a disposition to accept bets rather than a conclusion drawn from evidence, in which case both panels may be perfectly rational and the theorem simply has no purchase on them. We never settled which reading the result presupposes, and I expect the board to flounder in exactly the same place.

So please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Tuesday's vote. I need it to recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or amend his motion — with a procedure and timeline five non-specialists could actually implement, and to state plainly what the theorem does and does not license, in language that does not presuppose any game theory. Give the board criteria it could reuse: when does persistent expert disagreement warrant mandated reconciliation, when does it warrant publishing a range, and when does it warrant concluding that one side has failed a standard of rationality rather than merely holding a different view? Tell me whether requiring each panel to publish its prior separately from its likelihood would localize the dispute enough to resolve it, or would only relocate the argument one level up. Engage his strongest points seriously rather than dispatching them: that the panels exchanged documents rather than posteriors, that the politeness of the joint session meant no one ever said in terms that a rival's prior was unjustified, and that a published range invites every interested party to shop for the number it prefers — and if your recommendation is still to publish a range, say what the board should do about that. Finally, if you conclude that some of what he and I were arguing about was the word rational rather than the substance, identify which part, because I would like to know whether his motion is answerable at all or merely confused. I don't want a literature review or citations; I want a document the board can act on.
