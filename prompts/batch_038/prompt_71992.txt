Third-year jurisprudence PhD here — dissertation on constitutional entrenchment — and for four years I've been running a Tuesday-night Nomic game with about nine people from the department (the Suber thing that got published in Hofstadter's column: mutable and immutable rules, proposals adopted by majority, immutable rules have to be transmuted before you can touch them). We have a standing three-person Committee on Order that rules whether a proposal is in order before it goes to the floor, and I chair it. Over four years we've accumulated a body of rulings I want to write up as a little case study, partly because we've never articulated a doctrine and partly because I have to decide a nasty one on Thursday and I'd rather be consistent with ourselves than just vote my gut.

The skeleton, so the rulings make sense. Rule 204 is our rule of change and it's immutable: "a proposal to add, amend, repeal, or suspend a rule is in order if seconded and adopted by a majority of players present and voting; a rule marked immutable may be amended or repealed only by a two-thirds vote of all players." Rule 205 is precedence: immutable beats mutable, lower number beats higher. Rule 118, the elimination rule (a player at or below zero points is out), was marked immutable in year one. The rulings, with the single line we put in the minutes each time:

1. Year 1, Prop 4 — mark 118 immutable and append "and this rule may not be amended, repealed, or suspended." Adopted 6–3. Minute: "204 gives us the power to make rules immutable and says nothing against protecting them once made."
2. Year 1, Prop 11 — rejected 7–2. Would have ended the game outright once the pot passed a threshold, repealing 204 in the process. Minute: "if 204 is gone nothing makes our votes count; we have no power to vote ourselves out of a game."
3. Year 2, Prop 19 — amend 204 by adding "204 may not be amended or repealed." Rejected 8–1. Minute: "a rule of change that forbids its own amendment stops being a rule of change, and if we can't fix 204 we can't fix anything."
4. Year 2, Prop 23, three weeks after 19 — amend 204 to insert a separate tier: "a proposal whose only effect is to add or remove an immutability marker requires a two-thirds vote of all players." Adopted 6–3. Two of us objected that this gutted Prop 4's protection by going around it; the minute answers: "4 protects 118 against proposals about 118. This is a proposal about markers." Over the next two weeks the table used that tier to strip 118's immutability and then repeal 118, both adopted, no objection from the Committee.
5. Year 3, Prop 31 — a single proposal containing two clauses that flatly contradicted each other with no precedence hook. Rejected 9–0 as drafted and sent back; resubmitted with "in case of conflict the first clause prevails" and adopted. Minute: "we can't tell the table what to do with a ruleset that gives two answers to the same question."
6. Year 3, Prop 36 — "this rule is repealed at the moment any player proposes to repeal it." Adopted 5–4. Minute: "self-referential and strange but perfectly determinate; the Committee checks that a rule can be applied, not that it's ordinary."
7. Year 3, Prop 41 — "any person admitted to the game after this date thereby waives, permanently and irrevocably, the right to vote on proposals concerning rule 118." Rejected 5–4. Minute: "members hold the voting power by being members; we can't take it away from people who aren't here yet and can't object."

Thursday's case is Prop 47, drafted by a player who has clearly been reading my chapter drafts over my shoulder: "Rule 204 is amended by adding the following sentence: a proposal that would amend, repeal, suspend, or lower the voting threshold specified in this sentence may be adopted only by a vote of every player eligible to vote, and only if that same proposal also repeals every rule then marked immutable." There's a threshold drafting dispute — is that sentence part of 204, hence immutable, hence already amendable at two-thirds of all players under 204's own existing text, which the sentence appears to contradict? The proposer says no, it's a new rule sitting under 204, and that we're being pedantic. Note it isn't contradictory in the Prop 31 sense; you can always tell exactly what it demands, it just demands a lot.

So: work out which principles our seven rulings are jointly consistent with. I can see at least a formalist one (in order iff properly moved and determinate, never look through the text at what the rule does), a framework-preservation one (out of order iff it disables the machinery by which future change happens), a level-based one (entrenchment is legitimate downward but never on the rule of change itself), and something like a no-lock-in principle keyed to effects rather than wording — but tell me if there are others that fit better or if some of my four don't actually survive the full list. For each, say what it predicts for 47 and why, including how it handles the awkward ones (Prop 23's marker loophole is where I expect most of them to squeak). Then give me the ruling you think is most defensible as a continuation of our own line, name the closest analog among the seven, and write the actual one-line minute I could read out to the table without blushing. The part I most want is the doctrinal cost either way: if we adopt, are we committed to saying entrenchment is only ever a matter of the words used, so a unanimity-plus-poison-pill clause on the entrenching clause is as legitimate as the flat bar we rejected in Prop 19? If we reject, are we claiming a substantive power to look past text at consequences — which is precisely what Prop 36's minute says we don't have? And is form-versus-effect really the live axis here, or is the real question the continuing versus self-embracing sovereignty thing (Hart's vocabulary I think, don't quote me on that)? Also: is any one of the seven flatly inconsistent with the rest such that we should overrule it rather than distinguish it, and if so which? Finally propose one or two test proposals for next term that would actually come apart on the surviving principles — I'm vaguely thinking delay-instead-of-bar, or unanimity of those present rather than of all eligible players, but tell me which pair genuinely discriminates. No references or reading list, I've got the constitutional-law side covered; I want the reasoning about our own precedents.
