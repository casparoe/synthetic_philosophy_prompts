I serve on a five-member public standards tribunal in a mid-sized parliamentary system. We adjudicate complaints against office-holders, we decide by majority, and our written reasons are published. A matter is listed for next week and I am trying to work out two separate things: how I expect my colleagues to vote, and how I should vote if I want my position to cohere with our own record rather than with my private views about gift-giving.

Our rulings over the past six years, in the order decided:

1. A senior customs officer accepted, over four years, hospitality at international sporting events from a freight company whose consignments his office cleared. He declared none of it. No individual clearance decision could be traced to the hospitality, and he maintained that work was never discussed at the events. Held: corrupt conduct.

2. A regional development officer, herself raised in the district, accepted a hand-woven rug from a delegation of village elders who had come to request a road. In that district, refusal is understood as a public rejection of the delegation. She declared the rug the same day; its value was modest; the road request was determined months later by a published formula applied uniformly to all districts. Held: not corrupt conduct.

3. A minister's special adviser accepted an all-expenses study trip from the government of a country with a live trade file in her department. She declared it only after a journalist asked. Held: a breach. Our reasons said the wrong lay not in any promise made but in the position the acceptance put her in.

4. A mayor appointed his brother-in-law to head the municipal water utility. The post had been advertised, eleven candidates applied, and the brother-in-law scored highest before a panel that included two members from outside the municipality. Held: not corrupt conduct; we recommended a standing recusal protocol.

5. A legislator directed a discretionary grant to a hospital at which his wife sat as an unpaid volunteer trustee. He disclosed the relationship before the vote and abstained from nothing. Held: corrupt conduct. Our reasons said that disclosure permits scrutiny but does not cure.

6. A judge accepted and declared an honorarium for a lecture series at a law school. Two years later the school's dean appeared before her as counsel in an unrelated matter. Held: not corrupt conduct; recusal required in that matter.

7. A city councillor instructed her staff to move the housing applications of members of her own community association to the front of the queue, on the ground that they were her constituents and had come to her. The queue was governed by published allocation criteria. Held: corrupt conduct.

8. An ambassador received, as part of the host state's protocol for visiting officials, an engraved watch, and surrendered it to the embassy property register. Held: no breach. We expressly declined to extend the reasoning to gifts from private parties in the same country.

9. An office-holder accepted a lawful, published campaign donation from a company that eighteen months later won a tender awarded by an independent procurement panel on which she did not sit. Held: not corrupt conduct.

The new matter concerns a regional executive with responsibility for fisheries licensing, and it has three limbs.

(a) At a public holiday she received a hamper of local produce from an association of licensed fishers whose licences fall within her portfolio. Its value sat at the threshold in our own guidance at which declaration becomes compulsory. She declared it, and the produce was consumed by her staff.

(b) Twelve years ago, before she took office, she founded a scholarship fund for children of fishing villages. Three trustees, two of whom have no connection with her, award the scholarships under published criteria, and she has no role in awarding them. A licensed fishing company in her region has made three donations to the fund, each publicly announced by the fund, none to her personally. The largest fell about two months before a licence renewal affecting that company. Her name is on the fund.

(c) That renewal was decided by her deputy, on standard terms, under published criteria. She was not consulted, learned of it afterwards, saw no reason to disturb it, and did not.

What I would like from you, in this order:

1. Reconstruct the rule or rules our nine verdicts could be applying. I want at least two readings, each stated so that it decides all nine the way we did, together with an honest note of which ruling each has to work hardest on. My own suspicion is that the record supports both a reading on which the question is whether a personal or commercial relationship displaced an impersonal channel, and a reading on which the question is what an informed observer would reasonably suspect. One of my colleagues appears to be applying a third, on which the standard is set by the legitimate expectations of the community in which the office is exercised. I do not know whether these come apart anywhere in our past cases; please tell me where, if anywhere, they do.

2. Take each reading to limbs (a), (b) and (c) and state its verdict and its reasoning. Identify precisely where the readings diverge, and whether they diverge on the outcome or only on the reasons that would get us there.

3. Predict the panel. How do you expect five members to vote on each limb and on the overall finding, and what would the published reasons most plausibly say? In weighting our record, attend to what seems to me to matter: that 1, 3 and 5 each involved either concealment or a relationship that actually governed a discretionary act, while 2 and 8 involved neither; that the language we used in 3 was positional and the language we used in 5 was about disclosure failing to cure; and that in 8 we refused to generalize from the protocol context to the private one.

4. Recommend my vote. Which resolution of the three limbs, taken together, best coheres with our own precedents? Name the closest precedent for each limb and say where the analogy strains, rather than presenting the choice as obvious.

5. State what we would be committing ourselves to either way. If we hold (b) not corrupt, what rule about named foundations and regulated donors have we adopted, and does that rule oblige us to say that some earlier case was wrongly decided? If we hold it corrupt, do we thereby adopt an appearance standard that would also have swept in 2 and 8 — and if not, what exactly distinguishes them?

6. On the larger question: two of us think the record shows one standard applied to market settings and a softer one to communal and ceremonial ones, with 2 and 8 doing unacknowledged cultural work; a third thinks any such flexibility is simply a licence for the well-connected. On the reading of the record you find most defensible, are we applying a single standard of official impartiality with context-sensitive application, or two standards? And what would our finding on (b) imply for an official posted to a setting where presenting a gift to a regulator is a recognized obligation rather than a market transaction? I want an answer that avoids both conclusions I distrust: that every official relationship should be monetized and audited, and that invoking culture settles the matter.

7. Finally, give me one further fact pattern we have not yet had that would most cleanly separate the readings you identified, and say what each reading would decide about it.

I am not asking for sources or a reading list; I want your reasoning about our record. If you conclude that our nine rulings cannot be squared with any single rule, say so and identify the pair that is in genuine tension rather than forcing a fit.
