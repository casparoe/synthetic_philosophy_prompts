The unit on paid parental leave in my upper-division seminar has stopped working. I lecture on the fact that leave policy operates simultaneously at two levels — inside the household, where two adults decide who will scale back and for how long, and outside it, where employers must hire and promote under uncertainty about who will actually take the leave — and the students write it down dutifully. Then their papers reveal that what survived was a slogan about discrimination rather than the structure that generates it. They also reliably conclude that the remedy is a larger, more generous, formally gender-neutral entitlement, and they are surprised, in a way that no amount of lecturing has managed to fix, that generosity alone can leave the intended beneficiaries worse off. I have decided the session needs to stop being a lecture.

Twenty-eight juniors and seniors, one eighty-minute meeting, in a political philosophy seminar. Most have had one introductory course and no economics beyond it; several are non-traditional students, some of them parents, some working full time. I have a whiteboard, index cards, a printer, and one afternoon to prepare. No computers, no apps, and no money: if tokens help, they may be redeemable for nothing whatsoever, but losing them has to sting while the game is running.

Design a game — rules, roles, materials, an explicit payoff scheme — together with a debrief, that puts this two-level structure in the students' hands. What I want them to come away seeing, in this order, and with none of it stated to them until the debrief begins:

1. That an entitlement can be identical on its face for both members of a couple and still be taken almost entirely by one of them, for reasons that look entirely sensible from inside that household.
2. That employers facing such a pattern respond in a way that is neither malice nor prejudice but a rational hedge, and that the hedge alters the payoffs confronting households in the next round, so the pattern that produced it is reproduced by it.
3. That this is a coordination problem with more than one stable outcome, and that the better outcome is unreachable by anyone acting alone, however enlightened.
4. That the standard institutional fix — a slice of the entitlement reserved for each parent that simply lapses if that parent does not use it — works not by persuading anyone of anything but by eliminating an option, which makes it a philosophical problem about freedom and about who may design other people's choices, not a technical adjustment.

Requirements on the design:

- Two genuine levels. Households must be pairs of agents who bargain with one another, not single decision-makers carrying a hidden preference; firms must commit to hiring and promotion before they know what will happen later. Both games must run inside the same round, with the output of one becoming the input to the other.
- Roles assigned by card and labeled neutrally — worker one, worker two, firm A — never by gender, and never in a way that requires a student to perform or disclose anything about their own life. I do not want the room to discover which of them are parents.
- The game must not depend on knowledge of any country's leave system; the regime descriptions should be self-contained on the single handout.
- At least two regimes: first the generous transferable entitlement, then the reserved-and-forfeitable one. If the clock allows, a third round in which students draft a regime themselves and play it. I want it to be genuinely possible for them to draft a regime that makes caregiving itself pay rather than one that pushes everyone into employment, and I want that regime to be playable, not merely gestured at.
- Show me the arithmetic. Payoff tables for each regime and each role, the outcome you expect to emerge, and an identification of which parameters are load-bearing and how far each may drift before the round stops producing the discovery I want. I would far rather learn that on paper than in front of twenty-eight people.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for eighty minutes including the debrief, a variant that works with twelve students, and instructions for an odd number.
- The debrief as a sequence of questions in the order I should ask them, mapping particular moments from the play onto the four points above. Tell me precisely where I introduce the vocabulary — the popular label for the reduced-commitment career path employers assume mothers will prefer, the distinction between a universal-breadwinner model of justice and a caregiver-parity one, and the difference between discrimination grounded in animus and discrimination grounded in expectation — and give me the first sentence I should say when I finally say it.
- A candid account of how the first run will misfire: which rule will be misread, which role will be over- or under-powered, what the class will do when a couple discovers in round two that they have forfeited something they were entitled to, and a short protocol for rescuing the session if the intended outcome simply fails to appear.
- A closing section on what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. My own suspicion is that the game installs as a fixed type of worker precisely what a critic would say is manufactured by the arrangement: that the preference for weaker attachment to paid work is not a datum about people but a product of a division of labor that taught someone to have it. Tell me whether that objection lands, whether any part of it can be built into the design, and how to raise it in the debrief without dissolving what the students have just lived through.

Please do not give me a bibliography, and do not cite anything; where you want to invoke a familiar position, describe it instead. Write in American English.
