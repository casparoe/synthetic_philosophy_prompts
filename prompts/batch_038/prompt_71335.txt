A widely used scoring rule for the calibration of probabilistic forecasters — and, in a modified form, for the training signal of weather-prediction models — is the Brier score. A forecaster who assigns probability p to an event that turns out to occur receives a score of (1 − p)²; if the event fails to occur, the score is p². Lower is better, scores are averaged across a forecaster's questions, and the whole apparatus rests on the fact that this rule is *proper*: a forecaster who reports the probability she actually believes minimizes her expected score. Suppose a forecaster is asked to predict the outcome of a fair coin toss that she will herself perform and observe before reporting — a case in which she knows with certainty that she will end up believing either that heads occurred or that it did not, and knows that each is equally likely beforehand. What does this practice of proper scoring presuppose about the relation between belief and evidence? In particular, does it presuppose that a rational agent's credence must always be *conditional* on what she has actually learned, so that a probability attached to an event she has not yet observed is a different quantity from a probability attached to the same event once it is before her eyes — and that the scoring rule silently treats these two quantities as interchangeable? Reconstruct the implicit picture of belief update that makes proper scoring coherent, and identify any rival picture (for instance, one on which observation simply makes an evidential proposition certain, and updating proceeds by conditioning on a proposition the agent is not antecedently sure of) that the practice equally permits. Then say what would have to change in the design of a scoring system — the timing of the report, the choice of questions, or the rule itself — if that rival picture were the right one.
