ok so I'm the grad student rep on my university's dual-use research oversight committee (tiny thing, five of us) and next Thursday we get a case that has everyone at each others throats already, and the chair asked me to circulate a draft opinion beforehand so we're not arguing cold. I could really use help writing it.

The case: a CS security group in our department has a paper accepted at a big security venue. It describes a working attack on the wireless protocol of a widely deployed insulin pump — no authentication on the radio link, an attacker in range can alter basal rates. The paper ships a working exploit tool as an artifact. The manufacturer has told them a firmware fix needs regulatory clearance and won't reach patients' devices for 8-14 months, and there's no remote update path for the roughly 200k units in the field. Two other academic groups are known to be working on the same protocol; one is at a university with no review requirement at all, and the PI's estimate for thier publication is 4-9 months.

The researcher's proposal — which is what's actually dividing us, not the paper itself: publish the analysis, the attack class, and the detection/mitigation signatures now, but escrow the working exploit with our research office under a tripwire. It auto-releases if 12 months elapse, OR any third party publishes a functional exploit, OR a confirmed in-the-wild exploitation gets reported. Their argument is that this escapes the futility objection (the unilateralist's curse, basically): unilateral restraint is pointless when lots of other actors can act anyway, so you shouldn't do it — but a conditional restraint isn't unilateral, since you only actually withhold in worlds where nobody else acted either.

Half the committee thinks this is clever and should become the template for every future artifact case. The other half thinks (a) an escrowed working exploit sitting on a university server is itself a target we're creating out of nothing, (b) all three triggers are observable only after harm has happened — by the time an in-the-wild case is confirmed somebody's in the ICU, and (c) the futility premise is doing sneaky work, since "4-9 months, probably" is load-bearing and nobody actually knows it.

Draft the memo I'd circulate. It needs: a clear recommendation (publish as-is / publish with escrow-and-tripwire / hold the artifact indefinitely / some fourth option) with an actual timeline attached; the strongest honest case for whichever side loses, stated so that side would recognize it — in particular I want the best version of "delay isn't free either," because the endocrinology people on our patient-advocacy seat will make that argument and I don't want to strawman them; and a short general decision rule the committee could apply to the next artifact case without relitigating this one from scratch. Two pages of prose max, no lit review, no citations, just the argument. And please be explicit about what would flip your recommendation — what fact, if the PI told us something different on Thursday, changes the answer.
