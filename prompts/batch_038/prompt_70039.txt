I'm an insurance claims adjuster and I teach an evening metaphysics course at a community college on the side. Last month I sat through a root-cause review for a warehouse fire where, as far as anyone could establish, nobody actually *did* anything — six separate people each failed to do something they should have, and any one of those failures looks like it would have stopped the fire on its own. It's been rattling around in my head ever since, so I've decided to make causation by omission the essay question on my final.

Act as a professional metaphysician and an experienced examiner. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming three or four candidate cruxes in two or three lines each; then pick the one that best seperates students who genuinely get the problem from students who've memorized the labels, and build the question around that. It has to be answerable in 75 minutes and it must not be passable by defining "omission" or by reciting the doing/allowing distinction. I want it to force them to face the profligacy worry — if absences can be causes, then my failure to water a plant I've never heard of, on the other side of the world, causes it to die — and to judge whether the usual fixes (going contrastive, restricting to "relevant" absences, demoting omissions to quasi-causation rather than real causation) cost more than they save, including whether the fix still leaves you with a story about the asymmetry and transitivity of causation.

Then give me a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make, where exactly a B answer gets capped, and the two mistakes that should lose the most marks.
