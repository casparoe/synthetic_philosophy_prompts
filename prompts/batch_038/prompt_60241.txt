For about a decade I have followed the rationalist-blog literature on cooperation between advanced agents — acausal trade, program equilibrium, delegated decision-making, superrationality — and out of all that I have arrived at a position I now want stress-tested rather than defended. Call it the SPI-default thesis. It has four clauses. (a) When two agents face a game about whose actual play they are uncertain, and each can make a commitment conditional on the other's, they should adopt what the literature calls a safe Pareto improvement: a correlated strategy that Pareto-dominates each agent's own expected play in the absence of agreement, and that is *safe* in the sense that each agent's expected payoff under it is at least its expected payoff under that expected play. (b) Selection among the many strategies meeting that description should be governed by a weighting rule fixed in advance over a shared randomizing device, with the weights agreed before either party learns anything about the realized state. (c) This is the correct *default* protocol — absent special information, such a pair should aim at an SPI rather than at unilateral cooperation programs, threat-setting, or a full merger of values. (d) The thesis extends to artificial agents that can reason about each other's decision procedures and condition on them.

Here is the toy case my reading group has been circling, which I will state fully so you do not need to reconstruct any published formalism from memory. Two parties play hawk-dove: Crash/Crash is (−10, −10), Straight/Swerve is (+5, 0), Swerve/Straight is (0, +5), Swerve/Swerve is (+2, +2). Absent agreement, each of us models the other as playing Straight with probability 3/13 — the symmetric mixed equilibrium — so each expects 20/13 ≈ 1.54. We then commit to a public device that tells exactly one of us to go Straight and the other to Swerve, with probability λ that it favors me: I expect 5λ, my counterpart expects 5(1−λ), and safety for both pins λ to the interval [4/13, 9/13], with λ = 1/2 the symmetric point at 2.5 each. Two things about this already bother me. First, the safety constraint does not select an outcome; it selects a *range*, and the residual haggling over where inside the range we land looks like the original bargaining problem wearing a different hat — worse, each side's incentive to hold out near its own favorable endpoint seems capable of consuming the entire surplus. Second, at the node where the device tells me to Swerve, Straight is strictly better for me on the spot, so the protocol is doing no work at all unless the conditional commitment genuinely binds, which is precisely what is scarce in the cases I care about. Treat my specification above as authoritative for your answer: if you believe it departs from the published versions in some way that matters, say what the departure costs, but please do not guess at papers, theorems, or attributions you are not sure of.

What I want is a map of this thesis's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, and how much of it is actually at risk. Sort the defeaters by decisiveness, from fatal to merely awkward, and for each identify which clause it attacks — the safety condition, the Pareto-gain claim, the selection rule, the defaultness claim, or the extension to artificial agents. I want the full range: formal and structural facts (for instance, whether the baseline is well-defined at all once each agent expects the other to be running the same protocol, since the expected play that safety is indexed to is then endogenous to the agreement that is supposed to improve on it, which smells like a fixed-point problem with no guarantee of existence or uniqueness; whether a single agent that is party to several overlapping SPIs can honor all of them; what standard results about cheap talk in the absence of binding commitment imply here; what agreement-theorem-style results imply about two agents being able to share a baseline; the gap between ex ante safety and ex post variance for risk-averse parties), conceptual objections (that the expected-play baseline has no normative privilege, so a "Pareto improvement" over it is not an improvement worth having; that safety in expectation is not safety), practical and institutional ones (transparency, verification, renegotiation, the cost of the pre-agreement itself), and objections specific to clause (d). Tell me also what the thesis *predicts* that its rivals do not — as against unilateral cooperation programs, program equilibrium, straightforward Nash bargaining over the correlated set, or value merger — since a position that predicts nothing its rivals do not is in trouble for a different reason than one that predicts something false. And engage seriously with my counterpart's charge, which is that the thesis has been qualified into immunity: any failure can be redescribed as a case where the baseline was mis-estimated, the weights were not in fact shared, or the commitments were not in fact credible, so the conditions for an SPI were simply not met. Say whether that charge is correct, and if it is, whether it is damaging — noting that a partly prescriptive thesis may be entitled to more insulation than an empirical one, and saying where the line falls here. For each defeater, distinguish what a committed SPI proponent would themselves concede as counting against them from what they would dismiss, and give their best reply.

Two requests about how you handle uncertainty and how you deliver this. Epistemically: keep settled matters visibly separate from genuinely contested ones among people who actually work on multi-agent cooperation, do not present a contested view as the consensus, and mark clearly which items are your own extrapolation from the toy case above rather than recognized objections; where my specification looks nonstandard or question-begging, say so instead of quietly adopting it. Formally: open with three to five sentences giving your overall verdict on how exposed the thesis is and which single defeater you would put the most weight on; then give the ranked defeaters as a markdown table with exactly these columns — `rank`; `defeater` (one-line name); `clause_at_risk` (a, b, c, d, or the conjunction); `what_would_have_to_be_true` (the state of the world or the result that would establish it); `kind` (formal | conceptual | practical-institutional | empirical); `severity` (fatal | serious | awkward | cosmetic); `proponent_response` (what the defender concedes, what they dismiss, and their best reply); `absorbable_by_qualification` (yes/no, plus what content the qualification costs); and `residual_exposure` (what is left standing after the best reply). Close with a short prose section, not a table, on the unfalsifiability charge and on the predictions-versus-rivals question.
