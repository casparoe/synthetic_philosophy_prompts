Some background so you know who you're dealing with: I spent 37 years as a soil ecologist, mostly on seed banks — counting how many viable seeds are sitting in the top few centimetres of a field waiting for the right cue to germinate. Retired six years now, and I read philosophy the way I used to read other people's disciplines: badly and enthusiastically. Our Tuesday group (five of us — two retired teachers, a former prosecutor, me, and a woman who ran a hospice) spent last month on abortion, and I decided to build an argument myself in the style of the future-like-ours one, just to see whether I could make it airtight. I thought I had. Then it started producing conclusions I can't live with, and I can't find which step is the leak. I'm not after a defense of any position, only a diagnosis.

Here's what I wrote out. The numbering matters, please refer to the steps by number.

(1) What makes killing wrong, primarily, is that it takes away from the victim a future of value of the sort you and I have — call it an FLO — whether or not the victim is currently able to care about having one.

(2) Whether an act takes an FLO away depends only on this: would there, absent the act, have been a future of value belonging to some individual?

(3) A human gamete, and all the more so a freshly formed embryo, is such that absent contraception, or absent a decision not to carry, there would have been a future of value belonging to some individual.

(4) So contraception, and deliberately staying childless, take FLOs away, and are wrong for exactly the reason and to exactly the degree that killing is wrong.

(5) By the same route: a freezer failure at a fertility clinic destroys dozens of FLOs, so it is worse than a house fire that kills six children.

(6) And since (2) only asks whether there would have been a future belonging to some individual, the obligation scales with the number of conceptions you forgo — so we ought to be conceiving as many as we can support, more or less indefinitely, and every year of celibacy is a small massacre.

(7) This is what finally convinced me something's broken. I work with totipotency. In plants a single ordinary somatic cell can often be coaxed into a whole plant, and in mammals you can get an animal from a somatic nucleus dropped into an emptied egg. So each of my skin cells is such that, absent intervention, there would have been a future of value belonging to some individual. Then (2) and (3) make my morning shower a mass homicide and make pruning a hedge worse than a massacre.

(8) Therefore (1) is false: the wrongness of killing isn't about lost futures.

I'd much rather keep (1) than swallow (4) through (7). I've stared at (2) and (3) for days and cant tell whether the trouble is a missing subject — that at the gamete stage there is no one who is deprived of anything — or the counterfactual itself, which in my old field was never one determinate thing. A seed in the bank has many possible futures depending on whether the field burns, gets ploughed, gets a wet spring; we only ever talked about distributions of outcomes, never about "the" future that seed would have had. Maybe the quantifier shifts somewhere between there would have been an FLO and someone loses an FLO, and I just can't see the seam. Tell me where it is.
