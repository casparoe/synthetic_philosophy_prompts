I do the admin for a small neuropsychology unit — consent forms, participant codes, that side of things — and one of our ongoing studies tests people who've had their corpus callosum cut for epilepsy. I'm not a neuroscientist, I just push the paperwork, but the paperwork keeps throwing up something I can't stop thinking about. Every one of these people signs a single consent form with one signature, gets one participant code, occupies one row in our data spreadsheet, and when the write-up goes out it says something like "three patients took part". Outside the lab the same counting holds: one driving licence, one name on the tenancy, one person liable for their debts, one witness if they're called to give evidence, one patient in the notes. And yet the papers I file describe the same head as containing two sets of perceptions that can't be shared — flash something to one visual field and only one half knows about it — two hands that can be set against each other on the same object, and a speaking half that produces fluent, confident reasons for movements the other half started. There's a case I remember from one of the introductions, involving a teenager, where each half was asked separately what he wanted to be when he grew up and they came back with completely different answers, one of them something like a racing driver and the other something like a draughtsman. A friend of mine who reads this stuff says some philosophers take split-brain cases to show there are literally two people sharing one body, which sounds mad but I can't tell you exactly why it's mad.

So what I want to know is what our counting practice is actually committed to. When the unit treats one callosotomy patient as one participant, and the state treats them as one licence-holder and one defendant, what view of persons — of what a person is and what makes them one thing over time — does that presuppose? Is it the claim that there's a single subject of experience in there, or the weaker claim that there's a single locus of memory and character and long-term plans, or something even weaker about who's answerable to whom? Which rival pictures does the practice rule out, as opposed to merely sitting awkwardly with them — I'd like you to be careful about that difference, because I suspect a lot of the counting is just adminstrative habit and could survive almost any metaphysics. And is there a real tension between the two settings, or does the tension dissolve: in the lab we happily address the halves separately and score them as separate conditions, then in the courtroom we address one person, and I don't think anyone at our unit has ever noticed that they've changed the subject.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as fully as you can, then say where it's genuinely underdetermined — where two or three quite different accounts of personal identity or the unity of consciousness would license exactly the same forms and the same spreadsheet, so that our practice isn't evidence for any of them. I'd also like the counterfactual end: if a unit or a legal system actually adopted the two-people-in-one-body reading, what specifically would have to change in consent, in how you count participants in a study, in who is responsible for what the left hand does, and in what a witness statement from such a person is worth — and would any of those changes be workable, or would they collapse straight back into counting one? Answer in British English, and don't assume I know the technical vocabulary: if you need a term of art, define it in a clause the first time it comes up.
