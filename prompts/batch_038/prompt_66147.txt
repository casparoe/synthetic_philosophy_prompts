I'm a second-year philosophy graduate student, and last week our department's philosophy-of-science reading group had a visiting speaker whose talk was about scientific realism and the history of failed theories — caloric, the luminiferous ether, that sort of thing. I'm writing my seminar paper on the ether-drift experiments, so I was hoping to hear how he would handle Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction: the inference from the fact that our best past theories turned out to be false to the expectation that our best present theories will turn out false too. A classmate of mine pressed him on exactly that, and he answered, in effect, that the pessimistic induction simply overlooks the fact that science is self-correcting — that the community's whole apparatus of replication, criticism, and revision is what caught those old errors in the first place, so the track record of failures is evidence for our methods rather than against our theories. When my classmate objected that we only ever learn about the errors that got caught, and so the sample of "corrected errors" is selected for correctability in a way that tells us nothing about the errors still sitting undetected in current physics, he repeated the slogan with a bit more emphasis and moved on. Nobody in the room could say who originally coined the phrase, and I suspect part of why it worked rhetorically is that it sounds like a truism nobody would want to deny.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a single favored interpretation, the distinct precise claims that "science is self-correcting" might be standing for when it is deployed against the pessimistic induction? I have in mind something like a set of numbered readings, each stated as a proposition someone could actually assert or deny, differing along the dimensions that seem to matter: whether the claim is about the existence of error-detecting mechanisms, about the rate at which detected errors are actually abandoned, or about the eventual truth or merely the eventual empirical adequacy of the theories we converge on; whether the correcting agent is the scientific community as a whole, a particular discipline, or individual researchers; over what timescale the correction is supposed to occur; and whether the claim is empirical and testable, methodological and normative, or something closer to a Peirce-style thesis about what truth just is at the ideal end of inquiry. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something doubtful or flatly false, and — this is the part I most need — whether it actually licenses the move my speaker made, i.e., whether it undercuts the pessimistic inference rather than merely describing a feature of scientific practice that the pessimist can happily accept.

Please also address my classmate's selection objection directly: on which readings does that objection bite, on which does it not, and is there a version of the slogan strong enough to answer it without simply assuming that present-day physics is unlike its predecessors? If it helps, you can assume I'm comfortable with the standard technical vocabulary of the realism debate — reference failure, approximate truth, the distinction between a theory's working posits and its idle ones — but I'd rather not have the answer turn on exegetical claims about any particular author's intentions, since I won't be able to check them. I want something I can hand around at the start of the next meeting, so aim for clear labels and compact formulations, roughly a page, and finish with a short paragraph saying what I think the most charitable reconstruction of the speaker's position would have been and whether he would still have had an answer to me.
