I teach 7th grade at a small private school and I'm in charge of the character-education part of the program, which sounds grand but mostly means I run a twice-weekly period where we deal with how the kids treat each other. There's a running war in the staff room about how to do this and I can't settle it.

One colleague thinks the kids already have everything they need inside them and the job is to stop paving over it: kill the incentives to posture, stop embarrassing people in front of the group, hand them real responsibility, and the decent impulses will come up on their own. Her evidence is that every time we've cracked down harder the kids got sneakier rather than better.

The other one thinks that's a fantasy. Her view is that basically nothing good comes out of a twelve year old spontaneously, that decency is a thing you build from the outside in, by making them go through the motions — the apology script, the thank-you round, sitting still and listening to someone they dislike for ten minutes — over and over until it stops feeling like acting. She points out that every adult they look up to became that way through years of being made to do things they didn't feel like doing.

We ran her version last year. It worked better on the surface than I expected: they performed the apology script flawlessly. But two things bothered me. They performed it flawlessly only when a grown-up was in the room, and there was something flat about it, like reciting. And the year before, when we ran my other colleague's version — mostly just clearing obstacles and letting them talk things out — the term ended with them noticeably crueller to each other than they'd started, and noticeably better at explaining why what they'd done was fine.

So here's the real question, and I definately don't want to be told the answer is somewhere in the middle, because the middle is exactly what we've been doing for six years and the middle is what's failing. Is there any way to tell whether one of these two pictures of what kids are like is actually right, or is it just temperament dressed up as an argument? And connected to that: her view keeps running into something I can't phrase properly. If being good is entirely a thing installed from outside by practice and authority, where did the first authority's wish to install it come from? She just says somebody had to start, which doesn't feel like an answer to me, though maybe I'm missing something and there is one.

Concretely: I have one term left, about twelve sessions, and a tiny budget for materials. Tell me what you'd actually do with them and why. Give me your recommendation right at the top in the first paragraph, and please no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email. Its a longer thing to write than a list but I'd much rather read it that way.
