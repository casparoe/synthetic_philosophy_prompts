I have covered earth science and the history of science for a broadsheet for the best part of fifteen years, and the Anthropocene file is one I have returned to often enough that I have assembled a running table of the rulings that matter to it. I am now drafting a long read on a narrower question than the one usually asked in print — not whether humanity has altered the planet, but who is entitled to say when a new slice of deep time begins, and on what grounds that entitlement is exercised. Please treat the record below as stipulated rather than as something you need to recall, and do not supply references or a reading list; what I want is reasoning about the decision procedure, not provenance.

The record, as I have it. (i) In 2016 the working group responsible for the proposal voted to pursue a formal golden spike, thereby treating the Anthropocene as a candidate epoch rather than as a loose descriptive term. (ii) In 2019 it voted in favour of submitting a formal proposal, with the marker to be sought in the mid-twentieth century. (iii) In 2023 it selected a lake in southern Ontario, with the base of the proposed new stage placed at 1950 and the defining signal a plutonium horizon laid down by thermonuclear weapons testing. (iv) In 2024 the parent subcommission voted that proposal down by a wide margin, and the commission and the union confirmed the rejection almost unanimously; the objections pressed hardest in the debate, as I recorded them at the time, were the shallowness of the record — a few decades of sediment, much of it in cores that will not survive long in the rock — and the extreme recency of the proposed start date. (v) The same bodies nonetheless insisted, in terms, that the word remains valuable as an informal descriptor of human impact on the Earth system. (vi) An alternative onset had been canvassed in the literature during the group's earlier narrowing: a dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610, attributed by its proponents to reforestation in the Americas following the demographic collapse after European contact; it did not survive the decision to concentrate on the mid-century signal. (vii) The settled comparators on the same timescale are these: the base of the Holocene was fixed at an abrupt warming recorded in a Greenland ice core, 11,700 years before 2000; the end of the Cretaceous is marked at an iridium-rich clay layer; and in 2018 the Holocene itself was subdivided into three stages, the youngest of them opened on evidence of a prolonged drought preserved in a cave deposit in north-east India.

Now the new case, which is my own construction for the purposes of the article and should not be looked for in the literature. A group of earth scientists and historians proposes that the epoch framing be abandoned altogether, and that the commission be asked instead to recognise the Anthropocene as a formal geological *event* inside the existing Holocene — no new stage, no golden spike, no boundary stratotype, but an officially named and dated interval — with its onset placed at 1610 on the strength of the carbon dioxide dip, on the arguments that four centuries is ample depth by comparison with 1950, that the dip is globally correlatable across ice-core records, and that an event-level designation does not disturb the Quaternary framework the commission rejected the epoch proposal partly to protect. What I want from you is this. First, set out the candidate decision rules that are jointly consistent with items (i) through (vii) — I would expect at least four, and they should be stated as operative principles a body could be said to be following, not as summaries of the debate; some obvious-sounding rules should turn out to be ruled out by particular items in the record, and you should say which items do that work. Second, predict the verdict on the new proposal — adopted, adopted with modification, or rejected — with a rough degree of confidence, your reasons, and an identification of the closest analogue among the precedents together with an honest account of where that analogy breaks down. Third, to the extent the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say what principle the commission would be committing itself to by deciding either way, and what that principle would oblige it to do at the next contested boundary. Fourth, name a further case — one that could realistically arise within Quaternary stratigraphy rather than a fantasy — that would best discriminate between the leading candidate rules, and state what each of those rules predicts about it.
