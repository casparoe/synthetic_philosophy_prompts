I read a good deal of historiographical method as an amateur, and over the years I have kept coming across a dialectical pattern for which I have no name. It runs roughly as follows.

A historian argues, at length and with real evidence, that the outcome he is studying hung on contingencies: a courier detained at a checkpoint, a squall that scattered a fleet, an heir who died at twenty. Then, in a later chapter of the same book, he argues — again with real evidence — that the outcome was in any event forced by structural conditions: fiscal exhaustion, demographic pressure, the spread of printing; and that anyone who removed the contingency would find the outcome arriving by some other route. Neither argument is perfunctory, and no reader experiences the book as self-contradictory.

What I want to name is not the coexistence of those two claims, which strikes me as perfectly coherent, but what happens the moment either one is attacked. Press the contingency claim — "so your narrative is a chronicle of accidents and tells us nothing about causes?" — and the reply comes from the structural side: the accident merely selected among outcomes that were already going to be much the same. Press the structural claim — "if the outcome was forced, then your chapter on the courier is decoration" — and the reply comes from the side of incident: on the contrary, the actual course of events was genuinely open, and the structure fixes only the broad shape. The objection is consistently met one level of description away from where it landed, and the reply it receives is, locally, entirely correct. The same thing happens in the endless arguments about whether some great war was unavoidable: one side answers a structural challenge by pointing to the wrong turn taken by a motor car, and answers a challenge to that anecdote by pointing back to the alliance system, and neither side ever notices that it has changed registers, because both registers are ones it has been defending all along.

The vocabulary does not shift. "Inevitable", "contingent", "caused" and "open" keep the same sense throughout. What shifts is the resolution at which the outcome is being described: sometimes the revolution, sometimes a revolution of that general kind, sometimes the particular sequence of weeks in one autumn. Because the resolution is never marked, each claim can be defended with material appropriate to its own scale, and the pair of them together answer anything.

I have some candidate neighbours, and none of them seems to fit. It is not a motte-and-bailey, since there is no retreat from a bold claim to a modest one and back: neither of the two claims is a weakened version of the other, and they are not even commensurable. It is not equivocation, because no single word is being used in two senses — the words are stable and the objects they are applied to are what change size. "Having it both ways" and "special pleading" are too blunt to be useful. My guess is that the right neighbourhood has something to do with levels of description, or with the difference between a coarse-grained and a fine-grained way of carving up events, but I do not know whether anyone has isolated the dialectical use of that difference — the answering of every objection at an adjacent scale — as a pattern in its own right.

So: does this configuration have an established name, in the philosophy of history or anywhere else? If it does, I would like to know which of the neighbouring concepts it properly belongs to and how it is distinguished from each of the others I have mentioned. If it does not, I would like you to coin one, with a definition tight enough to be arguable, and — this matters more than the label — with boundary conditions. Because the very same movement, performed openly, looks like good practice rather than evasion: a historian who says in terms "the outcome was forced at this scale of description and open at that one, and here is why both are true" seems to me to be saying the correct thing about the past. Where, then, does the line fall between that and the pattern I have described? What feature of a given use makes it legitimate multi-scale explanation rather than a way of ensuring that no objection ever lands? I am not looking for references or a reading list; the conceptual carving is what I want. Please write your answer in British English.
