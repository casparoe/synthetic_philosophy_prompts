Our aesthetics reading group assigned me the task of defending a thesis I do not personally hold — that forgery is never itself an aesthetic failing — and the argument I assembled for the occasion has become a problem I cannot solve. It now appears to prove considerably more than the assignment required while simultaneously dismantling the premise it was built on. I would be grateful for a diagnosis before I have to present it.

The argument turns on a stipulation. Call a forgery perfect when the forged work and the original cannot be told apart by looking: no viewer, in any ordinary viewing situation, can discriminate between them, and every test that would discriminate — pigment analysis, canvas weave, dendrochronology, the paper trail of ownership — is not a way of looking at the work.

(1) Perfect forgeries are possible; the thought experiment is coherent.

(2) Where two works cannot be told apart by looking, no difference between them can bear on their aesthetic value. Aesthetic value must be available in experience, and a difference no experience could register is not a difference in what a work rewards attention with.

(3) So a perfect forgery is aesthetically on a par with the original it copies.

(4) Generalizing: any fact about a work that is not available in looking at it — who made it, when, whether first or fiftieth, what it answered to, whether it was achieved or reproduced — cannot be an aesthetic fact about it.

(5) Hence the difference between original and forgery is not aesthetic but legal and commercial. It concerns signatures, title to property, and prices; it says nothing about value.

(6) Hence nothing aesthetic is at stake in reattribution either. Move a panel from the master's own hand to his workshop, or a poem from the seventeenth century to a skilled modern imitator, or a slow movement from a bereaved composer to a program that assembled it from his sketches, and the marks on the surface are unchanged; so nothing about what makes the work worth attending to has changed.

(7) But (6) is false. Reattributions demonstrably change how informed viewers experience the very same surface, and it plainly matters to what a work is worth that something was done first, or done under difficulty, or done by a person rather than compiled.

(8) Therefore (2) is false.

And here the argument turns on me. Rejecting (2) commits me to the claim that two works might differ in aesthetic value while being indistinguishable to every viewer in every viewing situation — which abandons precisely the idea that made (2) attractive, namely that aesthetic value is a matter of experience. Worse: if provenance can enter perception, then the informed viewer and the uninformed one are not standing in the same perceptual relation to the canvas, and my opening stipulation can no longer be satisfied. There would be no perfect forgeries after all, (1) would be false, and the thought experiment that generates the puzzle could never begin. Whichever way I turn I lose a premise I wanted to keep, and I cannot tell whether the argument is invalid or valid but erected on a premise I should have refused at the outset.

So I have three requests.

First, identify the step you would reject, and explain why it looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down: what reading makes it plausible, and what the argument trades on to get from that reading to the next step. If you think more than one step has to go, rank them and say which failure is the more fundamental.

Second, price the repairs. At minimum I want the cost of each of these: restricting (2) to optical indiscernibility and accepting that some aesthetic properties are invisible; redefining indistinguishability relative to a fully informed viewer and thereby giving up (1); accepting (6) and treating the shift in how a work looks after reattribution as error rather than as insight; and relocating the relevant value in achievement rather than in appearance. For each, say which family of positions it corresponds to — empiricist or formalist accounts, historicist and relational-property accounts, achievement and performance accounts, and any informed-perception account you regard as distinct from these — and which intuitions it strands. I care less about the labels than about what each option has to surrender.

Third, tell me what to do with the historical case. Han van Meegeren's forged "Vermeers" were accepted as genuine seventeenth-century works by leading connoisseurs of the day, Abraham Bredius among them; after the exposure the standard post-mortem has been that they were never much good, and that seen without the label they belong stylistically to the interwar decades in which they were painted rather than to the century they imitated. Under (2) that shift in judgment can only be a confusion: the pictures were always mediocre, and the early praise was mistaken about a fact, not about a value. Under the rival accounts it is evidence that provenance has become visible in the work. Which is the better explanation, and does the case actually decide anything between the positions or merely look as though it does?

Two notes on how I want the answer. Be as committed as the material honestly permits: if one repair is clearly better than the rest, say so and say why, rather than serving me a balanced menu. But mark which of your judgments you take to be settled and which you take to be genuinely open, and do not manufacture confidence where the dispute is live. And if you think the way I have set this up is itself confused — that one of my steps is not a move any real position makes, that I have described the alternatives so that the choice I am asking you to price is not a genuine one, or that the third request cannot be answered from the case as I have stated it — say that plainly instead of working around it. I would much rather be told the question is malformed than be handed a polite answer to it.
