You're a political theorist who's spent real time on the democratic boundary problem — who's actually inside the demos — and who's also done some practical institutional design, which is the part I care about here. Think carefully. Before you draft anything, brainstorm out loud for a bit: what are the candidate principles for drawing the line, what does each imply in a weird case, where do they contradict each other, and which contradictions are fatal versus just annoying. Then build.

Context. I work for a small public authority in a newly built innovation district — call it the District. Roughly 9,000 residents, 22,000 people commute in every weekday, 3,000 term-time students, maybe 600 people in a temporary-housing program, four large corporate campuses (one of which houses 2,000 workers in dorms on two-year training contracts), and a phase two expected to about triple the population within five years. The District will run binding referenda plus a sortition assembly on local rules: budget allocation, short-term rentals, a congestion charge, stormwater and drainage, contracted policing. Our charter says the electoral roll has to be settled by the authority itself before the first vote, so we can't just hold an election about who gets to be in the election. That circularity is sitting right in front of us and I want the procedure to face it rather than paper over it.

Two design decisions are already made and you have to work inside them:

1. Rolls are domain-scoped. Different decision domains get different rolls — fiscal, policing, land-use/stormwater, and so on. So the output for an applicant is never just "in" or "out": it's "in on these domains, with this kind of standing," where standing is one of full vote, recorded-but-non-binding vote, notice-and-comment only, or a narrow veto on one specified thing.
2. My boss wants vote-weighting on the fiscal roll by contribution to District revenue. I think that's bad. The procedure needs to be able to actually answer that proposal rather than ignore it, either way.

What I need is a decision procedure our eligibility clerks can run. They're not lawyers, they get about ten minutes per application, there'll be something like 2,000 a month, and the applicant has to recieve a one-paragraph explanation with a reason code drawn from a fixed list that you supply. Decisions are appealable to a seven-member commission. It has to work on natural persons and on entities (employers, the campuses, a resident-run data trust). It goes live in six weeks, so nothing in it can depend on legislation we don't have.

Give me:

- the questions in order, and for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer and what an insufficient answer looks like in practice
- the fixed reason-code list, including codes that distinguish "not eligible anywhere," "eligible on domain X only," and "this isn't a clerk question"
- the point where the procedure stops at no, and the point where it hands the decision up to the commission because it's a values call wearing a fact question's clothes
- which principle is doing the tie-breaking work when the candidates conflict, named the way it's normally named in the literature (no citations or reading list, I just want the label), plus a defense of why that one and not one of the others
- the entrenchment piece: how the roll gets revised later without the whole thing collapsing back into the same circularity, and who holds that power
- an honest section on what the procedure can't do, which inputs it's faking precision about, and the categories of case it will systematicaly get wrong

Then run it on three applications: (a) a 16-year-old living in the employer dorms on a two-year training contract who wants the policing and land-use rolls; (b) one of the corporate campuses asking for a seat on the fiscal roll; (c) residents of the town downstream of us, who get flooded whenever our stormwater rules change, asking for standing on the stormwater roll. At least one of these three should come out in a way that either I or my boss really won't like. Don't sand that down — show the reasoning, then say who's going to be angry and what part of their complaint is actually right.
