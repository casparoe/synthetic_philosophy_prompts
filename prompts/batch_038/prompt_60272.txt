I'm a mathematics graduate student auditing a philosophy-of-mind seminar this term, and I've found myself unable to settle a dispute that broke out over the Lucas–Penrose line of argument. The classmate whose response paper started it put the case in a form I recognize: Penrose's argument, he says, is just Cantor's diagonal argument run on the mind. He notes that Penrose himself sets it up that way — you posit a family of procedures that, whenever they terminate, correctly certify that some specified computation fails to terminate; you build out of that family a diagonal computation; you observe that this diagonal computation itself figures somewhere in the enumeration of all computations; and you conclude that no member of the family can certify the non-termination of that particular instance, even though we can see that it does not terminate. My classmate's claim is that the structure is then exactly the structure of the proof that the reals are uncountable: given any assignment of reals to natural numbers, a new real can be read off the diagonal, and it lies outside the range of the assignment. On that basis he thinks the two most-pressed objections simply evaporate. The first is that we could never identify the algorithm we are ourselves running, so we could never construct the Gödel sentence for our own minds; he answers that Cantor's theorem is stated for an arbitrary assignment, whether or not anyone could write it down, and no one supposes that limitation weakens it. The second is that the argument requires us to know the procedure is sound, which we cannot do; he answers that this is just like assuming the assignment is total, an assumption nobody needs to have verified in advance.

Where I start to feel the analogy strain is in what the diagonal object turns out to be. In Cantor's proof the item produced from the diagonal is a real number — an inhabitant of the very collection that was listed, differing from each listed member in a specifiable place. In Penrose's construction the diagonal item is also, as he is careful to insist, an ordinary computation: it too sits in the enumeration, and the result says only that the family misclassifies it. So in both cases the diagonal item stays inside the domain, neither proof manufactures something that transcends its own universe, and neither needs a knower standing outside the argument to register the outcome. But the conclusion my classmate wants is not a theorem about listings; it is a claim about what we are, and it seems to require that somebody — the mathematician — actually be able to see the truth of the sentence the machine cannot establish, from a vantage the machine lacks. That looks like a demand with no counterpart in Cantor's proof at all. There is also the further wrinkle that Cantor's theorem sits perfectly well with the diagonal real being generated from the listing by an effective rule, whereas the anti-mechanist conclusion is supposed to be that the mathematician's recognition of the Gödel sentence cannot itself be captured by any rule we could write down; I cannot see how the analogy licenses that stronger step, given that in the mathematical case the diagonal construction is itself mechanical and in Penrose's case the diagonal computation is itself a machine.

So: audit the analogy for me. I'd like an explicit table of correspondences — the assignment of reals to naturals, the diagonal item, the hypothesis that the assignment is total, the conclusion that it is not surjective, and the standing of the whole thing as a theorem proved inside the theory it concerns — each matched against whatever plays that role in the Gödelian argument, including the soundness hypothesis and the step where the mathematician is said to see the truth of the sentence. Then give me the disanalogies, sorted into those that bear on my classmate's use of the comparison and those that are merely decorative. I especially want to know whether the soundness assumption and the unknowability of our own algorithm really enjoy, in the anti-mechanist setting, the harmless status their supposed counterparts enjoy in Cantor's, or whether the analogy conceals an extra premise about self-knowledge and self-application — a premise to the effect that the subject can be handed its own specification and reason about it — that Cantor's theorem never required. Finally, tell me what survives: if the comparison is trimmed down to only what it can genuinely carry, is there a residual argument against mechanism, and how much weaker is it than the one the seminar spent two hours debating? And if the diagonal picture is simply the wrong thing to reach for here, say what a better one would be, or whether no analogy drawn from the diagonal method can carry the intended conclusion at all.
