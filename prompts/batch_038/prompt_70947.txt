I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy — I work mostly on formal epistemology and decision theory, and our reading group has this tradition where each person has to give one talk laying out their *own* position on some topic so the room can attack it, which is terrifying but apparently good for us. My turn is next week and I picked dominance-based reasoning in normal-form games, specifically the version where you let mixed strategies do the dominating, because that's the bit I actually had to fight with when I was learning it. Below is the package I want to defend, written down as honestly as I can. I'd really rather be told it's incoherent in advance than be told in front of everyone. The group is half econ people and half philosophy people, all of them comfortable with normal-form games and best-response language, so no need to define terms.

Here's what I hold:

(1) I'm a rationalizability person. A strategy is ruled out only if it fails to be a best response to *some* belief you could coherently hold about the others' play, given that they're rational and that this is common knowledge. That's the whole justificatory basis for me.
(2) I refuse to smuggle in anything equilibrium-flavored. In particular I do not want to assume a common prior, and I don't want the concept to lean on any claim about how players' beliefs are actually generated. Common knowledge of rationality is all I'm paying for.
(3) By "a belief about the others" I mean a separate probability distribution over each opponent's remaining strategies — independent conjectures about different opponents. A joint distribution that correlates two *different people's* choices strikes me as needing a mechanism, and since (2) forbids me from positing one, I want to stay with product beliefs.
(4) Computationally I run iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, and I allow a mixed strategy to be the dominator, not just another pure one. I treat (4) as nothing more than the computational handle on (1) — I take it that the strategies that come out at the end are exactly the rationalizable ones, which is why I'm happy using dominance language to talk about best responses to beliefs.
(5) I'm also committed to order-independence: which strategy I delete first can't affect the answer, otherwise this isn't a solution concept, it's a story about my deleting habits. Though I have to admit that when I work examples by hand, different orders leave me with different sets after round one and sometimes a different number of rounds, and that worries me that (5) is just false.
(6) I half-worry that letting mixtures dominate is too aggressive: it feels like in something like matching pennies or rock-paper-scissors the mixed dominator should be able to eat the very strategies that carry the mixed equilibrium. But I also believe IESDS never deletes a Nash equilibrium, so those two feelings can't both be right and I don't know which one to drop.
(7) Once strict elimination stalls, I like to continue with iterated elimination of *weakly* dominated strategies as a tie-breaker. My justification is that it's the same argument as (4), just with a weaker inequality: a rational player won't play something that's never better and sometimes worse. Because the argument is the same, I take the safety properties to carry over — I never delete a strategy that could be a best response to something, and I never delete a Nash equilibrium.
(8) And for the same reason as (5), I assume the weak version is order-independent too, otherwise it isn't a well-defined refinement and I shouldn't be presenting it as one.
