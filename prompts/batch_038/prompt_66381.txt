Write an agony-aunt column installment of roughly 800 words: three reader letters, each with the columnist's reply, plus a closing sidebar and a short corrections box. The column is a professional advice service, and the columnist is a committed user of imprecise probabilities — she represents her degrees of belief as ranges or sets of probabilities rather than single numbers, and treats the range itself as the honest verdict on ambiguous evidence.

The satire has two targets at once, and neither may be named: the confidence of the advice-column genre, which assumes a stranger's problem can be settled in four paragraphs, and the allegation that a spread of probabilities cannot underwrite action at all. The columnist must stay inside her framework without exception. Every refusal, every hedge, and every piece of advice she gives must follow from the machinery, not from a joke about it: she is funny because she is rigorous, and the rigour keeps producing answers that a person with a life to lead cannot use.

The three letters should escalate:

1. A reader asking whether to accept a job offer. The reply must commit to an actual credal set — give the endpoints as numbers — and then choose one of the standard rules for acting under a spread of probabilities, stating in the columnist's own words what that rule says and why she prefers it to its rivals. Show the arithmetic; a formal epistemologist should be able to check it and find no error.

2. A reader reporting that she has just learned something plainly relevant and true, and that her uncertainty about the matter got wider rather than narrower. The columnist congratulates her. Explain the mechanism correctly and in the columnist's own idiom — why this can happen, and why she regards it as the update doing its job — without leaning on the technical name for the effect.

3. A reader whose friend has offered her a hundred successive even-money bets on the flips of a coin about whose bias nobody knows anything. The columnist's advice must have her decline every one, and must have an answer ready for the reader's objection that someone certain the coin was fair would accept all of them. The reader should be left feeling that something has gone wrong, while the reply remains perfectly defensible line by line.

In the sidebar, a rival columnist writing from a single precise probability answers the same job-offer letter in two sentences and is visibly happier about it; do not let the piece take either side. The corrections box should retract an earlier piece of advice on grounds internal to the framework.

Constraints: deadpan throughout, with no winking, no asides to the reader, and no character aware that they are comic. Use no real names, no citations, no titles of books or papers, and no attributions to actual philosophers; if the columnist needs authority, invent an in-universe handbook or professional body and signal plainly that it is invented. Keep the language readable for someone with no formal epistemology, so that the concepts are conveyed by the situation rather than the vocabulary. By the end, a reader should have felt the live question — whether a range of probabilities is a truer picture of thin evidence, or indecision in formal dress — without the column ever stating or settling it.
