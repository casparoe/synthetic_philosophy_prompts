**The examination question** (worth 40 marks; students were told to write about 1,000 words):

> Russell's paradox is standardly said to refute naive set theory.
> (a) State as precisely as you can what is refuted. Identify the principle that generates the contradiction, give the derivation, and say which assumptions the derivation does and does not require.
> (b) Describe two substantive restrictions on set existence that have been proposed in response. For each, explain how it blocks the derivation and what it costs.
> (c) Say whether you think the choice between these two restrictions is merely notational, and justify your answer.

**The student's answer:**

Naive set theory, as I will use the term, is the theory whose only set-existence principle is unrestricted comprehension: for any condition on objects, there is a set of all and only the objects satisfying that condition. Let the condition be non-self-membership, and let R be the resulting set, the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. If R is a member of itself, then it satisfies the condition and so is not a member of itself; if R is not a member of itself, then it satisfies the condition and so is a member of itself. Either way we have a contradiction. What has been refuted, precisely, is unrestricted comprehension, in conjunction with extensionality, classical logic, and the assumption that the domain of quantification is all-encompassing.

Three features of the derivation deserve emphasis. First, it is finitary: it needs no assumption of infinity and no assumption that a set of all sets exists. Second, the contradiction follows from a single instance of the comprehension schema, so the refutation is local: one instance is fatal, and no amount of care with the others helps. Third, the condition used is impredicative, since R is picked out by a condition that ranges over a totality including R itself.

The history bears on what counts as a response. Russell wrote to Frege in June 1902 to show that the antinomy can be derived within Frege's system by way of Basic Law V, and Frege appended an afterword to the second volume of the Grundgesetze in 1903 acknowledging the difficulty. Zermelo had found the antinomy independently by about the same time but did not publish it. Both of the standard responses accordingly date from 1908: Russell's theory of types and Zermelo's axiomatic restriction of comprehension.

For the first response, Russell restricts the language rather than the axioms. Entities are sorted into types, and within each type into orders, so that a propositional function cannot be applied to itself or to anything defined by reference to the totality to which it belongs. The condition of non-self-membership, applied to itself, is not a well-formed expression, so R cannot be stated. The cost is substantial. Ramification blocks impredicative definitions that classical analysis needs, such as the formation of least upper bounds, and Russell had to add the axiom of reducibility to recover them, an axiom that he and others regarded as unmotivated. There is a further cost specific to logicism: in Principia the existence of infinitely many individuals cannot be proved, so the axiom of infinity has to be laid down as a hypothesis about the world. Russell wanted arithmetic to consist of logical truths, and a hypothesis of that kind is not one.

For the second response, Zermelo leaves the language alone and restricts comprehension to sets already given: for any set M and any definite condition, there is a subset of M comprising exactly those members of M that satisfy the condition. Sets are built up from below rather than carved out of the whole universe. This blocks the derivation because R would have to be a subset of some previously given set, and no such set is available. More exactly, the argument shows that for any set M, the subset of M consisting of those members of M that are not members of themselves is itself not a member of M, though it is perfectly good as a subset of M. Separation does not require tampering with the logic, but it is not free either. Separation alone cannot establish that any infinite set exists, so Zermelo needed the axiom of infinity as a separate axiom, and his 1908 system also carried the axiom of choice, which was controversial for reasons independent of the paradox. It is also worth noting that in 1908 separation was a single axiom formulated in terms of definite conditions, not a first-order schema; the schema formulation comes with Skolem's clarification of 1922, and replacement was added around the same time by Fraenkel and Skolem because Zermelo's axioms could not reach such objects as the collection of the iterated power sets of the natural numbers.

One point is easy to miss. In ZF, given the axiom of foundation, non-self-membership holds of every set, so the collection it defines is the whole universe, and the universe is not a set, as Cantor's theorem shows when applied to it. The Russell collection therefore survives as a proper class. And in a sense Cantor's theorem is the deeper result: the Russell derivation is just the diagonal argument run on the identity map, which is why Cantor's theorem, the claim that no set can be matched one-to-one with its power set, follows from the paradox as a special case. Quine's New Foundations, finally, shows that the restriction need not be typed at all: NF keeps a comprehension schema for stratified conditions and it does have a universal set, while whether NF is consistent relative to ZFC remains open.

On (c), the choice between the two restrictions is largely notational. The cumulative hierarchy of ZF is the theory of types with the type indices deleted: the ranks play exactly the role the types played, and in both systems every set has its members at one and the same level, so nothing that mixes levels can be formed. Since the two systems agree about which collections exist, and differ only in that ZF lets us speak about all the levels at once, the decision between them is a decision about notation. It is also a decision that foundation has already made for us. Zermelo's system includes the axiom of foundation, and foundation is what does the real work against Russell, since by forbidding any set to be a member of itself it renders the defining condition of R vacuous and turns R into the universe, which separation then declines to treat as a set. The genuinely substantive questions, whether there are infinitely many individuals and whether an axiom of reducibility is needed, belong to the logicist programme rather than to the set-theoretic one, and both responses settle them in the same way.
