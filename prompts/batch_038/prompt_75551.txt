I sat in on a meeting yesterday that ended in an argument I couldn't referee, and I'd like you to referee it.

Context: our team forecasts regulatory outcomes. Sylvie and Omar were both assigned the same question — whether a national competition authority will block a pending merger within ninety days. They received the same briefing packet, read the same filings, and posted their numbers in a team channel that both of them read: 0.75 from Sylvie, 0.35 from Omar. They then spent an hour going through each other's reasoning line by line and finished at exactly the same two numbers. Our manager asked, half-jokingly, whether one of them must be reasoning badly. That question produced the following argument, which had nothing to do with the merger.

1. Sylvie opened with Aumann's agreement theorem: two Bayesian agents who share a prior and come to have common knowledge of one another's posteriors must have the same posterior. "We posted our numbers in a channel we both read, and we each know the other read it. So we have common knowledge of a disagreement. Either we share a prior — in which case one of us demonstrably failed to update on something — or we don't, in which case our disagreement isn't about evidence at all but about how the two of us are built. Both findings are things the manager should be told, and neither is 'we both might be fine.'"

2. Omar: the common prior is a modeling convention, not a fact about human beings. No one arrives with a prior stamped on them. A prior summarizes everything that shaped your dispositions before this evidence arrived — training, which cases you happened to see first, how many failures you personally witnessed. Two people can be perfectly consistent and still differ, because consistency is defined relative to a prior and therefore cannot be used to choose between priors. The theorem is true and inert here: its contrapositive tells us our priors aren't identical and nothing more. Getting from that to "one of us is irrational" requires an extra premise, that there is a uniquely correct prior, and Aumann never proved that.

3. Sylvie: that extra premise is just what Bayesian epistemology means by distinguishing evidence from bias. And your prior story is doing suspicious work. Training, cases you saw first, failures you witnessed — those are all evidence you have and I don't. So write them out. I'll update on them, and if we still differ afterward, the theorem has found its target. If you can't write them out, they aren't evidence, and whatever remains is a difference in processing rather than in information. Either disclose and converge, or concede.

4. Omar: false dichotomy. A prior isn't a list of facts; it's a weighting function, a set of likelihoods. I'll happily list the cases. You'll move a little and I'll move a little, but we won't land on the same number, because we assign different likelihoods to the authority's past conduct. And no test can settle whose likelihood function is right, since any test of a likelihood has itself to be interpreted through a likelihood. The regress doesn't terminate.

5. Sylvie: then you've dissolved the concept of bias. If every belief is rational relative to some prior, no belief can be criticized on evidential grounds at all, and the flat-earther and I are equally Bayesian, just differently weighted. An epistemology with no critical purchase on any actual disagreement is a reductio of your position, not a defense of it.

6. Omar: non sequitur. What dissolves is prior-independent criticism, not criticism. I can still call someone incoherent by the lights of their own prior, can still flag a failure to update on evidence they admit having, can still point at the gap between a stated prior and how someone actually bets. And notice what your reading licenses in practice: the manager goes hunting for the broken analyst. Mine licenses an honest report — the priors differ, here is the range, here is what would move each of us. Also, you've assumed we have common knowledge, and we don't. Attaining it means trading fine-grained information about each other's beliefs until nothing new is left, and the quantity of that can far exceed what an hour of conversation carries. Your theorem's guarantee concerns an idealization neither of us inhabits.

7. Sylvie: the common-knowledge escape is a distraction. The theorem needs common knowledge of the posteriors, not of the underlying information, and ours are posted in a public channel. More to the point, you're ignoring what my number does to you. Under a common prior your posterior is a sufficient statistic for your information, so learning that I said 0.75 is itself evidence about the world, and you should have moved. You didn't. That isn't a difference of priors; it's a failure to update on evidence handed to you in plain text.

8. Omar: circular. My posterior is a sufficient statistic for my information only if we share a prior. If we don't, your 0.75 tells me about your weighting function rather than about the regulator, and I'm entitled to treat it as carrying essentially no information about the merger. You're using the assumption under dispute to establish the assumption under dispute.

9. Sylvie, having the last word: on your account a prior built from thirty years of evidence is immune to criticism while the evidence in this room is criticizable, and you've given no principled place to draw that line. If a prior is shaped by evidence over a lifetime, then it is shaped by evidence, and bad evidence absorbed long ago should be correctable now. Otherwise you've built a firewall around precisely the beliefs that most need inspecting.

Nobody budged. The manager left saying both of them had a point, which satisfied no one.

Please adjudicate move by move. Begin with a bottom line of three or four sentences: who is more right overall, and about what. Then give a table with exactly these columns: `move` (number and speaker); `claim_under_dispute` (one sentence stating the specific proposition at issue, not the rhetorical thrust); `verdict` (one of: clearly right / mostly right / defensible / mostly wrong / clearly wrong); `deciding_consideration` (two to four sentences naming the principle, distinction, or result that settles it, or explaining why nothing available settles it). Follow the table with a short section titled "What both of them miss" covering assumptions they share without arguing for them, questions the manager actually asked that neither addresses, and distinctions or results neither deploys.

Throughout, separate what is settled — the logic and mathematics of the theorem itself, I take it — from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and don't dress a contested position up as the consensus. If a verdict depends on a result or distinction you aren't confident is standard, say that rather than asserting it.
