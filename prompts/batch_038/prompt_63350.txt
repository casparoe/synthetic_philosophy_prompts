A friend and I spent most of last night on Hilbert's Hotel and have arrived nowhere. He thinks no completed infinite collection can exist; I think the hotel is merely counter-intuitive. At the crux he invoked what he takes to be a necessary truth about any collection whatsoever, the Euclidean common notion that 'the whole is greater than the part'. His reasoning was that, once the new traveller has been housed, the guests already in the hotel form a proper part of the total guest list while remaining exactly as numerous as that total, so the hotel is not merely queer but contradictory — and contradiction is proof of impossibility. When I pointed out that Dedekind made this very failure the definition of an infinite collection rather than a refutation of one, he said I was simply assuming what he denies.

Could you draw up the distinct precise claims that one sentence might be making, as a list I can bring back to him? I can already think of several candidates: a principle about continuous magnitudes rather than about how many things there are; a principle about parthood rather than about cardinality; a claim that holds only where the collections in question are finite; and the bare metaphysical thesis that nothing stands in one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of itself. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false; whether it survives the Dedekind point or is nothing more than the conclusion he is trying to establish; and, above all, which of them would actually carry his step from 'the hotel is bizarre' to 'the hotel cannot exist'. Please write in British English.
