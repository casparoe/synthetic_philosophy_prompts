I'm one of five voting members on the technical steering committee for an open source library called Halyard. It's a reference implementation of a signed-message format that a bunch of payments companies use for audit logs — nothing glamorous, but it's load bearing for a suprising amount of infrastructure. We're all volunteers except me; my employer lets me spend a day a week on it. Two months ago a company we'll call Northmark sent us Contribution Proposal 41: they want to upstream roughly 30k lines of a high-throughput signer they built internally, plus fund three full-time maintainers for two years. In exchange they want two of the five TSC seats and a written commitment that the wire format won't change incompatibly for 24 months. Meanwhile Rivet, the rival project in this space, took a nearly identical deal from a different vendor fourteen months ago, and since then their adoption has roughly tripled and two clearing houses have moved off us onto them.

The committee is split 2–2 with me in the middle. The pro-accept side says if we say no, Rivet becomes the de facto standard anyway and the ecosystem ends up with a vendor-controlled format regardless, except we don't get the code or the maintainers, so we lose twice. The anti side says the moment we take the deal, every project like ours is up for sale to whoever pays most, and the thing that made an open standards body worth anything quietly stops existing — and we'd be the ones who set the precedent. Two members want to defer six months and see what Rivet's governance actually looks like by then. Our charter says decisions should be made "in the interest of the ecosystem as a whole," which obviously nobody ever defined. One colleague floated a middle option I don't fully follow: take the code and the funding, refuse the seats, and move the format spec itself into a seperate one-member-one-vote body so that no single contributor can ever buy control of the wire format even if they effectively own the implementation. I can't tell whether that's a real structural fix or just something that sounds good in a meeting and falls apart the first time Northmark threatens to walk.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's call. I need an actual bottom line — accept, decline, or accept under specific conditions — and if it's conditions, spell them out precisely enough that someone could turn them into charter language, not just "with appropriate safeguards." Say explicitly whether the six-month deferral is a live option or a decision in disguise, and what it costs us either way. Then give the strongest honest case for the option you're rejecting, phrased so the two people on that side of the table would recognise it as their own view rather than a strawman. Heads up: I read something once about "multipolar traps" and this feels like one, but I have no formal background in any of this, I'm just an engineer, so don't assume I know the vocabulary — and don't send me a reading list or references, I want your reasoning, not pointers. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the recommendation overall and single out the one point in it you're least sure about; if there's a load-bearing assumption I'd rather find out before Thursday than after.
