I am a staff writer at a monthly magazine covering science and ideas, and I sit on our five-person Standards and Corrections Committee, which has final say over changes to the archive and meets the first Tuesday of each month. I need a memo I can circulate to the other four members by Friday. Our charter requires a correction to be posted within thirty days of a substantiated complaint; this one arrived twenty-two days ago.

The policy we are applying has two clauses, written years apart and never, so far as I know, tested against a case like this. Clause 1: we correct any published statement of fact that we know to be untrue. Clause 2: we do not publish a correction we are not prepared to stand behind as accurate.

The case. Seventeen months ago we ran a feature on sleep and metabolic disease stating flatly that sleeping fewer than six hours a night roughly doubles a person's risk of the condition in question. That sentence was a fair summary of the best evidence then available. A larger and better-designed study has since appeared; it finds a much smaller association, confined to a subgroup, and several of its authors argue the earlier figure was inflated by confounding. The sentence as published is not true, so Clause 1 is triggered. What to put in its place is the difficulty, and four proposals are on the table.

(a) The scientist who wrote to us proposes this replacement: the elevated risk is real but modest, on the order of twenty percent, and appears only among carriers of a certain common variant. She is candid that it rests on one study with wide confidence intervals and expects the figure to move. On her own account, it is not something we know to be true. Clause 2 appears to forbid it.

(b) Our managing editor proposes a formulation we can guarantee: the evidence that short sleep raises the risk of this condition is weaker and more contested than many news reports have suggested. That is certainly accurate. It is also compatible with the risk being doubled, with its being nil, and with almost anything in between, and a reader who meets it learns very nearly nothing.

(c) Leave the sentence standing and append a note that the piece reflects the evidence as of publication.

(d) Run the scientist's replacement, flagged as provisional, with the uncertainty reported.

One member of the committee, a former logician and the sharpest of us, thinks the entire exercise is a fraud and that the memo should say so rather than paper over it. Her argument, as I understand it, has three parts. First, closeness to the truth is not one relation but a trade-off between two that pull against each other: a claim can be made more accurate only by making it less informative, and there is no principled exchange rate between the two, so the committee is not ranking proposals at all but choosing a point on a curve according to taste. Second, on the approaches that do try to rank claims by their parts, the verdict is not stable under rephrasing: two ways of stating the same claim, each translatable into the other without loss, can come out differently placed depending on which components you count, so what looks like a judgment about the world is partly a judgment about our sentence. Third, the obvious way of cashing out closeness — weighing what a claim gets right against what it gets wrong — is known to break down, in that under it no two false claims can be ranked against one another unless they say the same thing, which means the notion our policy implicitly invokes has already failed once, formally, and we would be reviving it in house style. Her conclusion: either we stand behind a sentence as accurate or we do not, and (b) is the only honest option, however empty. She calls the property at issue truthlikeness; I give you the term only so you know what she means, and I would rather the memo not become a survey of it.

Please give me a decision document. I want: a recommendation among (a) through (d), or a fifth option if you think all four are wrong, with a posting deadline; the reasoning in a form the other members can actually argue against; a specific revision to the policy language if you think Clauses 1 and 2 cannot both survive this case; a short set of criteria an editor could apply next Tuesday without consulting me; what this decision commits us to in the next such case, since there will be one; and two or three sentences I can send the complaining scientist, whose proposal we may be declining in part. Take the logician's objection at full strength — answer it rather than concede it, and say plainly which part of it survives your answer and which does not. Argue from the case; you do not need to cite anything or sketch the history of the idea.
