Next month's episode of a political theory podcast I produce features a guest who defends what she calls full sortition: the national legislature should be composed entirely of citizens selected at random, by stratified lottery, for short non-renewable overlapping terms, with service compulsory and enforced in the manner of jury duty, and elections for that chamber abolished outright. Three further commitments of hers should shape the questions. She holds that the familiar self-selection objection dissolves under compulsion, since a mandatory summons produces a microcosm by force rather than a gathering of enthusiasts and ideologues. She holds that electoral accountability is a defect rather than an asset — the need for re-election is precisely what bends judgment toward donors, news cycles, and the median swing voter — so she does not merely absorb the accountability objection but welcomes it. And she maintains that descriptive representation carries intrinsic normative weight: an assembly that resembles the electorate is better for resembling it, and not only because of the perspectives it thereby imports. She also has a signature deflection, which I want the sequence built against: whenever an institutional objection is pressed, she replies that the objection is really comparative, that elections fail the same test far worse, and that her critics are holding sortition to a standard no actual political system could satisfy.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each accompanied by a single follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. The questions should engage the substance of the position rather than invite restatement, and I want at least one on each of the following: how compulsion behaves in practice — exemptions, hardship deferrals, who actually ends up in the room, and whether a penalty-backed summons yields a chamber of the conscripted; what stands in for accountability once re-election is removed, including the mid-term removal of a member who proves corrupt or incompetent, and the unelected secretariat that sets the agenda, commissions the testimony, and briefs the members; whether descriptive similarity can bear normative weight when the members have no constituency to answer to, and what stratifying the draw by category does to her claim that sortition escapes identity-based campaigning; how a chamber with no parties and no mechanism of confidence produces an executive and keeps it under control; the distance between the mini-publics that have demonstrably worked — temporary, single-issue, expert-staffed, advisory, and legitimated afterward by an election or a referendum, as in Ireland, and in British Columbia, where the citizens' assembly's electoral-reform proposal went to a province-wide vote and did not carry — and a permanent, general, decisional legislature; the protection of small minorities under a statistically typical assembly; and why she rejects the modest versions of her own proposal, a sorted second chamber or sortition applied to discrete decisions, in favor of the radical one.

Sequence the questions so that the comparative deflection becomes progressively harder to deploy, and close with one that cannot be answered comparatively at all: what observation from a sorted legislature actually in operation would move her to abandon the view, or to retreat to the second-chamber version. Two further constraints: no biographical or scene-setting questions, and every question must be deliverable aloud in a single breath, with no stacking of three sub-questions into one. Finally, indicate which two questions you would cut if the first half of the recording runs long, and explain why those are the least load-bearing.
