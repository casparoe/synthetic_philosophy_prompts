I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar on modality in the spring and for week one I have the students attack my own written modal profile before we've read anything, which is a humiliating but effective exercise. I've just finished the profile and I want a second pair of eyes on it before I hand it out, because I have a nagging sense that at least two of these commitments are pulling against each other and I can't locate which. Here's the list, in the order I'd sign it:

(1) Origin essentialism about persons. I could not have come from any egg and sperm other than the ones I actually came from. I don't hold this on the strength of the usual table-and-molecule intuitions; I hold it because I think transworld identity has to be anchored in something non-arbitrary, and the zygote is the only candidate that isn't question-begging. (2) Anti-haecceitism. Two worlds that are exact qualitative duplicates are not two worlds; there's no bare thisness floating free of the qualitative facts doing individuating work. (3) A principle I take myself to need in order to argue for (1) rather than merely assert it: the same egg and the same sperm couldn't have given rise to two seperate people. Origin, when it's the right kind of origin, pins down the individual. (4) Monozygotic twins are two numerically distinct people. I've written this more or less in print. Nothing metaphysically interesting happens in twinning; it's embryology, and I don't think metaphysics gets a vote about it. (5) I'm not an animalist. What I fundamentally am is a Lockean person, a psychologically continuous being, and I take it I came into existence somewhere around when that continuity got going — well after fertilization. The organism sitting in my chair overlaps me but isn't me, and part of why I'm a Lockean is that the organism is contingent for me: the transplant cases seem to me to show that I could have been embodied by a different one. (6) I also want to keep the ordinary thought that my parents could have conceived me a few days earlier or a few days later and it would still have been me. I don't think this is in tension with (1), and I've said as much in class: what shifts is the timing, not the gametes. Same egg, same sperm, different evening. (7) Finally, I hold that my existence is contingent in the strongest sense — there are worlds in which I never existed at all — and a colleague once told me that's flatly incompatible with (1). I've never seen why; essentiality of origin doesn't look to me like a claim about whether I exist anywhere.

So: audit the package. I'm not looking for a survey of the literature and I definitely don't want a reading list — I know the literature, I just can't see the joints in my own view. What I want is the tracing: which of these can't all be true together, which merely look like they can't, and what would have to go. Don't flag everything as problematic to be safe; if only two of these are genuinely in trouble, say so and say which. Please use exactly these four sections in this order. "Bottom line" — four sentences maximum, no throat-clearing, tell me how bad the damage is. "Damage report" — the real conflicts, ranked worst to least bad, each tagged severe/moderate/cosmetic, each with a tight explanation of exactly why the two claims can't coexist (spell out the derivation, don't just gesture) and a statement of what would have to give. Around 700 words here. "False alarms" — the pairs that only look like they conflict and the distinction that dissolves each one; about 200 words. "Cheapest repair" — the single revision that buys back the most consistency across the whole set, what it lets me keep, and precisely what I'd have to stop saying as a result; about 250 words. If the cheapest repair is not the one you'd recommend, say that in one line at the end.
