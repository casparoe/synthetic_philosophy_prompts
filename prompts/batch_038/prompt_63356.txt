I want a short story, not an essay, about the doing/allowing line — killing vs letting die — but set somewhere the line is genuinely hard to find rather than being another trolley problem in a hard hat. Here's the setup I have in mind:

A regional reservoir, in one of those years where the drought breaks with a storm. There are two spillways: an old manual set of gates and a newer automatic system that got installed after an internal review found the operators too willing to dump water early and leave nothing in reserve. The authority had spent the previous decade under a "carry water forward" policy — hold back in the wet years against a dry year — and that policy is why the thing is now full to the brim when the rain arrives. When it arrives, the automation is doing exactly what it was built to do: hold. To get water out early, the night operator has to keep overriding it, cycle after cycle, for hours, and every override is a small deliberate act of interference with a system that is otherwise working. To let the reservoir do its own thing she has to keep her hands off — except that at some point the automation faults and defaults toward release, so "not intervening" starts to require intervention, and the other way round too. Downstream there are two valleys: the one that floods if she takes water out early, and the one that floods if she doesn't.

What I want the story actually chewing on:

1. One character holds that whether you did something or merely let it happen isn't about outcomes at all but about the shape of the alternatives — if only a few of the many things you could have done would have brought the bad thing about, you did it; if most of them would have prevented it, you let it happen. Have her notice, out loud and uncomfortably, that her own night is a mess by that measure: that "doing nothing" here took more movement, and more deliberate movement, than acting would have.
2. Another character thinks the whole distinction is bookkeeping that tracks nothing real. What she cares about is whether anyone ended up worse off than they would have under better choices, and she's irritated that people are going to fight over verbs in the incident report while the valleys are still underwater.
3. End on the wording of that report — the sentence neither of them can sign off on — and dont let the narrative take a side. No closing line that winks at the reader about how unsolvable it all is.

Keep it around 1,400 words. Make it concrete and physical: gate noise, the phone calls from the duty engineer, the shift handover at four in the morning, the coffee nobody drinks. Nobody gives a philosophy lecture; the arguments should come out as exhausted people talking past eachother.

Two asks about how you handle it. First, after the story, tell me where you bent or simplified the ideas — I know fiction can't argue a case properly, and if you leaned on a contested reading to make the plot work I'd rather you flag it than smuggle it in. Second: if you think what I've asked for is confused — if the automation twist actually collapses the distinction I want complicated instead of deepening it, or if you think a story framed this way can only end up as a transposition of a case everyone's already read — say so plainly before or instead of writing. I'd much rather get "this doesn't work and here's why" than a compliant story that quietly ducks the thing I care about.

Format: two sections in this order — "The Story" (~1,400 words) and "Where I Bent It" (250–350 words of prose, not a bulleted list of caveats).
