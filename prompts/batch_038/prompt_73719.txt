I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am assembling a light piece to run alongside a report on the doctrine that a person's preferences are nothing over and above her choices — the operationalist programme in economics that dispensed with feelings, wishes and inner states on the grounds that only behaviour is observable and therefore only behaviour is testable. I would like to commission a satire, and I would rather it be accurate than merely clever.

The form: an owner's manual, in the flat impersonal register of manufacturer's documentation, for a fictional instrument — call it the Revealer 7, made by Ordinal Instruments — which measures human welfare directly from observed choice. Around 800 words. Sections should include: what is in the box; first calibration; how to read the display; a troubleshooting table; care and maintenance; known limitations; and the limited warranty. Audience: readers who have never taken an economics course, but the jokes must land for someone who has.

A few beats to seed, the rest is yours. The display should show an ordinal position and nothing else, and the manual should warn, with real irritation, against adding readings, averaging them, or placing two units side by side; the warranty is void if a comparison is attempted. There is no microphone and no questionnaire port, and this should be presented as the instrument's proudest feature. Consent forms are not supplied, on the reasoning that a subject who remains within the observation field has chosen to remain there. Include a consistency routine of the kind economists actually use to test whether a body of observed purchases could have come from any coherent ranking at all, and say what the technician is told to do about a subject who cycles and can therefore be pumped.

The humour must come from the doctrine following through on itself rather than from economists being mocked, so the manual stays deadpan and never winks. Every limitation it discloses should be a genuine objection rather than an invented one. Specifically, I want it to cover: a subject who says she wants one thing while choosing another, which generates no error message, because on the instrument's own definitions her statement is not false but meaningless; the order in which options are presented and the status of defaults, and the resulting difficulty that the machine reports a firm preference for whatever the subject did not opt out of; subjects whose aspirations have contracted to fit their circumstances, whom the manual should classify as excellently calibrated and award the highest available reading; and subjects who do not recognise the choice attributed to them and who confabulate reasons for a choice they never made — there is a real experimental literature on this — whose reports the manual files under noise. Somewhere, ideally in care and maintenance, the manufacturer should explain in its own voice why the subject's own account of her welfare is never consulted, and this should be the most persuasive paragraph in the piece: the instrument is sold on being falsifiable, so the manual must say what observation would count against a reading.

Two further requirements. First, fairness: the doctrine had a serious purpose — to purge the science of unobservable mentalism and make its claims testable — and that purpose should come through as the manufacturer's pride, so that a defender of the view recognises it and finds the satire fair rather than a strawman. At least one clause should be one its defenders would happily sign. Second, the reductio should be structural: by the end, without a word of authorial commentary, the reader should have felt the question of whether an instrument that cannot register the difference between contented choice and resigned choice measures welfare at all, or has merely renamed behaviour and announced the renaming as a discovery. Do not answer the question and do not break register. No real economist should be named or quoted; the manufacturer, the model, the technicians and the safety symbols are all invented.
