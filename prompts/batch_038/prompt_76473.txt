I'm a staff engineer with no formal training in philosophy or the history of science, so please write for someone who will not recognize the vocabulary unless you gloss it on first use.

**The situation.** Our team shipped a distributed build cache last year. Nine months later a competitor shipped something functionally equivalent, and a third group released a preprint containing the same core trick, reached by a different route, with no citation to us and no citation between any of the three parties. In the retrospective the team split into two camps that have not budged since.

Camp A says the idea was in the air: anyone working under the same constraints — the same hardware, the same latency budget, the same public benchmarks — would have gotten there within a year or two, so our "invention" is really just our arrival, and the interesting question is why so many groups arrived at once.

Camp B says the trick was a coin flip: the specific move that made it work was not forced by the constraints, at least two competent teams tried and failed for years, and if the two of us who thought of it had been assigned to something else the technique would not exist yet.

I want to end this with an instrument rather than with more anecdotes. What I have come to suspect is that the real problem is not which camp is right about our build cache; it is that nobody in the room can say what "inevitable" is supposed to mean, and that the two camps may not even be contradicting each other.

**Deliverable.** A decision tree — or a taxonomy laid out as a tree — that sorts positions on two linked questions, *was the discovery inevitable* and *does it matter who made it*, into named positions. I want something I could literally walk a room of engineers through at a retro, one node at a time.

**Requirements.**

1. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a roster of famous names. First state, in order, the questions that separate positions and show which combinations of answers those questions permit; then locate actual positions in that space. Where a leaf has a name in the literature, give it plus a one-line note on how standard you think the name is; where it does not, label the leaf "unnamed" and diagnose that in one line — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Do the same for combinations nobody occupies.

2. Five to seven branch questions and eight to twelve leaves. Every branch question must be answerable by a working engineer reasoning about a concrete case, without knowing which leaf the answer leads to. So nothing of the form "do you think great individuals drive progress?" — that just asks people to pick a label and report it back to you. Good nodes look like: *if the two of us had left the company in 2022, would the technique exist today in a form someone else could actually use, and how confident are you?* or *does the second group's version count as the same discovery if the underlying idea is identical but the notation, the interface, and the way it composes with everything else are different?*

3. Three coinages of mine, at least one of which I expect is a distinction without a difference. Tell me which survive and which collapse.
   - **Arrival-window inevitability versus trajectory inevitability**: whether the result would have appeared inside some bounded window given the state of the field, versus whether the field was going to pass through that step on the way to whatever came next regardless of who took it.
   - **The usability threshold**: the moment a result stops belonging to its author and becomes something other people can use — a working implementation, a notation, a benchmark others can run against. My claim is that "who discovered it" and "who made it usable" come apart here, and that our cache crossed the threshold only when the third group published, not when we shipped.
   - **Silent multiples**: independent arrivals that are never recognized as the same result, and so never enter the statistics on multiple discovery at all. In software I think this is the common case rather than an edge case, because nobody reads the older literature — and if it is common, it may undermine any inference from the multiples we *do* observe to a general claim about inevitability.

4. Leaves the tree must be able to reach, whatever else it reaches: the view that multiple discovery is the normal pattern rather than a curiosity; the view that what decides a discovery is the readiness of the field rather than the person; the view that arrival order is contingent all the way down and only looks patterned because we notice the successes; and the view that inevitability is graded and relative to how finely you describe the result, so that the question is not well formed until you fix a level of description. Include at least one leaf on the credit question that gives a reason nobody in my team has offered — that the first version's framing persists and constrains everything subsequently built on top of it.

5. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, meaning two positions that give the same answer to every one of your questions, and say what evidence, result, or thought experiment would separate them if anything could. Do not paper over those pairs.

6. Calibration. Run three cases through the tree and give the leaf for each: Newton and Leibniz on the calculus; Darwin and Wallace on natural selection; and the two distributed version-control systems written within days of each other in April 2005, by Torvalds and by Mackall, after the vendor withdrew the free version of the proprietary tool the Linux kernel project had been using. Then tell me whether the tree delivers the same verdict for my own case — the build cache team and the preprint group — and, if it does not, whether that is a defect in the tree or a real difference between the cases.

7. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations. I have no library access and cannot check them, and a plausible-looking reference I cannot verify is worse to me than none at all. Names of positions are welcome; names of people only where you are confident.

**How committed I want you to be.** Separate clearly what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not let a contested view travel as the consensus. Where you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting one, mark it as yours. If one of my three coinages is confused rather than merely unorthodox, say so directly instead of accommodating it — I would rather have a correct tree than a flattering one.

**End state.** For the record, my own intuitions are: I think the result was going to exist within a couple of years; I think our particular formulation was not forced; I think the third group's publication, not our release, is what made the thing usable by anyone outside our team; and I think arrival order matters mainly because whoever gets there first sets the framing everyone else inherits. Run those intuitions through your tree, say which leaf they land on and whether they form a coherent position at all, and close your answer with one final line of the form \answer{...} containing nothing but the name of that leaf.
