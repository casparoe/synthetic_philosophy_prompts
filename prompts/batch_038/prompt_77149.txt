Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of four hours about whether reading lives teaches anything moral at all, and while neither of us persuaded the other, the argument dug up something I have been noticing for years and have no vocabulary for. I want to know whether it is a recognized phenomenon and, if it is, what it is called; if it is not, I want help defining it carefully, because I suspect the familiar candidates all name something adjacent rather than this.

Here is the pattern. I recently read a long, well-reviewed life of a nineteenth-century reformer — I will leave him unnamed, since the case does not depend on him. By every account he was unbearable: he humiliated his subordinates, drove his son abroad, and treated the movement's money as his own. The book is perfectly candid about all of it and argues, convincingly, that the traits are inseparable from the achievement; the same ferocity that wrecked his household is what kept a collapsing campaign alive for eleven years. I came away admiring him, which I expected. What I did not expect was to discover, over the following months, that my threshold for condemning that sort of conduct in other people had risen. When a colleague complained about a chair who behaves much the same way, I heard myself saying that results count and that we should be slow to judge people by how pleasant they are to work with. I had learned nothing about chairs. My sense of how bad the thing is had been quietly recalibrated by a book about somebody else entirely.

The reverse direction strikes me as more diagnostic. When someone produces evidence of a genuinely bad act by a figure I admire, my first move is never to subtract from the admiration; it is to upgrade the description of the act. Cruelty becomes severity, severity becomes firmness, firmness becomes the loneliness of a man who understood the stakes better than his critics. Each step is locally plausible, and none of them is prompted by new information about the act itself.

And a third thing, which my friend insisted was really the same thing wearing a different hat: I find myself treating contingent biographical detail as though it carried normative weight. The reformer's celebrated austerity turns out to have been enforced by a digestive illness; his most admired refusal to compromise follows hard on the death of his wife. Learning this does not dent the admiration — but neither does it stop me from citing the austerity as a model. The detail does no justificatory work whatsoever, and yet it functions in my reasoning exactly like a reason.

So: is there an established concept for this, for the way that sustained attention to an admirable life loosens the reader's grip on which of that person's traits are vices at all? Let me say where I have looked and why each attempt seems to miss. Hagiography names a genre's idealization of its subject, but what I am describing happens with sober, critical, fault-finding biographies — indeed I think it happens more readily with them, precisely because they have earned the right to be believed — and it happens on the reader's side rather than the author's. Plutarch plainly did not suppose that a subject had to be spotless to serve as a model, since his lives are full of faults recorded without apology, and yet I do not recall anyone in that tradition worrying that the honest portrait might damage the reader's standards rather than merely failing to inspire them. The halo effect, as I understand it, concerns inference from observed traits to unobserved ones — brave, therefore probably honest — whereas mine is not an inference about the person but a revision of the yardstick: brutality stops registering as brutality, in general and not only in him. There is evidently a body of work treating moral exemplars and admiration as an instrument for detecting goodness, and I imagine that is the nearest neighbourhood, but my impression is that it is chiefly concerned with whether admiration tracks the admirable, not with the collateral effect on the admirer's evaluation of neighbouring traits. And ordinary talk of role models concerns imitation of the trait one admires, whereas what I find myself imitating and defending is precisely the trait nobody would recommend imitating.

What I want, then, is the carving rather than the label. Tell me which established concept comes closest, and then say exactly what separates it from what I have described — if the difference is only one of degree, say so and tell me where the degree runs out. If nothing fits, coin a term and give me a definition with real edges, one that answers the questions my friend and I could not settle: whether the same thing happens with invented characters and with institutions such as universities or companies; whether it requires the subject to be admirable on the whole, or whether a largely wretched subject can produce it too; whether the upgrading of the bad act and the rising tolerance for the bad trait are one mechanism or two that merely travel together; and whether any of this is a distinct failure at all, or simply the ordinary tendency to be moved by vivid narrative, which would make my whole evening's argument about nothing. I should add that my friend's closing position was that I have described nothing more interesting than inconsistency, and that a person who cannot say why brutality is bad without a biography to remind him never knew it in the first place. If you think he is right, I would like to hear the argument properly rather than a name I can put on a shelf.
