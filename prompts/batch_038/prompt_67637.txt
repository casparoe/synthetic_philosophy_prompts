I'm a staff engineer on a payments platform team (fourteen of us, roughly forty production changes a day), and for the last quarter I've been drafting our deployment-gating policy — the document that says when an engineer is permitted to declare a change safe and ship it. Trying to write that policy forced me to notice that I have a set of half-formed views about what it takes to actually *know* something, and after a long argument with a colleague who reads philosophy on his own time, I suspect they don't cohere. He told me the position was unstable but wouldn't say where, and I have no formal background — my last humanities course was a first-year writing seminar about fifteen years ago — so I can't tell whether he spotted a real contradiction or was just trading on terms I don't know. What follows is my honest attempt to write down everything I actually believe about this, without dressing it up. I'd like a rigorous audit of the set rather than a tutorial.

1. Whether someone knows a proposition can depend on how much rides on being right, not only on their evidence. Concretely: same engineer, same log output, same priors, same understanding of the system. On a quiet Tuesday afternoon with nothing live in production, they know the migration is idempotent. The same person at 2 a.m. in the middle of a payment outage, deciding whether to re-run a schema change that could double-charge customers, does not know it — nothing about their evidence changed, only the cost of being wrong. I looked this up afterward and I believe the view is called pragmatic encroachment, and it seems right to me.
2. Separately, whether a knowledge claim is true seems to depend on the context of the person making it. If I type into our incident channel that the on-call engineer knew the retry storm was coming, that can be true; and it can also be true when our director says in the postmortem three days later that nobody knew. The engineer's evidence is identical across both utterances — only who is speaking, and what standards are in play, has shifted. I believe this as well, and I take it this is contextualism.
3. Justification, though, is purely a matter of evidence. Two engineers with the same evidence and the same competence are in exactly the same evidential position, full stop. What differs in (1), as I see it, is not how well supported the belief is but whether a belief that well supported clears the bar for counting as knowledge.
4. Knowledge is what entitles you to act and to assert flatly. If you don't know, you hedge or you go collect more evidence; if you do know, hedging is a kind of evasion. This is the principle I want the policy to rest on: ship when you know, don't ship when you don't, and don't pad the language in either direction.
5. Knowledge is closed under obvious consequence. If you know the migration is idempotent, and you know that idempotency implies a re-run cannot duplicate charges, then you are in a position to know that a re-run cannot duplicate charges. I don't want a theory on which you can know the premise, know the entailment, and still be barred from the conclusion.
6. We write everything down — runbooks, change tickets, dashboard annotations, wiki pages that get read months later by people in completely different situations. I'm confident that a sentence typed into a change ticket by an engineer on a slow afternoon can transmit knowledge to a director reading it during a Sev-1 six months later: the reader can come to know what the writer knew, and the writer's low-stakes context does not cap what the reader may properly rely on. Here is the specific verdict I'm most attached to. The writer was entitled to put "rollback path tested and verified" in the ticket; the director reading it during the outage is entitled to rely on it; and if the postmortem afterward says nobody on that call knew the rollback path worked, that postmortem is not merely adopting stricter standards, it is being unfair to both of them, and in some sense simply wrong.

Please go through these six and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and for each one state the cheapest revision that would resolve it together with what that revision costs me — in particular, if fixing a tension means (4) has to be weakened or replaced, tell me what could serve as the deploy criterion in its place, since the policy document has to say something. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most damage across the whole set. A few constraints on the answer: don't give me references, reading lists, or attributions to named philosophers beyond the two labels I already used, because I can't chase any of them and they aren't what I'm asking for; work entirely with my six claims and with cases in my own idiom. If any claim turns out to be ambiguous — defensible on one reading, untenable on another — split it into its readings and audit those separately instead of declaring the whole thing inconsistent. And if what I've written in one of these slots isn't a claim at all but a confusion about what the disagreement is even about, say that plainly; I suspect that's part of what my colleague was getting at and I couldn't extract it from him.
