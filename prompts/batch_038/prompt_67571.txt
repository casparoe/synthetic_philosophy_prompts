You are a philosopher of history who has also worked seriously on the metaphysics of causation and on moral and legal responsibility. Please think carefully before answering, and work through the structure of the dispute rather than producing a survey.

Last month I sat in on the informal discussion after a departmental reading group. I am not a member of the group, and neither of the two people arguing was addressing me, but I took notes, and I have not been able to resolve the exchange since. The nominal topic was the origins of the Cold War; the actual argument was about whether a certain question is well posed. I will reconstruct it in order, numbering the moves.

(1) Marcus began by saying that "who started the Cold War?" is an ordinary historical question, and that the popular answer — mutual suspicion, both sides, nobody — is not a neutral default but a substantive claim that has to be earned like any other. On his view, declining to assign responsibility is not a suspension of judgment; it is itself a verdict, and the least well supported one available.

(2) Ines said the question is not answerable because it is not well formed. A war can be started by an act; the Cold War was not a war but a condition of relations lasting four decades, and conditions have histories rather than authors. When we ask who started a condition, we select one moment and one description of it, and the selection, not the evidence, does the work. She added that even the noun was supplied by contemporaries within a couple of years of the Second World War's end, so the label had already packaged four decades of hostility into a single thing with a single beginning before any historian got to work on it.

(3) Marcus replied that long processes have origins, and origins can be asymmetric. Two neighbours who shove each other for years are engaged in a pattern too, yet there remains a fact about who shoved first and about whose shoves were retaliations of whose. He said the question can be made precise as a counterfactual — would the antagonism have taken the shape it actually took but for one side's decisions? — and that counterfactuals about states of affairs are no less respectable than counterfactuals about events. Her objection, he said, is a request for precision, not a refutation.

(4) Ines granted that the counterfactual is well formed but denied that it can settle anything, since for every candidate first act she could name an earlier act by the other side that made it foreseeable. Her illustration: a stretch of years in which one side held a monopoly of nuclear weapons while the other held overwhelming conventional superiority on the continent where the confrontation was actually going to be fought, and in which each side took measures — economic arrangements that excluded the other, spheres of influence consolidated by force or by veto — that look defensive from inside and aggressive from outside. Which of these gets nominated as the origin, she said, is fixed by what one already believes each side was entitled to want; the baseline is a normative judgment about legitimate interests wearing the clothes of a causal question. She further noted that a security-dilemma structure can generate antagonism neither side intends, so "neither started it" can be a positive causal claim about structure rather than a refusal to choose.

(5) Marcus said she had proved too much. Some baselines are not merely contestable but indefensible — for example, treating one side's security interests as presumptively legitimate and the other's as presumptively expansionist — and the choice among the rest is disciplined by ordinary evidence: internal memoranda recording what decision-makers expected, the sequence in which decisions were taken, whether an actor was responding to something already done. He also pressed a reductio: if her argument were sound, no one could ever be responsible for a long-running pattern, and he offered the case of a campaign of mass killing carried out over years through thousands of administrative decisions, which plainly has responsible agents even though it is a pattern and not an event.

(6) Ines said the comparison fails, because a campaign of killing has an identifiable act-type and identifiable agents whose intentions are documented, whereas starting a cold war has no act-type: the same move — a loan programme, a garrison, a veto, a speech — is aggressive under one description and defensive under another. She closed by saying that even if the question could in principle be answered, asking it in the singular distorts the record, because people slide from "X started it" to "everything X did afterwards was reaction, and reaction is excusable," and that slide is the real reason the question gets asked.

(7) Marcus said she had changed the subject: whether a question is answerable and whether it is worth answering are different matters, and her last remark conceded the first while objecting to the second.

Neither budged. I cannot tell which of them, if either, was making a mistake as opposed to a good point I am unable to answer.
