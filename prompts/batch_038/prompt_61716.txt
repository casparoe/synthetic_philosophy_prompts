I sit on the scientific review panel that decides whether a vertebrate population gets listed under the endangered species act. The statute defines "species" to include "any subspecies of fish or wildlife or plants, and any distinct population segment of any species of vertebrate fish or wildlife which interbreeds when mature" — and of course it never says what a species is, or what makes a segment distinct.

Case in front of us next month: petitioners want a high-elevation songbird population listed (call them the ridge warblers, ~4,000 birds on one massif). Genomically they're diagnosable — own mtDNA clade, a handful of near-fixed nuclear markers, their own cluster in the PCA. But they've been interbreeding with the abundant lowland relative for maybe 60 years; roughly 15–20% of the ridge genome is introgressed, hybrids are fertile, and the contact zone has been creeping upslope. Song and laying date still separate them and most matings look assortative. Two things make it worse: the lowland birds only reached the ridge because of a logging road plus decades of fire suppression, so the contact is arguably our doing; and the ridge birds hold the only copies anyone's found of certain heat-tolerance alleles, which arges that purity isn't the thing actually worth protecting here.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote, around 700 words: a clear recommendation (list / don't list / list with conditions, and which conditions), plus an explicit rule for which concept of species should govern when the concepts disagree — something we could apply again next quarter rather than a one-off verdict, and tied to what the statute is for. Petitioners have preemptively objected that the panel will hide behind an arbitrary genomic threshold, so answer that head-on. And give the strongest argument against your recommendation a real hearing, not a strawman. No citations or reading list needed, just the reasoning.
