I'm drafting an essay for a collection on philosophy and the novel and I'm stuck on the middle section. The book is Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, the lens is Benatar's procreation asymmetry together with Parfit's non-identity problem. I know the usual move — the clones are created, their lives are full of harm, but nobody was wronged, because the alternative was never existing at all — and I dont want to write that paragraph again. I want the piece to work through the novel rather than use it as a diagram.

Three things I want you to pressure-test. All three are my own coinages, so don't go hunting for them in the literature:

1. The use-clause. Benatar only asks whether a life contains more suffering than not. But the Hailsham students weren't merely brought into existence, they were brought into existence *for* something, and I think that's a wrong sitting in the creating itself that survives no matter how good the created life turns out to be. Does the novel dramatize that as its own thing, or does it collapse back into ordinary harm-plus-consent-talk?

2. Miss Lucy and Miss Emily have an argument in the book that Kathy overhears, about whether the children should be told. Is there a live question in procreative ethics that maps onto it — do you owe someone you created the truth about why you created them — and does the novel give us enough to say which of the two was right?

3. The deferral rumor. Tommy and Kathy believe real love can buy them three or four years. I want to read that as the novel staging the idea that goods inside a life can offset the harm of having been made, and Miss Emily's flat denial in the house at Littlehampton as the asymmetrist verdict that no such offsetting mechanism exists. This is the mapping I'm least sure of.

Then tell me where the novel resists all of this. Kathy is a nostalgic, unreliable narrator with a stake in not being angry; the book came out the year before Benatar's and isn't answering him; Ishiguro's interests are dignity and self-deception, not population ethics. Which parts of my reading are fair, and which are me forcing it?

Don't quote the novel — describe the scenes you mean (Tommy in that dark field, the end at the fence where the litter collects) and I'll check them against my own copy. No bibliography, no reading list, I have the books. American English please.

And at the end give it to me straight: if you land on a verdict about whether the book vindicates or undermines the asymmetry, say what consideration would flip it, and say which of my three claims you think is most likely to fail and why.
