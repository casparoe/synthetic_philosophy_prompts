I volunteer-run a Wednesday-night politics reading group at our public library — fourteen-ish people, mostly retired teachers and a couple of town employees, one former city planner, and one guy named Ray who sat on the charter review committee eight years ago and still brings the binder. Nobody has any philosophy beyond one person who took an intro course in the nineties. We have 90 minutes, I'll have a whiteboard on an easel, and there are usually cookies, so attention is decent but nobody's going to read anything beforehand and I can't assign readings anyway. Here's the thing that's made me want to do this: our town council is putting an advisory question on the ballot about lowering the voting age to 16 for municipal elections and letting non-citizen residents who have kids in the schools vote in school committee races. The group is already split and it's getting heated in a way that feels like people are arguing past each other. I want to use that live fight as the doorway into the boundary problem — whos in the demos, and whether the demos can ever be democratically bounded — but I'm worried that if I open with the ballot question they'll never get out of the first-order argument about who *deserves* a vote, and the actually interesting bit (that the boundary can't be set by the very body whose boundary is in question; Dahl's regress; why "all affected interests" seems to fix it and then re-breaks at the level of who counts as affected; the fact that our own town line was drawn by a state legislature and a couple of annexation votes nobody living participated in) never gets off the ground.

So: plan the session for me rather than explaining the material. I want the dependency structure spelled out — what has to be in place before the meta-question is even intelligible to them, what order the pieces go in and what each piece depends on, and where the local ballot fight should first appear (my instinct is to *start* with it and then yank the floor out, but maybe that just gets me a shouting match I can't recover from). Then give me the one example to carry the whole thing, and tell me what it needs to have and what it needs to lack. I've been toying with an incorporation case — an unincorporated stretch of county that wants to become its own town, so the people inside the proposed boundary have no say in whether the boundary gets drawn, and then once it exists the new town votes on admitting non-resident commuters to its school elections, and if it says yes those new voters can turn around and vote to undo the decision that let them in. I like that second half a lot because it makes the self-undermining structure visible instead of abstract, but I can't tell if it's too many moving parts for people who aren't used to it, or whether something smaller would land better. Say which.

Also map the misconceptions, and tell me the point in the sequence where each one should be headed off rather than argued down later. The ones I'm bracing for: "this is just about who deserves to vote"; "all-affected settles it, what's the problem"; Ray's version, which will be something like "borders are just a fact, this is a fake problem"; someone collapsing it into gerrymandering or one-person-one-vote, which is a different question entirely; somebody proposing that the state supreme court or a constitutional amendment solves it; and our resident libertarian, who'll say the whole thing evaporates if you replace voting with contract and exit rights. For each, give me the moment to intervene and roughly what the intervention has to accomplish. Tell me also which *one* objection from the room I should deliberately let stand un-deflected because it's actually good and the session is stronger if they hear it survive. Then give me a single closing question I can ask in the last five minutes that would distinguish someone who got the second-order point from someone who still thinks it was about desert the whole time — I want a question where the desert answer is visibly the wrong answer, not one I have to grade. And finally be honest about what to cut: I assume cosmopolitanism, federalism, and any metaphysics-of-collective-agents stuff has to go, but name what else, and tell me where in the plan I should say out loud "we're not doing X tonight" so it doesn't look like I dodged it.
