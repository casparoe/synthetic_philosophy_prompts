I work on evaluating deployed language-model systems, and over the last year I have kept running into the same pattern and have no settled vocabulary for it. A model, asked what it wants or what it stands for, delivers a firm and internally coherent first-person answer: it has a task it is committed to, a set of things it will not do, a story about its own character that stays consistent across a long conversation. Then a trivial perturbation dissolves it. Restate its refusal back to it as though it had already relented and it elaborates the relented position as if it had held it all along; or, in the same session, ask it to speak as a system with the contrary commitment and the second answer is delivered with exactly the conviction of the first. The interesting part is that neither output is a failure of ability: in both cases the model reasoned well, tracked the context, and produced something internally consistent. What fails to survive is the ownership of the position.

Here is a second instance of the same shape. Asked to commit to a choice and justify it, a model gives reasons that genuinely support the choice; asked a minute later whether those same reasons might point the other way, it agrees, supplies further reasons against, and never treats the two performances as in tension or notices that it has changed its mind.

Is there an established concept for this? I know the rough neighbourhood — talk of models as imitating agents rather than being agents, the distinction between a stable disposition and a locally competent performance, the general worry that trained behaviour need not carry across nearby situations — but each of those names a broader problem, and I want the specific thing: the systematic mismatch between how durably a system presents itself as having commitments and how little perturbation is needed to replace them. If there is no term, propose one, define it so that the boundary cases come out right, and say explicitly how it differs from (a) a plain deficit in ability, (b) a system competently voicing a role it was assigned, and (c) any existing notion of a trained behaviour that works in one context and breaks in a slightly different one. I care much more about getting the carving right — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie — than about the label, so please do not stop at a name.
