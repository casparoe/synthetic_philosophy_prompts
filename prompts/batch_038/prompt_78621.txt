I retired four years ago after a career in atmospheric chemistry, most of it spent on instrument calibration and on the unglamorous business of working out what a measurement protocol silently assumes before it can return a number at all. I have come to philosophy late and somewhat clumsily, and my habit is still to treat a social practice as if it were an instrument: look at its outputs and ask what model of the world it must be running internally. The practice I cannot make sense of is the formal apology issued by a modern state for wrongs committed by its own predecessors — for slavery, for the wartime internment of a minority, for the forced removal of indigenous children, for coerced sterilization, for colonial-era killings. I would like you to reconstruct the theory of responsibility that such a practice presupposes, and I want you to argue it from the practice itself rather than sending me a reading list, which at my age I have learned not to trust and cannot check anyway.

Let me set out the features that strike me as load-bearing, because I suspect the interesting work is in how they do and do not fit together. First, the person delivering the apology was almost never alive when the wrong was done, had no part in deciding it, and is in no way guilty of it; yet the apology is treated as something the holder of that office owes, and its absence is treated as a continuing failure. Second, the texts of these statements very often acknowledge wrongdoing in strong moral language while pairing it with some formulation to the effect that nothing said constitutes an admission of legal liability, and the softer vocabulary of regret or sorrow is sometimes preferred to the word apology for exactly that reason. Third, the apology is addressed to descendants and to communities, people who were not themselves imprisoned, removed, or sterilized, and their acceptance or rejection of it is treated as morally significant. Fourth, an official apology is frequently declared complete without any compensation, even though when one individual wrongs another we generally think that words unaccompanied by repair are worth little; and governments that do pay compensation often insist the payment settles the matter and forecloses further claims. Fifth, and this is what puzzles me most, the same legislatures that readily apologize in some cases have voted down apology motions in others on the ground that no living member did anything wrong and that today's taxpayers cannot inherit guilt — so the institution seems to accept transgenerational responsibility when it is convenient and deny it when it is not.

My own guesses are probably too crude: that the state is being treated as a single immortal moral person that survives the total replacement of its citizens, and that the apology is in truth addressed not to the victims at all but to the apologizing society, as a statement of which values it now disowns. I would like you to test both. Specifically: what view of agency, of identity over time, and of who can be harmed does the practice only make sense under? Which rival views does it quietly rule out — a strictly individual, control-based account of responsibility, or the view that obligations die with the person who incurred them? Please be careful to separate what the practice genuinely cannot do without from what it merely sits comfortably alongside, and tell me where it is underdetermined, so that several distinct theories (a corporate-continuity theory, a theory grounded in the inheritance of benefits rather than of guilt, a purely expressive theory on which the apology is not an admission at all) would each license the same ceremonies. Then say whether the five features above can be held together by any single theory, or whether one or two of them must be dropped or reinterpreted; I am especially interested in whether the second and fourth features — affirming moral fault while disclaiming legal consequence, and treating the words as sufficient without repair — are survivable in the same document. Finally, tell me what would have to change in actual state practice if the expressive theory were adopted consistently: what would disappear, what would be newly required, and what would become incoherent.

Please close with a single line of the form "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of COHERENT, COMPOSITE-BUT-STABLE, UNSTABLE, or UNDERDETERMINED, and then a phrase of no more than twelve words naming what you take the practice strictly to presuppose.
