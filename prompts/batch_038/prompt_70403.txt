So I'm in a little reading group on the philosophy of punishment — five of us, we meet Thursdays in somebody's apartment, mostly grad students plus my roommate who's in law school and keeps trying to turn every question into a question about standing — and next week it's my turn to present. I picked David Lewis's short essay on whether we believe in penal substitution. Here's my summary, for calibration, and please tell me if I'm flattening something: the standard objection is that punishing whoever didn't commit the crime is a perversion of justice, period, but Lewis thinks we're not actually that squeamish about it. Money penalties are the easy case, since money is fungible and nobody blinks if your brother writes the check. Non-monetary penalties are the hard case, and there his move — as I read him, and this is the part I most want checked — is that substitution stops being objectionable when the substitute and the offender are parts of one larger whole, so that what befalls the part is in some sense what befalls the whole; he leans on the same machinery he used in that paper where he argues he is many things rather than one, and he notes that the Christian version gets its unity from Christ and the church being a single body. Willingness on the part of the substitute also seems to matter to him.

My real question is that Lewis died in 2001 and the most interesting substitution cases are mostly newer than that, so I want to know what he'd have made of them. Four, roughly in increasing order of weirdness. One: China's blacklist of people who don't pay court-ordered debts, where the disabilities attached to the debtor spill over onto his kids, who get turned away from high-fee private schools — the household is made to carry the penalty. Two: the American fines-and-fees mess that blew up after Ferguson, where municipalities fund themselves on court debt, defendants sit in jail until somebody produces the cash, and the somebody is very often a mother or a girlfriend, plus billing for the days spent in the cell. Three: the enormous corporate settlements after 2008, where the penalty formally lands on the firm and actually lands on shareholders, employees and pension funds that had nothing to do with the conduct. Four, a thought experiment my roommate is oddly attached to: a jurisdiction that pilots letting a defendant hire a vetted, well-paid, fully informed volunteer to serve the sentence in his place.

For each of those, would Lewis even call it penal substitution, or something else — a collection mechanism, a tax on associates, collateral harm that isn't punishment in the technical sense? Does the plurality condition do any work here? I mean, would he say the Chinese case is a family-as-one-whole being punished and therefore not automatically a perversion of justice, or that a family is the wrong kind of whole for penal purposes — and if the latter, what makes a whole the right kind? Im especially stuck on whether the money case is really as benign as he treats it, because in the Ferguson material the payment isn't fungible in any relevant way: it comes out of a household that then can't make rent, so the badness settles on people who were never the offender, which looks like exactly what he was supposed to have explained away. And on the volunteer pilot, would he see a market in other peoples imprisonment as a genuinely new species of wrong or just the fine case with extra steps? Lastly, is there anything in his modal realism or his counterpart theory he'd have dragged into this, or would raising that be a category mistake on my part? Bonus points for flagging any spot where you think I'm anachronizing him, since I'd much rather hear it from you than from the one guy in my group who actually specializes in this.
