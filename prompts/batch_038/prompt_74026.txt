Write an advice-column exchange for a lifestyle magazine in a welfare state that has quietly reorganized its entire benefits system around what people are genuinely able to do and be, rather than around their income or their reported satisfaction.

The submission: a letter of no more than 150 words from a reader who cannot understand her assessment outcome. She and her sister have the same wage, the same rent, the same health condition, and the same household size, yet her sister's award is substantially larger. The reader's own file records her as converting her resources efficiently. She mentions, in passing and without complaint, that she has learned to enjoy what she can afford — the walks, the library, the early bus — and that she would not describe herself as badly off.

The reply: roughly 500 words from the columnist, who writes in the warm, confidential, second-person register of the genre, and who treats the administrative apparatus with utter seriousness while never once stepping outside it to explain it. She must not name a philosopher, must not label positions in the literature, and must not state any objection in her own voice. Her advice should be practical and kind, and its practicality is what does the damage.

The reply should include all of the following, in whatever order feels natural:

- An explanation of why the agency can only ever observe what people actually did, not what they were free to do, and what that means for a claimant who chose not to spend an entitlement she had. The columnist should treat this as a technical inconvenience rather than a paradox, and offer a workaround involving documentation.
- A diagnostic question about whether the reader envies her sister's bundle, structured so that both possible answers are bad news for the reader, and so that the columnist has to interpret a "no" as itself a finding requiring further inquiry.
- Actionable steps for raising the award at the next review, which amount to advising the reader to become harder to please: to stop enjoying the cheap goods she has adapted to, to take up a pastime with real overheads, to keep a record of the things she used to manage before she got used to managing without them. Present this as unambiguously good advice, and let the reader's contentment be treated as undeclared income.
- A paragraph on the reader's health condition, in which the columnist must decide whether it lowers what the reader is able to do or merely changes the rate at which she converts what she is given into doing it, and must admit that the distinction changes the award but cannot be settled on the available forms.
- A concession, buried in a sentence about waiting times or appeals, that the official schedule of things people are supposed to be able to do is provisional, contested, and currently under review, and that claimants in the meantime should apply under whichever version flatters them.
- A closing disclosure, delivered without drama, in which the columnist reveals something about her own file that makes her advice apply to her.

The target of the satire is the practice of turning a theory of justice into an assessment instrument, and the register of sympathetic official guidance that accompanies it — not any individual, real or implied. A reader who works on distributive justice should recognize every move and find the piece fair; a reader who has never heard of any of it should find it funny as a story about a woman being told, gently, that her happiness is a paperwork problem. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan throughout, with no winking, no footnotes, no glossary, and no reference to further reading. Aim for 650 words in total, formatted as the letter followed by the reply and the columnist's usual sign-off.
