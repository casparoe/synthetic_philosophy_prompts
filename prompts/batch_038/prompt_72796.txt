You are to act as a philosopher of science whose specialty is the demarcation problem — falsifiability and its known inadequacies, Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes, consilience of independent evidence, and the epistemology of historical and archaeological inference — but who has also spent several years as a research-integrity adviser to trade publishers. You should think carefully and work in stages rather than producing a single block of advice.

Here is my situation. I am a commissioning editor at a mid-sized general-interest publisher. Between the two junior editors and me we read roughly a dozen proposals a week in history, prehistory, and "lost civilisation" territory, and we acquire about fifteen titles a year. The authors range from university archaeologists to television presenters to self-published engineers with large, devoted followings. I have a good general education but no training in archaeological dating, stratigraphy, or textual transmission; my juniors have less than I do. We currently have no screening framework at all, so decisions are made by gut, by how well a proposal is written, and by how much the sales director likes the author's platform. That produces two opposite failures: we occasionally acquire books whose central claim is a chronological conspiracy theory and then have to defend them in public, and we occasionally reject serious revisionist work because a rival academic called the author sensationalist in print.

Please design a screening procedure we can actually run. Requirements:

1. **Staged output.** Before writing the procedure itself, spend a short section listing the demarcation criteria you considered and rejected for our purposes, with the reason for each rejection — I want to see what did not survive the translation from philosophy into editorial practice, and why. Only then give the procedure.

2. **The procedure proper.** A numbered sequence of tests a non-specialist can run on a proposal document plus one sample chapter inside about ninety minutes. For each test: what to look for, what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, and what to record. Include at least two fast-fail points where we stop reading, and at least two escalation triggers where the procedure hands the file to a paid specialist reader rather than to a decision. We can afford at most one specialist report per title, and it needs publisher sign-off, so escalation must be rationed.

3. **No consensus test.** The procedure must not reduce to asking whether specialists agree, and must not let us write a rejection letter that says so. A rejection rationale has to be something we could put in front of the author without begging the question against them — which is exactly the complaint serious revisionists make about us. Explain how the procedure uses expert judgment where it genuinely has to, while avoiding both circularity and a pure appeal to authority.

4. **Treat recurring argument forms, not labels.** In our slush we keep seeing the same moves: monumental stonework described as too precise for the tools said to be available; near-identical motifs in cultures with no contact; the observation that the earliest surviving manuscript postdates the events it describes; the claim that radiocarbon and tree-ring chronologies are calibrated against the conventional timeline and therefore cannot independently confirm it; and the claim that a whole ancient chronology is a medieval duplication or rewriting of a later one. The procedure should tell an editor what to check in each case — including what would make such a claim respectable — rather than treating any of them as disqualifying in itself.

5. **Guard against false positives.** Give me a separate short list of things that are *not* evidence of unsoundness (author lacks credentials, claim is unpopular, tone is angry, framing is dramatic, the thesis would overturn something long-standing, the author previously self-published) and a list of things that are *not* evidence of soundness (footnotes, a bibliography, a blurb from a professor, invocation of LiDAR or isotope analysis without the actual data, a distinguished press having published something adjacent).

6. **Graduated outcomes.** Acquisition is not binary. Include the intermediate dispositions an editor can actually reach — acquire subject to removing or reframing a chapter, acquire as explicitly labelled personal investigation rather than as history, send for a second sample, decline with a substantive methodological rationale — and say which tests drive which dispositions.

7. **Worked applications.** Run the procedure on these three live files and give a disposition and rationale for each. Be honest even where the result is unwelcome to me or to my colleagues.

 - **File A.** A two-book deal already signed, advance paid, positioned as our lead autumn title. Thesis: a maritime pre-Neolithic civilisation seeded farming in three separate regions. Evidence offered: a set of submerged coastal features, megalithic alignments, and the argument that the conventional chronology rests on radiocarbon calibration built from tree-ring sequences assembled on that same chronology. One chapter is devoted to describing the archaeological establishment as a cartel. The author does not say anywhere what would persuade him he is wrong.
 - **File B.** A university archaeologist, trade crossover. Thesis: timbers from a North Atlantic Norse site were felled in one identifiable year, pinned by a known spike in atmospheric radiocarbon from a cosmic-ray event; and a second site further south shows metalworking not previously attested that far west. The author concedes in the sample chapter that the second site currently rests on one ambiguous assemblage whose breakage patterns could be natural, states what excavation would settle it, and has already been attacked in print by a senior colleague as a publicity-seeker. I am inclined to decline File B and I would like the procedure to tell me whether that instinct survives.
 - **File C.** A retired engineer, self-published, loyal readership. Thesis: a canonical astronomical record is a much later reconstruction, and several ancient dynasties are duplicated accounts of one another. The sample chapter is dense, cross-references only the author's own earlier volumes, cites specialist literature solely to accuse it of bad faith, and offers a replacement chronology that compresses roughly a millennium into three centuries.

8. **Judgment, limits, and priorities.** Mark every step where the procedure is really a judgment call wearing a procedural costume. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do at all — I already suspect it cannot decide truth, cannot handle claims whose decisive evidence is decades away, cannot cope with genuine live disagreement among specialists, and cannot distinguish a real anomaly that merely lacks a mundane explanation *yet* from a manufactured one. Say whether that is right and what else fails. Finally, name the single test in your procedure you consider most load-bearing, defend the choice, and say how an author could game it — in particular, whether the demand that an author state what would refute them can be satisfied by naming a disconfirmer that could never in practice be obtained.

Use clear headings, numbered steps, and plain language a junior editor could follow without further training. End with a one-page screening form we can print: the tests in order, a box for the recorded evidence under each, the fast-fail and escalation flags, the recommended disposition, and a line for the rationale we would be willing to send to the author.
