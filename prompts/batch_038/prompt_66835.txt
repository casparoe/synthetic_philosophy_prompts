I spent thirty-eight years running a trace-metals laboratory, mostly inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry on estuarine sediments, and I retired four years ago. Since then I have been reading political philosophy with something like the doggedness I once applied to method validation, and it has landed me, unexpectedly, on a body that must decide rather than merely discuss: I sit on the Charter Review Commission of a city of about 90,000 in the upper Midwest, and our enabling resolution charges us with recommending to the city council whether to (a) lower the voting age to sixteen for municipal elections, (b) extend the municipal franchise to non-citizen residents of five years' standing, and (c) adopt a "civic duty" clause along Australian lines, under which failure to vote carries a modest administrative forfeiture. The council has promised to place whatever we recommend before the voters in November, either as a single question or as three separate ones, at our discretion, and it wants our report by the second Tuesday in September. A petition with 4,100 signatures asks us to recommend the first two. No municipality in our state has attempted either.

I have tried to bring some evidence to a debate that has so far run entirely on principle. In our last three municipal elections, turnout was about 68 percent among registered voters over sixty-five who own their homes and 11 percent among registered voters under thirty; the storm-drainage and flood-mitigation bond, which would have served the two low-lying wards that are 61 percent renter-occupied and where roughly one household in five includes a non-citizen resident, failed by 214 votes out of about 19,000 cast. I have also read that Takoma Park, Maryland, lowered the age to sixteen for city elections in 2013 and that a number of Maryland towns, several Vermont cities, and the District of Columbia now permit non-citizen residents to vote locally, and that as many as forty states allowed some form of alien suffrage before 1926. What unsettles me most is a further report: in some of these jurisdictions, turnout among the newly enfranchised non-citizens has come in at a fraction of one percent of the votes cast. Our city attorney believes the age question is within our home-rule authority, but thinks the citizenship question is likely barred by our state constitution's definition of an elector, and would have to be litigated. The commission's vice chair, who was our city attorney for twenty-two years before this, presses three objections I do not know how to answer: that recommending a provision the state courts will void will consume the public goodwill the whole charter revision depends on and may take the age provision down with it; that a half-percent inclusion may be worse than none, because registering non-citizens creates a list that is in principle discoverable by federal authorities, and because the city gets to say it enfranchised people it has not actually enfranchised in any operative sense; and, most fundamentally, that we are ourselves chosen by the current electorate, so any rule we adopt about who may vote is adopted by a body whose composition that very rule would alter — there being no way to bootstrap a democratic answer, the only non-question-begging baseline is the law as it stands.

Please draft the memorandum I intend to circulate to the commission, about 1,200 words. It should open with a one-sentence disposition for each of the three items — adopt, adopt with conditions, defer, or reject — and then give the reasoning, including a recommendation on packaging and sequencing and an explanation of what turns on that choice. I would also like it to propose a decision rule the commission could apply to the next franchise question that reaches us, whether that is seventeen-year-olds in primaries or electors for the regional water district's board; in the laboratory, when a signal sat near the detection limit, we had written conventions for reporting it as below detection rather than pretending to a measurement, and I suspect what we actually lack is something of that kind here. State the vice chair's strongest argument in terms she would recognize as her own, and say honestly whether it defeats the recommendation or merely prices it in. Do not include footnotes, citations, or a reading list — several of us would have to guess at them anyway, and I want the argument, not the authorities. Write it so that a council member who has never read political theory can follow every step, which means that if you use a term of art, you define it in a subordinate clause the first time it appears.
