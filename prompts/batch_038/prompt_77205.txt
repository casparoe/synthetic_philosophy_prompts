I teach philosophy of history and have spent some years reading about how defeated causes are remembered. There is a structural feature I keep meeting that I have never been able to name, and I should like to know whether a name exists.

Take Masada, Thermopylae, the Alamo, and the Serbian memory of the battle of Kosovo in 1389 with its account of a prince set a choice between an earthly kingdom and a heavenly one. In each, a small force is destroyed, and the commemorative power seems to depend on two things holding at once: that the doom was certain and known in advance, and that it was freely embraced rather than merely suffered. What interests me is the corollary, which runs against the usual direction of mythic repair. A flattering national narrative normally reaches for whatever consolations are available — treachery, a stolen victory, an enemy who prevailed only through weight of numbers, a defeat that was really a near-win — and these traditions duly supply them: the Serbian cycle has its traitor, Thermopylae its local guide who showed the Persians the mountain path. Yet alongside those consolations, and in tension with them, runs a demand that no honorable way out existed. Historians who show that withdrawal was feasible, that terms were genuinely on offer, that the defenders were misinformed about the odds, that a commander's decision was a blunder rather than an election, or that the outcome turned on an accident — a delayed column, a storm, an order to halt — find themselves attacked not by the heirs of the victors but by the heirs of the myth, who insist that the doom was known and unavoidable. In the Israeli case, scholarly challenges to the traditional account of what happened on the rock, both to who the defenders were and to how the last day went, met resistance well beyond the academy, and the resistance was pitched in terms of what the story is for rather than in terms of the evidence. The damaging revision is damaging precisely because it is favorable in ordinary terms: it makes the ancestors likelier to have lived.

My working gloss is that what is commemorated here is not achievement but price, and that price requires there to have been no cheaper way of paying it. If an honorable alternative existed, the deaths stop being a sacrifice and become a mistake; hence the scholarship that would exonerate the decision is received as defamation. Note also the double bind this imposes: maximal freedom at the moment of decision, maximal necessity in everything surrounding it, and hostility to any finding that loosens either.

So I want to know whether this has a name, and I shall not be satisfied with a name alone. If a term exists, tell me how well it actually fits, since I suspect the available candidates capture the trauma, or the heroism, or the politics of grievance, but not this refusal to let the outcome become contingent. If none exists, coin one and define it precisely enough that I could say what falls under it and what does not: the nearest established neighbors and exactly where each comes apart from what I have described; a diagnostic test I could apply to a fresh case; and the boundary cases that worry me, of which four are these. The familiar insistence that the fallen did not die in vain, which denies futility rather than requiring it. Confederate memory, which concedes the loss but explains it by overwhelming force and so preserves the thought that the cause was all but winnable. Dunkirk, whose story welcomes an accident as a providence and whose emphasis falls on a rescue rather than on a doom elected. And religious martyrdom, in which a way out is offered and refused — is my phenomenon different only in that the refused alternative must be dishonorable rather than merely fatal, or does the difference run deeper?

Please open with your bottom line in the first sentence and set out the reasoning after it. No preamble, and no restatement of my question.
