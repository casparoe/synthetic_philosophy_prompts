Everybody reads the chart in the Autobiography — thirteen virtues, one per week, cycling the whole list four times a year — as a charming bit of self-help that predictably fails, with the speckled-ax episode as Franklin honestly admitting defeat. But he doesn't pick that order at random. He gives a reason for putting temperance at the head of the list, and he talks as though some of the virtues are what make the others attainable at all. That's a priority ordering on virtue-acquisition, and it's a substantive claim: it collides head-on with the unity of virtue that most virtue ethicists want, on which you can't fully have one without the rest. If unity is right, the serial rotation is incoherent as a method; if it's wrong, then partial virtue is a stable state and the ax story isn't a confession of failure, it's the project's actual endpoint.
