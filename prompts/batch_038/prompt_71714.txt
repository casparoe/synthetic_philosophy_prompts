**Question (10 marks, ~400 words).** Mechanistic interpretability treats a "feature" as, roughly, a direction in a model's activation space, and supports that treatment by intervening on directions. Suppose that for some concept C and some residual-stream direction d: (i) a linear probe trained on activations recovers C from d across many contexts; and (ii) adding a suitable multiple of d at inference reliably changes the model's output in a C-appropriate way. Does (i) together with (ii) establish that the model represents C? In your answer, carefully distinguish two theses a person might lump together under "realism about interpretability": **(A)** features are real, localisable structures in the model rather than artefacts of the analysis we bring to it; **(B)** there is a determinate fact of the matter about what the model represents, independent of our interpretive scheme. Say separately for (A) and for (B) whether (i) and (ii) settle it, and say why.

**Student answer:**

Short answer: yes, mostly. (i) on its own can't establish anything, because a probe is a classifier we fit to the activations afterwards, so a direction can be perfectly linearly decodable for C and still be ignored by everything downstream — decodable isn't the same as used. (ii) is the real evidence, because it's an intervention. If I add the Eiffel Tower direction and the model starts saying the tower is in Rome, then the model was plainly doing something with that direction, and doing something with a direction just is what representing amounts to. There's no extra ingredient left over to demand.

This is also why I don't think polysemanticity helps the sceptic. The whole point of the superposition results is that a model can represent more features than it has dimensions by tolerating interference between overlapping directions. That's a claim about the model's actual structure, not about our descriptions of it, and the overlapping doesn't make the features less real — it just means the one-feature-one-direction picture was wrong from the start. The linear representation hypothesis is the realist thesis here, and the Toy Models of Superposition paper is evidence for it, so a sceptic who says we're merely imposing our own categories has to explain why our categories keep intervening successfully.

The caveat is that (i) and (ii) can come apart in the other direction too. A single direction often does causal work for several concepts at once, so the intervention shows that the model represents C but not that it represents C rather than C-or-something-adjacent — the same direction might be doing work for "things in France" as well. That's the disjunction problem from philosophy of mind transplanted into interpretability: causal-covariance accounts of content can't seperate C from C-or-C'. So there's an indeterminacy that no amount of extra patching will remove, since the causal facts are the same either way.

So the intervention evidence settles the realism question and leaves the content question open. But it can't settle whether there is a determinate fact of the matter about what the model represents at all, because that turns on which of the equally causally adequate readings is the correct one, and nothing in the model's own causal structure selects one. Which makes this closer to the instrumentalism debate about unobservable entities in a scientific theory than to a question about the model, and that's where I'd want to push back on how interpretability people usually phrase it.
