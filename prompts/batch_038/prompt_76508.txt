A reading group I belong to set out to reconstruct the colour-phi argument against the Cartesian theater — the one that motivates Dennett's multiple drafts model — and ended up with a derivation of eliminativism about phenomenal consciousness that nobody in the room is prepared to sign. The reconstruction runs as follows.

(1) Two subjects differ in their conscious experience only if they differ in something that could show up in their behaviour, including what they say and what they are disposed to say.

(2) When a green light and then, after a suitable interval, a red light are flashed at two spatially separated positions, subjects report a single light crossing the gap and changing colour partway along its path, though no moving or colour-changing light was ever presented.

(3) Any account of what happened in such a subject falls into one of two classes: either the illusory motion and colour change are constructed before becoming conscious (the Stalinesque option), or the two flashes are initially experienced veridically and that experience is later overwritten in memory (the Orwellian option).

(4) Proponents of these two accounts can be stipulated to agree about every neural event, its timing, and its downstream causal consequences; they disagree only about whether a given locus of processing is to be counted pre-experiential or post-experiential. Nothing of the sort mentioned in (1) distinguishes them.

(5) So there is no fact of the matter whether the phi subject's experience is Stalinesque or Orwellian.

(6) Now admit a third account, on which the flashes are registered and correctly discriminated without generating any visual experience at all, the report and its associated dispositions being produced entirely by subpersonal processing. Nothing of the sort mentioned in (1) distinguishes this account from the first two either.

(7) So there is likewise no fact of the matter whether the phi subject has any visual experience at all.

(8) Nothing in the passage from (2) to (7) depends on phi; the same reasoning applies to any pair of hypotheses about a subject's experience that leave behaviour and dispositions to report untouched.

(9) Therefore, for any subject, there are no facts about conscious experience over and above facts about behaviour and dispositions to report, and talk of experience as something that might obtain or fail to obtain independently of these is empty.

(10) But (9) is eliminativism about phenomenal consciousness, whereas the argument was meant to establish only that experience does not arrive at one privileged place and time — a thesis that presupposes that experience does arrive somewhere and sometime.

I am after a diagnosis rather than a defence of anyone's position. Which step do you reject, and why did that step look entirely innocuous when taken on its own — what work was it doing that made it hard to refuse? Then price the alternatives: what is lost if we instead give up (1), which is the premise doing the real work against the Cartesian theater; or (4), whose claim of empirical indistinguishability is what licenses (5); or (6), where a third account is admitted on the same footing as the first two; or (10), the premise asserting that (9) is a reductio rather than simply the correct result. Say also whether the failure you identify is local, so that the argument can be repaired by amending that step, or whether any repair reinstates the trouble elsewhere. For what it is worth, I would rather keep (1) and (10) than (5) or (7), but I do not trust that preference, and in particular I am unsure whether a theorist who accepts (1) is entitled to (10) at all.
