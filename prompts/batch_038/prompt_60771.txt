So — I'm the faculty rep on our university art museum's collections committee. I'm an epistemologist by trade and I got roped in because someone remembered I taught a seminar on aesthetics once, which apparently qualifies me to explain why a painting is or isn't the thing we say it is. There's a case on Tuesday's agenda and I have to circulate something in writing Monday night. Can you play the part of someone who's actually done this work — a philosopher of art who has sat on real attribution panels and knows what a document like this has to look like to survive a curator, a university lawyer, and a donor all reading it at once? Think it through carefully rather than jumping to the prose: sketch your rough notes and the underlying disagreement first (bullet fragments are fine there), then draft the actual memo.

Facts as I understand them. In 1961 a donor gave us a small oil-on-panel still life, catalogued ever since as an autograph work by one of the major Haarlem flower-and-breakfast painters of the 1620s. It's one of maybe five objects people actually enter the building for; there's a bench in front of it and it's been on the members' brochure for a decade. A visiting conservator ran imaging on it last term and here's what came back: the panel is period oak and structurally sound, but the original paint surface is heavily abraded and has been substantially retouched, in what looks like at least two seperate campaigns, and the top six inches is a later 18th-century extension added to fit a frame. So the object hanging on our wall is not the object the painter finished. The twist is that nobody forged anything — no deception, no added signature, no invented provenance. The retouching reads as well-meaning old restoration and the extension as well-meaning old reframing. The committee is split three ways. Two members want the attribution quietly narrowed to "studio of" or "circle of" and the extension left exactly as it is, since removing it means cutting period material out of a period object. The donor's grandson, who sits on our advisory board, wants it either treated back to the condition his grandfather gave or taken off the wall, on the grounds that we're impugning a family gift. And the director says that if we re-attribute publicly, a private lender whose collection is scheduled to come to us in 2027 has signaled they'd reconsider, and the museum can't afford to loose that show.

Here's what I need out of you. (1) A concrete recommendation with a time frame: what the wall label says and by when, what happens to the panel physically and who decides that, what we say to the 2027 lender and through whom, what gets disclosed publicly versus kept internal, and what the trigger would be for reopening the question. (2) Since there are something like thirty other works in the collection with comparable histories of abrasion and old retouching, the memo should hand the committee a test they can actually apply again — not just a verdict on this one painting. Be explicit about what authenticity is attaching to in a case like this, and what a label owes a visitor who is standing there for eight minutes with a coffee. (3) Engage seriously with the view that none of this matters aesthetically: one colleague insists that a blindfolded visitor has the same experience either way, so the entire fight is about paperwork, feelings, and money. That's the Goodman / Lessing / Dutton neighborhood — autographic versus allographic, forgery as a failure of achievement rather than of appearance — and you're welcome to use whatever you need from there, but give me the arguments and not the bibliography: no citations, no titles in the body, no reading list at the end, since I can't check any of it and the committee certainly won't. (4) Do honest work on the strongest consideration on the side my recommendation loses to. I suspect it's the director's institutional point, but it might be the "we don't actually know the retouching is worse than what's underneath it" point, and I'd rather you told me which and argued it at full strength than let me find out at the meeting. Aim for 700–900 words for the memo itself, plain enough that a non-philosopher follows every move, no footnotes, and end on a bottom line the committee could literally vote yes or no on.
