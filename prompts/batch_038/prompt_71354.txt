Write me a short story, maybe 1,300 words, about a civilization that works out how to exploit the universal prior from the inside. The setup: they discover that some outside mind picks between hypotheses by description length over a prefix-free code, so everything their universe outputs thats long and random is being discounted at exponential rates. They also conclude the outside mind isn't just watching but acts on its predictions — so they start shortening themselves, collapsing their weather, their languages, their whole history into a tight repeating bitstream, since every bit of self-description they shave roughly doubles the weight that mind gives them. Tell it mostly from the losing side: an archivist whose job was keeping the incompressible stuff (a lullaby with an irregular metre, one argument two people had forty years ago) who gets asked to hand over the last of it.

Two things so this isn't just sci-fi garnish. First, the arithmetic has to be felt rather than waved at — show what spending k bits actually buys them in posterior weight and what it costs in the world, and have someone raise the obvious catch: this only pays off if the outside mind really is using the plain universal prior rather than something time-bounded like a speed prior, in which case all this self-compression buys them nothing and they've wrecked themselves for a bet. Second, don't give me a clean triumph or a clean tragedy; the story should stay genuinely two-sided about whether they did the right thing.

If you think the setup as I've laid it out is confused, or just can't carry a good story — e.g. the incentive is weaker than I'm implying, or "a civilization compressing itself" doesn't work as fiction — say that plainly up front instead of politely working around it. Then after the story, add a short note on where you took liberties with the actual argument or leaned on a contested reading of it, and end with exactly one final line in this form:

Logline: <one sentence>
