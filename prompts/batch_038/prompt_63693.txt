I am preparing a teaching unit on historical explanation — specifically the quarrel between those who hold that the Third Reich's trajectory turned on Hitler personally and those who hold that a fascist takeover in Germany in the 1930s was overdetermined by structural conditions, so that the question of whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without him reduces to the question of how much causal work one individual can do inside a system that supplies its own candidates. I want to pair that debate with Chaplin's The Great Dictator (1940), because the film strikes me as running the very substitution experiment the historians argue about: Chaplin plays both Hynkel, dictator of Tomania, and the Jewish barber, and the plot ends with the barber standing at the reviewing platform in Osterlich, in the dictator's place, addressing the same crowds.

Read the film through that lens. Does the double role function as an argument that the man is the cause — that Hynkel's particular vanity, paranoia and megalomania are what generate the regime's behaviour, so that a different occupant of the office would produce a different politics — or does the film undercut itself, since the apparatus around Hynkel (Herring's failing weapons programmes, Garbitsch's propaganda ministry, the rivalry with Napaloni of Bacteria, the stormtroopers who arrest their own leader at the border and then cheer the impostor at the podium) looks like a machine whose parts are interchangeable, exactly as a structuralist would say? Please work through the globe ballet, the decision to invade Osterlich, the duck-hunting mix-up, and the closing address, saying for each which reading it supports and where an honest critic must concede that neither reading can claim it.

Two harder questions should not be dodged. First, what does the film assume about how much of the past has to be held fixed for a substitution of persons to mean anything: the barber speaks differently, but nothing about the camps, the ministries or the army has changed, so is the ending a counterfactual demonstration or a change of subject? Second, is Chaplin's later remark in his autobiography — that he could not have made the film had he known the actual scale of the atrocities — itself a Great Man concession, an admission that his target was too small for the thing that happened?

Deliver the reading as continuous prose. Then append a table with these columns: "Moment" (the scene or element under discussion); "Great Man reading" (what it implies about the causal role of the individual); "Structural reading" (what it implies about the regime as a system); "Favours" (Great Man, Structural, Both, or Neither); and "Cost" (what that reading has to ignore or explain away).
