so i'm in the second year of a public health masters and something in the coursework has been bugging me for weeks and i genuinely can't tell whether it's a real tension or whether i'm just being dense.

On the methods side we get drilled hard on the ecological fallacy. Correlate two group-level quantities — average income across counties, per-capita alcohol sales across countries — and you are not allowed to read anything off that about individuals. Aggregates can be wildly misleading about the individual-level relationship, there's a named fallacy for it, and a reviewer will make you cut the sentence. Fine, I follow the statistics of why.

But then nearly every flagship population measure is justified in exactly that group-level register. Fluoride in the water, salt taken out of bread at the manufacturing stage, sugar taxes, indoor smoking bans, trans-fat bans. And behind them sits an explicit doctrine: the choice between chasing the high-risk tail of a distribution and shifting the entire distribution a couple of points to the left, plus the prevention paradox — roughly, that most of the cases in a population come out of the enormous low-and-moderate-risk middle rather than the small high-risk tail, so the intervention with the biggest payoff does close to nothing for any given participant. That's Rose, eighties, I think.

Here's what's bugging me. What does that whole apparatus presuppose about what does the causing? It looks to me like it needs population-level things — a distribution, a mean, a food environment, a norm about where you may smoke — to have real causal standing, and not just be a bookkeeping summary of what lots of individuals separately did. Otherwise "shift the distribution" is only a fancy way of describing a great many individual changes, and the population strategy has no content beyond the high-risk strategy plus arithmetic. But if that's right, the same discipline is an individualist about inference and a holist about intervention, and I don't see how it gets to have it both ways.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of causation and explanation as carefully as you can, and then separate what this practice strictly needs from what it merely sits comfortably with. A few places I'd want you to push:

- Does fluoridation actually require any of it? Nothing about it changes anyone's beliefs, desires, or choices — the mechanism seems to be chemistry happening inside individual mouths that just happens to be delivered at scale. Whereas a smoking ban or a sugar tax works only by altering behavior, or maybe by altering what's considered normal. Do those two kinds of measure presuppose different things, and if so does the field's one-size-fits-all justification paper over that?
- Can an instrumentalist reading rescue the consistency — aggregate quantities as useful decision heuristics while all genuine causation stays at the individual level? What does that cost? Does the population attributable fraction survive it, given that it's explicitly a statement about how many cases would vanish if a whole population's exposure changed? What about cluster randomized trials, where you assign entire schools or villages and then have to argue about the unit of analysis?
- Which rival account of the social does the practice quietly rule out, and would a hardline individualist have to abandon fluoridation itself or only the way it gets argued for? Those feel like very different stakes.
- Where is the practice underdetermined — how many distinct views about levels would license the exact same set of studies, the same numbers, and the same policies? I want to know which parts of my supposed inconsistency are actually load-bearing.

And finally, if the field swapped its implicit view for a strict individual-level one, tell me what would concretely have to change: in the textbooks, in study design, in what counts as sufficient evidence for a mandate that nobody opted into. Is there anything the current practice delivers that no individual-level story could reproduce, or is the population register dispensable all the way down?
