I'm a senior econ major taking a seminar on social learning and contagion, and our final project has turned into an argument with our TA that I'd like to settle before Thursday, when we present to a room that's about half econ PhD students. We scraped three years of edit history plus talk-thread comments from a collaborative wiki where every contributor can see exactly what the previous few editors changed, which seemed like a decent natural setting for sequential choice under uncertainty. What we concluded is strong, and here it is: when you watch people copy each other, the default explanation should be rational herding and not some loose appeal to "social influence" or a taste for conformity — copiers are doing something approximately Bayesian, treating the observed choices of others as evidence about the state. And the second half, which is the part we leaned on hardest: cascades are fragile. The standard result is that a cascade gets shattered as soon as a bit of genuinely independent public information arrives, so a consensus that survives repeated public counter-signalling over a long stretch can't be a cascade at all — it has to be tracking something real. Our data seemed to line up. The edit streaks that looked like cascades got interrupted pretty reliably whenever somebody dropped a citation or a dataset into the thread, and the positions that never budged were the ones where the public evidence was truly ambiguous, not the ones people were most socially invested in.

So: what would count against this view? I want the whole exposure map, not one good objection. Which experiments, field findings, formal results, and plain facts about how the world looks would undercut each half separately, and which would take down the conjunction even if each half survived on its own? I'm most worried about the identification problem — whether "rational herding" can actually be distinguished from "people just enjoy agreeing" using choice data alone, or whether you can always re-tune the machinery (priors, signal precision, cost asymmetries, who's in the observation set) until it fits whatever pattern showed up. If it can always be re-tuned, does that make the view empty, or just underdetermined in the ordinary way a lot of economics is, and is there a principled line between those two verdicts?
