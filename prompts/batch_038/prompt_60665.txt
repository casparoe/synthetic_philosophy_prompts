ok so I've been messing with Fitch's knowability argument for a seminar and I built a version that seems to prove way more than the textbook one, and now I can't tell whether I've got a result or a screwup.

Setup. Write Kp for "someone, at some time, knows that p", and ◇ for plain metaphysical possibility. Premises:

(1) Knowledge is factive: Kp → p.
(2) Knowledge distributes over conjunction: K(p & q) → (Kp & Kq).
(3) Knowability: for every p, p → ◇Kp. Note that (1)–(3) aren't empirical. (1) and (2) are conceptual, and nobody offers (3) as a contingent regularity about our universe — it's meant to be an account of what truth *is*. So if they're true at all, they're true at every world.

Then:

(4) Pick any world w. Either every truth true at w is known at w, or some isn't. Suppose some isn't: for some A, (A & ¬KA) is true at w.
(5) By (3) at w, ◇K(A & ¬KA). So there's a world w' accessible from w where someone knows (A & ¬KA).
(6) At w', by (1) and (2), that person knows A and knows ¬KA; and by (1) again, ¬KA holds at w'. But knowing A at w' makes KA hold at w'. Contradiction.
(7) So (5) was impossible, hence by (3), (A & ¬KA) isn't true at w after all. So at w, every truth true at w is known at w.
(8) w was arbitrary. So at every world, every truth true there is known there. That's not just actual omniscience, its necessary omniscience.
(9) Now take w₀: a world with no knowers in it at all — rocks and vacuum, no minds, no deity. There are truths at w₀ (all the necessary ones, plus "there are no knowers at w₀"). By (8) they're all known at w₀. Nothing at w₀ knows anything. Contradiction.
(10) So (8) is false, and since (1)–(2) are conceptual, it's (3) that fails — and fails at w₀, not just here. Given (3) was supposed to be necessary-or-impossible, (3) is *impossible*. It isn't that some truths happen to be unknowable; it's that no world could be a world where all truths are knowable.
(11) And that's where it stops feeling like a result. Swap "known" for "provable in principle" or "in principle discoverable by some possible science" — proof is factive and distributes over conjunction too — and the same template says those theses are impossible as well. So I've apparently shown that any optimism whatsover about discovery is metaphysically impossible, which is a mood dressed up as a theorem.

For the record I'm fine with ending up rejecting (3), that's the whole point of the exercise. What I'm not fine with is how I got there, and (11) makes me suspect the route is broken somewhere rather than just expensive. I know there's a literature of responses to the original paradox — restricting the principle, tinkering with the logic, adding operators — but I built this one specifically to be annoying, so tell me whether any of those actually block *this* version. Which step do you reject? Don't just name it: tell me why it looked completely innocent when I wrote it down, and price the alternatives.
