I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I would be grateful for a disambiguation of a slogan that has been doing a great deal of unearned work in my supervision meetings.

The context: I am drafting a chapter on whether a nation can be guilty. My working thesis is that guilt in the strict sense is non-transferable — it attaches to agents who did something or failed to do something — and that what survives across generations in cases like postwar Germany, settler colonial dispossession, or slavery is not inherited guilt but something more specific: the continued liability of a persisting corporate agent (the state) for acts done in its name, plus remedial obligations on particular descendants grounded in benefits actually received and retained. My supervisor's standing response is the proverb about the sins of the fathers being visited upon the children. He uses it loosely, in the form people repeat it rather than as a citation, and he deploys it at exactly the point where I insist that a distinction matters: when I say that the grandchildren of perpetrators are not guilty of anything, he says that this is a legalistic fiction, that the taint does not respect the boundary between persons, and that my framework is "too clean" because it cannot accommodate what the proverb records. He has also invoked Jaspers's division of guilt into criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical kinds as though that division licensed his slogan rather than mine.

I suspect the slogan is being made to cover at least four different claims, only one of which could possibly be what he needs, and that the argument has stalled because nobody has said which. I would like you to prise the readings apart.

Please treat the proverb as a piece of language whose content is indeterminate, not as a thesis to be defended or refuted wholesale. In particular, I want to know which predicates are candidates for transmission (guilt, responsibility, liability, obligation, shame, taint, corruption, debt), which subjects are candidates for bearing them (individuals, families, peoples, states, "a nation" as a corporate agent persisting through time), what mechanisms of transmission each reading presupposes (causal, legal-institutional, identity-constituting, benefit-based, or merely metaphorical), and over what temporal span each is supposed to hold.

Format. Please use the following sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated:

1. **Bottom line** — about 120 words. State directly whether any reading of the proverb both is defensible and would support my supervisor's dismissal of the non-transferability claim, or whether the defensible readings are idle and the load-bearing readings are false.

2. **The menu** — about 900 words, as a numbered list. For each candidate reading: a one-sentence formulation as precise as you can manage; a verdict, choosing among *platitude*, *substantive and defensible*, *substantive and contentious*, and *false*; a note on whether the reading is even about guilt or has silently switched to some neighbouring predicate; and whether it licenses the conclusion that my grandchildren-of-perpetrators claim is a legalistic fiction. Order the readings from the most trivial to the most radical.

3. **The axes of ambiguity** — about 300 words. Identify the small number of parameters along which these readings differ, and say which parameter my supervisor is most likely sliding along when he moves from a claim about consequences to a claim about culpability.

4. **Provenance and its embarrassments** — about 250 words. The proverb has a scriptural ancestry, and that same tradition contains passages in which a prophet explicitly repudiates the idea that children bear their parents' iniquity, alongside legal provisions forbidding punishment of relatives for one another's crimes. Explain whether this internal tension is a resource for the disambiguation — that is, whether the tradition itself already distinguishes the readings I am asking you to distinguish — or whether it is beside the point.

5. **Against the Jaspers framework** — about 350 words. Map whichever readings survive onto his four categories of guilt, and say for each category whether it makes the proverb true, false, or simply inapplicable. If I have misdescribed that framework anywhere above, correct me.

6. **Questions for supervision** — about 150 words. Three or four short questions I can ask that would force him to specify which reading he means, phrased so that any answer concedes something.

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the reasoning, not the references. Where you think a reading is one I have mis-stated or a further reading is missing from the ones I have gestured at, add it and say that you have added it.
