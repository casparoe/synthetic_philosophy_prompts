Just had a four-hour row with my brother about Kelly betting and I still don't know who's right, which is why I'm asking you instead of him.

My aunt Dot stands down on Saturday as treasurer of a small friendly society — twenty-six years in the job, about three hundred members, and a reserve fund she treated like a living thing. The dinner will be half members and family who couldn't tell you what a logarithm is, and half people from the auditor's office plus two economists who've come because they know about 2008. That year a lender offered the society a deal that would roughly have doubled its annual returns but required putting the whole reserve behind it as security. She turned it down and got shouted at for months.

Her rule was the 'one bad winter' rule: no single decision may be able to take more than a fifth of what it would cost to rebuild. She always said the question is never whether a bet is good, only how much of it you can afford to be wrong about.

Write the speech I'm giving — five minutes, in British English. It has to make the growth-rate-versus-average-outcome idea land with the members without a single equation, the word 'optimal', or any mention of utility functions, and it has to face down the economists' objection that with one shot and no next round there is no long run to be rational about. Don't be neutral on 2008: my brother says she was squeamish and got lucky, I think she was right for reasons she couldn't have articulated. Pick a side and make it convincing to both halves of the room. One good joke, and end on affection rather than a lesson.
