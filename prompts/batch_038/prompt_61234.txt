I am a third-year history undergraduate, and our department runs a scheme in which students may each propose and lead one 60-minute workshop for a mixed cohort drawn from a second-year survey course on the making of the modern world. Mine has been accepted. I will have eighteen participants in a seminar room with movable chairs and a whiteboard: roughly half are history majors, half are science and engineering students taking the survey as an elective, and none of them should be assumed to have done the reading. I can print at most four pages of materials. No laptops, no money, no prizes worth having.

My session is about periodization — specifically, whether the labels this survey course is organized around correspond to anything in the world or are artifacts of the people who attached them. I want the participants to run into that question rather than hear about it.

**The conceptual target.** Two things are run together in everything I have read, and I want the workshop to prise them apart. Call them the boundary problem and the direction problem. The boundary problem is the familiar one: no year possesses a property that the year next to it lacks, so wherever anyone cuts, a rival can always ask why not one year earlier or one decade later. The direction problem is less discussed and, I think, the more interesting one. Even granting a boundary, a periodization is not a description of a stretch of time; it is a claim about which changes were worth tracking, and that claim can only be issued from somewhere — from a present that has already settled what mattered, and which therefore gets to say what the earlier stretch was building toward. My working view, which I would like the workshop to test rather than assert, is that the boundary problem is insoluble but harmless, whereas the direction problem is soluble but corrosive: it does not move the dates, it undermines the idea that the dates are about anything other than the person doing the dating. There is a related feature I would like built in: some period-names are residual, defined by what they sit between rather than by any content of their own, and a residual period is one that its own inhabitants could not have used of themselves.

**Material you may draw on in the debrief** (I have checked these; please use them as given rather than supplying attributions of your own). The Latin phrase for a middle stretch of time first appears in 1469, in the preface of an Italian bishop and editor, Giovanni Andrea of Aleria; a German professor of history in the 1680s built the threefold ancient–medieval–modern division into a universal-history textbook and that scheme became standard. Vasari used an Italian word for rebirth of the arts in 1550, but the Renaissance as the name of an epoch was a French nineteenth-century invention, given as a volume title by Michelet in 1855 and fixed by Burckhardt's book on Italian culture in 1860. The Scientific Revolution is later still: the phrase only became a standard organising category in the mid-twentieth century, through Butterfield's 1949 account of the origins of modern science and Koyré's French usage, and Steven Shapin opens his own book on the subject with the sentence "There was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution, and this is a book about it." Le Goff's last book, whose English title asks whether we must divide history into periods at all, argued for a long Middle Ages running well past the conventional end of the medieval period.

Think carefully, and before you write the workshop, sketch three or four candidate mechanics for making the direction problem bite, say in a sentence or two each what a participant would actually feel under each, identify which you are rejecting and why, and commit to one. The full materials list, including the complete card deck you want me to print — around twenty-eight cards spanning roughly 400 to 1750, each carrying only a year and a one-line description of an event, institution, text, or practice. The first round should generate the boundary problem: groups cut the deck into exactly three named periods and then face structured challenges to their cuts. The second round should generate the direction problem, and it must do so by handing each group a sealed commission specifying a terminus — a later state of affairs that their periodization has to make look like the point of the whole sequence — with different groups receiving incompatible termini from the same deck. If you think a third round is needed to capture the power of naming, propose one, but only if it fits in the hour.

5. A scoring scheme that uses tokens or points rather than money and that does not simply reward getting history right, since there is nothing to get right.
