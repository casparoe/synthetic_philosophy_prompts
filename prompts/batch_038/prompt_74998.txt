**Question 3 (25 points, ~400 words).** Explain Davidson's project of radical interpretation: what the interpreter is given to work from, what the deliverable of the project is supposed to be, and how the principle of charity is meant to handle the fact that belief and meaning cannot be pinned down one at a time. Close by saying what Davidson thinks all this does for skepticism about the external world.

**Student answer:**

Davidson's radical interpretation takes over Quine's radical translation but changes the target. Like Quine, he asks what an interpreter could know who starts with no dictionary, no bilingual informant, and no language shared in advance with the speaker, and who has to work entirely from publicly observable behavior. The evidence is the speaker's hold-true attitudes: which sentences she assents to or utters, in what observable circumstances, at what times. The difference is the deliverable. Quine wants a translation manual pairing the sentences of one language with sentences of another. Davidson wants a truth theory for the speaker's own language in Tarski's form, whose theorems state the truth conditions of her sentences; the meaning of a sentence is whatever an empirically adequate such theory assigns to it.

The trouble is that this evidence underdetermines the theory, because belief and meaning come apart: given that a speaker holds a sentence true, you can't tell whether the sentence means roughly what it appears to mean and she's mistaken, or whether it means something else and she's right. Davidson's fix is the principle of charity — the interpreter assigns meanings so as to maximize agreement between herself and the speaker, holding fixed as much as she can of her own logic and of the plainest observable facts. Charity isn't a counsel of generosity or a bet that people are reasonable; it's constitutive, since without a broad background of agreement there'd be nothing there to interpret. Given that, the theory gets built in two stages. The interpreter first settles the meanings of the simplest sentences about the immediately surrounding environment, where the correlation between utterance and event does the work; with that base of meanings fixed independently of any beliefs, she then works outward through the rest of the vocabulary, reading off beliefs sentence by sentence. Charity licenses the first stage and the first stage makes the later inference possible.

This is also what handles skepticism. If broad agreement has to be assumed for interpretation to get going at all, then anyone interpretable at all has beliefs that are mostly true, at least about the middle-sized things around her. The omniscient interpreter makes the point: an interpreter who knew everything would find us largely correct, so a coherent set of beliefs can't be mostly false. Coherence yields correspondence. And it follows that what look like deep theoretical or moral disagreements with a foreign speaker are never really disagreements — they're always interpretation failures at bottom, since real disagreement needs agreement first.
