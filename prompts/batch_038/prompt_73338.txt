I teach a first-year graduate seminar on the ethics of research practice — 24 students, mixed across biology, chemistry, and CS, one 75-minute session in a plain seminar room with a whiteboard, no guarantee of computers, and no budget for real money. Most have had one semester of probability and none is a game theorist. My problem is that we spent the first weeks on the standard catalog — tragedy of the commons, free riding, prisoner's dilemma, principal–agent problems — and my students now pattern-match every case of collective risk onto one of those four templates. When I describe a lab considering whether to publish a protocol, release a dataset, or open-source a model, half the class immediately says "commons problem" and stops thinking. I want an activity that breaks that reflex by making them live through a structurally different failure mode before anyone names it.

The failure mode I have in mind is what I know as the unilateralist's curse, and since it is not part of the standard curriculum, here is the mechanism as I understand it, so that the design does not depend on your recalling the label: there is an action whose true net value depends on a state of the world that the actors do not know; each actor forms an independent, noisy estimate of whether acting is net beneficial; each actor is individually able to act alone, without anyone's consent; each acts whenever their own estimate is favorable. Under those conditions, adding more well-intentioned, individually prudent actors makes a bad outcome more likely, because the pool of estimates grows and with it the chance that at least one actor receives a spuriously favorable signal and acts on it — and a single bad act is not diluted or offset by the many good decisions that were made, since the acts are not rivalrous and nobody is taking more than their share of a common resource. The harm comes from selection over noisy estimates, not from defection, externalities, or overconsumption. The usual institutional remedy — requiring consultation, a second signature, a veto, or unanimity before anyone acts — genuinely reduces bad acts, but it also suppresses good ones, and that cost is real and should be visible in the same numbers rather than asserted in the debrief. Participants should come away seeing three things: that the outcome got worse purely because there were more of them, with nobody irrational or malicious; that this is not the commons and not free riding, and being able to say precisely where it differs; and that the coordination fix they will propose on their own carries a measurable price.

Please design the activity so that the payoff structure and the noise process are held exactly fixed while only the number of people who may act varies — the same scenario run with a single actor, then a small team, then the whole room — so that any difference in outcome is attributable to group size alone and cannot be explained away by the scenario changing. Give me: the hidden-state and signal-generation mechanism implementable with dice, cards, or sealed envelopes rather than software; a points-based payoff scheme in which the losses are felt collectively rather than by punishing an individual student in a way that would distort their incentives; the verbatim text of anything I need to print or write on the board, none of which may contain the name of the phenomenon or a synonym such as winner's curse or selection effect; a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes; and the actual arithmetic for each group-size condition — the probability that at least one bad act occurs, worked out from your chosen signal distribution, not asserted — along with your judgment about whether the effect will reliably appear in the number of rounds that fit the session, and what I should do if it does not, including the specific embarrassment of the single-actor condition producing no bad act at all, which will look to the room like evidence against the whole idea. Then give me the debrief: questions in the order I should ask them, each mapped to a moment of play, the point at which the concept finally gets its name, and prepared responses to the two objections I expect — the student who concludes we should simply always consult, and the student who complains the signals were rigged against them. State what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to recover in real time; include a scaled-down variant for a session where only a dozen students show up; and close with a frank account of which feature of the phenomenon a classroom game cannot reproduce — I suspect correlated estimates among people who share a literature, and the fact that real acts are irreversible rather than repeated — together with the two or three sentences I must supply verbally to cover the gap. Begin your response with one sentence stating the single moment in the activity where the idea lands, and then give the design; no preamble, no restatement of my request, and no closing commentary.
