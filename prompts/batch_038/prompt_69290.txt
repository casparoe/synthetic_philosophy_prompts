Our formal epistemology seminar this term is devoted to the common prior assumption and to the classical agreement theorem that is usually thought to rest on it. The fellow convening it does not lecture; instead she stipulates a case each week, in the manner of a puzzle, and announces a ruling on it — whether the disagreement the case describes is possible at all, whether it is coherent if possible, and whether either party must be convicted of an error. Her rulings so far are these.

**Case 1.** Two agents are stipulated to be unbounded and ideally coherent, to share a single common prior over a finite state space, and to have common knowledge of one another's posterior probability for a proposition P. The posteriors differ. *Ruling:* this is not a genuine possibility but an incoherent description; one of the stipulations must be dropped, or somebody has botched a calculation.

**Case 2.** As Case 1, except that no communication of any kind has taken place: each has private evidence, and their posteriors for P differ sharply. *Ruling:* wholly coherent; there is no disagreement here in any interesting sense, only reciprocal ignorance, and the case teaches nothing about the common prior assumption.

**Case 3.** Two agents are stipulated to have different priors — one assigns probability one half to a coin landing heads in the absence of any evidence, the other three quarters — and to disclose everything to each other, including their priors, reaching common knowledge of their differing posteriors. *Ruling:* coherent, and no theorem forbids it; but she refuses to describe the two priors as equally reasonable, and adds that a difference in priors is not a difference in evidence and ought not to be flattered by being described as one.

**Case 4.** Two agents are produced as duplicates from a single source and therefore share a common prior over how the world is. Each wakes in one of two rooms that are, so far as either can tell, indistinguishable. They later meet, pool everything they know, and reach common knowledge of their posteriors for the proposition that the coin tossed in the room one of them woke in landed heads. The posteriors differ and remain different. *Ruling:* coherent, and explicitly not a counterexample to the agreement result. Her gloss is that the prior is shared at the level of the world but not at the level of the agent, and that ignorance about which agent one is is a different commodity from ordinary evidence.

**Case 5.** Two agents share not a single prior but a set of priors — the same imprecise credal state, arrived at honestly by both — and after full disclosure they report ranges of posteriors that overlap without coinciding. *Ruling:* coherent; she treats the two agents as genuinely having the same doxastic state going in, and says the disagreement is not to be explained away as a difference in priors.

**Case 6.** Two agents share a common prior and have common knowledge of their differing posteriors, but one is bounded: she cannot enumerate all the consequences of the evidence she has received, and reports as her posterior the best she has actually managed to compute. *Ruling:* coherent; the bounded party is not convicted of an error but only of a limitation, and she remarks that the agreement result presupposes a kind of agent nobody has ever met.

**Next week's case.** Two agents are stipulated to be unbounded and ideally coherent. They share a single prior, but it is improper: it is uniform over the positive integers, is never normalised, and is used by both only to generate ratios of likelihoods. Each privately learns a finite piece of information about the value of some unknown positive integer N — one learns that N is even, the other that N exceeds one hundred — and they then exchange their posterior probabilities for the proposition that N is a power of two, reaching common knowledge of them. The posteriors differ and remain different.

I should like your prediction of her ruling on this case, but the prediction is worth much less to me than the reconstruction behind it. Specifically:
