Construct a matched pair of thought experiments designed to test whether competence without comprehension — the strange inversion of reasoning Darwin and Turing performed, on which a process can be reliably good at something with no grasp of what it is doing — picks out a real explanatory category or merely relabels well-situated luck.

Case A: a system that carries no representation of the task's goal and yet succeeds across a range of perturbations to the task. Case B: a system with the same poverty of internal states, the same success rate, and the same range of inputs tried, which we should refuse to credit with competence.

Constraints: no appeals to natural selection and no artificial intelligence, since those are the stock illustrations and I want the verdicts to turn on the structure of the cases rather than on familiarity; ordinary mechanical, institutional, or biological settings are fine; one paragraph per case.

Then, for each case, say whether the inversion licenses an attribution of competence and on what grounds; isolate the single structural feature that makes B fail where A succeeds; say whether that feature is itself a species of comprehension, in which case the inversion buys less than it advertises, or something a system can have without any; and close with the best reply available to someone who insists that B is competent too and that our reluctance is a residue of the top-down reasoning the inversion was meant to overthrow.
