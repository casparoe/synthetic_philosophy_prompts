I'm a software engineer auditing a part-time evening seminar in social theory at a local university, mostly to keep my reading habits from atrophying after work. Last week's session was on historical materialism, and the instructor distributed a two-page precis that tried to state the base–superstructure claim in a form precise enough to argue with. As best I can reconstruct it from my notes, the handout offered the following. **Thesis D:** for any society *S* at time *t*, the form taken by *S*'s non-economic institutions at *t* is explained by the fact that those institutions are functionally adapted to the requirements of the mode of production dominant in *S* at *t*. It then added two clarifying clauses. **(i)** An institution is functionally adapted to a mode of production *M* just in case, had the society instead adopted one of the institution-types readily available to it at the time, *M* would have been less likely to reproduce itself over the succeeding period. **(ii)** Where an institution becomes systematically dysfunctional for *M* — that is, where its continued operation lowers the likelihood of *M*'s self-reproduction relative to available alternatives — it will be transformed or displaced within a lag *L*, where *L* is finite, may differ across institution-types, and is not specified by the thesis. The accompanying exercise asked us to consider a society in which a rule of inheritance restricting the descent of agricultural land to a single designated heir persists for two centuries after the mode of production it once stabilized has been replaced by one whose requirements include the free alienability of land and the mobility of capital; by stipulation, the rule operates throughout that period in a way that lowers the likelihood of the new mode's reproduction, and it is eventually abolished by statute. The instructor was called out of the room partway through the exercise and did not return, which left the discussion unmoderated, and two other participants — I'll call them Nadia and Owen — argued it out while the rest of us watched.

Their exchange ran roughly as follows. (1) Owen said the stipulated case refutes Thesis D, because clause (ii) demands transformation or displacement within a finite lag, and two centuries of continuing dysfunction outruns any value of *L* anyone would be willing to name; a thesis that tolerates a two-hundred-year lag is not a thesis about determination but a promise that things will eventually work out. (2) Nadia replied that the case never gets off the ground, because the stipulated dysfunction smuggles in an answer to a question the handout left open: functional for what, and for whom? A rule that immobilizes land is perfectly functional for the stratum that holds political power and depends on that land for status and revenue, and since a mode of production is not a machine but a set of relations among people, the reproduction of those relations is precisely what the rule secured. (3) Owen said that substitutes a different explanans for the one actually on the page: if the referent of functional adaptation can be shifted from the mode of production to whichever group happens to be powerful, then in any society at any time some institution is functional for someone powerful, and Thesis D becomes incapable of being contradicted. He added that on Nadia's reading the stipulated dysfunction evaporates, so she is not defending D against the counterexample but denying that it is one, at the cost of denying D any content. (4) Nadia said content is retained, because D does rule something out — the indefinite survival of an institution dysfunctional for the reproduction of the prevailing relations — and because the case as stipulated ends in abolition by statute, which is exactly what D predicts; Owen, she said, wants to book the eventual abolition as the theory's due and the two-century delay as its refutation, charging the same fact twice. (5) Owen said the timing question is therefore where the theory is weakest, and that predictions of direction without timing are respectable only where there is an independently characterized mechanism that lets one say what would count as that mechanism's being blocked: a geologist can say a coast will erode without attaching a date because erosion is a specified process. Thesis D specifies no mechanism linking institutional form to mode of production, so the situation in which the lag has not yet run out and the situation in which the thesis is false are observationally indistinguishable, and an *L* that is finite but unspecified is compatible with any duration at all. (6) Nadia said this applies a standard that would dismantle most of the social sciences, including ones Owen presumably accepts: nobody can attach a date to the lag by which a currency's depreciation feeds through into a trade balance, and that has not been taken as a reason to abandon the claim. If a lagged thesis is inadmissible unless its lag is datable, almost nothing in the field survives, which suggests the objection is not really about D. (7) Owen conceded that requiring datable lags everywhere is too strong, but insisted that the economist is nonetheless better placed, because she can name what would interfere — a simultaneous collapse in foreign demand, say — whereas Thesis D supplies no list of conditions that would block the displacement clause (ii) promises, and without such a list the concession in the first half of his own point gives Nadia nothing she did not already have.

Please work through this exchange move by move rather than settling for a verdict on the whole. For each of the seven numbered moves, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, a wash, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and justify the rating in a sentence or two — I want to know, for instance, whether Nadia's move (2) is a legitimate appeal to the relational character of a mode of production or an illicit retreat from what clause (i) actually says, and whether Owen's move (3) establishes that D has become irrefutable or merely that it has been restated. Then tell me who is right about which specific sub-questions, who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing — in particular whether there is a way of formulating the disagreement, or of reading Thesis D, that neither of them put on the table during the argument. For what it's worth, Nadia's move (6) struck me as decisive while I was sitting there, and Owen's move (7) struck me as decisive on the walk home, which is why I'm asking instead of settling it myself.
