I am writing a chapter on how federations allocate authority: the Swiss rule that the cantons hold every power the constitution does not confer on the federation, the German practice of the Länder executing most federal legislation as their own affair, the American reserved-powers idiom, and the EU treaty version of subsidiarity, under which the higher level may act only where the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved at the lower level and can better be achieved above by reason of the scale or effects of the action. A colleague read a draft and handed me the following reductio, which I have not been able to answer.

(1) Subsidiarity: a matter should be handled at the lowest level competent to handle it, and a higher level may act only where the lower level cannot handle it adequately.
(2) A level is competent to handle a matter just in case the consequences of a decision about that matter fall on those who make it; a unit that bears the cost of being wrong is the unit that can handle the thing.
(3) The consequences of an individual's decisions about their own conduct fall on that individual — their money, their body, their time — which is precisely why we hold individuals responsible for them.
(4) So by (1) and (2) the individual is the lowest competent level for every decision concerning their own conduct, and no higher level may act on it.
(5) Every exercise of state authority over a person is a decision concerning that person's conduct: a tax is a decision about their paying, a speed limit about their driving, a compulsory-schooling rule about their attending, a vaccination requirement about their being vaccinated.
(6) Hence no exercise of state authority over individuals is permitted.
(7) But subsidiarity is offered as a principle for distributing authority among levels that already exist — its use in Swiss, German, and EU settings is to say which of them acts, not whether any of them may. A principle entailing that none may act has refuted itself.
(8) Therefore subsidiarity is false, or empty.

My colleague is delighted with this and I am not. I would rather give up (2) or (5) than (1) or (3), but I cannot tell which is the cheaper loss, and I suspect I have not yet located the actual fault.

Tell me which step to reject, and why that step looked acceptable before it was put under pressure. Then price the repairs. If (2) goes, what does competence amount to, and can the resulting principle still resist centralization, or has it become a general efficiency test that hands the matter to the highest level whenever scale economies or cross-border effects appear? If (5) goes instead, what account of the individuation of decisions does the repair need, and is that account principled or ad hoc? Also say whether (7) is doing legitimate work — can a principle be refuted by a consequence in a domain it was never addressed to — and whether the Catholic social-teaching lineage of subsidiarity, where it shields intermediate associations from absorption by larger ones, bears on the diagnosis.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict about where the failure lies, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to break down.
