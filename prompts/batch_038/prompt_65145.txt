Assigned question: Is a genuine moral dilemma — a situation in which an agent is morally required to perform each of two actions that cannot both be performed — possible? Address both the logical and the phenomenological dimensions.

My partner's answer:

Genuine moral dilemmas appear to be impossible at the level of logic yet inescapable at the level of experience, and I want to argue that the tension between these two verdicts is only apparent.

Start with the logic. In standard deontic logic, obligation is modelled by a necessity operator, and the system therefore accepts that whatever follows from an obligatory state of affairs is itself obligatory. Suppose an agent ought to do A and ought not to do A. Agglomerating these two obligations yields the obligation of a contradiction, and since a contradiction implies anything, the logic makes every action obligatory and collapses into triviality. Any ethical theory that admitted real dilemmas would therefore lose all predictive content. The usual escape routes are to deny agglomeration or to weaken the underlying logic, and there are deontic systems built precisely to hold conflicting obligations without explosion; the difficulty is that a theory which refuses to tell the agent that she cannot be under both obligations has given up much of what we wanted it for.

Phenomenology points the other way. Sartre's student, torn between his mother and the Resistance, does not experience a puzzle about which of two competing considerations is weightier; he experiences two demands, and whichever he answers he has failed the other. Williams describes the feeling left behind in cases of this kind, such as the driver who runs over a child through no negligence of his own, as agent-regret. Agent-regret is a species of guilt: to feel it is to acknowledge that one has done wrong, and that is why the driver, though he broke no rule, is properly blamed. The persistence of such feelings after the best available choice is evidence that the obligation not acted on never ceased to bind. Ross's apparatus of prima facie duties does not dissolve the problem either, because on Ross's account the agent who acts on the weightier duty still fails, all things considered, to do something she ought to do.

So dilemmas are logically impossible but morally real, and the honest response is to treat the logical result as an artefact of an over-ambitious formalism.
