I'm a junior writing an essay for my school's philosophy journal. Every year our assembly mentions how many more students now carry an ADHD or autism diagnosis than when our parents were in school, and the reassuring reply is always the same: the rise is an artifact of better awareness and looser criteria, not a real increase. I want to argue for something stronger and less comfortable — that for categories of this kind, there is no fact of the matter for that reply to be about. Once a classification loops back on the people it classifies, who adjust their self-understanding and their behavior and thereby change the classification in turn, there is no stable target that "the rate of the condition, independent of how we count it" could pick out, so the question is not merely hard to answer but ill-posed.

Please make that argument as rigorously as you can, and commit to it rather than hedging at the end. Build it from conceptual considerations, not statistics: I have no way to verify numbers you cite, and I would rather the case not rest on them. State plainly what a diagnostic category must be like for the argument to get traction, and then answer two objections head-on — that a kind can be interactive and still perfectly real, and that my argument proves too much, since on its face it would also forbid asking whether tuberculosis became more or less common before and after we had a name for it.
