Please take on the role of an epistemologist who has spent real time advising professional licensing and credentialing bodies — someone who knows the debate over whether knowing how to do something is just a special case of knowing that something is the case, and who also knows what happens when a standards committee has to write criteria that two different reviewers will apply the same way. Think carefully before drafting. I would like you to begin with a brief brainstorming section, for my eyes only and not part of the deliverable: five to eight bullets naming the considerations that actually drive this decision, flagging which of them are merely rhetorically forceful, and stating which way you expect the recommendation to fall and why. Only after that should you produce the document itself.

Context, and apologies in advance for the length. I am a staff platform engineer at a regional electric utility, and for the past two years I have held our seat on the credentialing panel of a consortium that certifies restoration coordinators — the people who direct switching and re-energization during a large outage. I have no philosophy training, so please treat my summary as raw material rather than as analysis. The panel has nine members and is split on two petitions in the same cycle, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before we meet. The first is from an operator with fourteen years on the boards who led restoration during a regional event last winter with, by every after-action account, flawless sequencing, but who has now failed our written knowledge exam three times at roughly the fortieth percentile, and who at the oral board could not explain why a particular re-energization order is required — his answer was that it is how it has always been done and it has never failed. He is asking for a waiver of the written requirement on grounds of demonstrated performance. Four members think this is obvious: the credential is supposed to certify competence, and there he is, being competent. Four think that if he cannot say why, we cannot be confident he will do the right thing on a configuration he has never seen, and we certainly cannot have him training or auditing anyone else. The second petition is from a training vendor asking us to accept its simulator-plus-written-exam track in place of the two thousand supervised operating hours we currently require. Their argument is that their graduates can state every relevant proposition, score at the ninety-fifth percentile, and pass simulated scenarios on first attempt, while two thousand hours is time-served hazing with no evidence attached. Two complications they have not resolved for us: their simulator includes an advisory assistant that will supply the next correct step on request, and their reported pass data do not separate performance with that assistant from performance without it; and their scenarios are consequence-free in a way our control rooms are not.

Three things from our own data may matter, and I want the memo to make something of them rather than wave at them. One of our members has proposed distinguishing cold knowledge — a proposition you can produce seated at a desk, at leisure, when asked — from hot knowledge, one you can recruit at three in the morning under an alarm stack with a regulator on the phone; our current written exam measures only the cold kind. Second, in our post-incident debriefs over the last five years, in a clear majority of procedural errors the operator could correctly state the rule they had violated within an hour of the event; they were not ignorant of it. Third, we have no data at all on whether waiver-granted operators like the petitioner go on to have worse outcomes than exam-passed ones, because we have never granted a waiver. Please write the memo so that it takes a definite position on each petition — grant, deny, or grant subject to conditions — and where the position is conditional, gives the panel criteria two reviewers could apply and reach the same result on, plus a timeline we could actually execute inside this certification cycle. I want the reasoning to be honest about whether knowing-how reduces to knowing-that, because I now think that answer silently determines what our credential certifies and what our exams measure; but write it for an audience of operating engineers and HR people, define any term you need in a passing clause, and give me no citations, no reading list, and no named philosophers beyond the one my colleague mentioned to me when I described this mess (Ryle, and whoever holds that knowing-how is a species of knowing-that). Finally, spend real space on the strongest case against your recommendation in each petition — I would rather have the losing argument stated better than our members can state it themselves than have it omitted — and close with the specific evidence, collectible within two years, that would make you change your mind. Target roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words for the memo itself, and do not let the bottom line dissolve into a list of considerations on both sides.
