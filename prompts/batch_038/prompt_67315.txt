I am auditing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of personal identity this term, and the reading on mind uploading keeps circling back to the gradual-replacement argument: if the neurons of a brain are replaced one at a time with functionally identical artificial components, there is no moment at which anything could plausibly be lost, and so consciousness and the identity of the person must survive the process — a conclusion then recruited in support of the broader claim that a sufficiently faithful emulation is a continuation of the original rather than a mere copy. I follow the logic of the argument well enough; what my reading group has now failed twice to settle is whether it was already put to a crude empirical test decades ago by the split-brain patients. What I would like is a disciplined case analysis of that episode through the lens of the gradual-replacement argument, running in both directions: the theory illuminating the case, and the case interrogating the theory.

So that you can work from the same starting point I am, here are the facts as I understand them. From the early 1960s, surgeons in the United States began severing the corpus callosum in patients with intractable epilepsy, and Sperry, Gazzaniga and their collaborators studied the consequences; the work was widely reported in the late 1960s and eventually earned Sperry a Nobel Prize. In the standard testing arrangement, an image flashed briefly to the left visual field of such a patient could not be named aloud, yet the left hand — controlled by the hemisphere that had seen it — could select the matching object from an array, and could sometimes spell it out with letter tiles, while the speech-competent hemisphere sincerely disclaimed any knowledge of what had been shown. There are the well-known reports of the hands working at cross purposes (one pulling trousers up while the other pulled them down; one striking out while the other restrained it) and the celebrated confabulation finding in which a patient shown a chicken claw in one visual field and a snow scene in the other chose a shovel, then explained the choice by reference to the chicken, supplying a connection to which he had no access. Nagel argued in the early 1970s that this material threatens the very idea of a single unified consciousness, and Parfit's treatment of Division uses bisection as a case with no single continuer, in which the one-to-one requirement of identity fails even though everything that plausibly matters is preserved.

On that basis, please work through the following. First, set out precisely what the gradual-replacement argument presupposes at each step — not merely that behaviour and local causal organisation are maintained, but that there is a single subject throughout whose experience there is, and that no step is large enough to matter. Second, map the split-brain episode onto those presuppositions: what is the analogue of the replacement, and does severing a commissure count as a small change in a functional architecture, given that it is a subtraction rather than a substitution and that what it removes is integration rather than any locally specifiable function? Third, say which features of the episode the argument can accommodate and which it misdescribes or cannot reach at all — including whether the patients' own reports and behaviour are the right evidence for a question about experience, and what the interpreter findings do to any inference from report to phenomenology. Fourth, address the sorites-shaped premise directly: the argument needs it to be true that no individually negligible alteration can destroy the subject, yet here a lesion involving a tiny fraction of the brain's mass produced, on the standard reading, two apparently separate streams of visual knowledge and volition in one skull. Does that constitute a counterexample to the premise, or only a demonstration that the premise was never about physical quantity? Fifth, and most importantly, tell me what the case implies for uploading specifically: if gradual replacement is carried out on a system whose functional architecture permits branching, does the theory deliver one survivor, two, or neither, and does that turn uploading into fission — with all the consequences Parfit draws for identity and for what rational concern tracks? Sixth, treat the fragility of the record as a live issue rather than a footnote: later work has reported considerably more interhemispheric integration than the classic picture implies, whether through surviving commissures and subcortical routes, through the brief and artificial lateralised presentation the tests depend on, or through the confabulatory gloss that the speaking hemisphere lays over everything. Say what would follow for your analysis if the classic results are substantially over-read, and what would follow if they stand. Finally, give an honest account of where the gradual-replacement argument is simply silent about this episode, and a verdict on whether the split-brain case functions for that argument as a confirmation, an anomaly, or something it was never designed to anticipate.

Please write in British English. I am not after references, a bibliography or a reading list — I want the reasoning, with any uncertainty about the empirical record flagged as uncertainty rather than smoothed over.
