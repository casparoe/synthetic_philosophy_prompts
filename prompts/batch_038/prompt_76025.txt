Last autumn I sat on the committee that ran our institute's open week, and we tried an unusual contest, modeled on the Luring Lottery that Scientific American ran in 1983 out of Hofstadter's column. The rules were these: entries were free and unlimited, one click apiece on a web form, with nothing preventing a script; the pot was $1,000; one entry would be drawn at random from all the entries received; and the winner of that draw would receive the pot divided by the total number of entries. So if a thousand entries arrived, the winner took a dollar. Our announcement stated the rules, mentioned the original, and recommended nothing. In the end about nine hundred people entered. Roughly seven hundred of them submitted exactly one entry. A dozen others, between them, submitted about forty thousand. We made the draw and paid out two and a half cents.

Two members of the committee, Marta and Jonah, argued about what the thing had shown, at length, over two days. I stayed out of it and was the only one who took notes, so I have the exchange in order but no view I trust.

Marta began by saying the result vindicated superrational reasoning and indicted the dozen scripters. The superrational answer, she said, was to submit one entry; the seven hundred who did so had found it without being told; and the scripters had taken a scheme whose value depended on restraint and burned it down for two and a half cents.

Jonah said she had the wrong answer and the wrong diagnosis. In a game where the pot is divided by the total, an everyone-submits-one world is nearly worthless: with nine hundred entries the winner would have collected a dollar and eleven cents, and each player's expectation would have been a tenth of a cent. The value in this game lies in almost everyone staying out, which is why the magazine's own superrational recommendation had not been to submit a single entry but to roll a simulated die with as many sides as the expected number of entrants and to enter only on a one. Had nine hundred people done that, there would have been better than a one-in-three chance that exactly one of them entered and walked off with the whole thousand, and each player's expectation would have been of the order of half a dollar — several hundred times Marta's figure. As for the scripters, he said they were not confused at all: the game tells you to submit as many entries as everyone else puts in between them, so there is no finite stopping point, no equilibrium, and the runaway was designed in.

Marta said the die-roll recommendation was where the whole idea came apart. Superrationality works by assuming that every player runs the same reasoning and so arrives at the same answer. But a mixed strategy requires players to arrive at the same procedure and then diverge in execution: nine hundred people rolling the same die must somehow get different numbers. If like-mindedness is deep enough to guarantee the same answer, she asked, why isn't it deep enough to guarantee the same roll? She added that Jonah was applying a double standard, since he accepted precisely this move in the Platonia dilemma — twenty people, one of whom gets a billion dollars if and only if exactly one of the twenty sends a telegram, nobody getting anything if two send one or none does, communication forbidden — where the superrational answer is likewise to randomize, sending with probability one in twenty, for about a 37 percent chance that exactly one sends. Our contest, she said, was the better testbed because you could watch the failure happen.

Jonah said the objection proved too much. The premise of superrationality, he said, is sameness of reasoning, not sameness of physical noise: there is no contradiction in nine hundred people running one algorithm on nine hundred independent sources of randomness. He granted the worry was not empty — if the rolls did correlate, everyone would enter or no one would — but said it is answered by insisting on genuinely independent dice rather than, for instance, everybody consulting the same page of the same newspaper. He then said Marta's appeal to the Platonia dilemma was a retreat to a game missing the feature that made ours interesting: there the strategy space is two options and the prize does not shrink, whereas in the lottery the pot collapses as entries rise, so the two are not variants of one another.

Marta's last move was about the data. She said Jonah's own recommendation was untestable in principle, because entering only on a one looks exactly like not being bothered to click twice; so if the modal submission of one entry proves nothing about superrationality, as he claimed, then the outcome he predicted proves nothing either. Jonah agreed the confound was real but said it cut against the experiment rather than against him: what we measured was not whether people can reason alike but how many of them own a for loop. He pointed out that when the magazine ran the original, entrants submitted googolplexes and postcards crowded with expressions designed to denote the largest number that would fit, the prize shrank to a minuscule fraction of a cent, and the magazine could not work out who had won — reportedly to the alarm of the publisher, who did not care to bet the company on a game in which universal compliance with the superrational rule might have cost a million dollars. Our two and a half cents, he said, was the same result at a smaller scale, and Marta was calling a failed experiment a successful one because she liked the moral.

Then say who was more right overall, and separately say what the two of them between them missed — about the contest, about whether it was worth running, and about the relationship between the two thought experiments they kept trading. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
