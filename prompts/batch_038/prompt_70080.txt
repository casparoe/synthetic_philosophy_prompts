For the past two years I have convened an interdisciplinary reading group on the ontology of social categories. We are about a dozen: mostly social ontologists working in the tradition that treats things like money, borders, and races as mind-dependent but causally serious, one historical demographer, a sociologist of youth culture, and two people who came to the group from consumer research and never quite left. This term the topic is whether named birth cohorts are real kinds, and we have arrived at a standstill that I do not know how to break.

The first camp holds that generations are genuine social kinds, real in roughly the way a recession is real: not part of the furniture of the world independently of us, but constituted by our practices and classifications while exerting causal force in virtue of that constitution. Their argument is that partitioning people by birth year is not arbitrary, because formative conditions — a war, a housing regime, the arrival of a particular communication technology during adolescence, a shift in the age of first parenthood — fall on people at approximately the same ages, so cohorts acquire durable and mutually distinct dispositions that persist across the life course and are not merely age or period effects wearing a costume. The second camp holds that generations are artifacts of journalism, publishing, and marketing: the boundaries are drawn retrospectively to suit whatever narrative is being sold, the same person is recruited into two adjacent generations depending on which outlet you read, the internal variation within any cohort exceeds the variation between cohorts, and once you control for age and period nothing explanatory is left over. What strikes me is how little purchase either side has on the other. Everyone accepts the same survey tables. Everyone agrees the boundaries are contested — we have spent two whole sessions on whether someone born in the early eighties belongs to one generation or the next. Everyone agrees that people experience the labels as applying to them. And nobody has moved an inch in ten weeks.

My own working hypothesis, which I put forward as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a conclusion, is that neither side has noticed that the empirical burden they are fighting over cannot in principle be discharged. In any longitudinal data set the three candidate explanations — the age of the respondent, the calendar period of measurement, and the birth cohort — are linearly dependent on one another, since cohort is simply period minus age. On that view, age, period, and cohort effects cannot be separated without importing an assumption the data themselves cannot supply, which would mean that the realists keep reaching for evidence that is structurally unavailable while the deflationists keep demanding evidence that could never have existed, and the stalemate is a symptom of that rather than of any philosophical disagreement. I am not confident this is right, or that it is not too quick: I understand that some researchers claim hierarchical or multilevel models get around the constraint and that others claim they merely bury it inside an assumption, and I cannot tell which of those two claims is correct.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey of positions and not a verdict on which side is right. Please tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — whether the two camps differ about the world, about what counts as evidence for a kind being real, about which phenomena are the relevant data, or about a starting intuition neither can argue for; whether they are in fact answering different questions under one heading, with the ontologists asking what carves the social world at its joints and the deflationists asking what predicts variance in a regression; and whether the metaphysical vocabulary of social kinds and joint-carving is doing any real work here or is functioning as a stand-in for the statistical question. Assess my identification hypothesis honestly, saying where it illuminates the impasse and where it overreaches, and address whether it would even be the kind of thing that could settle a metaphysical dispute. I would also like your view on the boundary cases we cannot stop arguing about, specifically whether the endless quarrel over where one generation ends and the next begins is evidence of vagueness in a real kind, evidence of unreality, or a sign that we have mis-specified what a generation is supposed to be. Finally, there is a member of the group who keeps reaching for Mannheim's old essay on the problem of generations and his distinction between a shared location in the birth sequence and the smaller units that actually cohere within it; tell me whether that is a genuine third position, a way of dissolving the dispute, or an evasion that both other camps would rightly reject.

End with a clear statement of what would count as progress for this group — a distinction we have not drawn, an argument one side would have to give up, a change in the question itself — or, if you think no progress should be expected, an argument for why not. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a considered letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables, even though the material invites them. Aim for something substantial enough to argue with, in the region of twelve hundred words.
