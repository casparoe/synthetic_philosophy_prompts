I am preparing a single 90-minute session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in practical rationality: 24 students, a mix of philosophy and economics majors, four or five of whom have had a semester of game theory and will recognize the material on sight. I want an in-class activity in which the students run into the breakdown of backward induction in a finite game of perfect information for themselves, and then discover that the explanation they grab for first is not the one their own data supports. No lecture beforehand, and the phrase "backward induction" should not be spoken aloud until the debrief.

Build the spine of the session around an alternating centipede-style game: 12 anonymous pairs, at least ten decision nodes, a pot that grows when a player passes and a premium to the mover for taking it. Three conditions, run in sequence, each with fresh pairings so that nobody can build a reputation with the same partner across conditions — tell me explicitly what this strangers design removes from the strategic situation and why removing it is part of the point.

1. **Full ladder.** The standard thing, played from node one to termination.
2. **Deep subgame.** The same ladder, but play begins partway down it, with the pot already accumulated and credited to the pair as a real endowment that they will actually lose if the game ends badly. Students in this condition never see the early part of the game at all. I want this condition to isolate whether unraveling is driven by literal reasoning about the final node or by something else, so make the entry point late enough to be diagnostic and explain why you chose it.
3. **Random termination.** Before each round, a die or a card determines that the game ends after that round with some stated probability, so no player ever knows whether the current node is the last one. Have the students work out what happens to the argument from the end when there is no known end, and build that realization into the debrief rather than announcing it.

Constraints on the payoff scheme. The temptation to take must exceed what the mover gets if they pass and the other takes, and both must be below what mutual passing yields — the usual structure — but I also want the temptation premium to *shrink* as a share of the pot as the ladder deepens, so that the last few nodes are the ones where defecting is most tempting in relative terms. Specify the terminal division explicitly, since the game has to pay something if everyone passes to the end. No real money: tokens convert into a share of a capped pool of extra credit, with a conversion rule that bounds the total credit distributed, guarantees nobody's baseline grade can be lowered, and still makes losing a round feel like losing something.

Add a private prediction round before each condition: every student writes down, and stakes tokens on, what they think the pair at a specified node will do. I want the gap between their stated theory of other people and the observed play to be recorded, not just discussed. Also give me a rule for handling the students who already know the game, so that their knowledge does not destroy the conditions for everyone else.

Then the debrief. It must map what actually happened onto three candidate explanations — reasoning backwards from the final node; belief that some fraction of the population consists of committed or other-regarding types, which can rationalize early cooperation even in a finite game; and inequality aversion about the split — and for each one it must say which pattern of results across my three conditions would confirm it and which would refute it. This is where the deep-subgame and random-termination conditions should earn their keep: tell me plainly what each is diagnostic of. Follow that with an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce — I expect it cannot manufacture common knowledge of rationality, and I want to know what I have to supply in words instead — and with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, each problem paired with a fix.
