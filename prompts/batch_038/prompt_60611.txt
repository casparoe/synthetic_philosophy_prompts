I'm in asset pricing and I've written the methodology chapter of my thesis as a basically Lakatosian defence of my own programme against the behavioural rivals, and I want a coherence check before my commitee does it for me. Here's the package I explicitly endorse:

1. Nothing is ever refuted by evidence alone — when a prediction fails you can always adjust somewhere in the protective belt, so the Duhem-Quine problem is real and unavoidable rather than a symptom of bad science.
2. The hard core is held irrefutable by decision rather than by evidence, so a researcher can keep a core indefinitely and keep redirecting anomalies out into the belt.
3. Whether a belt-adjustment is ad hoc is an objective, semantic matter about the adjustment and the theory: it's ad hoc just in case it adds no excess empirical content beyond the anomaly it was built to soak up. Nothing about who discovered what, or when, enters into it.
4. Same for progressiveness: a problemshift is progressive iff it leads to novel facts, and "novel" means unanticipated by the theory — a property of the theory-fact pair, not of the history of discovery.
5. But appraisal is retrospective. You can't tell at the time whether a shift is progressive; it takes a run of results, which is why instant rationality is a myth and why we owe the history.
6. I'm no anarchist — contra Feyerabend I think there are rational constraints on what a researcher may do next, and I want to be able to say that some moves are simply not permitted.
7. And I do think a long-degenerating programme can be rationally abandoned even while its core goes on absorbing anomalies just fine.

I'd really rather not give up 1 or 5, those are load-bearing in the chapter. Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions worst-first, and for each one say what has to go and what that costs me. Exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up at the end.
