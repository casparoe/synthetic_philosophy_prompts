You are a political philosopher who works on the epistemology of institutions and who has taught both the conservative tradition (Burke, Oakeshott, Hayek) and its rationalist critics. Please think carefully and treat what follows as an audit of a stated position rather than an occasion to explain conservatism to me: I know the genealogy, I have read the usual suspects, and I do not want a survey, a bibliography, or a list of further reading. What I want is pressure-testing of a specific package of commitments that I have written down for other people to attack, and I would rather be attacked by you first.

The occasion is this. I belong to a working group at a school of public policy — mostly people working on housing, occupational licensing, and criminal justice — and we are drafting a one-page statement of the principles that will govern when the group is willing to endorse abolishing a long-standing rule. I volunteered to write the first draft of the principles section. Two colleagues read my outline and gave me opposite diagnoses: one said I am a traditionalist who is embarrassed about it and has dressed his traditionalism up as methodology; the other said I am an ordinary evidence-driven rationalist with a nostalgia filter, and that nothing in my outline is conservative except the rhetoric. I do not believe either of them, but I also cannot yet demonstrate that they are wrong, and I would prefer to find out whether they are right before the meeting rather than during it.

Here is the package, as I intend to defend it.

1. In any dispute about abolishing a long-standing practice — a licensing regime, a land-use rule, a form of legal proceeding, a customary way of administering something — the entire burden of justification falls on the person who wants it removed. Defenders of the practice owe nothing at the outset. The would-be remover must first be able to say why the practice arose and what work it has been doing; until they can say that, the practice stands. This is Chesterton's fence, and I mean it strictly as a rule about the allocation of argumentative burden, not as a taste for old things.

2. My reason for holding 1 is epistemic rather than sentimental. Long-lived practices carry practical knowledge of the kind Oakeshott and Hayek described: knowledge embedded in habits, procedures, local judgment, and settled expectations, which does not survive translation into explicit propositions without loss. A practice's longevity is evidence that it has been solving problems its own participants could not state if you asked them.

3. At the same time, I do not think longevity is generally evidence of function. A great many durable practices persist because they concentrate benefits on the organized and disperse costs on the unorganized, and their survival records the effectiveness of their beneficiaries rather than the usefulness of the practice. Many fences were erected by people who wanted the road closed, and were given a respectable justification afterwards.

4. When I want to know whether a practice is doing useful work, I trust comparative empirical evidence: natural experiments, cross-jurisdictional variation, before-and-after studies, randomized evaluations where they happen to exist. That is how an invisible function is made visible. A claim about what a practice is really for that cannot be brought into contact with such evidence strikes me as no better than a story, however venerable.

5. I reject the claim that age confers merit, and I would be embarrassed to defend any practice by appealing to the wisdom of our ancestors. What makes me cautious is irreversibility, not antiquity: where a change can be undone cheaply, reformers should be let through on quite thin evidence; where it cannot be undone, the evidential bar rises steeply.

6. On the three live questions our group is actually working on — occupational licensing, restrictions on housing supply, and mandatory minimum sentences — I have concluded that the burden in 1 has been discharged, and that these should go, and go quickly rather than through a decade of pilots and phase-ins.

7. I want to keep 1 as a genuine presumption and not merely as a reminder to do one's homework, because I think the rationalist alternative — work out what the right institution would be and then build it — has a dismal record, and because I think a rule that can be satisfied by producing a plausible causal account has no teeth at all.

Begin, before you render any verdict, with a short analytical pass that classifies these items: which are principles about evidence, which are principles about the allocation of burden, which are empirical claims about why institutions persist, and which are conclusions about particular cases. I suspect some of the friction in the package is a category confusion on my part, and I would like that identified separately from the substantive disagreements. Then deliver the audit:

- Rank the tensions you find by severity — severe, moderate, cosmetic — rather than listing them as equally bad, and say explicitly which pairs you regard as only apparently in tension and why, since I need to be able to answer both colleagues with something more than an assertion.
- For each genuine tension, state what has to give, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package. I am not attached to any particular item and I am not telling you which one I would prefer to abandon; the ranking should come from the analysis, not from my phrasing.
- If any item is ambiguous between two readings, such that the tension arises only on one of them, give both readings and say which one I should adopt.
- Flag any item that is doing work it has not earned — a verdict on a particular case masquerading as a principle, or a stipulation that quietly settles one of the questions I am asking you to examine.
- Conclude with the single revision that would clean up the most, and then tell me what is left standing after it: whether the repaired package is still recognizably a conservatism, or whether it has become a generic caution about large-scale change that the least traditionalist person in my working group could sign without embarrassment. That last question matters more to me than any of the others.

Keep the whole thing under roughly twelve hundred words, cite the item numbers throughout, and do not recommend anything for me to read.
