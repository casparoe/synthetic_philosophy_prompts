Last week I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether the Roman Empire fell, and we came away less interested in our disagreement about the facts — we agree on nearly all of them — than in a peculiar thing that kept happening to the argument itself. I have been trying to describe that thing to other people ever since and have not managed it, so I want to know whether it has a name.

The exchange went roughly like this. I said that the Western Empire ended in the fifth century: the last emperor was deposed, and the imperial administration, the standing army, and the tax system in the West disappeared with him. My friend said that this was precisely what was in question — the Empire did not end, it continued at Constantinople for another thousand years, and in the West the church, the law, and Latin carried on. I replied that a Greek-speaking eastern state ruling none of the western provinces was not the same thing, and that its self-description as Roman was a courtesy. My friend replied that the church, the law, and Latin carrying on was likewise not the Empire carrying on, but at most the Empire's effects on something else. At that point I noticed that we had each rejected the other's continuity claim while accepting our own, and — this is the part I cannot name — that neither of us had stated a criterion in advance that would decide which continuations count. When my friend finally produced one, to the effect that what persists is the legal and administrative order rather than the territory or the reigning house, and I applied it consistently, it disposed of half of his own case: the eastern order that persisted was recognizably Roman in law and administration, but the western church was not an institutional continuation of anything, so the limb of his reply he cared most about was the one his criterion excluded. I am confident the mirror image holds for mine. If what persists is the tax-raising state holding the provinces, then the East did not fall, and the sentence I wanted to defend comes out false.

So the pattern does not look like a single candidate referent being tested by shifting standards. It looks like a family of candidates — the city, the western provincial state, the eastern empire, the legal order, the civilization, the idea of Rome — where each piece of evidence is assigned, silently and at the moment it is introduced, to whichever member of the family makes it support the conclusion its presenter already holds, and where the remaining members simply go unmentioned for that datum.

Here is what I do not think it is. It is not moving the goalposts, because the standard applied to any given piece of evidence never changes; what changes is the thing the evidence is evidence about. It is not the "no true Scotsman" move, which rescues a generalization from an awkward instance by narrowing the predicate; here the subject is what gets re-selected, and the move is symmetric and offensive as well as defensive. It is not equivocation or the fallacy of four terms, since neither of us was holding steadily to two meanings — the meanings were recruited individually, per datum, and there were more than two of them. It is not the ship of Theseus, which is a puzzle about one case rather than a pattern in argument. And it is not merely the complaint that the dispute is semantic: we did try stipulating definitions, and the dispute returned at once in a stronger form, as a claim about which candidate was the real Rome, which no longer seemed to be a question about words at all.

What I want from you is not a label but a carving. Is there an established concept or body of work that covers this, and if so, does it cover the whole of what I have described or only part of it? If there is nothing established, coin a term, define it, and give me its edges: what counts as a case of it, what is a near miss, and what merely looks like it? I especially want to know where it stops being a defect in reasoning. If the subject term genuinely denotes several equally real things and there is no fact about which is the true one, then perhaps sliding among them is not a fallacy but a symptom of a badly posed question, and the thing to criticize is the question rather than the arguers. Tell me how one would distinguish those two situations from inside an argument like the one I had, if that can be done at all — and tell me whether the phenomenon generalizes. I suspect the same thing happens when people argue about whether a given state was really socialist, or whether the Enlightenment succeeded, or whether a friend who has changed over twenty years is still the same friend, but I do not know whether those are instances of one thing or of several things that look alike from where I am standing.
