I write about consciousness research for a general-audience science desk, and I've been stuck for weeks on heterophenomenology — Dennett's move where you take a subject's verbal reports as data describing a heterophenomenological world and stay scrupulously neutral on whether anything in that world actually exists, the way an anthropologist takes notes on a village's gods without ruling on them. Can you write me a short story that makes holding that stance feel like something, instead of explaining it?

The seed I keep coming back to: a lab recruits two subjects who are identical on every task and every scan but give opposite testimony. One describes their experience in exhausting, baroque detail. The other says flatly that theres nothing there at all, that they're the zombie the philosophers keep arguing about, and have said so their whole life. The researcher writing it up slowly realizes her own method forbids her from treating either report as evidence of anything, so the disagreement between her subjects is unresolvable by construction — and she starts to notice that the neutrality she was trained to think of as rigor looks, from the chair of the person being studied, a lot like being told you're fiction. Then something happens in the second week of sessions that turns staying neutral into a kind of betrayal of one of them.

Close third person on the researcher, about 1400 words, invent the lab and the people, no real studies or names. I'd rather the metaphysics not get tidied up in the last line — leave the reader in the same trap she's in.
