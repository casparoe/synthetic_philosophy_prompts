I direct a small research center devoted to the comparative study of moral cultures, and for two years I have had two colleagues arguing past each other in our seminar room. Both accept the same background vocabulary, which descends from Donald Black's work on conflict and social control and is now most familiar from Campbell and Manning's 2014 article on microaggression and moral cultures and the book they published four years later: an honor culture, in which a person's worth is publicly ranked, insults are the gravest injury, and the victim holds low moral status until he converts injury into a public claim by his own hand; a dignity culture, in which worth is held to be inherent and therefore untouchable by slight, so that grievances are ideally tolerated, quietly avoided, or referred to impartial institutions; and an emerging pattern in which moral standing accrues to those who can show they have suffered, and in which grievances are publicized to third parties and to administrative authorities rather than answered directly. Neither colleague wants to praise or condemn that emerging pattern, and neither disputes the tripartite scheme. Their quarrel is taxonomic, and it has never been stated precisely enough to be settled.

One holds what I shall call the Discontinuity Thesis. On her account, the emerging pattern is a genuinely third moral culture rather than a variant of either predecessor, because it has its own moral ontology: standing is conferred neither by public ranking nor by inherent worth but by recognized vulnerability and demonstrated suffering; its characteristic trigger is the unintentional slight, which honor (oriented to deliberate insult against reputation) and dignity (oriented to violations of rights and to material harm) both treat as peripheral at most; its characteristic sanction is not retaliation or litigation but the appeal to institutional third parties; and its characteristic demand is for acknowledgement rather than for satisfaction or for repair.

The other holds what I shall call the Recrudescence Thesis. On his account, strip away the enforcement technology and what remains is the status logic of honor, intact: worth is publicly ranked rather than inherent; sanctions track insult rather than harm, so that a passing remark can end a career while a large anonymous injury goes unremarked; bystanders are not permitted neutrality, and silence is itself discrediting; standing is zero-sum, so the aggrieved party's rise is the offender's fall; and the currency is public acknowledgement rather than private settlement. What looks new, he says, is a substitution of means and setting — bureaucracy for the duel, a networked audience for the village, suffering for courage as the ground of claim — and not a change of kind.

To give the argument a fixed object, both colleagues have agreed to work from the following case, which you should treat as stipulated fact. At a mid-sized research university, a senior visiting scholar remarks in the discussion after a colloquium, without evident malice, that the philosophical writing of a junior faculty member's home country is "somewhat provincial in idiom," and mangles the country's name. Within two days a graduate student collective circulates a statement naming the incident; a petition asks the chair to bar the visitor from future colloquia; several faculty who had no contact with him sign it, two of them citing their own histories of exclusion. The visitor publishes an apology in the student paper and offers to endow a lecture series in that country's philosophical tradition. The chair accepts, the petition is withdrawn, and the matter closes with a mandatory workshop. Over the following year the junior faculty member receives three outside invitations and is introduced at them by reference to the incident; the visitor's invitations stop.

Please draft the joint statement these two should have produced. I want, first, the claims both can sign — about what honor and dignity cultures involve, about what the emerging pattern observably involves, about what it takes for a set of norms to constitute a moral culture at all, and about which features of the case above are not in dispute. I want, second, the residual disagreements formulated exactly, and each stated twice: once in wording its own advocate would accept without wincing, and once in wording the opponent could accept as a fair characterization. I want, third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side — and for each crux a concrete case, comparison, or research design that both would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance, before the result is known. Four variations on the stipulated case should be pressed into service where they help: the visitor refuses to apologize and instead defends the remark in an op-ed; the remark is made in an empty corridor and never publicized; the grievance is brought by a third party on behalf of a victim who is absent and, when consulted, disavows it; and the senior figure is himself a member of a group that has historically been excluded from the profession. For each crux I also want the statement to say which side presently has the better of it, and to record any ground either party has conceded. Then I want the two to identify which part of their dispute is merely verbal — my own suspicion is that much of it turns on whether a culture is a taxon or a region in a space of dimensions, and on whether honor is defined by its terminal sanction or by its status logic — and to say explicitly why they take that part to be verbal rather than substantive. Finally, each party should state what evidence or argument would count against their own thesis, and the two should say jointly whether anything now on offer would in fact move the debate, or whether the disagreement is one that available evidence cannot reach.

Two constraints on the document itself. Refer to the literature by describing ideas and findings rather than by citation; I do not want a bibliography, footnotes, or titles and dates, and I would rather you omit an allusion than guess at one. And please write it as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized emphasis, and no tables. If you need to distinguish the agreed claims from the cruxes, do it by signposting within your sentences and by paragraph breaks, and if the two parties' competing formulations of a disagreement need to be set side by side, put them in adjacent sentences rather than in parallel columns.
