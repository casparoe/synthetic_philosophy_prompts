I am a professor of philosophy, and I have been asked at fairly short notice to deliver a single fifty-minute session to a mixed group of about twenty upper-level undergraduates — mostly philosophy students who have had one course in ethics, plus a handful of history students who will know the events better than I do. The session is advertised as being about why people participate in mass atrocities, and I intend to run it through the exchange between Christopher Browning's study of a Hamburg reserve police battalion and Daniel Goldhagen's rejoinder to it. I should be clear that my interest is philosophical rather than historical: I want the students to leave understanding what it takes to explain a person's participation in atrocity, and how thin the evidence typically is for the explanations we find most satisfying.

Please answer as a colleague who has taught this material repeatedly and who moves easily between the situationism controversy in moral psychology and the historiography of the perpetrator debate. Think carefully before you write anything. I would much rather you spent your first few paragraphs reasoning out loud about what the conceptual dependencies are — which ideas cannot be grasped until others are in place — than handed me a timetable. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, and do not quote from either book; I will supply all materials myself.

Here is the framing device I want the session built on, and I would like you to work within it rather than substitute a better one of your own. I tell the students that any explanation of perpetrator behaviour locates the causal work in one of three places: in the situation the agent occupies (orders, roles, peer expectation, routinisation, physical and psychological distance from the victim); in the culture the agent brings to the situation (the categories, schemas and vocabularies of justification that make an act intelligible and even gratifying); or in the agent's own character (settled dispositions of the kind that would travel with him into other situations). The thing I want them to feel is that these three are not rival bidders for a single explanatory slot, because they operate at different levels and answer different questions, and yet the public controversy proceeded as though Browning and Goldhagen had offered two competing answers to one question. I also want them to confront a second wrinkle, which I doubt they have met before. Almost everything we know about the inner life of that battalion comes from statements the men themselves gave to West German prosecutors in the 1960s, decades after the events, in the position of suspects. So the material that makes a situational account vivid is precisely material that a situational account tells us to discount, and there is no control condition — no comparable unit that behaved otherwise, no counterfactual to appeal to.

With that in hand, tell me how to sequence the fifty minutes, and in particular whether the historical case must be on the table before the three-place framework or after it, and what breaks if I reverse the order. Then tell me about the one case that should carry the whole session. My candidate is the battalion's first mass shooting in occupied Poland, and specifically the moment when its commander, visibly distraught, told the assembled men what was to be done and added that any of the older men who thought himself unequal to the task could stand aside; roughly a dozen did, were given guard and transport duties instead, and suffered no punishment, while the rest proceeded and most of the battalion went on killing for over a year. Tell me whether that is the right thing to build everything on, what features it must have in order to do the work, what features it must not have, and what a better case would look like if you judge mine unsound.

Next, map the misconceptions in the order they will arrive, and tell me at which point in the sequence each should be headed off and whether it is better defused before anyone voices it or allowed to be voiced first. I expect these: that Browning's title means anyone, so that the student himself would have gone along; and its reflexive twin, that he certainly would not have, so Browning must be softening what these men were. That Goldhagen is making a claim about Germans as a kind of people rather than about beliefs they were brought up in. That the celebrated obedience and prison experiments were studies of perpetrators. That the existence of men who stood aside settles the character question in the affirmative. That because the testimony is self-exculpatory it is therefore worthless. And the one I fear most, which will surface within ten minutes and threatens to eat the hour: that to explain is to excuse.

I also want you to tell me what I should openly decline to teach at this level and say so to their faces — I assume the methodological quarrels over Goldhagen's handling of interviews, and the legal history of the prosecutions, but tell me what else, and give me the sentence I should use to decline it. Then give me one closing question that will show me whether they have grasped the problem or have merely been moved by the material; I want a question on which a student who has absorbed only the moral horror answers badly and a student who has grasped the underdetermination answers well, and I want you to describe what each of those two answers sounds like. Finally, I have resolved not to run a poll asking them whether they would have stood aside. Tell me whether I am right about that, or correct me, and if you agree with me, offer something that gets the same self-involvement without the self-congratulation.

Please write your reply to me as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter from one colleague to another: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no bold or italic emphasis. If you need to mark off the parts of my request, do it in sentences and follow the order in which I have given them, so that I can read the whole thing straight through.
