Okay so I just spent about three hours in an arguement with my friend Dana about Whiplash and I need a third party who isn't either of us. She thinks Tolstoy's essay on art basically settles it and the movie is bad art — close to a counterfeit. I think she's using him as a bludgeon. Neither of us has actually reread the essay in years, so we were both half making it up, which is why I want you to do it properly. Don't quote him at me and don't give me a reading list, I'm not going to go verify anything. Paraphrase, and flag where you're extrapolating past what he actually says.

Her case, roughly: the movie is set in a conservatory, and he was against the whole professional-training-and-criticism apparatus that produces that world; it worships difficulty and mastery for their own sake, which is exactly what he says makes art inaccessible to plain people; the feelings it transmits are rivalry, contempt, and the buzz of dominating someone, not anything that binds people together; the festival set at the end is all effect and technique with no sincere feeling underneath it; and the film lets Fletcher be right, or at least lets him have the last word. She also says he'd rank it miles below the novels he did praise — the compassion-and-dignity-of-the-poor ones — and I couldn't answer that at all.

My case: by his own definition the thing works, hard. It transmits feeling and it lands on nearly everyone, including people who couldn't tell you what a double-time swing is. Sincerity was his top criterion and the director wrote it out of his own conservatory years. And the stretch of the movie that feels dead to me is the one where Andrew has quit drumming and is sort of living an ordinary life — if his moral-content test really drove artistic value, that stretch should be the best part, and its not. So I think he'd get pushed into some category like "genuinely infectious bad art," and I want to know whether that category is even coherent inside his system or whether one of the two tests has to give.

What I actually want out of you:

1. Separate the two questions his theory asks — is it art, and is it good art — and run the movie through each. Tell me if they come apart here, and which one Dana was really using when she kept saying "counterfeit."
2. Can a film be sincere in his sense at all? It's industrial, collaborative, re-editable, scored and cut by other people, and the feeling in the drumming scenes might belong to an actor's performance and an editing pattern rather than to anyone who experienced it. Does that make cinema structurally counterfeit for him, or is there room for a film to count as one person's sincere transmission?
3. The reflexive bit: Fletcher's whole creed in the bar scene — the story about the cymbal thrown at Charlie Parker — is basically the aesthetics Tolstoy spent the essay attacking. 4. Here's my maybe-stupid idea: he liked pictures of working people done with respect and love. Are the practice-room scenes — the bleeding hands, the hours alone — that genre? Or am I straining to save the movie?
5. The dad, who's about the only source of ordinary tenderness in the thing and whom Andrew mostly treats as an obstacle. Does how the film frames him settle the moral-content question either way?

Then finish with what the film gives this debate that Tolstoy's own argument can't — something about infectiousness with no community behind it, maybe?
