I do the newsletter for our town's historical society — unpaid, four times a year, read mostly by people over 70. Last issue I ran a long piece (about 4,000 words) on why Heseltine's bakery shut in 2011. That place had been going since 1919 and got through the war, the bypass opening in '94, the recession, all of it, and then it was gone inside nine months. People still talk about it like it was a death in the family.

My problem: one of the committee, a retired engineer, read it and told me straight that I hadn't explained anything. Not a bad man, and he wasn't being nasty about it. His point, roughly, was that I said what happened, in order, with a lot of nice detail, but I never said what general truth makes a shop shutting down follow from those facts. Without that, he says, it's a chronicle with feelings. He said if I want to claim the bypass did it, I have to state what regularity connects losing through-traffic to a business closing, and then show the bakery actually met the conditions — and he said the rest of the committee agreed with me only because the story was moving, which isnt the same as being convinced.

Thing is, I tried his way. I drafted a version with the general statements sitting there in the open, and it got worse two different ways. Either the general bit was so soft it said nothing — businesses that keep losing customers eventually close, fine, and? — or it was just false, because Nether Crompton and Barrowfield both got bypassed and their bakeries are still trading. And when I take those statements back out, the piece reads better and people tell me they finally understand what happened.

So I've been working out a test of my own, which might be nonsense. I think a piece like mine explains something if a person who knew everything up to, say, the week the lease renewal letter landed could carry the story forward and beat a coin flip on how it turns out — and if, once they know the ending, they can look back and not feel it was arbitrary. Mine does that. The law-version doesnt do either.

I have to answer him at the next meeting, three weeks out, and I want to know whether to rewrite the piece or defend it as it stands. Don't give me a fair-to-both-sides summary — pick a side, say who's right and why, and if some part of his objection survives my little test, tell me exactly which part, because I'd much rather hand that over myself than get ambushed with it in front of everyone.
