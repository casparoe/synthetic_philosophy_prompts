I teach an upper-level seminar in political philosophy on the family and distributive justice: 22 students, one 80-minute session. They have already worked through the standard economic account of why a two-earner household with unequal wages will rationally have the lower earner supply unpaid care, and through a defense of the view that family policy ought to be strictly gender-neutral — that anything else substitutes the legislator's judgment for the family's own. What they have not yet felt is the shape of the counter-argument: that a rule can be neutral in its formulation and non-neutral in its operation with nobody acting on any objectionable intention, and that the resulting distribution is not simply the sum of free choices, because each household's choice alters the wage structure under which later households choose the same thing. I want a game in which they arrive at that on their own, from the numbers, before anyone names it.

Design the activity and everything needed to run it.

**Core mechanics.** Pair the students into households of two earners with a young child who requires care each round. Care must be met either by one earner's time — forgone at that earner's own wage — or by purchased childcare at a price posted to the whole room. Set that price so the cheaper option for most households is the lower earner's time, and the dearer option is the higher earner's; the opportunity-cost asymmetry should be real and checkable, not a trick of the arithmetic. Each household also holds a single leave entitlement usable by either earner and fully transferable between them. Then introduce a wage penalty in later rounds for the earner who supplied parental care, so that the intra-household wage gap widens endogenically and round two's "choice" is more lopsided than round one's. They should be able to watch that compounding happen to their own numbers. Partway through, change the rule: replace the transferable entitlement with individual, non-transferable, use-it-or-lose-it quotas. Some households should find the quota worth using and some should find it a dead loss and let it lapse — I want the lapse in the game, because I want the debrief to ask whether a lapse is a policy failure or a free choice, and whether that distinction carries the weight the neutrality defense puts on it.

**Statistical discrimination.** The penalty should have a component that is not a fact about the individual's productivity but a belief about people like them, applied where the employer cannot observe who actually took leave. Give me at least two implementations — one that penalizes the individual who took leave, and one that penalizes everyone sharing an unobservable characteristic with those who did — and tell me which is more faithful to the phenomenon and which is more runnable in the time I have. Also advise me whether to have a student play the employer, forming beliefs from aggregate behavior the room can see, or to keep the penalty as a facilitator-administered rule; say what each version gains and costs.

**Stakes.** No real money, but the payoffs must matter to them: a token pool convertible into a small share of a graded component, or an equivalent. Tell me exactly how the conversion works and how you keep the session from collapsing into a detached maximization puzzle.

**Deliverables.**
1. A facilitator's sheet: materials, an 80-minute timeline by the minute, the posted prices, the entitlement and quota values, and the full payoff tables. Work the arithmetic through for at least three household types — a large wage gap, a small one, and one where the higher earner specializes — so I can see that the intended outcomes really are the equilibria rather than artifacts. State explicitly what specializing *against* the grain costs a household, in points; I want that cost to be real but survivable.
2. The debrief, as a sequence of questions that maps moments in the game onto the concepts, with the technical names withheld until then (opportunity cost, statistical discrimination, endogenous preference formation, formal versus substantive neutrality, the collective-action structure across households). Include the question of who in the room acted wrongly, whose answer should come out as "no one," and the follow-up about what that shows concerning where responsibility for the outcome sits. Include at least one question that lets a student defend the neutrality position from inside the numbers they just produced: if some households genuinely came out ahead by specializing, that has to be conceded and argued with, not glossed.
3. Failure modes for a first run, and how to handle each. Address at minimum: a pair that splits care on principle and is then punished in front of everyone, and how to keep that from becoming a moral spectacle; two students who are romantically involved being paired together; a group that spots the compounding mechanism early and pre-commits, which destroys the effect for everyone else; arithmetic slow enough to eat the session; and the risk that the whole exercise reads as advocacy for a policy rather than an investigation.
4. A frank section on what the game cannot reproduce: the affective value of caring for one's own child, which is a genuine good and not merely a distorted preference; the fact that real wage asymmetries between partners are themselves sediment from earlier discrimination, so my exogenous starting wages understate the circularity; the absence of social stigma, which in the real world pushes take-up up among higher earners; and the short horizon, which makes myopic specialization rational in a way a career-long horizon might not. Say which of these you can partially build into the design and which I will have to supply in words.

Keep the room to eleven households if you can, but if a three-person unit works better, tell me and I will adjust the roster. No lecture beforehand, and no reading of the payoff scheme aloud as a moral.
