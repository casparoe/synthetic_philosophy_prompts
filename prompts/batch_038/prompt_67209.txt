reread Of Mice and Men over the holidays and the bit I can't shake isn't the ending itself, its what the ending implies about equality. Here's the argument I've half picked up from podcasts and a library book: when people fight about equality they're really fighting about what to equalize — welfare (how happy or satisfied people are), resources (the stuff and the stakes people hold, Rawls's primary goods, Dworkin's auction and his idea of an insurance market you'd buy into to cover bad luck you didn't choose), or capabilities (what people can actually do and be — Sen and Nussbaum's answer, where the key move is that the same bundle converts into a wildly different life depending on who's holding it, so a disabled person needs more resources than an able bodied one to reach the same functioning).

I'd like a proper essay-length reading of the book through that question, one that takes a position instead of just surveying. Steinbeck gives Lennie an equal share of everything — a stake in the dream farm, the same work, George treats him as a full partner — and it buys him nothing, because there's no structure around him to convert any of it into a life. The ranch is a distribution scheme that prices people by output: Candy once his hand's gone, Crooks with his crooked back, Curley's wife. And the only support Lennie has is one man's private, unpaid, exhausted care, which is why the mercy killing of Candy's dog reads as a rehearsal for the last page.

So: does the novella refute resource egalitarianism, or just show it's under specified? And do capabilities actually do better, or does the threshold-list approach miss him too — because what Lennie lacks isn't some general good at any level, its George, a specific person, and that doesn't sound distributable at all. Tell me which scenes you'd hang this on, and be straight with me about where the book resists the reading: Steinbeck wasn't writing theory, and the ending lands as love more than indictment. And if you think the book points at a fourth answer to the equality-of-what question that the philosophers miss, say what it is and defend it.
