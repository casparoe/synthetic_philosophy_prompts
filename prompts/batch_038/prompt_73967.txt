I am at work on a novel whose middle movement turns upon a taxonomic quarrel, and I should like the biology to withstand the scrutiny of those reviewers who are professional systematists. Construct me a thought experiment, in no more than a page: a single invented population of organisms, with its field and laboratory data sketched just fully enough to be checkable, on which three species concepts deliver three incompatible verdicts — the biological concept says it is not a species, the phylogenetic concept says it is, and a niche-based or ecological concept proves genuinely indeterminate rather than merely silent. My constraints are these: it must be a plant or an invertebrate, so that no reader can smuggle in intuitions borrowed from mammals or birds; no hybrid swarms and no ring species, devices I consider exhausted; the divergence must emerge from data a field biologist would ordinarily collect — pollinator or song records, allopatry, sequence divergence, breeding trials — rather than from a contrivance; and a statutory conservation decision must hang on which concept the panel adopts, so that the disagreement carries consequences beyond terminology. Then show me your working: each verdict and the precise datum that generates it, the single feature of the case that is doing the most work, what would collapse were I to remove it, and how a taxonomic pluralist — one who holds that these concepts answer different legitimate questions and that my quarrel is therefore illusory — would attack the scene, together with whatever answer I might make to that attack.
