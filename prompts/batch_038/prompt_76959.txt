I serve on the committee that allocates a modest endowment — enough for roughly six projects over five years — supporting conceptual and methodological work on whether culture can function as a cause in social-scientific explanation. Our interest is not primarily historical. We are not trying to settle whether Weber was right about the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism, though that argument remains the paradigm case we keep returning to, and his comparative studies of religious ethics elsewhere raise the same difficulties in a different key. What we want is to leave researchers with better tools for stating, and where possible testing, claims of the form "this set of values, norms, or dispositions caused that economic or political outcome."

To fix ideas, I have been working with a distinction you are entirely free to reject or redraw. Cultural variables seem to play at least three separable roles in such explanations: culture as *cause*, where values and dispositions themselves do the explanatory work; culture as *carrier*, where a cultural pattern is the medium through which non-cultural causes — property law, credit markets, administrative capacity — are transmitted, stabilized, and made self-reinforcing; and culture as *certificate*, where a cultural marker reliably signals unobserved traits of individuals or groups, so that the observed correlation is real but the explanans is not cultural at all. My working suspicion is that most of the field's argumentative energy has been spent defending the first role while the second and third remain conceptually underdeveloped, and that this is a large part of why the debate has been so inconclusive. Weber's own notion of an elective affinity strikes me as an attempt to describe something like the carrier role while talking as if it were the causal one.

Given six projects, five years, and no capacity to fund large-scale data collection (small teams working with existing datasets, archives, and argument are fine), which open questions should we prioritize, and in what order? Please give me a defended agenda rather than a survey, and not a reading list — I want the reasoning, not references. Specifically:

1. Rank the questions you would fund, and for each state the criteria behind its place: how many other questions depend on its answer, how tractable it is for a small team without new data, how neglected it is relative to the attention already lavished on it, and whether settling it would change how researchers or policy practitioners actually proceed.
2. Say explicitly where those criteria conflict in your ranking, and how you resolve the conflicts. I expect at least one question that is maximally upstream but untractable on our budget; tell me whether you would fund it anyway and why.
3. Name at least two questions that look important to most people in this area and that you would deliberately decline to fund, with your reasons. Historical adjudication of Weber's own case is a candidate, but make the argument rather than assuming it.
4. Tell me whether the cause/carrier/certificate partition is the right frame for organizing the program. If it is not — if the roles blur, if a fourth role is missing, or if the partition itself smuggles in an answer about what counts as a cultural property — propose a better one and re-rank under it.
5. Indicate what developments over the next five years would move each funded question up or down, including the possibility that one of them gets dissolved rather than solved.

Throughout, please distinguish clearly between what is settled or common ground among people who work on these problems and what is genuinely contested, and do not present your own position on a live dispute as though it were the consensus; where you take a side, mark it as yours. Write in American English.
