hi — so I'm in a seminar on automation and the future of work, and our tutor has this habit of making us hand in our actual commitments in writing the week beforehand so that everyone can spend the session taking them apart. Mine is due Thursday and honestly the whole thing is a bit of a mess, so I'd rather have it audited by something disinterested first. There's one guy in the group who reads everything through analytical Marxism and will absolutely go for the weakest joint, and another who thinks the entire frame is a category error, so I want to know where the joints are before I walk in. Background: a term of reading Capital's chapter on machinery and large-scale industry, the Grundrisse notebooks, and then a stack of more recent automation-and-work stuff which I won't bore you with.

Here's roughly the package I want to defend, in no particular order:

(1) Marx isn't a crude technological determinist. Machinery is not neutral — under capitalism it's introduced as an instrument of control, deskilling and disciplining the workforce and stretching the working day, and the very same physical machine would do different things inside different relations of production. You can't read social effects off the technical characteristics of a device.

(2) Nonetheless socialism inherits the productive forces capitalism has built up and points them somewhere else, and that inheritance is a big part of why Marx calls capitalism historically progressive. The machinery is already there; what changes is who it serves.

(3) On crisis, the mechanism is the tendency of the rate of profit to fall: mechanisation raises the organic composition of capital, and since living labour is the only source of surplus value, the general rate of profit tends downward and you get crises.

(4) But the fragment on machines in the Grundrisse is the more interesting claim and I lean on it harder: once production rests on the general intellect — science, automation, socially accumulated knowledge — direct labour time stops being a remotely sensible measure of wealth, so the law of value doesn't so much decline as lose its object altogether.

(5) I don't think (3) and (4) are competing theories of crisis. They're one contradiction seen from two ends: capitalism is driven to minimise necessary labour time while simultaneously insisting that labour time is the sole source and measure of wealth.

(6) Politically I'm broadly with the accelerationists: automation should be welcomed, the machines already produce plenty, so the live fight is distributive — a UBI or a job guarantee carved out of the surplus.

(7) Though I also think Marx's real target was never that capitalism produces too little, or even that it distributes unfairly, but that it subordinates people to the self-expansion of value — so the aim is abolishing the value form, not redistributing its output more generously.

(8) And I take the reserve army argument seriously: displacement under capitalism holds wages down for those still in work, which is part of how accumulation disciplines labour generally.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I'd like them ranked by severity rather than dumped out as an undifferentiated list of worries — tell me which one tension is load-bearing for the whole package and which ones I could shrug off. For the bad ones, say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what I'd lose by making it; and if there's a single change that cleans up the most at once, name it. If you think one of these isn't in tension with anything but is just confused as stated, say that instead of being polite about it. No reading lists please, I've got plenty, I just want the audit.
