My friend and I spent most of last night on the Condorcet jury theorem and did not converge, so I would like a third party's numbers rather than another summary of the debate. Her position: the theorem is self-undermining as a defense of democracy, because the moment you grant that individual competence above one-half is what makes the majority reliable, you have granted the only premise needed to weight votes by competence or narrow the franchise — so the theorem can justify aggregation as such, but never majority rule specifically. My position: that objection is idle, because the independence premise fails so thoroughly in real legislatures (party discipline, shared information environments, correlated training among the credentialed) that the theorem never gets far enough off the ground for the weighting move to become a live institutional option; the binding constraint is correlation, not the theorem's political implications.

Give me your credence, as a single number, in this conjunction: for ordinary policy questions put to a modern representative legislature, (i) there is a determinate correct outcome in the sense the theorem's setup requires, (ii) the median individual legislator's competence exceeds one-half on such questions, (iii) residual dependence among votes is weak enough that the majority is still more reliable than a randomly chosen single member, and (iv) no competence-weighted rule that a real institution could actually implement would outperform simple majority rule. Assign a rough probability to each conjunct with one or two lines of justification, state explicitly how you combine them, and then identify which conjunct the final number is most sensitive to — that is, where a modest revision in that term moves the product furthest. Finally, given the two positions described above, say whether my friend and I genuinely disagree about any of the four terms or only about which failure the argument reaches first, and say which of us you think is more likely to be wrong about (iii) and why.
