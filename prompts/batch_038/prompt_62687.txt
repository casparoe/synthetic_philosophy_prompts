I am second-marking for a third-year philosophy of history module I convene — this year it is running under the title Objectivity, Narrative, and the Past — and I have come to one script that I want a genuinely fresh read on before Thursday's examiners' meeting. I am handing it over cold. I have withheld my own provisional mark, both because a second marker who knows it tends to converge on it and because I do not regard my impression as worth reporting; please treat the answer as arriving to you without a prior verdict attached, and give me your own.

Some course context, since the question was designed against it. Throughout the term I have pressed a distinction of my own coinage, which for want of better names I call the sieve and the mould. The sieve: the anti-Rankean point that the past is inexhaustible and the historian must filter it down to the comparatively few items that will count as evidence, so that the filter is already doing work before any argument about those items begins. The mould: the point that whatever survives the filter must then be poured into a shape — an account with a beginning, a turning point, an outcome, and parties who fare well or badly — and that the surviving items do not by themselves possess that shape. My emphasis has been that these are two different arguments with two different targets, and that a critic of Rankean objectivity may accept one while resisting the other. Candidates were told they could use the distinction if they found it helpful, that they would not be penalised for ignoring it, and that they would get credit for arguing that it does not cut the joint where it is supposed to.

The question, worth 25 marks in a two-question, 45-minute paper, read as follows:

Question 2. Ranke held that the historian's business was to show the past as it actually was; Carr and White are customarily cited together as the two most influential attacks on that ideal. Set out the two attacks, and explain in what respect, if any, they differ. Then assess whether either thinker is committed to the conclusion that one historical account can be more objective than another, or whether both must deny it. Marks are awarded for the assessment as much as for the exposition.

Our bands are 20–25 first, 15–19 upper second, 10–14 lower second, 5–9 third, 0–4 fail; the cohort median on this question was 13. Two scripts were jointly marked last week and the first and second markers finished four marks apart on one of them, which is why I am being more careful than usual about calibration this round.

The candidate's answer, transcribed as submitted:

Ranke's dictum expresses what might be called the mirror ideal. The historian is to suppress his own interests and let the documents, once properly collated, speak for themselves; the past is then displayed as it was, and the only source of distortion is the historian's failure to get out of the way. Carr's attack in What Is History? (1961) begins from the observation that there is no such thing as a purely documentary starting point. He distinguishes the facts of the past — everything that ever happened — from the facts of history, the far smaller set that historians actually work with; which of the former become the latter is settled by the historian's own standards of relevance, and those standards are products of his age and his social position. History on this account is a continual back-and-forth between the historian and his material rather than a one-way transmission from archive to page. Because the facts of history are constituted by the historian's thinking, Carr's position lies close to Collingwood's, for whom history is the re-enactment in the historian's mind of the thoughts of past agents; Carr adds only that the re-enactment is socially conditioned. It follows that there is no neutral vantage point from which an account can be checked, since checking already presupposes the standards of relevance that were at work when the facts were selected. Carr is therefore a relativist about historical truth: any two accounts, provided each is internally disciplined and faithful to the documents it has admitted, are equally good.

White goes further along the same line. In Metahistory (1973) he argues that a history is not a list of true sentences about the past but a story, and that the past does not supply stories: real happenings do not come with beginnings, middles and ends, or with heroes and victims. The historian therefore emplots, casting his material as romance, tragedy, comedy or satire, and the choice of emplotment is not answerable to the evidence, since the evidence is compatible with each of them. The same sequence of events can be written as the triumph of liberty or as its betrayal without a single one of the sentences about those events being altered. Carr and White thus agree in substance: what makes an account historical is not its correspondence to the past but the historian's constructive activity, and White merely applies the point at the level of form where Carr applies it at the level of content. Since neither thinks construction can be checked from outside the construction, neither can hold that one account is more objective than another; historical writing can be more or less useful to present purposes, and that is the only gradation left to it. Ranke's ideal is not merely difficult but unintelligible, since it asks the historian to be a mirror when the very material he mirrors has already been shaped.
