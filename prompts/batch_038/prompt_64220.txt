Three years ago I sat through a public lecture in which the speaker argued that the universe must have had a beginning, and he made the case with Hilbert's Hotel: a hotel with infinitely many rooms, every one of them occupied, which can nevertheless take in a new guest by moving each existing guest up one room. That, he said, is an absurdity — a full hotel that never fills up — and it shows that an actually infinite collection of concrete things cannot exist in the real world, so the series of past events cannot stretch back forever. It worked on me completely. I have been repeating the hotel ever since, mostly to a friend who teaches philosophy of religion, and next month I am giving a talk at our local philosophy society in which the hotel is the centrepiece of my case for the second premise of the kalam cosmological argument.

The trouble is that I have spent the past year auditing a set theory course. In week four the instructor told me, kindly but flatly, that the hotel is not an argument at all: nobody is left without a room, nobody is displaced to their detriment, the newcomer is accommodated, and the exercise demonstrates only that intuitions trained on finite collections fail to survive the move to the infinite. My friend offers a different diagnosis, which I find harder to dismiss: even if the hotel is perfectly coherent as mathematics, it is smuggling in a picture of the past as a warehouse of equally real items standing ready to be counted, rather than as something that was lived through and is now gone, and the argument may need that picture more than it admits.

I would like a careful audit of the analogy itself. I am not asking you to settle whether God exists, and I am not asking for references — I want the reasoning, not a reading list. Please organise your answer in exactly the following sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** (about 100 words). One paragraph stating whether the hotel can carry the weight my talk puts on it, and whether I should keep it.

2. **The job it is being asked to do** (about 200 words). Restate the kalam argument, isolate the second premise, and say precisely what inferential work the hotel performs inside it — in particular, whether it is meant to establish the impossibility of an actual infinite in reality, the impossibility of completing an infinite by successive addition, or something weaker, and whether it is even the right instrument for the task it is given.

3. **Correspondences** (a short table plus about 150 words). A two-column mapping of each significant feature of the hotel onto its intended counterpart in the case of an infinite past. Please cover at least the rooms, the guests, the fact that every room is already full, the arrival of the new guest, the reshuffling, the absence of any guest who loses a room, and the counting. For each row, state whether the correspondence actually holds or merely looks as though it does.

4. **Disanalogies that matter** (about 450 words). Only those that bear on the use I am making of the story. For each one, identify the specific premise or inference it damages, and say how badly.

5. **Disanalogies that do not matter** (about 200 words). The objections people commonly press that are beside the point — including mere counterintuitiveness, and any complaint that reduces to the hotel being physically impossible to build — together with a short explanation of why each is irrelevant to the argument's fate.

6. **What the analogy imports** (about 350 words). The unstated metaphysics the picture brings with it and the argument needs: whether past events are available as a completed totality at all, whether that totality has a definite size, and what difference it makes that the past is accumulated rather than laid out. Here I want a direct answer to my friend's charge, and an explicit assessment of whether the argument's success turns on a tensed rather than a tenseless conception of time.

7. **Verdict and replacement** (about 250 words). Whether I should keep the hotel in the talk, keep it with a warning label attached, or cut it outright; and, if you think some other analogy does the same job better — for either route to the second premise — sketch it and tell me in advance where it will start to leak.
