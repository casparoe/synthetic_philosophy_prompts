You are to work as a professional moral philosopher whose specialty is normative ethics — in particular the theory of supererogation, moral demandingness, and the structure of obligation. I am a professor of philosophy preparing a reply to a referee report, and I need your help with a disambiguation task. Please think carefully about this before you begin writing: start with a short brainstorming pass in which you list, in rough and unordered form, every distinct claim you can see packed into the target phrase, and only then sort that material into the deliverable I describe below. Do not skip the brainstorming pass; several of the readings I expect to matter are ones a first draft would miss.

The phrase is "above and beyond the call of duty." It functions as the standard gloss on supererogatory action, and in my paper I deployed it in a frankly unexamined way, as the fixed point against which I argued that certain demandingness objections misfire. The referee objects that it is not a fixed point at all but a piece of compressed rhetoric, and that at least two of my arguments trade on shifts between its readings. Her charge, in compressed form, is that the phrase is being made to do two incompatible jobs at once: it asserts that the act is better, morally speaking, than the permissible alternatives, and it asserts that the act is nonetheless not required — and that once "better" and "required" are pinned down, either the comparison is doing more work than the permission can bear, or the permission is doing more work than the comparison can bear. Something has to give, she says, and the phrase is too short to show which.

There is a second pressure on the phrase that I want you to take seriously, because I think it is where the interesting analysis lies. One of my graduate students has pressed the metaphor rather than the logic. She observes that a call is a summons issued by someone to someone — a commander to a soldier, a professional code to its members, a contract to its parties — and so the phrase may not be making a claim about moral requirement in the abstract at all. On her suggestion, "duty" here is implicitly relativized to a source: a role, a code, an institutional specification, or the demands of a particular person. The moral "beyond" is then measured against that specification rather than against the moral facts as such, and much of what looks like paradox in this area may be an artifact of comparing a source-relative measure with an all-things-considered one. I do not know whether she is right, and I do not want you to assume she is; I want to know whether any reading of the phrase can accommodate her point, and what such a reading costs.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict on the phrase and not a general essay on supererogation. Lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, ordered from the most deflationary — the reading on which it asserts almost nothing and is correspondingly hard to falsify — to the most radical, the reading on which it makes a substantive and contestable claim about the structure of morality. For each reading, give me four things:

1. A one-sentence statement of the claim in plain terms.
2. A more explicit formulation, deploying whatever distinctions you find genuinely useful: what the domain of comparison is and against which alternatives the act is said to stand higher; whether the "beyond" is scalar or threshold-relative; whether the permission to omit is agent-relative or agent-neutral; whether omitting the act is blameless, merely not blame-worthy, or something weaker still; whether the reading builds in cost, risk, or sacrifice on the agent's part; whether the "call" is a decorative synonym for moral requirement or a real relativization to a code, role, or person; and whether the claim is ultimately about obligation or a covert claim about praise, merit, or the fitting response.
3. A verdict — trivial, substantive but defensible, or false — with a sentence or two of argument rather than an assertion.
4. An assessment of whether that reading could bear the weight I put on it: could it serve as a stable characterization of a category of action that a demandingness objection must reckon with, and could it survive the referee's charge that the comparison and the permission pull against one another?

After the menu, address the following directly and briefly. Say which reading you think people who repeat the phrase most often have in mind, and which reading the referee is actually attacking — these need not coincide, and if they do not, say so and explain the mismatch. Say whether the graduate student's suggestion picks out a reading that already exists on your list or a genuinely additional one; if it is additional, add it as an entry rather than discussing it in the abstract. And say, finally, which single reading you would recommend I adopt in a revision if I want the phrase to do the least work I can get away with while still marking out a nonempty category.

Two constraints on the response. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to particular works, articles, or authors; I know the literature and it is the analysis I am missing. And do not resolve the referee's charge in my favor by stipulation: if the most natural readings of the phrase turn out to be ones on which her objection lands, I need to hear that plainly, in which case tell me what would have to be added to the phrase to make it express something that survives. Present the menu as a numbered list with each entry running to roughly a paragraph, and close with a short section headed "What I should say in the reply."
