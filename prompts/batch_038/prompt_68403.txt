I'm running a second-year epistemology seminar and we're doing a fortnight on the norms-of-assertion debate — knowledge norm vs. truth norm vs. belief norm, plus the deflationary types who think there's no constitutive rule at all and it's all just Gricean pragmatics, or blame-and-excuse all the way down. To stop it being pure armchair I'm screening 12 Angry Men beforehand, which I know is an obvious pick (jury, evidence, doubt, the whole thing), but I want to steer the discussion away from the standard eyewitness-reliability and group-polarisation readings, which the students will have heard a hundred times, and onto assertion itself. Problem is I cant quite tell whether the film cooperates or whether I'm forcing it, and my colleague thinks I am.

Here's the reading I want you to build up and then stress-test honestly. My hunch is that Juror 8 is a walking counterexample to the knowledge norm. He pointedly refuses to assert the kid is innocent — he says something more like he simply doesn't know — and yet the whole film is him getting eleven other men to withdraw assertions they'd made with total confidence: that the switchblade was one of a kind, that the old man downstairs could have reached the door in the time he claimed, that the woman across the way saw the killing through the windows of the passing train, that the boy's alibi collapsed because he couldn't recall the details. So the film seems to treat openly professing ignorance as a virtue and confident assertion as something close to a moral failing. Is that a vindication of assert-only-what-you-know, or is it something the knowledge norm can't actually capture, because what Juror 8 is policing isn't knowledge but the stakes of being wrong? I keep coming back to pragmatic encroachment here — in a capital case with a mandatory death sentence, surely the evidential threshold for asserting guilt is way higher than for asserting the kid was the shortest person on the block — and I'd like to know whether that genuinely unifies the film's epistemology or is a philosopher's toy I'm bolting onto it. There's also a wrinkle I find fascinating and dont know what to do with: the film twice raises the question of whether "I'll kill you" asserts anything at all — once about the boy's supposed threat to his father, which the jurors decide was just a figure of speech, and once when Juror 3 lunges at Juror 8 shouting much the same thing, and Juror 8 coolly points out that nobody means it when they say that. Does that mean the film has a view about the boundaries of the category — that threats and venting are non-assertoric and so never engage the norm — and if so, is a lot of the drama just a bunch of utterances that look like assertions and turn out not to be?

The bit I most want you to chew on is a distinction I half-invented for the class, so please tell me if it's confused. Call the jurors' claims about the world — the knife, the limp, the marks on the witness's nose, the angle of the stab wound — content-assertions, and call the final vote a verdict-act. My thought is that they answer to different norms: a content-assertion wants knowledge, but a verdict-act inside a reasonable-doubt system wants something weaker and asymmetric, since it doesn't require you to know the boy is innocent, only to lack the grounds for knowing he's guilty. If that's right then Juror 8's vote was never an assertion at all, and the film is about a man who declines to assert for ninety minutes while performing an act that everyone keeps mistaking for one. Push on the hard cases: the impatient juror who flips to not guilty purely so he can get to the baseball game, and the immigrant juror who takes him apart afterwards for having no reasons whatsoever; Juror 3's collapse at the end, when he tears up the photograph of his son; the foreman, whose votes are procedural but still votes; and the two men quietly exchanging names on the steps as they leave, the only names the film ever gives us after ninety minutes of numbered strangers and a defendant who never gets one. Tell me which of those fit my split, which ones wreck it, and whether there's a scene where the content-assertion / verdict-act distinction just doesn't hold up and an honest critic has to say so. And please don't end with the film as a mere illustration — I want the last section to say what it adds to the norms debate that the journal articles miss, something about the social setting of assertion maybe, that these claims are made to eleven people who will hold you to them for hours, and that it's the public pressure to retract that gives the norm its teeth rather than any rule in the head. British English throughout please, and don't quote the screenplay — describe scenes and paraphrase lines instead.
