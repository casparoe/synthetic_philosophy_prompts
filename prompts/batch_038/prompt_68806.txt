Here's the half-baked thing I keep circling. The moral equality of combatants fight is always set up as binary: either combatants on both sides have the same permissions and the same liability regardless of whether their war is just (the traditional, Walzer-ish line), or the revisionists are right that liability tracks whether what you're doing actually contributes to an unjust threat, so symmetry is a moral myth that only survives as a legal convention. What bugs me is that both sides argue as if "combatant" picks out one morally homogeneous kind. It obviously doesn't. A conscripted nineteen-year-old infantryman, a logistics NCO moving fuel, an intelligence analyst who tasks strikes, a drone pilot on the other side of the planet, a cyber operator, a staff officer who signs the operations order — these differ wildly in how directly their action constitutes a threat, how much they know, and how much risk they personally run. And I have a suspicion that a lot of the intuitive pull of the traditional view is actually coming from that third thing, from reciprocity of risk: we feel bad saying the guy who's about to be shot at was acting impermissibly, partly because he's in the same boat as the guy shooting at him. If that's right, the traditional view isn't exactly false, it just has a hidden premise that only applies to a subset of combatants, and a graded picture falls out of it.
