I co-organize a film-and-philosophy screening series at my university, and next month we are showing Alfonso Cuarón's Children of Men, the 2006 adaptation of P. D. James's novel. I have been asked to give a short framing talk beforehand and to moderate the discussion afterwards. My own training is in population ethics, and I want the talk to take the question "is a larger population better?" seriously rather than settling for the usual line that the film is "about hope." The premise is the limiting case of that question, since a world in which no one is born any more is a world in which the number of future people has gone to zero, and the film's opening — the public mourning for Baby Diego, the youngest human being, dead at eighteen — presents that as an unambiguous catastrophe. What I want from you is a genuine reading of the film through population ethics, at the level of scenes and choices rather than plot summary, and one that is prepared to say where the film resists the frame.

Here is the distinction I have been circling and would like you to test against the film, and please tell me if you think it collapses under pressure. Call a view elegiac pronatalism the claim that the absence of future people is bad because of what it does to those who are alive: it strips their projects of point, their grief of an object, their politics of a beneficiary. Call additive pronatalism the impersonal claim that more people with lives worth living is itself better, the sort of aggregative claim that makes Parfit's mere addition problem and the repugnant conclusion so uncomfortable. My suspicion, which I would like you either to confirm or to demolish, is that almost everything the film shows us is elegiac — Theo's collapse into cynicism, the mass despair of a Britain with no children, the way Kee's pregnancy is received as news rather than as a demographic datum — and that additive pronatalism is imported by the audience, not offered by the film. If that is right, then the film never actually defends the total view, and a philosopher of population ethics should note that a person-affecting restriction, or something close to the asymmetry, is fully compatible with everything the film dramatizes. Relatedly: the moment in the Bexhill fighting when the guns fall silent at the sound of a baby crying seems to me to assign overwhelming weight to one particular child, which is exactly the move an aggregative view cannot easily explain, since for the additive view what matters is the total and one birth is a marginal increment. Does the film thereby undercut the very conclusion people take from it? And what should I say about the wrinkle that the same film shows us a resource-starved state caging migrants at the coast — does the film treat the value of an additional person as conditional on a society's capacity to receive them, which would put it much closer to a quality-per-person view than to any kind of pronatalism at all? I would also like you to engage the anti-natalist foil directly, since a student will surely raise it: the film's world is grim, cruel, and collapsing, so one could read it as evidence that a world without new people is not obviously a tragedy, and I want to know whether the film has any resources against that reading beyond the fact that Kee's baby is beautiful.

Two requests about how you handle the material. First, no bibliography and no citations, and please describe scenes rather than quoting dialogue; if you are unsure whether a detail belongs to the film or to James's novel — they diverge substantially — flag the uncertainty instead of asserting it. Second, and more important for my purposes as a moderator: keep a clear line between what is settled and what is genuinely contested. Distinguish points on which people who know this literature simply agree from points that are live disputes — for instance, whether the repugnant conclusion is a reductio of the total view or a cost to be accepted, whether person-affecting restrictions can be made coherent, and among critics of the film, whether the ending is hopeful, ironic, or deliberately indeterminate about whether the Human Project is real. Do not present a contested position as the consensus, and say so explicitly wherever your own reading leans on one. Close with a short note on where the reading takes liberties: what the film's texture — its long takes, its refusal to explain the infertility — would be distorted by treating it as an argument about population size at all.
