I direct the clinical ethics consultation service at a large public safety-net hospital and chair the standing panel that reviews decisions for adults who lack decision-making capacity and whom no one in our statute's hierarchy can speak for — the patients our risk office calls "unbefriended." We receive four to eight such consults a month. Our current method is a patchwork: a best-interests worksheet, the state's default surrogate list, and, when both run out, a petition for emergency guardianship that takes three to six weeks while treatment decisions cannot wait.

Last quarter the panel voted to reorganize its work around a relational conception of personhood — the family of views, of which the ubuntu-influenced moral theory developed in southern African philosophy is the most systematic version, holding that what makes someone a person is not an intrinsic property they carry around but their participation in communal relationships; that such relationships have two distinguishable components, a shared identity in which each party thinks of themselves as part of a "we," and a solidarity in which each acts for the other's good out of genuine goodwill; that personhood therefore comes in degrees; and that relationships built on domination, deception, or extraction are degraded instances of the same thing rather than counterexamples to it. We now need this turned into something a duty social worker and an on-call hospitalist can run at two in the morning without having read anything.

Please write that procedure. Specifically:

1. A triage screen of no more than ten minutes and a full procedure of no more than ninety, in ordered steps. For each step: the question asked, of whom, what counts as a sufficient answer, what an insufficient answer sends you to next, where the procedure stops with a "no" (this person may not speak for this patient), and where it stops with a "refer" (this is a question of values or of law, not of fact, and must go to a human or to the full committee).

2. The relational commitments have to actually bite; a procedure that reduces to "find whoever is most devoted" is not what we are buying. Tell me which distinctions must survive the simplification — I expect at least the identity/solidarity split, and the difference between having moral standing and having authority to speak for someone — and which you are deliberately dropping and why. Then guard against the misreading I fear most: that a patient with fewer or worse relationships has less claim on us. If your procedure contains no explicit mechanism converting relational deficit into an institutional obligation, say so and explain the cost.

3. Include a one-page escalation table giving a one-line disposition for each of: a relationship that is warm but has never involved decisions; a paid caregiver who has crossed into friendship; a claimant who is a minor or himself lacks capacity; a relationship the patient repudiated while capacitated; a relationship founded on the patient's own past abuse; and a collective claimant, such as a congregation or extended kin group, asserting that the decision is communal rather than individual.

4. Run the full procedure end to end on three cases. (a) A 78-year-old man, three weeks post-stroke, aphasic: his only living relative is a son first on the statutory list, estranged twenty-five years over a disputed inheritance and the object of the patient's recorded anger, who now visits daily and wants everything done; a neighbor of thirty-one years, who held his keys and drove him to dialysis and is the only person who can describe what his days were like, is off the list and has been told to wait for guardianship. (b) A 91-year-old woman with advanced dementia, no identifiable kin or friends, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, claimed only by the nursing assistants who have cared for her six years and by a deacon from a church she stopped attending a decade ago. (c) A 44-year-old woman with severe traumatic brain injury whose religious community's elders present a written communal decision to continue all treatment, and who, while capacitated a year ago, told us in the chart that she did not want to be kept alive on machines and that she had left that community. One of these three should produce a result I expect to dislike: I chair this panel with a strong prior that the faithful off-list person beats the cold on-list relative, and I want to know whether your procedure licenses that prior or overrides it. Say plainly which case is the unwelcome one and why the procedure lands there.

5. Close with a failure-mode section: where the procedure will produce a confident wrong answer, how staff will game it under time pressure, what it costs in minutes, and what it cannot settle at all — including the unidentified patient with no history, and the question of whether a framework drawn from African moral thought may be applied to patients with no connection to it.

Mark every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedure's clothes — a threshold, a ranking, a tiebreak — and say whose judgment it should be. Constraints: the attending must sign the recommendation; anything touching withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment goes to the full committee; and each step's output has to be recordable in one or two sentences of chart language, because these recommendations sometimes end up in front of a probate judge. No bibliography, no reading list, and no terminology the team would have to look up — everything needed must be inside the procedure itself.
