I cut docs for about 20 years now — archive-heavy stuff, some vérité, a few films about the history of industries and families that I was part of. Philosophy is basically a closed book to me, I read a decent summary of Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and something clicked in a way I can't quite cash out. So here's what I want.

Translate his three kinds of history — monumental, antiquarian, critical — into the vocabulary I actually work in. And I mean my real vocabulary, not a dumbed-down version of his: assembly vs. rough cut vs. picture lock, coverage, what we shot vs. what we could license, A-roll and B-roll, talking heads, the spine of the film, killing your darlings, the cutting-room floor, test screenings, delivery specs, the fact that the archival producer decides before I ever get a timeline what the past is going to be allowed to cost. Map each of his three modes onto something in that world, and do the same for the part of his argument I find most interesting, which is that each mode is fine up to a point and then turns into a disease of the same organ — the reverent one starts venerating everything just because it's old, the one who builds monuments starts using them as a club, the one who judges the past finds out he's the product of the thing he's sentencing. I want to know whether my craft has anything like that dose-dependency, where a move that serves the film at 40 minutes of runtime stops serving it at 90.

Then the part I actually care about: my own theory, which I want you to test rather than flatter. I've come to think editing is always antiquarian work wearing a critical alibi. The reason is that I can only cut what exists — I can't shoot the past, I can't get new coverage on 1974, so every judgment I make is a judgment about material that was already preserved by someone else's choices, and my act of cutting things out is a preservation act in disguise, because I'm just picking which of the surviving fragments get to keep working. Is that a Nietzschean point about the editor, or am I confusing my constraints with his problem? Note that he says something adjacent about critical history — that the judge is himself an outcome of the generations he's condemning, so the condemnation is partly self-inflicted and can't be clean. Tell me whether my version is the same shape or just sounds like it.

And then give me the residue report, honestly, in three buckets: which claims translate cleanly into edit-bay talk, which ones I have to bend to make them fit, and which ones have no counterpart in my world at all. Two I specifically want you to take a run at. First: his idea that action and happiness need an ability to forget, that the animal grazing in the field is happy because it's unhistorical and the human being is unhappy because he drags his whole past behind him wherever he goes, plus his idea of a plastic power — the capacity to grow out of yourself, absorb the alien and the past, heal wounds, rebuild broken forms. In a cutting room nothing is ever actually destroyed. The cut is non-destructive, the outtakes sit in a bin, the archive outlives me, and there's always an assembly from two years ago that someone can restore. So does my world have any analogue of forgetting, or is total recall exactly the condition Nietzsche thinks makes the modern type sick? Second: the standard he judges everything by is life, and health, and whether a relation to the past increases or decreases someone's power to act. My standard is whether the film works. Those feel like the same shape of criterion, and I'm suspicious of that feeling. Tell me whether it's a real match or whether I'm smuggling my own craft standard in and calling it translation.

While you're at it, if there's something in his essay that isn't about history at all once you translate it — some place where the argument quietly stops being about the past and starts being about something else — flag it. I want to end up knowing whether the residue is a defect in my vocabulary, missing something his has, or a sign that his own standard is doing more unexamined work than the essay admits. Don't grade the film trade and don't tell me my terms are secretly his just to make the mapping look tidy; if a piece doesn't fit, say it doesn't fit.
