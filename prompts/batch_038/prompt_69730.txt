I am a third-year philosophy student currently enrolled in a seminar on Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, and last week I attended the open session of my university's 175th-anniversary planning committee as a member of the public. Two of the committee's graduate-student members, whom I will call Owen and Dana, spent the better part of forty minutes arguing about what the anniversary exhibition in the library foyer should do about the founder. The relevant facts are all matters of public record at my institution: the central library and the university's oldest endowed lectureship bear the founder's name; the foyer contains his portrait and a case of his letters; and one chapter of the single book he published defends a hierarchical account of the races and argues for restricting reproduction among the people it classifies as unfit. Three proposals are live — leave the foyer as it is, keep the name and the portrait but add an explanatory panel, or remove the name and the portrait and rebuild the exhibition around the founding, that chapter and those letters included.

Here is their exchange, in order, as nearly as I transcribed it. Neither was speaking for the committee, and I sat two rows back and said nothing. I am now to submit a short response paper on the argument for my seminar, and my instructor has asked for a judgment rather than a summary.

1. Owen: The exhibition should be monumental. Nietzsche's claim is that history is worth anything only where it serves life, and what serves the life of a nineteen-year-old walking through that foyer is a model of what one person without resources or connections built. Leading with the worst chapter of his book serves no one; it serves resentment, and resentment is precisely the affect Nietzsche distrusts in people who want everything pulled down to their own level. A panel nobody stops to read is a fair price for that.

2. Dana: That is not monumental history, it is commemoration wearing a philosophical coat. Monumental history, as the essay describes it, is a way of facing the past that beautifies it on purpose and discards the particular in order to reach the general; Nietzsche treats it as nearer to art than to a science of what happened, and he makes it available only to a culture strong enough to know that it is selecting. What Owen is describing is a room that keeps every object it already holds and adds eleven lines of small type — the antiquarian mode at its degenerate limit, keeping things because keeping is what one does, and losing the capacity to discriminate between what deserves preservation and what deserves burial. The antiquarian who degenerates ends up revering whatever is merely old.

3. Owen: Grant all of it. But the alternative is critical history, and Nietzsche is blunt about the price: we are ourselves the outcome of the generations we sit in judgment over, and the critic of the past is condemning his own ancestry, which is why the essay says we may try to give ourselves a second nature but that the attempt usually fails and the first nature grows back. Removing a name from a building takes more strength than a six-person committee with a discretionary budget line possesses, and a critical exhibition mounted without that strength will be a soft one — the letters stay in the case with the offending sentences out of sight, and everyone leaves feeling absolved.

4. Dana: The strength requirement does no work. Those remarks are about what breaking with an inheritance costs a person; they are not a standing condition on whether a decision may be made, and if they were, no one could criticize anything, since every critic is a product of what she criticizes. Worse, the test can only be applied afterward: if the removal holds, we learn the committee was strong; if it does not, we learn it was not. That is not a criterion, it is a verdict issued late. And the chapter is not an inheritance in any case. It was a decision he made against alternatives available to him in his own decade, so the fatalism Owen is asking me to respect has no purchase here.

5. Owen: Then you have made the case for monumental history rather than against it. If he chose against what his century offered, he belongs in the register Nietzsche reserves for the exceptional individual, who is exceptional exactly because he stood against his time. You cannot maintain both that he was substantial enough to found a university and that he was too much a creature of his century to be judged. Either he had alternatives — in which case the exhibition should show what he chose and what it cost him, which is a monumental exhibition — or he had none, in which case your condemnation has no object.

6. Dana: Choosing against one's time is not what makes an achievement monumental, or novelty would be a virtue and every heretic a great man. What makes the mode monumental is that the past contains something the present wants to do again, an accomplishment a later generation can take up as a task. Nobody standing in that foyer wants to do again what that chapter does. So the founder's institution can be monumentalized only by keeping the chapter out of the room where his portrait hangs, and keeping it out is the very dishonesty the explanatory panel was meant to repair. Owen is not choosing between two histories; he is choosing a history that cannot be displayed in the same room as the record.

At that point the chair floated a fourth option — retain the name and commission a critical essay to be read aloud at the gala — whereupon Owen said this is what a healthy culture does, preserving, honouring, and judging in proportion to its strength, and Dana said it is the worst of the three registers, because it converts a judgment into a ceremony and lets the institution feel that something has been done while nothing has.

Could you adjudicate this? Specifically:

(a) Take the six numbered moves one at a time and rate each — clearly right, defensible, confused, or clearly mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification per rating, rather than a general verdict at the end.

(b) Say which of the two, if either, has Nietzsche's three modes right as a matter of interpretation, and mark the points where one or both are simply fastening a Nietzschean label onto a position they had already reached. I do not want to assume in advance whose usage is the sloppy one.

(c) Move 5 has been circulating among us for three days without resolution. Give it its own paragraph: is the dilemma it poses to Dana sound, and if not, what reply was available to her that she failed to make?

(d) Tell me what both of them are missing. In particular: can the taxonomy of the three modes settle a question of this kind at all, or does it only tell you something about the person who answers it? And is there anything in the essay's own apparatus — its account of the unhistorical, of forgetting, and of the plastic power of an individual, a people, and a culture — that supplies an option neither of them raised?

(e) Wherever a rating turns on a specific claim about the text and you are not fully confident of the detail, say so plainly rather than giving me a section number or page you may be reconstructing. I have the essay in front of me and would prefer to be told where you are guessing.
