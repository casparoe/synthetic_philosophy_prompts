For thirty-six years I directed a laboratory doing low-temperature transport measurements, and in that time I fitted, or had fitted for me, more curves than I care to count; induction never struck me as a problem worth losing sleep over, chiefly because the extrapolations mostly worked and the ones that failed were caught by the error bars. Retirement has brought me an auditing seat in the philosophy department at the state university and a monthly reading group at the public library whose members are almost all retired laboratory people — two chemists, a hydrologist, an epidemiologist, a former actuary — comfortable with expectation values and deeply suspicious of isms. Lately the phrase no free lunch has been bandied about in our meetings, usually by the graduate students who visit or by my granddaughter, who trains models for a living, and it operates rather like a conversation-stopper: someone announces that Wolpert's results prove mathematically that no learning algorithm can be better than any other across all possible problems, and the discussion simply ends there. I should like an instrument that lets my group determine, from what they already believe about the mathematics and about their own decades of practice, where they actually stand.

Please construct a decision tree, or a flowchart paired with a scoring key if that suits the material better, whose sole entry condition is that the reader grants the proofs are sound — that is, grants that when error is averaged uniformly over every possible target function, off-training-set error in the supervised-learning version and the corresponding averaging over objective functions in the optimization version, the totals come out independent of the algorithm chosen. Every subsequent node must be a question that a working experimentalist could answer from the innards of the theorem or from laboratory experience, never from prior acquaintance with the labels. I have in mind questions of this sort: whether the uniform average includes target functions deliberately built so that the observed data are maximally misleading, and whether one regards that inclusion as a technical convenience or as the entire substance of the result; whether the assumption that the data are independent draws from a fixed distribution is itself already a uniformity-of-nature premise doing quiet work in the background; whether a uniform distribution over targets counts as a neutral backdrop or is in fact a prior, hence an inductive bias, which would mean the theorem presupposes something like what it is invoked to establish; whether a result about algorithms is even the right kind of object to bear on a question about the structure of the world; whether the stubborn success of simple fitted models in physics constitutes evidence that the space of possible targets is heavily non-uniform, so that the theorem's antecedent is never instantiated in nature; and whether error assessed at points outside the training set is the quantity we actually care about when we extrapolate a decade beyond the measured range. Give me eight to eleven such questions, and then the leaves, which should include at minimum: one on which the theorem is a formal restatement of Humean skepticism; one on which it is a trivial counting identity with no empirical content whatsoever; one on which it begs the question against the inductivist; one on which its true moral is that inference is impossible without a prior, which is a Bayesian conclusion rather than a skeptical one; one on which it is simply inapplicable to the world we inhabit; and a dissolution leaf for the reader who arrives already convinced there was never a problem to solve. For each leaf, add one sentence naming the most awkward commitment its occupant thereby acquires.

I would also like the tree built out of the logical space first and the labels second. After the questions, tell me which combinations of answers are jointly available and which are empty, and for at least one empty cell diagnose why: whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or because nobody has thought to occupy it — the last possibility interests me most, since our group has twice produced odd positions of exactly that description. Where two of your leaves cannot in practice be told apart by any question in the instrument (I rather suspect the triviality leaf and the question-begging leaf will be in that boat), say so plainly instead of pretending the tree separates them. Finally, because I will be handing this to people with no means of checking anything you assert, please be explicit about your own confidence: mark each leaf with how certain you are that it corresponds to a position real people defend rather than a strawman you have built to keep the tree tidy, and single out the one node at which you are least sure that the two answer options genuinely come apart rather than merely sounding different. Descriptions in your own words are all I want; no reading lists, please, since a guessed-at citation is worse to me than none at all.
