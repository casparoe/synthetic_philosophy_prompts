I run standards and clearances at an independent production company that makes prestige limited series and feature films out of real events, and I am the person who signs off on what we invent about people who are dead. Two or three projects a year land on my desk, each generating somewhere between thirty and two hundred individual dramatization decisions. Our current internal rule is one sentence long: if a claim about a real person appears in a reputable published source we may put it on screen, and if it does not we may not. It has failed in both directions. It blocked a scene in which a thoroughly documented public figure says something privately to his wife that no source records but that any historian of the period would regard as near-certain. And it permitted a scene in which a mid-level supervisor in a documented industrial disaster is shown reading a warning and deciding not to pass it on, because a single contemporary newspaper account had described his shift as having disregarded a warning — nothing in the record establishes what that man knew, or when, or what he decided.

I would like that sentence replaced with a real decision procedure. Please write it as something my team can actually run: a short ordered set of questions to put to any proposed scene, line of dialogue, character, or structural rearrangement; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; where the procedure stops with a refusal; where it stops with a conditional clearance and names the condition; and where it stops being a procedure at all and hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of value rather than fact. Two constraints on the machinery. It must be runnable by a competent producer with no training in philosophy — about half a day per project, plus a fifteen-minute fast track for individual scenes flagged late in post-production. And every decision must leave a written reason in a file that could be read aloud, without embarrassment, to the family of the person portrayed. Our showrunner may override any clearance, but I want the procedure to specify what an override costs: what has to change elsewhere in the project to keep the whole defensible.

The procedure has to preserve certain distinctions that our one-sentence rule flattens, and I want you to tell me where my list is wrong. Inventing what someone said, versus inventing what they did, versus inventing why they did it, do not feel like the same offence to me, and I suspect the third is the worst and the least discussed. A composite built out of several real people is not the same as a wholly invented character who interacts with real ones. A person who was already a public figure in life is not in the same position as a private individual dragged into the record by an event. A portrayal that adds to a reputation is not the same as one that subtracts from it, and neither is the same as one that is morally neutral but intimate. And I do not know what a disclosure card at the end actually accomplishes: whether stating that characters have been composited and events rearranged discharges an obligation, or merely announces that we chose not to discharge it. If the philosophy in this area has better distinctions than these, use them; if it says some of mine collapse into each other, say which and why.

Two further things I want handled explicitly rather than assumed away. First, identifiability: what we seem to owe varies enormously between a nameless face in a crowd, a named victim, a named perpetrator, and a household name, and it seems to vary in a second dimension too, since a person about whom a great deal has been published is more easily checked by a viewer than a person about whom almost nothing has. I want to know whether the obligation scales with how readily the audience attaches our portrayal to a specific human being, with how readily that portrayal can be corrected, or with something else, and how those factors trade off. Second, objections from living relatives: our lawyers remind us that in most jurisdictions the dead cannot be defamed and their relatives cannot sue on their behalf, so the law sets a floor far below anything we plausibly owe. The procedure must say when a family objection binds us, when it is evidence to be weighed, and when it is simply not our call to defer to — and it must not pretend that this last category is empty.

I should also flag a background worry, because I do not want the procedure to resolve it silently. I understand there is a live philosophical dispute about whether a person can be harmed at all by things that happen after they die, and about whether duties to the dead are really duties to the living, or to the record, or to ourselves as people who tell stories. Whichever of those views your procedure presupposes, state it, and state what would change if the opposite were true.

Finally, run the procedure on three cases. They are invented, and I would rather you not substitute real films or real people for them. At least one of the three must come out in a way I will find unwelcome; tell me which one, and why the procedure says what it says rather than bending.

Case A. A six-part series about a woman who organized a strike at a textile mill in the 1940s. She is a well-documented public figure: trial transcripts, extensive newspaper coverage, two serious biographies. The record is almost entirely silent on her private life. The writers want her to have a sustained romantic relationship with a composite character assembled from three real organizers, because it supplies the series with an emotional through-line and because the alternative is a protagonist with no interior life on screen. Nothing in the proposed portrayal is discreditable. Her two grandchildren are alive, have cooperated with the production, and object.

Case B. A feature about an industrial disaster in which eleven men died. One character is a named shift supervisor who died in the event himself. An internal log shows that a warning was recorded on the preceding shift; nothing shows whether he saw it. In the film he reads it and chooses not to pass it on because his shift is behind schedule. The film closes with a card stating that events are based on the official inquiry. His adult children live in the town where the disaster happened and have not been contacted.

Case C. A biopic of a very famous writer. The key scene is a cruel remark he makes to his daughter, taken word for word from a letter of his own — but a letter written to a third party in the middle of an argument he was trying to win. His biographers note that the letter overstates what he felt, and his daughter's own much later memoir records that he apologized. The film keeps the line and drops the apology, because the apology belongs to a scene that was cut for length.

Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I cannot check them and they are not what I am asking for. I want the reasoning and the machinery, and I want you to be candid about which of the steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure, and about what the procedure as a whole cannot do.

For each of the three cases, end your discussion of it with a line in exactly this form:

VERDICT: [A|B|C] | [CLEAR / CLEAR-WITH-CONDITIONS / REVISE / BLOCK] | [the step number of your procedure that decides it]

The final line of your response must begin "Verdict:" and must state, in one sentence, which single step of your procedure you expect to be abused most often once it is in the hands of a production under schedule pressure.
