I'm auditing a methodology seminar in the history department this term and my paper is due in about ten days. The topic I've landed on is the old fight over when a legendary narrative stops being legend and starts counting as historical evidence — basically the Troy / King Arthur / Exodus triangle. What struck me reading everyone's position papers is that these three cases have wildly different evidence profiles but people argue about all of them with the exact same rhetoric, and my advisor leans maximalist on Troy while my roommate is a hardline minimalist in biblical studies, so I've been eating a lot of very tense dinners. Anyway, I've cooked up my own little criterion and I want an essay that stress-tests it rather than one that just surveys the controversy.

Here's the criterion. Call it the ballast test. Some details in a traditional narrative do no work at all: they aren't glamorous, they don't advance the plot, they flatter nobody, they aren't the kind of formulaic filler a singer reaches for to hold the metre together, and a later inventor or propagandist would gain nothing by adding them. They're ballast, not sail. The thought is that ballast details are unlikely to be invented and disproportionately likely to be transmitted faithfully, so a tradition that's dense in ballast is carrying genuine information about the past even with zero independent corroboration — whereas the stuff people usually point to as evidence (the great battle, the named champion, the miraculous deliverance, the founding ancestor) is precisely the material a tradition manufactures for itself out of nothing.

So: write me an essay that takes this seriously as a live proposal and argues either for or against it, grounded in the three real cases rather than in abstraction. Use Hisarlik and the stratigraphic mess of which layer could be Homer's Troy, plus those Hittite archives referring to a place called Wilusa and a western power called Ahhiyawa, for the first case. For Arthur, note that nearly everything runs through that ninth-century list of twelve battles in the Historia Brittonum, where he's a dux bellorum rather than a king, and then gets spectacularly inflated three centuries later by Geoffrey of Monmouth. For the Exodus, the total absence of Egyptian records and Sinai archaeology has to be squared with the Merneptah Stele already putting something called Israel in Canaan as a people by around 1208 BCE. Treat those as tests of the criterion, not decoration — I want to know whether the ballast test actually sorts the three cases the way our intuitions do, and if it sorts them differently, whether the intuitions or the test is the thing that has to go.

Please make sure you handle the obvious attacks head-on instead of waving at them: that oral-formulaic composition means a singer is neither inventing nor remembering but recombining stock material, so "the poet had nothing to gain by it" is the wrong frame entirely; that a tradition's ballast may accurately describe the poet's own world rather than the events it narrates; that unglamorous detail can be imported wholesale from a neighbouring tradition and still be unglamorous; and that the palimpsest problem — Mycenaean bric-a-brac sitting comfortably next to Iron Age practice in the same poem — might make density-of-ballast unmeasurable in principle, since you can't tell which stratum a given detail belongs to. Also, and this matters for my grade, don't hedge into mush at the end. Commit to a verdict, even a conditional one like "ballast is probative only when condition X holds." Roughly 2000 words. No bibliography or reference list needed, I just want the argument — and if you think the whole criterion is a dressed-up version of something people already use, say so and tell me what it is, but then explain what it adds.
