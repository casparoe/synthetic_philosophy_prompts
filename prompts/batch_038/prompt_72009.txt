So I'm a fourth-year grad student and in about three weeks I'm giving a paper at our department's little philosophy-of-history workshop — maybe fourteen people, twice a term, someone always brings bad cookies. The thesis is a kind of peace offering that I suspect will just annoy both sides, so I need to be well prepared. Roughly: the Ranke / Carr / White fight is mostly an argument about what unit you're evaluating. Ranke's ideal — write it the way it really happened — attaches to particular claims and to what the documents license. White's scepticism attaches to whole narrative shapes: the choice of tragic versus comic versus satiric employment, the master tropes, the ideological implication that allegedly rides along with each. Carr sits in between, worried about the historian's own present doing the selecting and about causes getting ranked by the present's concerns. My claim is that you only get from "narrative form is chosen, not read off the evidence" to "historical claims aren't objectively true" if you accept a composition principle on which whatever holds of the shape of the whole transfers down to the truth-evaluability of its parts — and nobody in that literature actually argues for such a principle, it's just assumed in the rhetoric. So: form-level choices are real, they're underdetermined by evidence, they're answerable to standards that aren't truth-standards, and none of it touches whether a claim about the harvest yeilds in the Île-de-France in 1788 is true or false. I add one positive constraint of my own, which I've been calling the no-vacuous-shape constraint: an employment has to be doing work such that a differently-shaped but equally accurate account of the same events would be recognizably worse at some actual historiographical job; otherwise the shape is decoration and the narrativist's own claim of significance collapses.

The room: two people who basically work in the White tradition — one does tropes and nineteenth-century historiography and is a hard formalist about it, the other is a visiting literary theorist who will say that any parts-versus-whole distinction I draw is exactly the content/form split that poststructuralism dissolved, and she'll be right that I'm relying on it. There's a working social historian who builds datasets out of parish registers and will not care about tropes at all but will absolutely go after me for implying her narrative shape is underdetermined by her evidence, because in her experience the evidence screams at you. An analytic philosopher of science who thinks this whole debate went stale decades ago and will ask why I'm not just doing this with inference to the best explanation and underdetermination. And our own Kantian, who will want to know whether "perspicuity" and "doing historiographical work" are carrying real normative weight or are just dressed-up taste. Nobody present is a Ranke defender, which may be its own problem, since I'm partly rehabilitating him.

Give me the prep sheet: the seven or so objections I'll actually get from *this* room — not the generic "but isn't all history written from somewhere" ones I'd get from an undergrad seminar — ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper. Phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it; the literary theorist and the parish-register historian have almost no vocabulary in common and I want to hear both registers. For each: a reply I can actually deliver in about ninety seconds, what I should concede straight away to keep the room with me, and a note on where the reply is genuinely soft versus where it holds up.
