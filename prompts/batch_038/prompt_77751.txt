I convene a reading group on late Victorian political philosophy, and one of our members has constructed an argument that she says shows Mill's late position on property to be not merely unstable but self-defeating. She insists that every premise is Mill's own, taken from the discussion of property in the *Principles of Political Economy*, from the *Autobiography*, and from the posthumous *Chapters on Socialism*:

(1) Property is not natural but a creation of law: every distribution of holdings is whatever the rules of a given society make it, so no distribution carries authority in virtue of its origin alone.

(2) Since nobody holds anything prior to institutions, the only thing that can justify a set of property rules is its consequences — utility in the largest sense — and the burden of proof consequently lies on any existing arrangement asked to defend itself.

(3) The present distribution in England descends from conquest and confiscation rather than from the labour or free agreement of those who now hold; existing titles therefore have no weight beyond the weight of the consequences of maintaining them.

(4) Compensating an owner for expropriation presupposes the validity of the very title under question; so where maintaining a title is worse on balance than abolishing it, the title may be abolished without compensation.

(5) On Mill's own reckoning it is at least plausible that a co-operative or communal organisation of industry would yield better consequences for the labouring classes than the existing one does.

(6) Therefore the state may transfer the means of production from present owners to the community without compensation — which is precisely what Mill spends the *Chapters on Socialism* resisting.

(7) Yet nothing in (1)–(5) distinguishes land, capital, and a person's own labour: if no pre-institutional claim blocks the redistribution of holdings, none blocks the conscription of labour either. The same argument therefore licenses compulsory labour whenever the calculation favours it.

(8) Mill denies the conclusion of (7). So Mill is either inconsistent or committed to a natural right of the sort he spent his career denying.

She maintains that the argument is valid and asks us to name the premise we would abandon. I should rather keep (1) and (2), because without them Mill's whole treatment of property collapses back into something like the natural-rights theory he plainly rejected; and I should rather keep (8), because he obviously holds that a person's own faculties are not at the community's disposal. What I cannot see is which of (3) to (7) is supposed to go, or why it sounded acceptable when she read it out.

So: is the argument valid, or does it turn on a shift somewhere between the steps in what is being evaluated? Identify the step you would reject, explain what made it plausible at first hearing, and then say what each of the remaining candidates — (3), (4), (5), (7) — would cost a Millian who gave it up instead, in terms of commitments he is on record as holding elsewhere. If you judge that Mill really is caught, say which of the two horns offered in (8) is the cheaper and why.
