In three weeks I deliver the departmental research seminar at an institute whose centre of gravity is later prehistoric Aegean archaeology, ceramic petrography, and a well-established interpretive tradition in material culture studies. The room is roughly a dozen faculty and postdocs, four or five doctoral students, one visiting economic historian of premodern markets, and a quantitative archaeologist who does Bayesian chronology modelling and compositional data analysis; the culture is to interrupt, and the format is forty minutes of talk followed by a half hour of open questions with no slides after the fortieth minute. My thesis is that a particular class of Late Bronze Age transport and storage jar from a coastal region I have surveyed for six seasons licenses an inference to institutionalized, centrally coordinated provisioning — not to household self-sufficiency, and not to a market with a de facto standard. I say in the talk that the assemblage is too regular to be a market and too small-scale to be a state, and that what it shows is an administrative apparatus of modest reach with a real capacity to impose norms on production.

The evidence I will present, all of it my own fieldwork and analysis, runs as follows:

- Capacities reconstructed from complete and near-complete profiles of the jar class cluster tightly around two modal values, with a coefficient of variation well below that of the hand-made cooking and serving wares from the same settlement contexts.
- Petrographic and portable-XRF work puts the coarse fabric of these jars in no more than three source groups, and those sources sit near second- and third-rank settlements in the regional hierarchy rather than at the largest site.
- A row of magazine-like rooms at the largest site, whose floor area I have used to model standing storage volume, is far too small to have held a season's regional surplus but roughly right for seed stock plus something like a ration supply for attached personnel.
- The jars are disproportionately recovered from burned destruction deposits and from fills tied to a single construction episode, rather than from ordinary midden accumulation.
- At the two most distant peripheral settlements there is a distinct variant cluster: the same profile in a local fabric, but twelve to eighteen percent smaller in capacity. Here I make my boldest move — I argue that this peripheral irregularity is evidence of the institution's reach rather than of its absence, incorporation without the capacity to enforce the norm at the edge of the system.

Methodologically, I want to insist that this is not an ethnoarchaeological analogy wearing a quantitative costume. My claim is that the inference is robust in the sense that it survives deletion of any single evidential stream, and that each rival explanation — shared vessel tradition among independent households, a transaction standard emerging from repeated exchange, a feasting or prestige-display account — needs an extra assumption that my data independently disfavor. A friend of mine in philosophy of science tells me that this is just Whewell's consilience of inductions with the serial numbers filed off, which may be true but does not help me in that room. I also want to resist the interpretive framing according to which any move from residues to institutions is an unearnable leap in meaning, while conceding that vessel form can carry significance I cannot recover.

What I need from you is a question-period prep sheet, not a defence of my view. Please give me the eight or ten objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by the product of how likely each is and how much damage it would do if I fumbled it, and labelled by who raises it. Phrase each one in that person's own idiom — the petrographer who will want to know how many thin sections sit behind my three source groups, and who suspects I have split one widely distributed clay source into three, should not sound like the economic historian who will ask why a repeated transaction standard requires a coordinator at all, or like the behavioural archaeologist who will point out that a burned floor is not a frozen snapshot of an activity and that my sample is event-locked and taphonomically skewed. The quantitative person will go after the capacity data directly: how many complete profiles, how reconstruction assumptions were handled, whether comparing the variability of wheel-made jars with hand-made cooking wares is even a meaningful comparison, and whether my two modal values are an artefact of my binning. The interpretive pair will object that I have standardized the product while ignoring variation in the chaîne opératoire, and that institution is a theoretical term I have not cashed out.

For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver aloud in about two minutes without notes, a candid note on where that reply is softest, and a recommendation to concede, reframe, or hold. Please also separate the objections that would be fatal to my methodological claim from those that are merely fatal to this case study, since I suspect I have been conflating the two and would rather know which is which before I walk in. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so that it is not extracted from me at minute fifty-five. Give special attention to the peripheral variant cluster: produce the strongest available version of the charge that I am immunizing my hypothesis against disconfirming evidence, and tell me whether I can answer it without abandoning the move. There is one question I am hoping nobody asks, and my guess is that it is the demand that I state in advance which observation would have forced me to the exchange explanation instead of the administrative one, because my honest answer is that the peripheral variants should already have done it — tell me whether that guess is right, and if not, what the real worst question is. Finally, flag any objection that rests on a misunderstanding I should pre-empt in the talk itself rather than argue about afterwards, and please do not pad the sheet with references or with citations you cannot be sure of; I know the literature, and what I lack is the phrasing and the composure.
