**Question 3 (20 pts).** Consider the following entirely hypothetical case.

Ancestors of a cave-dwelling fish had skin glands that secreted an oily film across the scales; in those surface-dwelling ancestors the film screened UV radiation, and the glands were maintained because of that. The lineage moved underground. In the deep lake there is no UV and also no parasites, so the secretion now makes no difference whatever to survival or reproduction. Call this **population A**: the glands are still there, they still secrete, and the secretion smells faintly of almonds. Nothing detects the smell.

Some members of the same species live in an inflow stream (**population B**) that is full of a parasitic copepod. There the same secretion coats the gills and kills the copepod; fish that secrete more leave more offspring, and the glands are maintained in that population on that basis. The secretion also causes the fish to fluoresce very faintly, and it also smells strongly, which attracts a cave eel that preys on the fish. So the secretion's overall current contribution to reproductive output in B is positive but noticeably smaller than its anti-copepod contribution alone.

Finally, **C**: one individual in population B carries a mutation that knocks out secretion altogether. Its glands are anatomically normal but inert.

(a) State the etiological account of function and the causal-role account of function, and say what each makes the function (or functions, or absence of function) of the gland to be in population A and in population B. Say why, using each account's own definitions rather than its label.

(b) For the individual in C, what does each account say? Which account gives the better verdict here, and what does the better account have to give up in order to get it?

(c) In population B, does the etiological account say the gland's function is UV-screening, or parasite-killing, or both? Explain what the account's own resources commit it to, and say what turns on your answer for how A and B differ.

---

And here's the student's answer, more or less verbatim:

> (a) On the etiological account (Millikan) the function of a trait is the effect that accounts for the trait's presence in the population — roughly, what ancestors did with it that caused descendants having it to be reproduced. On the causal-role account (Cummins) a trait's function is its causal contribution to some capacity of a containing system that we're interested in explaining. No history enters, and functions are always relative to a containing system and a capacity.
>
> In population A the gland contributes to nothing: it secretes and the secretion smells. So on the causal-role account it has no function, or trivially its function is to make the fish smell of almonds. On the etiological account it still has a proper function, because it's present as a result of a history in which the secretion did something that mattered, and proper functions don't have to be currently performed. Vestigial organs are the paradigm: an organ can have a function it no longer performs. This is a real advantage of the etiological account, and A shows it, since the glands in A are obviously leftovers of something that used to matter, and the causal-role account has to say either "no function" or "function = smelling," neither of which captures that.
>
> (b) For the mutant in C, the causal-role account can't say the gland malfunctions, because it contributes to nothing, so there's no function and hence nothing to fail — C is on a par with A. The etiological account says the gland has the proper function of secreting the anti-copepod oil and that this individual's glands are malfunctioning, which is the distinction we want. So the etiological account does better here. What it gives up is that malfunction becomes a matter of lineage history rather than of anything assessable in the individual as it is now: two fish can be internally identical, one malfunctioning and one not, depending on where they come from.
>
> (c) I'd say the etiological account still assigns UV-screening as the gland's function in B. The account defines proper function by the effect that got the trait into the lineage originally, and once it's in, descendants inherit that proper function. Millikan does have the notion of a recent function, but a recent function only becomes a proper function where the effect was already being performed by the relevant ancestors, and here it wasn't — nothing in the ancestral line was killing copepods. So parasite-killing in B is a new use of an old organ rather than a new function of it, and the account has no way to upgrade it. That's the standard complaint that etiological accounts are conservative and can't recognize genuinely novel functions. It also matters for how A and B differ: on this reading the proper function is the same ancestral one in both populations, and the only difference is whether the organ is currently performing it.
>
> One further point about B. The secretion kills copepods, fluoresces, and smells. On the causal-role account all three are equally functions of the gland, since all three are contributions to capacities of containing systems — predator avoidance, chemical ecology, whatever capacity the analyst picks out. So that account is too liberal: it can't privilege the anti-copepod role, which is the one that explains why the fish in B have glands at all. Though that's not obviously fatal, since Cummins isn't offering an account of why traits are there, just of a kind of functional description. Really the two accounts only come apart in odd cases like A and C; for a normal organ still doing what it was selected for they give the same answer.
