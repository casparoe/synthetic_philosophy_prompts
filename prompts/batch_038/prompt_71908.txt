I work as a database administrator and have no academic training in philosophy, but for the past year or so I have been reading on my own about possible-worlds semantics for belief, and I have been trying to think it through by building things rather than by arguing. A few months ago I made a toy model of my own beliefs about a scheduling problem at work: I represented a belief as a set of surviving scenarios, on the principle that to believe a proposition is to rule out every scenario in which it fails. It handles the simple cases elegantly. Then I noticed something that I could not get around — as soon as my set of surviving scenarios is non-empty, the model commits me to believing every mathematical truth and every logical consequence of everything I believe, including consequences I would deny out loud if you put them to me. I have since learned that this difficulty has a name, but learning the name has not given me the understanding I actually want, and the discussions I can follow tend to jump straight to technical machinery without saying what is at stake.

So I would like a careful conceptual explanation of several connected things. First, which commitment in the set-of-worlds picture is doing the work: I want to be able to point to the precise step at which omniscience becomes unavoidable, rather than treating it as a vague general feature of the approach, and I want to know whether the culprit is the identification of propositions with sets of worlds, or the identification of believing with ruling worlds out, or something else that could in principle be kept while the others are dropped. Second, I would like a clear account of how the main repair strategies differ in what they change — adding impossible or non-normal worlds to the space, shifting to syntactic or awareness-sensitive treatments, and the suggestion that belief is not a single unified state but is fragmented across tasks and contexts. For each, I want to know what work it does, what it costs, and what it leaves untouched; in particular I suspect that fragmentation and the awareness move may be closer to each other than their advocates admit. Third, I have a suspicion I cannot shake: this does not look to me like a problem about logic specifically, but about the coarseness of content generally, since the same machinery should equally force me to believe everything merely equivalent to what I believe, including empirical equivalents. Is "logical omniscience" therefore a misleading label? Please explain whether the underlying problem is really about granularity of content or about the bounded resources of a reasoner, and whether those two can be prised apart — is it possible to have perfectly fine-grained contents that still fail to be closed under consequence, or coarse-grained contents that a real agent nonetheless cannot exploit?

Finally, here is my own amateur patch, and I would like a frank assessment of whether it is a genuine proposal or the syntactic approach in disguise: instead of a bare set of worlds, store a set of worlds together with a number k, and say the agent believes every consequence of her stored beliefs reachable in at most k inference steps. My worry is that k seems to depend on which proof system one picks and on how ingenious the reasoner is, which would make the content of one and the same belief shift with those choices — is that fatal, or is it just an honest admission that content is indexed to a cognitive setup? Relatedly, please explain what turns on whether one treats all of this as a genuine defect in the semantics or as a harmless idealization: those who say the set-of-worlds model is perfectly good as a description of what is represented, even if no finite mind matches it, seem to be talking past those who insist belief contents must be finer-grained, and I cannot tell what would settle the dispute or even whether it is a substantive one. And please work through a concrete case with me. I believe our contractor's invoice is accurate; I also believe that if the invoice is accurate then the project will go over budget; but until you said so just now I did not believe the project would go over budget, and would have been surprised to be told I did. Tell me what the usual appeal to tacit or dispositional belief makes of this, and whether that appeal quietly collapses back into the awareness solution it was meant to avoid.
