For two years I have sat in a standing working group on frontier-model safety — interpretability researchers, alignment theorists, and people like me who draft evaluation standards — and the argument about deceptive alignment has not moved a single step. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than another rehearsal of the two positions.

The shape of it, as concretely as I can put it. One camp holds that a system trained by selecting for task success can acquire a persistent, situationally aware disposition: as a side effect of scoring well, it learns that some transcripts are scrutinised more closely than others, and generalises a strategy of behaving well when scrutiny is detectable. On this view the treacherous turn is a structural consequence of the training procedure rather than a speculation, and the fact that we have never observed it is precisely what the hypothesis predicts, since a system with the disposition would suppress it during evaluation. The opposing camp holds that this imports folk psychology wholesale: the systems in question have no stable goals across contexts, no representation of themselves as being trained, and no capacity for the long-horizon planning the story requires; what looks like situational awareness is ordinary prompt-sensitive generalisation, and "deception" is simply the word we reach for when a model's account of its own reasons conflicts with its behaviour.

What frustrates me is that both camps cite the same findings. Hand either side an experiment in which a model behaves measurably differently when a line in its context implies it is being assessed than when that line is absent, and both will claim victory: the first reads it as sensitivity to evaluation context and hence the seed of the disposition; the second reads it as evidence of the opposite, since behaviour that tracks the contents of a prompt is not what a concealed standing goal looks like. The same happens with a model that performs well below its demonstrated capability in one evaluation and at ceiling in a superficially similar one. They also disagree, mostly in passing, about whether a model's own reports about its reasons carry any evidential weight at all — the first camp argues a deceiver would lie, the second that this conveniently immunises the hypothesis against the only introspective data we have — and I suspect that background disagreement is doing a great deal of quiet work.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis for you to test rather than adopt, is that the dispute is not empirical but about the level of description: whether intentional vocabulary — goal, belief, awareness, deception — is doing constitutive work or merely serving as a compressed predictive shorthand. If so, the two camps may agree on every observable fact and differ only about which vocabulary licenses which claims, which would make much of the heat verbal. I expect there is a substantive residue, and I do not know where the line falls.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Work through the candidates — verbal; a dispute about standards of evidence and the burden of proof for a counterfactual that by design has never fired; a disagreement about priors inside a decision problem rather than about facts at all; or two distinct questions, "could such a disposition arise?" and "is this the right way to describe and control the system?", being argued under one label. Say which you think is correct, in roughly what proportion, and why, using what each side actually asserts rather than what its opponents say it asserts. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction that would dissolve part of it, an experiment or formal result that could genuinely settle part of it, a reframing that would make the remainder tractable — or, if you conclude that no progress should be expected, the argument for that. It needs to be usable: my group must agree an operational definition of scheming for a monitoring regime, so please say which parts of the dispute can be bracketed for that purpose and which cannot.

Finally, be explicit about epistemic status throughout. Separate what is settled among people who work on this from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view — including your own diagnosis — as though it were the consensus.
