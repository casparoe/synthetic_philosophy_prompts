I run a grad seminar that's half philosophy-of-mind people and half ML people, and next week's session is on whether the things interpretability folks call "features" are representations in anything like the sense philosophers mean. I want a decision tree I can hand out that sorts the class by what they actually believe rather than by which camp they say they belong to.

Rules of the road: every branch node has to be a question someone could answer from a concrete case or a bare claim, without knowing which leaf it leads to. Nothing like "are you a realist about features?" — save the names for the leaves. Aim for about seven branch nodes and somewhere between nine and twelve leaves. Where a position has a name in circulation, use it; where it doesn't, coin one and mark it as coined. Don't attribute anything to a real person or a real paper unless you're genuinely confident — I would much rather have an unnamed leaf than a made-up citation.

The cases I want doing the work, because these are the ones the two halves of my class actually come apart on:

- two different sparse dictionaries trained on the same model's activations, both yielding clean interpretable features that predict behavior, disagreeing about what's in there
- a feature that shows up in recognizably the same form across three model families trained on different corpora
- a feature you can clamp and get a behavioral change nobody predicted when they found it
- the old disjunction worry: a direction that fires for dogs and for pictures of dogs and for the word "loyalty" — is that one messy feature, or a reason to deny there's any fact of the matter?
- a feature that's extremely useful to an alignment team for steering the model but plausibly doesn't pick out anything in the world
- a hypothetical interpreter with radically different interests and sensory apparatus arriving at a different but equally predictive feature list
- a representation that's clearly present in the base model and clearly gone after a round of fine-tuning, with no behavioral trace left of it

Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of isms. Before you hand me the tree, lay out the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space. And tell me about the cells nobody occupies — for each empty one, say briefly whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered yet. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate rather than papering over it.

Then mark which nodes are load-bearing — where does the whole thing fall apart if I delete that question — and tell me which of your leaf placements you're least sure about.

One thing before you start, though: if you think this request rests on a confused premise, say so at the top instead of politely building it anyway. I half-suspect it might. Specifically, I'm not sure "does the model have representations?" and "are these features representations?" are the same question at all, and I worry my case list slides back and forth between whole-system talk and component-level talk. So if the tree can't be made coherent without seperating those two levels, tell me that first, and then show me what it looks like once you've pulled them apart — including which of my seven cases survives the split and which ones turn out to be doing double duty. And if you think wether some of these cases even have determinate answers is itself one of the things at issue, build that in rather than assuming it away.
