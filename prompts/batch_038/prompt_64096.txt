So next month I'm giving a talk at a work-and-inequality conference in Aarhus — room is mostly Danish and Norwegian sociologists who study trust and egalitarianism, a couple of cultural historians, and one labour economist who works with cross-country survey data. Basically no Americans, and nobody who's read my stuff.

My thesis is that Janteloven and the tall poppy thing are almost always described as norms against standing out or succeeding, and I think that's just wrong: what gets sanctioned is credit-claiming, not achievement. On my account the norm is an attribution rule — you can be enormously rich, famous, brilliant, whatever, as long as you book the outcome under luck, timing, or the collective; the moment you book it under your own merit you get cut down. And the American Dream is the mirror image: it licenses merit-attribution and makes luck-talk suspicious, so saying "I mostly got lucky" reads as false modesty or as an insult to your own hustle. I want to get predictions out of this, e.g. why a giant state-owned wealth fund is a source of national pride in Oslo while a self-made founder is eyed sideways. The evidence I lean on is a coding of founder interviews and newspaper obituaries: Nordic ones load up on family, chance and colleagues, American ones load up on grit and vision.
