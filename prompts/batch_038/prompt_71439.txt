I'm auditing a class of arguments, not writing a term paper, so what I want is the spec. The arguments all have the same shape: "since it's common knowledge among the players that they're rational, and common knowledge that they know the game, we can rule out this outcome / we're licensed to play that one." Before I let any of them pass, I want to know what an account of common knowledge of rationality has to be able to do to count as licensing anything. So: write the desiderata. For each one, the case or puzzle behind it, and whether it's non-negotiable or just the taste of one camp.

seeds — add to these, or tell me some are bogus:

- the infinite iteration has to be earning its keep. why isn't "everyone knows p, everyone knows that, everyone knows that…" out to any finite depth already enough? the muddy children case is the test: the parent announces something every child already knew, nothing happens for days, and then something does.
- unattainability. two generals sending messengers over a line where any messenger might be captured can never get to common knowledge of the plan — that's a result, not a complaint. and yet people coordinate, on conventions, on which side of the road to drive. so the account has to either say common knowledge is an idealization and name the weaker thing that does the actual work, or explain how joint attention gets you what message-passing can't. no just noting the tension.
- continuity. in the email game each confirmation has a small chance of being lost, and for every nonzero loss probability the players land on the cautious action even though the good one is available when the loss probability is exactly zero. any account that treats common knowledge as the limit of near-common-knowledge has to absorb that.
- agreement. two bayesians with a common prior can't agree to disagree, and yet here we are. which ingredient gives — the prior, the common knowledge, or the bayes — and does your account commit?
- the off-path problem, which is the one I actually care about. in a long centipede-style game the nodes the backward-induction argument needs are nodes you only reach if somebody already played irrationally. what is common knowledge of rationality doing there? if it says the node is unreachable it says nothing at the node, and if it says something it has to say what a rational player infers from watching an irrational move.
- descriptive honesty: people stop iterating after two or three levels in a guessing game. whatever this assumption is, it isn't a description of us, so the account has to say what sort of claim it's making instead.

then: which single requirement knocks out the most accounts, and is there a subset of them that can't be jointly satisfied? I have a suspicion that "common knowledge of rationality holds at every node including the ones reached by a deviation" plus "a rational agent's beliefs are consistant with what she's observed" plus "common knowledge survives learning" is already an inconsistent triad, but I can't tell if I'm mangling it. take a stand — give me a verdict and the reasons, not both readings and a shrug. if you think one of my seeds is a prejudice rather than a requirement, cut it and say why.

finish with a couple of lines each on two contenders: (1) common knowledge of rationality is a device for running the argument at nodes that are never reached and shouldn't be read literally; (2) replace it outright with common p-belief, with p tuned to the stakes of the particular game. the spec is the point, the scoring isn't.
