I am in the third year of a philosophy of physics doctorate and am drafting the central chapter of my dissertation ahead of a departmental workshop in the spring; my committee includes one working physicist who is politely sceptical that the chapter contains anything beyond textbook statistical mechanics. The chapter concerns the familiar reconstruction of macroscopic irreversibility: the microscopic dynamical laws are, to an excellent approximation, invariant under time reversal, and the observed asymmetries of the macroscopic world are recovered by supplementing those laws with a probability postulate over the microstates compatible with a given macrostate, together with the hypothesis that the early universe occupied an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate. I take the classic objections from reversibility and recurrence to be more or less answered; what I cannot figure out how to weigh is the status of that final ingredient, the special initial condition, and the various attempts to displace it — by deriving the low-entropy early state from something deeper, by locating the asymmetry in a genuinely time-oriented element of the dynamics (a fundamental collapse mechanism, say, or a gravitational proposal on which the specialness of the early universe consists in the near-vanishing of its Weyl curvature), or by denying that the arrow is a feature of fundamental physics at all and treating it instead as an artifact of our epistemic situation: we have records of the past and not of the future, we can intervene toward the future and not toward the past, and counterfactual dependence runs one way only.

Let me state a single proposition whose credence I would like from you, and I have tried to state it precisely enough to have a truth value. Call it C: in the correct and complete fundamental description of the physical world — if such a description exists, and if we had it in hand — the temporal asymmetry exhibited by thermodynamic phenomena is grounded in the dynamics rather than in a time-asymmetric boundary or initial condition; that is, the true fundamental laws are not time-reversal invariant, and the violation in question is of a kind and magnitude that does genuine explanatory work in accounting for the macroscopic arrow, rather than being an inert curiosity. I am aware that the weak interaction already violates T-symmetry, as established in neutral-kaon and B-meson systems, and I do not think that fact should be permitted to settle C on its own, since its magnitude looks far too feeble to be doing any work in explaining why heat flows from hot to cold; so please treat C as demanding the substantive clause. My own credence in C is about 0.05. A friend and fellow graduate student, who is much more impressed by collapse models and by the general thought that quantum gravity will not end up looking like a classical dynamical law, puts hers at about 0.3.

Give me your number, but above all give me the decomposition that produces it. Assign rough probabilities to at least the following sub-questions, with a sentence of justification for each, and then show explicitly how they combine into your credence in C: (i) whether a single correct and complete fundamental description exists at all, as against a permanent plurality of effective theories; (ii) conditional on its existing, whether its laws are time-reversal invariant; (iii) conditional on their not being, whether the violation is plausibly of the sort that could underwrite the thermodynamic arrow; (iv) whether, if the laws are symmetric, a special low-entropy initial condition is forced on the theory by something deeper rather than simply postulated alongside it; and (v) the rival hypothesis that the whole question is misframed — that the arrow is not a matter of fundamental physics but of records, agency, and one-way counterfactual dependence — together with the probability you assign to that dissolution. If you think C is ill-posed, that is precisely term (v), so give it a number rather than declining to answer. Add any further term you think belongs and that I have omitted. Then tell me which term your final figure is most sensitive to: which single sub-probability, shifted by twenty points, would move your credence in C furthest. Finally, say which sub-question my friend and I are most likely disagreeing about, given that our numbers are 0.05 and 0.3 and we have encountered the same arguments, and name the one piece of physical evidence — a confirmed fundamental collapse mechanism with a built-in preferred direction, a derivation of the early universe's specialness from something prior, or whatever else you have in mind — that would move you most.
