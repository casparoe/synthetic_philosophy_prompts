So I've talked myself into a pretty strong claim about a question that gets asked constantly in economic history, and I want you to try to break it.

The claim: contrastive why-questions of the form "why did industrialization take off in Britain and not in China?" are well-posed and answerable, and the answers historians give are explanations of a fact about the world rather than artifacts of how the asker happened to carve up the contrast. I'll already concede some ground — asking why China never produced a Newton is a different and much worse question, and I'm not defending that one. But the coarse-grained, society-level version is a legitimate target, and there's a real fact about which differences between the two cases did the explanatory work. When people argue about coal geology and New World land, or about whether cheap labor made labor-saving machinery unprofitable, or about the high-level equilibrium trap, I think they're arguing about something, and that some of them are wrong.

Here's what I want from you:

1. What would have to be true for my claim to be false? Give me the defeaters — arguments, results about the structure of explanation, features of contrast classes, kinds of historical evidence — and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Don't pad the ranking with things that are just embarrassing.

2. The foil-swapping test. Hold the underlying causal evidence fixed and move the contrast: Britain vs. the Netherlands; Britain vs. the Yangzi delta instead of China as a whole (this is the move Pomeranz-style arguments make, and I think it's the most dangerous substitution for me); Europe vs. everywhere else, where the honest answer is "everywhere, eventually, at different times." For each, say whether my position survives, weakens, or dies. Then tell me whether there's a substitution that kills it outright — some pair of contrast classes where the same body of evidence supports incompatible difference-makers and neither question is any less well-posed than the other. If that exists, that's the one I care about most.

3. Make the base-rate objection against me as sharply as you can. If the base rate of industrial takeoff is something like one per planet per few millennia, then arguably the only good question is "why anywhere at all, once," and "why here and not there" is the lottery-winner-from-Springfield move dressed up as an explanadum. Is that a real defeater or is it a confusion about what contrastive explanation is for? I keep flip-flopping on this and I want to be pushed.

4. A hypothetical discovery, and I want you to argue both sides before picking one. Suppose historians turned up solid evidence that Song-dynasty China had a worked-out theory of atmospheric pressure and had built small steam-driven pumps for brine extraction, then dropped them as uneconomical. Does that make my question better-posed, because it removes the "China was just missing the ingredients" escape and forces a genuine difference-maker? Or worse-posed, because it makes the whole thing look like a coin flip, so "why not China" has no non-trivial answer? Commit to a verdict.

5. What does my position predict that its rivals don't? Set it against the interest-relative view of explanation, on which there's no context-independent fact about which differences matter, and also against the friendlier view that contrastive questions are always answerable once you've pinned down the foil. My claim is supposed to be stronger than both. Say what the extra content is, and describe how the world or the historiography would have to look for that extra content to come out false.

6. What would defenders of this position themselves concede counts against it? Not the critics' list — what the people who hold it admit. And has the position been insulated by qualification, especially by retreats like "the Needham question is really a family of questions, each well-posed relative to its own contrast class"? Is that a legitimate refinement or a slide into untestability? I'd rather hear a blunt answer that I'm holding a view that risks nothing than a diplomatic one.

Two things I'd personally find most persuasive: a case where the answer genuinely flips when you swap the foil while holding all the causal evidence constant, and anything showing that explaining an absence is structurally different from explaining a presence in a way that makes "why not China" unanswerable even in principle. If you think neither of those can be delivered, say so instead of manufacturing them.

You don't need to name-drop sources or give me a reading list — I care about the arguments and about how exposed the view actually is. Somewhere around 1200-1600 words, and please keep the ranking in section 1 visibly ordered rather than just dumping everything in one pile.
