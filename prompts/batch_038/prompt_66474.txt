So, three weeks from now my grocery co-op turns forty and I've somehow been talked into giving the main speech at the anniversary dinner, and I have not started and every attempt so far has come out sounding like a chamber-of-commerce newsletter. Some context. I'm the board chair — unpaid, I still work the register on Saturdays — of a worker- and member-owned food co-op in a town of about sixty thousand, and before I ended up here I spent six years in a philosophy PhD program mostly reading Aristotle's practical works. I left without finishing. Everyone in town who matters knows that, so there's no need to be precious about it, but I do want the speech to actually use what's left in my head rather than be a warm blob of gratitude. The room will be around a hundred and eighty people: members and workers and their kids, a lot of them shopping here since the eighties, nobody in that group has read any philosophy; the mayor; a reporter from the local paper; two people from an impact-investment fund who will be sitting at the front table and being thanked by name; and, awkwardly, three of my old cohort from grad school who drove in and who will clock every move I make, including the ones I try to hide.

Here's the situation the speech has to carry. Six weeks ago we closed a four million dollar deal with that fund to buy out our wholesale distributor and build a proper kitchen — good deal on paper, seven-year term, a board seat for them, and a 6% annual return, which their term sheet keeps calling "patient capital." The thing is, for forty years this co-op never borrowed from outside itself. In 1985 the founders turned down a bank loan and instead had two hundred-odd members lend the store money at zero percent, in writing, on index cards; some of those cards are framed by the deli counter. And the original wooden cash box sits in a case near the door with the founding rule scratched into the inside of the lid — money is a tool, not a master. So the anniversary is also, obviously, the end of a streak, and if I get up and just celebrate growth and scale I'll be lying to a room full of people who remember 1985 and some of whom signed those cards. It needs to be honest about that without turning into their chair apologising to them, and without the fund people at the front table feeling like they've been invited to a dinner to be denounced.

What I want, and this is the part I can't do myself: build the speech out of Aristotle's distinction between the getting that a household does in order to have things to use — which has a natural limit, because you only need so much — and the getting whose point is just more of the money itself, which has no limit at all because money isn't the kind of thing you can ever have enough of. Also the shoe business, that every possession has two uses, its own proper use and exchange, and that trading a shoe isn't using it as a shoe. Also the thought that money has no value by nature and exists only because we agreed on it as a stand-in for need, with need being what actually holds a city together. And the claim I keep circling, that the most detestable way of getting wealth is the one where money makes money, since money was invented for exchange and not for breeding — which is, precisely, what we just signed. Those four things should do the structural work: they're what makes this a bittersweet anniversary rather than a nice one, and they should also give me a way to describe the fund's business to their faces that is accurate and not an insult, which I think is possible if it's the same standard I apply to us. Hard constraints: about seven minutes, call it nine hundred words; no Greek words, no book or chapter numbers, no "as Aristotle says in the Politics," his name at most twice; the words usury and interest never used as an accusation anywhere. But I want the three philosophers in the room to be able to pick out each of the four claims exactly, from plain talk about bread and shelving and index cards alone — that's really the whole challenge, so please don't solve it by explaining the theory. And if you can make something out of "patient capital" — what would it even mean for money to be patient, and whose patience is it actually, since money doesn't wait, people do — I'd like that, but only if it earns its place and doesn't get cute. It should end with something the room can actually do together, not a peroration, and it should be warm. These people are my friends and I've known most of them longer then I've known any philosophy.
