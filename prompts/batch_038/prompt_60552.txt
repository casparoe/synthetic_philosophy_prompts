Bit of a ramble, sorry — I'll try to keep it tight.

I've been chewing on the separateness-of-persons objection, the Rawls/Nozick one, where utilitarianism supposedly goes wrong by extending to a whole society a kind of trade-off that's only legitimate inside a single life. Inside one life the person who pays the cost is the person who collects the benefit; across people the one who loses out never sees the gain, and there's no social super-person for whom the sum adds up to anything. As far as it goes, I buy it.

Then I tried applying it to my own life and it seemed to detonate, and I can't tell whether I've found something or just made a mess of it.

Concretely: I'm 27 and I've just signed up for six years of a genuinely grim stipend in order to have a career I'll enjoy from about 33 onwards. My reading group calls this the deferred diner problem (yes, that typo is now canonical). Here's the worry. For all the objection cares, the person who pays is a stranger to the person who's paid for. Six years is longer than most of my friendships have lasted. My tastes, my politics, my sense of humour have already been rebuilt from scratch twice since I was 18 and will be again. Nobody compensates the 27-year-old: he gets the grind and none of the payoff, and he can't even be bought off with the later self's satisfaction, because that satisfaction isn't his to have. That looks to me like the exact structure of the objection, mirrored.

And a second case, which is the one that really undid me: my mother says she can barely recognise the fourteen-year-old she used to be, and has no idea what that girl would have wanted — but she can read my mind better than I can. So on the psychological-connectedness metric, there are pairs of distinct people who are closer to each other than one person is to their own past self.

So it seems to me the objector has to pick a horn:

(a) the objection proves too much, prudence is on exactly the same footing as aggregating across people, and the intrapersonal analogy the whole thing rests on was never available in the first place — in which case the objection doesn't just fail to refute utilitarianism, it fails to get off the ground; or

(b) something extra in the intrapersonal case is doing the real work, in which case it can't be strict identity (identity is all-or-nothing and plainly the wrong shape for something that comes in degrees), and if it's connectedness or something like it, then it can be had across people too — so the objection quietly stops biting in precisely the cases it was built for.

Either way I can't see how they keep the conclusion. Which horn do they take, and what am I missing that makes this not fatal? Relatedly: I'm fairly sure Parfit has something to say about identity and this objection, but I genuinely cannot remember whether he was wielding it against utilitarianism or handing utilitarianism a get-out clause. Set me straight on that too.
