I work on compilers — source-to-source transformation, mostly — and I've read Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, the book built around the many English renderings of Marot's short poem to a sick young woman. Please restate his position on translation under formal constraint in the vocabulary I actually use: syntax trees, semantics-preserving transformation, desugaring, observable behavior, load-bearing invariants, and what it takes for one program to be a correct translation of another. My own hypothesis is that his quarrel with the literalists reduces to a disagreement about which invariants matter — that they treat rhyme, meter, and line count as syntactic sugar, decoration with no semantics and freely discarded, while he treats them as part of the program's observable behavior, so dropping them is not a simplification but a change in what it does. Render the argument as faithfully as you can, and don't smuggle in assumptions my field makes that Hofstadter would reject; where the fit is forced, say so rather than papering over it. Then give me the residue report: what maps cleanly, what survives only if I stretch my own terminology, and what has no counterpart at all — I suspect that last category has something to do with the reader, and with the absence of any test that could settle whether two versions are equivalent. End by telling me whether that residue is a defect of my vocabulary or evidence that Hofstadter's position is less determinate than it sounds, which piece of your mapping you most expect to be wrong, and what consideration would change your mind about it. Continuous prose only, please: no headings, bullets, bold, or tables — write it the way you would write a letter.
