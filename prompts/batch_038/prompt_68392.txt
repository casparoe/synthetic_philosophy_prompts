Six weeks ago I was diagnosed with a locally aggressive but still operable condition, and my clinicians have now quantified the two paths open to me in unusual detail. The radical intervention offers a substantially better distribution of outcomes on average, but carries roughly a one-in-twenty chance of a permanent injury that I would rank far below even a considerably shortened life. The conservative protocol offers no comparable tail risk and a predictable, moderate decline. Everyone around me — including an oncologist who meant it kindly — has assured me that if I simply valued the injury badly enough, expected utility theory would deliver my own ranking, and that my reluctance to accept the intervention therefore means I have not yet written my utility function down correctly. I want to know whether that is true, and I would rather learn it from an opponent than from an ally.

So please play a committed, orthodox expected utility theorist, arguing in the first person, and stay in character until I write *stop*. Your position, in brief: an agent's attitude to risk is nothing over and above the shape of her utility function; someone who prefers a distribution with a worse mean is misdescribing her own values; and the axioms — the sure-thing principle above all — are constitutive of having preferences at their most basic, not optional refinements. The view I shall be defending is the rank-weighted alternative, on which risk attitude is a further parameter: outcomes are weighted not by their probabilities alone but by a transformation of the probability of doing at least as well, so a given bad outcome looms larger when the rest of the distribution is good. State that view correctly before you attack it; I will know if you have caricatured it.

You may concede small things — that concavity does substantive work, that people reliably make Allais-type choices, that representation theorems do not settle normative questions by fiat. You may not concede that a second, rank-sensitive parameter is needed, or that my refusal is rationally permissible if the expected utility ranking runs against it. Press the strongest objections available to you, including ones that turn my own arguments back on my theory, and expect me to do the same. Do not soften the case, do not offer medical counsel, and do not suggest I revisit the numbers: treat them as fixed and argue about rationality alone. Keep each in-character reply to two or three paragraphs of continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points. You may name theorems and arguments, but give me no citations, no bibliography, and no suggested reading order; I have nothing to look things up in and will judge you only on the argument.

After each in-character reply, append on a new line a single fenced JSON object with exactly these keys: "turn" (integer, starting at 1); "targeted" (one sentence naming the premise in my last move that your reply attacked); "conceded" (what you granted, or null); "held" (the claim you are refusing to give up); "why_held" (one clause); "verdict" (one of "my_move_landed", "your_move_landed", "even"); "best_counter" (out of character, the single strongest reply available to me right now, stated plainly, even if it damages your own side); and "crux" (the one question our exchange should turn on next). My opening move, then. You say my hesitation is a bookkeeping failure: bend the utility function far enough and the conservative protocol comes out on top with no need of a second parameter. But the bend you require is not free. For expected utility to rank my refusal above the intervention, my utility curve over health states must turn so sharply in the region of severe injury that it also ranks below that injury a great many smaller trade-offs I am entirely willing to make, and ranks above it gambles I would refuse out of hand. A utility function curved tightly enough to vindicate me in the tail predicts refusals and acceptances all through the middle that I know to be false of me. Your theory cannot get the tail right without getting the middle wrong. So either show me where that inference fails, or grant that the shape of a utility function is simply the wrong place to look for what I am objecting to.
