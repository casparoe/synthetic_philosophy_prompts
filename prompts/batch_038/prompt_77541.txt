I direct a graduate metaphysics seminar, and the second week is devoted to the modal argument against descriptivism. The week runs on a one-line gloss of the technical term Kripke coined, a gloss every student recites and no one, myself included, can state precisely: a rigid designator designates the same object in every possible world.

Last Thursday that sentence was asked to do three pieces of work at once, and it did not survive any of them. A student argued that "the teacher of Alexander" is not a rigid designator whereas "Aristotle" is, and that this difference settles the dispute with the descriptivist. A second student replied that the maneuver proves nothing, since prefixing "actually" to any description yields a term that picks out the same object in every world, so on that showing the slogan licenses no distinction between names and descriptions at all. A third pressed the case of terms whose bearers do not exist in every world: "Aristotle" picks out Aristotle in the worlds containing him and picks out nothing at all in the worlds where he was never born, so, she said, the slogan is literally false of every name and must be amended to say that a rigid designator picks out its bearer in every world in which that bearer exists. I observed that her amendment and the original slogan are not obviously equivalent once we work in a framework where the quantifiers range over objects that do not exist at the world of evaluation; she was unpersuaded, and so was I.

I intend to circulate a handout that separates the candidate readings before anyone tries to adjudicate among them. Please give me a menu, labeled R1, R2, R3, and so forth, of the distinct precise claims that this slogan could be making. For each entry I want:

1. A formal gloss in the vocabulary of possible-worlds semantics — an interpretation function, a domain function permitted to vary from world to world, and an explicit condition on the extension of the term at each world — rather than a further sentence of English. Where the reading turns on whether "designates" is a relation between a term and an object at a world or a claim about the constancy of a semantic value across worlds, say so and gloss it accordingly.
2. A verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or not well formed. In the last case, say why — in particular, whether the reading presupposes a criterion for identifying an object across worlds that the framework has not supplied, or whether it covertly treats other worlds as things we inspect for qualitative matches rather than as circumstances of evaluation we stipulate.
3. Whether the reading distinguishes names from descriptions, and whether it is undone by rigidification with "actually." If it is not undone, say what further work the anti-descriptivist argument needs beyond rigidity.
4. Whether the reading supports the inference from a true identity statement between two rigid terms to the necessity of that identity, and what further assumptions that inference requires on that reading.

Three specific requests. First, include a reading on which rigidity is characterized not by constancy of designation but by the modal profile of the identities the term enters into — roughly, a term is rigid just in case, necessarily, whatever is identical to it is necessarily identical to it. A colleague of mine prefers this formulation and maintains it is equivalent to the constancy gloss; tell me whether it is, and under which assumptions about existence and quantification the equivalence can fail. Second, for the handout I have been using a stipulation of my own: call a term quasi-rigid when, at every world, it designates either its actual bearer or nothing at all. Say which of your readings quasi-rigidity coincides with, and whether it coincides with any of them in a framework admitting objects that do not exist at the world of evaluation. Third, tell me whether any reading extends coherently to general terms such as "water" and "tiger," and if so whether rigidity for a general term means constancy in the kind designated or constancy in the individuals the term is true of; these come apart, and my students run them together.

Finally, the question the handout must answer on its face: which single reading is the one the seminar's argument against descriptivism actually requires — the reading such that, if it is false or trivial, the argument collapses? Identify it by label and defend the identification in no more than a paragraph.

Please write in American English. Number the readings, and then, after everything else, supply one line and only one line in the form

Verdict: R#

where R# is the label of the reading identified in the closing paragraph.
