Throughout the course we used two labels for the two moves in the standard argument from Libet's clock experiments, and the exam question uses them without redefining them, so for your benefit: the timing premise is the claim that the reported onset of the conscious urge comes after the onset of the relevant preparatory brain activity, and the causal-relevance premise is the claim that an event can only cause the movement if it precedes the movement's initiation in the brain. Here is the question as it appeared:

(a) [10 pts] State the timing premise and the causal-relevance premise. (b) [8 pts] Libet did not think his results eliminated free will. Explain the role the veto is supposed to play in his account, and evaluate whether it can do that work.

And here is the student's script, unedited, as typed into the exam portal:

(a) In Libet's set-up subjects sat in front of a modified oscilloscope with a dot sweeping round a clock face, and were asked to flex a wrist or finger whenever they felt like it, at an arbitrary moment of their own choosing. The EEG was averaged backwards from EMG onset, which served as time zero, and subjects reported the clock position at which they first became aware of the urge to move. The readiness potential begins roughly 550 ms before EMG onset, while reported awareness of the urge falls about 200 ms before it. So the timing premise is well supported: the conscious urge is reported as arising some 350 ms after preparatory activity in the supplementary motor area has already begun. The causal-relevance premise is a further, philosophical claim, and the data don't establish it on their own — you need the extra assumption that the reported W time is a faithful read-out of when the intention actually occurred, rather than a judgement the subject constructs while watching a moving dot. Note too that this experiment concerns when to move and not what to move, since subjects had no reason to flex at one moment rather than another. Given all that, the natural conclusion is that conscious intention is at best an epiphenomenon of the processes that really produce the movement, which is what Libet concluded.

(b) Libet's escape route is the veto. Because he took conscious will to be causally inert with respect to initiation, he located whatever causal power it still had in inhibition: the veto window Libet allowed is generous, on the order of half a second, so free will survives as the power to say no rather than the power to start. Whether this works is debatable. It concedes everything part (a) established about initiation, and it's unclear why a faculty that was too late to cause an action is not equally too late to stop one.

(c) Schurger et al. model the readiness potential as the buildup of ongoing, partly random neural noise toward a threshold, so that movement happens when the accumulator crosses rather than because of a discrete unconscious decision. On their view the slow negative shift is just noise, so there is no evidence at all that the brain begins preparing before the subject reports an urge. That is fully compatible with treating the onset of the readiness potential as the moment of the unconscious decision, and it means the argument in (a) can stand exactly as it is — the only thing that changes is that we now know why the shift is slow.
