And here's the student's answer in full:

Everettian QM has a probability problem because the dynamics is deterministic and, on the Everett picture, every outcome the wavefunction allows actually happens on some branch. So it is unclear what room is left for probability at all. The problem is standardly split into two parts.

First, the incoherence problem: if all outcomes occur, it looks like statements about the probability of an outcome have no content. This is handled by decoherence. Decoherence picks out a preferred basis of stable, quasi-classical branches, and once we have that we know what we are asking about when we ask for the probability of an outcome: we are asking about the chance that a given branch will be the one that obtains.

Second, the quantitative problem: granting that probability makes sense, why should it go by squared amplitude rather than, for instance, by counting branches?

Deutsch and Wallace attack the second problem decision-theoretically. Rather than deriving the Born rule from the physics, they derive it from rationality. They consider an agent facing a branching measurement who has preferences over payoffs on the various branches, and they show that if her preferences satisfy a set of plausible axioms — ordering, continuity, and crucially measurement neutrality and equivalence — then she must behave exactly as if she assigned the branches probabilities equal to the squared amplitudes. Notably the axioms never mention amplitude, which is what makes the result a derivation rather than a restatement of the rule. Wallace stresses that an agent should be indifferent to how a measurement is physically implemented so long as the branch structure and payoffs are unchanged, and this is what blocks branch counting: you can always fine-grain a measurement into more branches without changing anything the agent cares about, so branch number cannot carry probabilistic weight. Amplitude is the only thing left.

The strongest objection is circularity. Critics argue the axioms smuggle in the Born rule, in particular that measurement neutrality and equivalence are only plausible if you already think equal-amplitude branches are equiprobable. If that is right, the derivation repackages the rule it was supposed to explain. A related worry is that the whole framework helps itself to rational degrees of belief, which is precisely what was supposed to be in question.

Zurek's envariance argument is essentially the same strategy from a different starting point: he argues from symmetries of entangled states that equal-amplitude branches must be equiprobable, and reaches the general case by fine-graining. Because the equiprobability step here comes from a physical symmetry rather than from axioms about rational agents, envariance escapes the circularity objection that Deutsch and Wallace face.

Finally, the quoted claim overstates things. Since the wavefunction evolves deterministically in Bohmian mechanics too, Bohmians are in the same boat and have to postulate the Born rule as an additional law.
