Second-year history PhD in the UK — thesis on interwar British agricultural administration, so I spend half my life in county record offices and the other half in a department where nobody says the word 'theory' out loud. I've been reading round the philosophy of history side (done the whole Ranke/Carr/White circuit in my first year) and there's a pattern I keep running into that I can't find a name for. I want to know whether it's got one.

The pattern: a historian writes an account and explicitly disclaims having an argument — the preface says something like I've tried to let the record speak, and in seminar they'll say I'm not making a claim, I'm describing. But the account has a definite shape and that shape is doing argumentative work the whole way through: which things get treated as events and which as background; whose reasoning gets reported and whose behaviour merely gets recorded; where the chapters break; which of two things that happend at the same time comes first; what gets called a consequence and what gets called a coincidence. Not one sentence is disputable. Every one of them is a fair report of a fair document. The contention lives entirely in the joints.

Then — and this is the half I actually care about — when someone tries to push back there's nothing to get hold of. You can't quote a claim and ask for the argument for it, because there isn't one on the page. And the disavowal has the effect of turning the critic into somebody who is denying what the archives say. This came up at a conference last month: someone asked a panellist what her thesis was, the answer was that she didn't have one, the chair nodded as though that had settled it, and the exchange closed down. The account isn't less arguable for lacking an argument — it's less arguable, full stop, and the lack is what buys it.

Second instance, closer to home. My supervisor read a chapter of mine and wrote in the margin that it reads as though the outcome were the only one available. He's right. And if you asked me to point at the sentence that says that, I couldn't — the ordering says it. My defence to myself was but that's just what happened, which is exactly the move I'm trying to pin down, because I meant it sincerely and I still think each of my sentences is defensible on its own.

Nearest things I know of, none of which I think is it: loaded language and question-begging epithets (no — the vocabulary here is flat, it's mostly plain reporting verbs); Barthes's reality effect (not the same — that's about detail whose only job is to make things feel real, whereas my thing is functional detail that just doesn't do its work in any sentence); theory-laden observation and the myth of the given (right neighbourhood, but those are about perception, not about the protection the disclaiming buys you); selection bias or unconscious bias (no — the account needn't favour anybody, it may have no side at all); the view from nowhere (a journalism phrase and about stance); and White's point that everything has to be put into some story shape (too general — that's true of all narrative, mine is narrower, it's about the disavowal doing something).

What I want, and a bare label won't help me: the carving. Does this have an established name — in philosophy of history specifically, not just literary theory or the sociology of knowledge? If it does, which concept covers it best, and does it cover both halves (the smuggling, and the immunity the disavowal confers) or only one? I suspect they come apart, since you can smuggle without disavowing — that's just a bad historian nobody is fooled by — and you can disavow without smuggling, in something genuinely chronicle-like. If there's no term, coin one and give me the definition with its edges marked. In particular: is sincerity necessary to the thing, or does the cynical version count as the same phenomenon? Does it require that every sentence be individually faithful to the sources, or only that the author be able to gesture at sources when pressed? And where does it stop being this and become just the selection every historian has to do and none of us could avoid without writing nothing at all — is there a threshold, or is that the wrong way to think about it?

Not after a reading list, I'll chase anything you mention myself. Please answer in American English — I'm drafting the theory section for a US journal and I've been told my spelling keeps sliding.
