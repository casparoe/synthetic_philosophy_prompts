I am in the sixth form, and for an essay prize run by our school philosophy society I have been arguing with myself about whether states can apologise for historical wrongs. To test the sceptical position, I built the following argument as strongly as I could, intending to knock it down afterwards; I have now reached the point where I cannot tell whether it is subtly invalid or valid but resting on a premise I should simply abandon, and I would much rather have a diagnosis of the machinery than a tour of the debate, which I have no room for before the deadline. The argument runs as follows.

(1) An apology is apt only if it comes from the agent who committed the wrong; a bystander's expression of sorrow, however heartfelt, answers to nothing and discharges nothing.
(2) Agency is not transferable: one cannot become the agent of another's deed by sympathising with it, by inheriting its benefits, or by being authorised to speak on its author's behalf.
(3) When a state apologises, the apologising is done by particular living people — a prime minister, a head of state, a majority in a legislature — none of whom committed the wrong.
(4) The wrong was committed by different people, all of them dead, acting through a body whose membership, constitution, name and franchise have since changed.
(5) If the entity now apologising is not the entity that did the wrong, then (3) and (4) show the present apology to be a bystander's apology, and by (1) it is inapt.
(6) Therefore no present state can aptly apologise for a historical wrong. At most it can compensate; but compensation is not apology, and it can be paid without admitting anything.
(7) The same reasoning, with the word "apology" struck out, shows that a present state cannot be bound by treaties its predecessors signed, cannot be liable for the debts they ran up, and cannot own the property they acquired, since in each case the entity being asked to perform is not the entity that undertook the obligation.
(8) But (7) is false. Successor states do inherit treaties and debts; and the very state that would apologise still holds the treasury, the archives and the territory the wrongdoing state held, and still administers the legal order under which the wrongdoing was once lawful.
(9) Therefore something above must go, and since a state's inherited obligations are not negotiable, it must be (1) or (2): apologies are transferable after all.

Two things trouble me. First, one wrinkle that perhaps weakens (8) more than I have allowed: as I understand it, states have sometimes repudiated a previous regime's borrowings as odious debts, and newly independent states have sometimes been permitted to start with a clean slate on treaties, so inheritance of obligations may be a practice with exceptions rather than a fact of metaphysics. Second, in order to avoid arguing from a real case whose history I might get wrong, I have invented a stipulated one to test the argument against: a state that has changed its name, its constitution and its franchise twice since the atrocity, whose present government includes descendants of both the perpetrators and the victims, but which has never been conquered, has kept the same treasury and the same treaty relationships throughout, and still enforces laws whose wording dates from the period in question. My questions are these: which step do you reject, why did that step look perfectly innocent when I wrote it, and what does rejecting it cost? If instead you think the reasoning is valid and one of its premises must be given up, tell me which is cheapest to surrender and what we lose by surrendering it. Please say explicitly whether (7) genuinely follows from (1) to (5), since (9) depends entirely on it, and whether the argument's own split in (6) between compensation, which it allows, and apology, which it forbids, is something the premises earn or something they merely assume. I should add that I would far rather keep (8) than (2), because (8) looks like a plain truth about how states work, though I accept that a better philosopher than I am might dispute it. For my own notes afterwards, please set out the essentials a second time as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: "diagnosis" (one of "equivocation", "scope_shift", "false_dilemma", "hidden_premise", "valid_but_unsound", "other", plus a one-line justification); "failing_steps" (an array of objects, each with "step" as a number, "problem", and "why_it_seemed_fine"); "reductio_check" (with sub-fields "does_step_7_follow" and "is_step_8_solid"); "the_stipulated_case" (how the invented state above bears on your diagnosis, in two or three sentences); "repairs" (an array of objects, each with "drop_step", "cost", and "cheapest_or_not" as a boolean); "what_the_argument_still_shows"; and "bottom_line" (no more than two sentences, stating what I should write in my conclusion).
