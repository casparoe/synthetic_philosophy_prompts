I am one of five commissioners on our standing Law Reform Commission, which advises the Minister for Justice; we decide references by majority and the Minister is free to ignore us. Over eleven years we have issued recommendations on seven references touching the enforcement of morality. We now have an eighth, and my colleagues have already staked out positions that I cannot reconcile with our own record. We were all taught the Hart–Devlin exchange at university and I do not want a summary of it. I want to know which principle our record actually runs on, and what it commits us to now.

Our seven recommendations, stripped of the reasoning we gave at the time, were these:

1. Repeal the offence prohibiting adults from possessing sexually explicit material in private, while retaining restrictions on its open display and on sales within a prescribed distance of schools.
2. Retain a general offence covering acts of a lewd or grossly disgusting character done where members of the public are likely to be unwilling witnesses.
3. Repeal the restrictions on Sunday opening of shops, although our consultation showed a clear majority of respondents thinking Sunday trading wrong and a substantial minority thinking it gravely wrong.
4. Decline to permit an adult to sell a kidney to a willing buyer, on the express assumptions that the donor is competent, fully informed, uncoerced and fairly paid, and that a regulated market would save lives and displace the existing illicit trade.
5. Retain criminal liability for adults who inflict injury on one another in private for sexual gratification, where consent is genuine, no participant complains, and no injury requires hospital treatment.
6. Leave tattooing, piercing, branding and scarification between consenting adults lawful but regulated rather than prohibited, notwithstanding that a number of consultees described these practices as repugnant and as mutilation.
7. Decline to ban the sale of cigarettes to adults, and to ban high-strength alcohol for off-licence consumption, notwithstanding the evidence of self-harm.

The new reference concerns a private member's bill that would create an offence, carrying up to five years' imprisonment, for a suitably qualified surgeon to amputate a healthy limb of a competent adult at that adult's settled request. The facts are stipulated for us, and they are these: the patient has had two independent psychiatric assessments across four years, both concluding that the wish is settled, autonomous, and not the product of psychosis or pressure from anyone else; the operation would be performed in a licensed clinic; the surgeon would receive the ordinary fee for a comparable procedure and no more; no limb or tissue would be sold, displayed or put to any other use; nobody but the patient would be affected and no person who had not consented would have any ground of complaint. Two considerations cut the other way. A petition of some forty thousand signatures describes the practice as an assault on the community's sense of what a human body is for, and warns that licensing it would corrode the standing of medicine. And the evidence placed before us on outcomes is thin and contested: some who have sought such operations report lasting relief, others come to regret them, and the underlying condition sometimes remits without surgery.

Two colleagues have already made up their minds. One says the record is Devlin from start to finish, and that the petition settles the new reference. The other says it is the harm principle with a narrow exception for what one person does to another's body, and that the petition is legally irrelevant. I do not think both can be right, and I suspect neither is. I must circulate a note before we meet in three weeks.

Please answer the following, and answer it as a committed judgement rather than a survey. Take a definite position on which principle our record runs on and on what we should recommend about the new bill, and give your reasons; do not lay out the candidates as a menu and leave me to choose. If you conclude that our record genuinely underdetermines the case, say so once, plainly, then tell me which reading you would bet on and what you would recommend to the other four commissioners.

Write the note under these headings, in this order, and to roughly these lengths:

- Bottom line — up to 100 words. Your prediction of our recommendation and your own recommendation, which may differ.
- The principles our record could be running on — about 350 words. Each candidate that fits, the precedents it explains cleanly, and the precedents it strains or must explain away. Address in particular whether precedent 3 is fatal to a Devlin-style reading, or whether the distinction between a majority merely disapproving and the stronger threshold of shared revulsion that Devlin required rescues it; and whether precedent 7 sits consistently beside precedents 4 and 5.
- The new reference under each — about 300 words. What each surviving principle recommends here, and which of the stipulated facts does the decisive work.
- The closest precedent and where it breaks — about 150 words. One of the seven, with an honest account of the disanalogy.
- What we would be committing ourselves to — about 250 words. The principle we adopt by recommending either way, and the two or three later references that principle would force us to decide against our own instincts.
- The reference that would settle it — about 150 words. One further hypothetical reference that would best discriminate between the two principles you take most seriously, and what each would say about it.
- The strongest case against my recommendation — about 150 words. Stated as its proponent would state it, then answered, not merely acknowledged.

Give me no references to the literature and no list of further reading: I cannot check them from where I am sitting, and I want your reasoning rather than your sources. Keep the whole note to about 1,400 words.
