I've been reading the debate-as-scalable-oversight stuff for years and I think I've talked myself into a version of it that's more specific than what people usually say, and I want it stress-tested before I commit any further.

Here's the position: the reason two-prover debate is supposed to work isn't that the judge is smart, and it isn't the slogan that lying is harder than truth-telling. It's an asymmetry in *search*. For a bounded judge, generating the decisive objection yourself is hard, but recognizing it once an adversary has produced it is easy. So the honest side wins in equilibrium because it can point at the flaw and let the judge check, while the dishonest side has to hope the judge fails to check something they'd have caught if prompted. The empirical hook I'd cite is the QuALITY result — non-expert judges going from roughly 50–60% answering directly to 76–88% reading two strong debaters argue the question, which looks exactly like the recognition-beats-generation signature.

What would count against this? Not the objections debate fans have a canned reply to. Specifically:

- What would have to be true for the search asymmetry to not exist, or to run the other way? Are there classes of question where producing the decisive point is easier than verifying it — and would that break debate on exactly the questions we care most about (long-horizon plans, value-laden calls, things where the flaw is a missing consideration rather than a wrong step) while leaving it intact for reading comprehension and arithmatic?
- Which results would be fatal vs. merely awkward? Say: debate beating a single model asked several times and majority-voted, or failing to; gains shrinking as judges get weaker; the honest side winning only because the dishonest side's best play is expensive rather than because truth is favored; and optimizers trained *on* debate outcomes discovering judge-pleasing rhetoric instead of truth.
- What do people who actually work on this concede counts against it? I've got the sense that the sparse-vs-dense reward choice, the equilibrium-uniqueness assumption, and the whole "the judge has to be competent enough for the bootstrap to start" caveat are places where the view has quietly been made harder to falsify. Is that fair or am I strawmanning? And if it is fair, is it a problem — a design heuristic maybe doesn't need the kind of exposure an empirical claim does.
- What does my version predict that the obvious rivals don't: prover-verifier games, recursive reward modeling / amplification, and weak-to-strong generalization with a strong non-adversarial judge? If all four give the same curves on the same benchmarks then I'm not buying anything with this and should drop it.

Rank everything from kills-the-view down to annoying-but-survivable, and be willing to conclude that this is mostly a slogan with no teeth. What would move me most: a concrete case where a judge who'd have gotten it right on their own gets argued *out* of the right answer by a well-formed transcript.
