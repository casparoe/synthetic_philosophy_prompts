I am giving six minutes of opening remarks at a one-day public workshop on social conventions and moral obligation, held at a university in the United States, where I am a visiting professor this year. Half the room are philosophers — two of them work formally on norms and coordination and will notice every slip — and half are townspeople who came because the poster promised a real argument about tipping. The awkward fact I want the speech to use rather than hide is that I am a bad local: I still do not tip the way people here expect, and I step off the curb against a red signal on an empty street, having come from a place where you wait for the green man even when nothing is coming.

What the remarks must do is hold three cases apart so the audience feels the difference between them — the queue, the tip, and the crosswalk. As I see it, they are not the same kind of thing: in one, the convention is the whole of the obligation, and the free rider's harm is exactly the harm of the convention being broken; in another, the convention is a privately enforced patch over a public failure, so that conforming may be part of what holds the failure in place; in the third, the convention is barely doing any work at all, since the people involved were already coordinating. Develop that contrast without jargon and without letting it harden into a slogan, in a way that is legible to the townspeople and not trivial to the formal people; do not turn the speech into either a defense of my own non-conformity or an apology for it, and hand the day's speakers a live question rather than an answer. Write it as something I can say aloud, with the rhythm of speech. One constraint: there will be a tip jar on the coffee table at the back of the room, and the speech must never mention it — though by the end everyone should be thinking about it.
