Last night a friend and I argued about Skolem's paradox until two in the morning, and we have arrived at the point where each of us believes the other is no longer hearing the question. I am looking for a third party's estimate rather than another exchange.

She is a logician; I have enough model theory to follow the constructions but no research-level training. Her position is that the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem gives us, for any consistent first-order axiomatization of set theory, a model whose domain we could in principle enumerate; that within such a model some element plays the role of the power set of the naturals and is not bijectible with the model's own natural numbers by any function the model contains, even though from outside both are countable and a bijection between them exists in our universe; and that Skolem's own 1922 response — that countability is relative to the model — is a concession dressed as a dissolution. On her reading, if a sentence can come out true inside a domain that is exhaustively listable from outside, then there is no fact of the matter, independent of the theory through which we characterize them, about how many sets there are. Her conclusion is that the paradox defeats realism about the set-theoretic universe, not merely confidence in first-order axiomatization as a way of capturing it.

My position is that the result is a theorem about the expressive poverty of a particular formalism, and that its philosophical reach extends only as far as the thought that a countable list of first-order sentences could pin down a structure up to isomorphism. Zermelo's complaint — that the framework was never the right one, and that the notion of set we are actually working with outruns anything such a list can characterize — looks to me like it is still standing. Nothing in the construction forces me to doubt that there are uncountably many sets; it forces me only to doubt that I have characterized them exhaustively.

The proposition I want a number on is hers, stated as tightly as I can manage: that no adequate resolution of Skolem's paradox preserves both the determinacy of the domain our set-theoretic talk is about and the claim that this talk is about a domain fixed independently of our means of characterizing it. My credence is 0.2; hers is 0.85.

Give me your own number, and decompose it. I want the sub-questions the number genuinely depends on, each with a probability and a sentence or two of justification: whether 'set' as used by the metatheorist and as used inside the countable model express one concept or two; whether the paradox is aimed at first-order axiomatization or at set-theoretic practice as actually conducted, with its appeals to reflection principles, the iterative conception, and a background universe; whether there is a coherent notion of an intended model here, or whether that notion is itself what is in dispute; how much of the total weight is carried by the further attempt to transport the lesson from mathematics into ordinary language, and whether that extension can fail without touching the original claim; and whether the response on which truth is relative to a universe rather than absolute is a genuine third position or a restatement of hers. Then say how the terms combine, and check the arithmetic against the number you land on.

Two further requests. First, sensitivity: which single term would move the total most, and what sort of argument or result — not whose authority — would move it. Second, and this is what I most want adjudicated: I have a suspicion I could not get her to engage with, and I do not know whether it deserves its own term or collapses into one of the above. It is that the paradox is generated entirely by the metamathematical stance. It seems to require a viewpoint from which one can survey both a model and its outside. A mathematician working wholly inside one countable model, with no reason to suppose there was anything beyond it, would find nothing whatever paradoxical in any of these facts. If that is right, the paradox may be an artifact of a methodological luxury rather than a discovery about sets, and neither of our positions is aimed at the right target. Tell me whether that is a real third position, whether it is coherent, and what probability you would assign it.

I am not asking for a survey of who said what. Argue from the mathematics and from the structure of the disagreement.
