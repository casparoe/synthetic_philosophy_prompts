Ok, so I'm a novelist, four books out, and I'm maybe sixty percent through a new one that's giving me trouble for reasons I don't think are really about prose. It's set on a small island a few hours off the coast after a storm takes out the ferry and the grid. The only clinic has one vial of antivenom. A fisherman — the narrator's brother-in-law, which shouldn't matter, though the book kind of insists it does — has been bitten and will die without it. Meanwhile a school bus went into the ditch on the coast road and five kids have each been bitten badly enough that they'll die too without it. The vial can do one job or the other, not both, and there's no third option coming by morning. My doctor character weighs the two situations, says nothing for a long time, and then announces he's going to flip a coin. Later the narrator confronts him about leaving it to chance and he says: "There's no one out there who gets saved five times over. Every one of them only ever gets to die once." That line is the hinge of the whole book — I've built three chapters around it — so I'm attached in a way I know isn't rational.

My beta reader did a philosophy degree and she hated it. Her margin note said: the numbers count, you've written a sentimental doctor, and worse, you've made him articulate — fix the coin flip or he's a monster. I went looking and I think she's pointing at some argument by a guy called Taurek, though I might have the name wrong and I couldn't stay awake through the lectures I found; everything else was a wiki stub or a paywall. So I'm not asking you to walk me through the debate. What I want is the phrase pulled apart. Give me "the numbers count" as a numbered menu of the distinct precise claims someone could be making with it, and for each one the thesis in plain English plus a verdict: platitude that nobody really disputes, substantive position that has to be argued for, or just false / question-begging. I especially want the readings that turn on what kind of counting it is — whether the numbers make saving the five required, or merely permitted, or just better in some sense that leaves the coin flip perfectly fine. And the ones about whether you're allowed to add up harms that happen to different people at all, as against the view that each of the five individually has a stronger claim on the doctor than the fisherman does without anything ever getting summed. Does that second route still land on "save the five"? Does my doctor's line only survive if it doesn't? There's also the ex ante business I half-remember from something: that if you give everyone a fair chance at the outset the numbers count somehow even when the coin ends up saving one. Separate reading, or aggregation in disguise — and does it convict him or exonerate him?

Then the part I actually need. For each reading on your menu, say whether it refutes his line or leaves him standing, and which one my beta reader is most likely running on without knowing it. If there's a reading where flipping the coin isn't just permitted but required, flag it loud, because I'd restructure the scene so the narrator accuses him of hiding behind the coin instead of making a choice, and honestly that might be the better book. And if all she means is the tie-breaking platitude, tell me straight — I'd rather know her note is wrong then spend six weeks fixing a sentence that was never broken.
