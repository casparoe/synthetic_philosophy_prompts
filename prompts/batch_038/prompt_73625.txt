I am in the third year of a PhD in history and philosophy of science, with prior training in economics and statistics, and I need to submit a two-page project outline for a departmental summer fellowship that funds four months of full-time work toward a journal-length paper of roughly nine thousand words. I would like you to draft that outline for me. Please do not supply a bibliography, footnotes, or specific publication details; I will insert the references myself. Naming authors and positions is welcome, and where a claim about the historical record matters, flag that I should check it rather than asserting it.

The idea I want developed is this. Long-run predictions that fail on schedule are ordinarily scored as failures, yet when the predicted state of affairs eventually arrives — decades later — it is common to say the theory was "right but early," and I am not persuaded that this phrase picks out a single coherent epistemic category rather than a respectable-sounding rescue. The case I intend to build the paper around is Malthus. His principle that population, when unchecked, increases at a rate that outstrips the growth of the means of subsistence, and that real wages are therefore pressed toward subsistence, failed to hold across most of the world for the better part of two centuries; the standard defences are that the mechanism was genuine but masked by technological change, that the theory never fixed a date, and that the preventive check he added in the heavily revised 1803 edition — "moral restraint" — supplied the missing element. Some of that looks like a progressive development, some of it looks like a textbook immunizing stratagem, and my suspicion is that the difference lies specifically in the temporal dimension, which the existing methodological apparatus handles badly.

The outline should do the following. First, formulate a question precise enough to have a determinate answer — roughly: under what conditions does deferring the expected date of a failed prediction constitute legitimate revision rather than rescue, and can a theory ever be credited with confirmation for a prediction that eventually comes true after its scheduled failure? Second, explain why the familiar resources do not settle it — falsificationism, the progressive/degenerating distinction, Duhem–Quine holism, severe-testing accounts, the prediction-versus-accommodation debate, and the literature on ceteris paribus laws and tendencies — while being fair about what each of them already handles. Third, set out my intended method and defend it against the obvious alternatives. I am drawn to specifying a small taxonomy of ways a dated prediction can be late rather than wrong: a real mechanism with a mis-estimated schedule; a genuine tendency that is permanently counteracted; a self-defeating prediction whose publication changes the world; and an open-ended forecast retrofitted with a date after the fact. For each, I want to know what a forecaster would have to have committed to ex ante for later success to count as confirmation. Given my statistical background, I would also like the outline to confront whether any scoring rule can be sensitive to timing in the right way — what becomes of a proper scoring rule when the resolution date is itself a free parameter — and to give an honest verdict on whether that formal apparatus earns its place in a philosophy paper or merely decorates it. Fourth, say what a successful result would look like and, with equal specificity, what would count as the project failing; one live worry of mine is that whatever criteria I produce will turn out either to license every postponement or to exclude cases I want to retain, and the outline should show how I would detect that early. Fifth, assess scope realistically, identifying what must be cut to fit four months. Finally, if you think a short section on contemporary analogues — the recurrent date-shifting in resource-depletion and technological forecasting, or the treatment of unresolved long-horizon forecasts in forecasting practice — would strengthen the paper rather than dilute it, say so and indicate where it belongs and what it would displace.
