I TA an intro ethics section and this week we did the whole moral luck thing. One of my students brought up the standard pair of cases — two people drive home equally drunk, one gets home fine, one hits a pedestrian and kills him — and started saying the killer is clearly worse off morally, and another student cut in with "well, it's the thought that counts," and like half the room nodded and the first guy just sort of sat back down. Nobody actually argued, it just ended. I want to go into section next week and be able to say what that nod was nodding at, because I'm pretty sure "it's the thought that counts" isn't one claim but maybe five or six, and only some of them are any good.

So can you take that proverb apart and give me the precise theses it could be standing for? For each one I want to know what it commits you to, and whether it's just a platitude everybody already accepts, or a real and defensible substantive position, or actually false, or a slide past the question rather than an answer to it. Pay attention to the two words doing the work. "Counts" is ambiguous — counts for something, counts for everything, counts for the only thing that matters, counts for blame but not for regret or for the law's grading of homicide versus attempted homicide — and those come apart a lot. And "the thought" is even worse: is it the intention to do the act, the intention to bring about the harm, the general recklessness of your will, some maxim or policy you were acting on? Because in the driver case nobody intended to hit anybody, so I'm not even sure there's a relevant "thought" for the proverb to grab onto, and if that's right then which of the readings survives it? Tell me explicitly which reading, if any, would actually block the intuition that the one who killed is worse, and which ones leave that intuition completely untouched while sounding like they answered it.

Then stress-test whatever reading you end up defending. Does it carry over to the lucky and unlucky negligent surgeon, to attempted versus completed crimes, to the person who fully intended harm and was stopped by sheer chance versus the person who never formed the intention at all? And what happens to the whole thing once you notice that the thought itself is luck-dependent — your temper, your upbringing, whether life ever put you in a position to be tested? Does the proverb then just relocate the problem instead of solving it, or is there a version that doesn't. A list I can literally hand out or put on a slide would be great, in plain language — no references needed, I'll chase down anything I want to read later on my own.
