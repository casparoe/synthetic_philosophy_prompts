I sit on a review panel that funds cliodynamic work — quantitative models of long-run historical change that treat polities as generating time series (demographic pressure, the number of aspirants to elite positions, state fiscal capacity, incidence of collective violence) and search for recurrent oscillations spanning decades or centuries. Before the next round we want a written specification of what such a model must deliver to count as a predictive theory of history rather than a curve fitted to a handful of cases. Two criteria are already settled and need not be argued for: the model must be estimable from data available before the period it forecasts, and no predictor may be a coded measure of the thing being forecast.

Supply the rest of the list, each requirement accompanied by the case or consideration that motivates it: what must be true of the units of analysis, of the sample, of the stated mechanisms, and of the forecast itself for a miss to be recognizable as a miss. Attend particularly to the fact that polities are not independent observations — they inherit from, conquer, and imitate one another — and to the fact that a single trajectory contains only a few cycles' worth of data, so that periodicity may be indistinguishable from trend plus noise. Settle also, with reasons, whether accurate retrodiction of the past can substitute for a risked forecast, or whether "history is predictable in aggregate but not in particulars" is a substantive commitment or a refuge.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp, and say plainly if some subset cannot be jointly satisfied. Close by naming the single requirement that would eliminate the most existing work. No scorecard of particular models.
