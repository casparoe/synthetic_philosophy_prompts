I am three years into a novel whose engine is a marriage conducted almost entirely in true sentences. My surgeon, Aurel Vaszary, answers every question his wife Ilse asks him about a dying friend with wording that is accurate, and Ilse ends the book believing three things that are not so. My readers will have to decide whether he wronged her, and whether what he did differs in kind from lying. I am not willing to build that decision into a plot I could not defend in an argument, so I want the argument written first, by two parties who disagree.

Suppose an adversarial collaboration has been convened between the following camps, and draft the joint statement they publish at its close.

**Camp A, the symmetrists:** lying and misleading differ only in the contingent respects in which any two routes to the same false belief differ — reliability, deniability, cost to the speaker, susceptibility of the hearer. Control for these and no distinctively lying-shaped wrong remains.

**Camp B, the asymmetrists:** the difference is one of kind. To assert what one believes false is to recruit a practice the hearer is entitled to trust without checking, or to forfeit a standing that later candor cannot restore, or both; no true utterance does either.

The collaboration is to run on my cases rather than on the stock examples, because my cases hold fixed what the stock ones leave loose. In every variant, Ilse is eleven days from learning the truth from the dead man's family; her resulting belief is identical in content and in confidence; nothing else about her circumstances varies.

1. *Engineered.* Asked how the operation went, Aurel says, "He was awake and talking when I left the ward." True — and chosen because he knows exactly what she will take from it.
2. *Permissive.* Same question, same sentence, same result, but Aurel has no aim beyond saying something true and kind, and when he sees the inference forming he does not correct it.
3. *Matched lie.* Same question. Aurel says, "The operation went well." He believes it is false.
4. *Transparent evasion.* As in 1, but Ilse hears the evasion for what it is and thinks the worse of him — and still believes the operation went well, because she needs to.
5. *Open door.* As in 1, except that Aurel adds, "If you want the plain answer, ask me for it and I will give it." Ilse does not ask.

Note the two properties I treat as separate and want kept separate throughout: whether the speaker engineered the inference, and whether the truth was available to the hearer at no cost on request. I suspect a great deal of this debate has been conducted as though they were one property.

Please deliver, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — ten to fifteen. Each must be one at least a camp could have been imagined denying; where a claim holds only because of my stipulations, mark it as stipulated and say which stipulation carries the weight. No padding.
2. A disagreements table with four columns: the question at issue; Camp A's formulation of its own position; Camp B's formulation of its own position; and whether the difference is substantive or verbal. Each side's wording must be one its own side would sign unaltered — neither a caricature nor a translation into the other camp's vocabulary. Follow the table with a short note identifying which disagreements dissolved once the definitions of *lie* and *deceive* were pinned down, and why they dissolved.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each, name the case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it — my five are candidates, but say what makes them bear on it — and record each camp's prediction in advance, including at least one prediction that is uncomfortable for the camp making it. Then state what result would genuinely shift each camp, as distinct from what it would explain away.
4. A separate treatment of the methodological crux: whether ordinary judgments about such pairs are evidence about moral kinds or only about a folk concept with no authority over them. Give this its own section, because I expect the two camps are not so much disagreeing as reading the same data differently, and the statement should say so plainly if that is what is happening.
5. A closing paragraph, jointly signed, stating what both camps can say about Aurel — or, if no such statement is available, naming precisely the element that blocks it.

Keep the document free of citations, attributions, and reading lists; I want the reasoning, and my characters have not read the literature. Where a term does question-begging work, say whose term it is and supply a neutral replacement. Above all, write it as a text both parties could put their names to, not as a brief for either one.
