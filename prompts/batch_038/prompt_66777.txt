I'm a grad student co-running a reading group this term that's half philosophy of mind people and half CS grad students who actually train models, and it's going about as well as you'd expect. Every session collapses into the same loop: somebody points at a system doing something impressive — a network, an octopus arm, a slime mold — and says "see, no understanding in there," and somebody else says "you're just moving words around." The spine of the term is that inversion-of-reasoning move, the Darwin-then-Turing idea that competence can come first and comprehension gets built out of it rather than the other way round, and for next week I want to hand out a self-diagnostic so we can stop arguing in circles and see where people actually sit.

So: write me a sorting quiz, seven questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto actual positions. A few things I care about:

1. No question may contain the word "understand" or any of its cousins — comprehension, semantics, meaning. The point is people sort themselves by how they react to cases, not by which buzzword they'd reach for. Same for the options: nothing should be phrased so it's obviously the Serious Answer.

2. Two of the cases have to come from current ML practice rather than from a 1980 thought experiment. I especially want one built on unfaithful explanation — a system that produces a fluent, confident account of why it gave the answer it did, and you can show the account wasn't the cause (poke at the internals or flip some irrelevant feature and the output changes while the story stays exactly the same). That case is doing a lot of work for me, because it drives a wedge between "if it can tell you why, that's the relevant thing" and "what matters is what's actually in there, whether or not it can report it." Also want one where the system handles a genuinely novel variant of a task but fails a trivially reworded version of something it otherwise aces.

3. One case from biology. Blindsight is fine, or the distributed octopus nervous system, or those slime mold experiments where the tube network ends up looking like an engineered transport system. Pick whichever buys you the most leverage and say in a sentence why you picked it.

4. The key has to land people on positions, not vibes: the syntax-isn't-semantics camp, the competence-first/inversion camp, plain causal-role functionalism, the grounding-or-intentionality-required camp, the "read the weights, it's an internal property" camp, the distributed one where the human-plus-tooling is the unit that does the understanding, and the deflationary "drop the word entirely" option. Use standard labels where they exist, coin a handle and define it in one line where they don't.

5. My prof has a pet distinction she keeps hammering, which she calls the reportability gap — the difference between a system that can do X and a system that can do X and also explain X. She thinks half the room treats that gap as marking something deep and the other half treats it as an artifact of how we test. Build it in as an explicit axis, and then tell me which of your questions is actually probing it, because I want to be able to point at it in discussion.

6. Finally, tell me what's empty. For each combination of answers your key never sends anyone to, say whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, because it's merely unpopular, or because nobody's thought of it yet — and if it's the last one, is it worth defending? And flag any pair of leaves your instrument genuinely can't seperate. I'm pretty confident at least one pair collapses (some functionalists and some deflationists will answer identically everywhere), so say what an eighth question would have to look like to pry them apart.

Keep it handout-sized: each case under about 120 words, no preamble, no reading list at the end, and please don't write it like a magazine quiz. It has to be usable by someone who's never read a paper in this area, including the engineers.
