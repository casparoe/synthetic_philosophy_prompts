A friend of mine writes a monthly column for a general-interest magazine and asked me to sanity-check a passage before it goes to print. I'm an epistemologist — phil of medicine is adjacent to my work but I haven't kept up with the literture, and I don't want to wave through something I'd be embarassed by later. Here's the paragraph:

"Disease isn't a moral verdict, it's a biological fact. On the strongest version of this view, a part of you is healthy when it performs the function evolution gave it at a statistically normal level of efficiency for members of your species, age, and sex. That makes health as value-free as boiling point: no judgment about how a life ought to go enters the picture, so whether something is a disease gets settled by data rather than by argument. It also means that if a condition is statistically normal for your age and sex it simply isn't a disease, no matter how much you dislikes it — which is the point, not a bug. The rival camp holds close to the opposite: a condition is a disease when a community judges it bad, so the real question is what we disapprove of. That would turn diagnosis into a popularity contest, so the biological view wins by default."

My hunch is that the opening is roughly fair and the bit about the rival camp isn't, and that "value-free" is doing double duty somewhere in the middle — but I couldn't say exactly where if she pushed back. Can you go through it claim by claim and sort what a defender of the naturalist account would happily sign off on, what's a defensible simplification for a general audience, and what's actually wrong or leaves out something load-bearing? I also want to know whether the two camps she sets up are really exhaustive, what the people she's calling the rivals would say about her description of them, and whether that last sentence survives once the earlier ones are fixed. Answer in American English, please.
