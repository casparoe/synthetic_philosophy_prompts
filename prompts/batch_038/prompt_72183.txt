Consider a bundle of ordinary property-law doctrines, described here without technical apparatus so that the philosophical question can be isolated.

1. In most common-law and civil-law systems, a person who occupies land without the owner's permission — openly, exclusively, continuously — for a fixed statutory period acquires legal title to it, and the paper owner's title is extinguished. The classical common-law version required no good faith whatever: the occupier could have known from the outset that the land was someone else's. A number of jurisdictions have since added a good-faith or color-of-title requirement by statute.
2. Limitation statutes likewise bar the true owner's action to recover stolen movable property after a period of years, and in some systems a buyer who acquired stolen goods without notice of the theft keeps them. Other systems run the clock only from the moment the true owner locates the object, demands its return, and is refused.
3. Where the object was taken under conditions of persecution many decades ago — the paradigm case being artwork confiscated from Jewish owners in the 1930s and 1940s — courts, museums, and intergovernmental instruments have increasingly declined to apply those same limitation rules, favoring instead investigation of provenance and negotiated return to heirs, even though the present possessor is often entirely innocent and the object may have passed through several good-faith purchases.
4. Throughout, the criminal law continues to treat the original taking as a crime. No doctrine claims the original taking was rightful; the law merely stops unwinding it after a time.

Against the background of Nozick's entitlement theory — just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification of injustice, that third principle being the one its own author left conspicuously undeveloped — answer the following.

(a) Reconstruct the theory of entitlement this bundle presupposes. A historical theory holds that the justice of a holding depends entirely on how it came about; yet here the validity of a title turns on how much time has elapsed and on the present expectations of innocent parties. Does the practice therefore presuppose that entitlement is not purely historical after all, or can the extinguishment of stale claims be located inside rectification itself?

(b) Separate the candidate rationales: the decay of evidence over time; the independent value of repose and settled expectations; and the idea that long use and reliance themselves generate entitlement. Which does the doctrine strictly require, and which does it merely sit comfortably with?

(c) The celebrated basketball-player example concerns voluntary transfer between consenting parties. These doctrines concern a different link in the chain — the origin of a title and its gradual laundering through possession. Does that difference expose a gap the example was never built to handle?

(d) Point 3 has the same factual structure as points 1 and 2 and the opposite result. Does the bundle thereby presuppose an inconsistent theory, or a graded one — the strength of a claim scaling with the gravity of the originating wrong, the identifiability of victims, and the involvement of public institutions? Say whether that gradation is principled or merely political.

(e) State which rival accounts — labor-mixing, utilitarian security of expectations, a straightforwardly end-state distributive view, and strict entitlement — could each license most of the practice, and identify the single doctrinal detail that best discriminates among them.

(f) Describe what a system of pure historical entitlement would require: no limitation periods, no acquisitive prescription, every holding either traceable to a just origin or rectified. What breaks first, given that many current titles originate in conquest or dispossession for which no defensible baseline survives?

Finally, identify the one commitment whose abandonment would force the largest change in actual doctrine, and say in which direction.
