I am assembling the background chapter of a dissertation on the epistemology of prediction, and last month a friend who works on machine learning lent me a trade book on the philosophy of computing that he was sure I would enjoy. For the most part I did enjoy it, but one passage has stuck in my craw. It is confident, well written, and plainly the work of someone who has done the reading, yet I came away from it unable to say whether I had learned something about induction or had simply been handed a theorem as though it were a refutation of my opponents. We read it aloud at our reading group on Friday and disagreed about how much of it to believe; I was the only one who had taken a machine learning module, and even I could not put my finger on where the passage stops reporting an argument and starts performing one. It runs as follows:

> Suppose you are handed two ways of learning a pattern from data — or two strategies for hunting through a vast space of possibilities, such as finding a short route through a thousand cities. Which is better? The no-free-lunch theorems, established in the 1990s, give an answer that has unsettled machine learning ever since: averaged over all the problems you might face, the two are exactly as good as one another. Whatever advantage one enjoys on some problems is paid for, point for point, by an equal disadvantage on others, so the totals come out identical. Guessing at random, grinding through every possibility, and the most ingenious heuristic ever devised are, in this precise sense, indistinguishable. The reason is that the theorems range over every logically possible way of pairing inputs with outputs, including those pairings in which the labels are sheer noise and those in which the pattern runs backwards; every piece of structure an algorithm assumes is a wager that the world is not like that, and there are exactly as many worlds in which the wager loses as in which it wins. This, its proponents say, is Hume's problem of induction at last reduced to a theorem. No amount of data can entitle you to assume that the future will resemble the past, since for every body of data consistent with a world that has been regular all along there is one consistent with a world that became regular only this morning. It follows that there is no such thing as a good inductive bias, only a bias that happens to suit the problems you have been given; that simplicity is no guide to truth and Occam's razor a prejudice without rational foundation; and that machine learning succeeds not because we have discovered anything about how to learn, but because we have quietly chosen the easy problems. It is small wonder that a variant of the same mathematics, going under the name of the conservation of information, has been recruited by some writers to argue that the order we find in nature is more than any unguided search could have produced.

Could you take this claim by claim and tell me which parts a learning theorist or a philosopher of science would sign up to without qualification, which are honest compressions that cost nothing important, and which are quietly doing something else — a stronger conclusion smuggled in behind a weaker premise, two distinct results presented as one, a technical phrase pressed into its everyday sense? Four places in particular I cannot resolve on my own. First, whether the theorems really concern an average taken over all logically possible pairings of inputs and outputs, as the passage twice implies, or whether that formulation obscures what the results are actually establishing about the comparison between algorithms. Second, whether the result about learning and the result about search are one result or two, with different content and different assumptions, and whether anything important is lost by treating them as interchangeable. Third, and this is the one that matters most to me, whether reducing Hume to a theorem is legitimate at all, or whether the theorem already presupposes the very assumption Hume questions, so that at best it restates his problem in formal dress rather than settling it — and if the second is right, what a defender of the passage could still say on its behalf. Fourth, whether any correctly stated version of the mathematics reaches the closing claim about nature's order, or whether that conclusion is doing independent work under a borrowed name.

Then tell me what survives the correction. If the passage were rewritten so that each sentence was defensible, would there still be a substantive point left that a philosopher of induction ought to care about, or would fixing the errors drain it of interest entirely? And where the book is simply right, say so plainly: I should rather know which sentences I can keep and build on. I would ask that you give me your own assessment rather than a reading list — I have no library access this term, and citations I cannot check are worse than useless to me. Finally, say how confident you are in the audit as a whole, and single out the one point in your own answer that you are least sure of: the place where you think a specialist would be most likely to correct you, and what would settle the matter.
