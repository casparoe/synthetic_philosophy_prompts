I have been commissioned to write a long first-person piece for the ideas desk on what economists are actually doing when they claim to measure well-being, and my editor has asked me to make the argument explicit rather than atmospheric. Before I file, I want a consistency check on the position I intend to state on the page. I am not looking for a tutorial on the literature; I know it well enough to have written the piece. What I want is an audit of whether the following hang together, because I suspect — without knowing where — that they do not.

My commitments:

1. Preferences are constituted by choice. I take this strictly: it is not that choosing reveals an independently existing mental state, but that to prefer one thing to another just is to be disposed to choose it in specified circumstances. Self-reports, introspective testimony and neural data are at most evidence of what a person would choose; they are not what the preference is.

2. There is no cardinal utility independent of that. I accept the standard representation results — a coherent agent's preferences over risky prospects can be represented by an expected-utility function unique up to positive affine transformation — and I use those numbers freely when discussing risk. But I deny that there is any such thing as measuring the intensity of a satisfaction, and I follow Robbins in holding that interpersonal comparisons of utility are value judgements rather than matters of fact.

3. Nevertheless I am a welfarist. What makes one policy better than another is that it does better by people's preferences, and the aggregation across persons is a substantive part of that claim, not a convenient fiction I could dispense with.

4. The operational form of (3) that I defend in the piece is the compensation test: a change counts as an improvement if those who gain could compensate those who lose and still be ahead. I present this as tracking (3), not as a rival to it or a substitute for it.

5. Preference formation is endogenous to circumstance. I hold that some preferences — those shaped by manipulation, by deprivation, by systematically narrowed options — deserve less weight in policy, and that in certain cases satisfying such a preference does not benefit the person at all, however sincere and however consistently acted upon it is.

6. The test for which preferences count is counterfactual and idealised. A preference counts when the person would hold it given full information, unimpaired deliberation and the absence of manipulative influence; when actual and idealised preferences diverge, the idealised ones govern.

7. I am optimistic that the neuroscientific search for a single neural scale on which different rewards are represented can eventually supply what (2) says is missing, and so give interpersonal comparison a factual rather than an evaluative basis.

Some of these look to me as though they must conflict, and I expect I am wrong about which. Do not take my word for any of it. Please go through the set and tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity, with the least costly revision for each and what that revision would cost me in the column — that is, which sentences I would no longer be entitled to write; which pair only looks contradictory and why, so that I can say so in the piece rather than appear not to have noticed; and which single change would repair the most damage. Finally, tell me whether the position that survives those repairs is still recognisably the one I set out to defend, or whether I have talked myself into something else entirely.
