**Question (20 points, ~600 words expected):** Explain Kuhn's claim that competing paradigms are incommensurable. Does it follow that the choice between them is not a rational one? Give reasons.

**Student's answer:**

Kuhn's incommensurability thesis is that two paradigms cannot be compared by any neutral standard, because the meanings of a paradigm's terms — theoretical and observational alike — are fixed from inside that paradigm. Newton's mass and Einstein's mass are not the same quantity, so when the Newtonian says mass is constant and the relativist says it varies with velocity, they are not contradicting each other so much as talking past one another. Kuhn brings this out with the gestalt-switch cases: after a revolution scientists work in a different world, and what they see when they look at the same data changes, roughly the way a picture that was a duck becomes a rabbit.

This is also why a paradigm shift is not a refutation. Normal science does not test the paradigm, it uses it to solve puzzles, so an anomaly is a puzzle that fails to yield rather than a counterexample, and a paradigm is only ever given up once an alternative is on offer — nothing gets falsified in Popper's sense.

The consequence is that the choice between paradigms cannot be a rational choice. Kuhn does allow that scientists on both sides appeal to values like accuracy, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness, but since those values are themselves internal to a paradigm, and each side weights them differently, there is no shared standard by which one side can be shown to be correct. Conversion is therefore much closer to a religious or political conversion than to being won over by argument, and Kuhn accepts this when he says the supporters of rival paradigms live in different worlds. In practice the changeover happens through a turnover of generations: the older scientists do not convert, they die, and the discipline moves on with the next cohort.

Critics spotted early on that paradigm gets used in something like two dozen different senses in the 1962 book, running from a whole world-view down to a single textbook exercise, and Kuhn never tidied this up — the ambiguity is still there in the later editions.
