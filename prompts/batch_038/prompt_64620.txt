No math or philosophy training here, I taught myself this stuff off blogs and one very patient librarian, so please tell me if I've mangled the setup.

I've been chewing on Aumann's agreement theorem (the 1976 one, "Agreeing to Disagree") and I built an argument out of it for a friend, and the conclusion is so bad that one of my steps has to be broken. I just can't see which.

Setup: my coworker Priya and I are trying to work out whether the new bus lanes downtown actually cut commute times. Neither of us is an expert, neither of us has inside info, and we're about equally careful -- I genuinely can't think of a reason to trust my own head over hers. We swapped every article and dataset either of us found, we said our probability numbers out loud to each other, and we both know that we both know that.

1. Aumann's theorem: two rational people who start from the same prior, and whose posteriors are common knowledge between them, cannot have different posteriors.
2. We started from the same prior -- that's just another way of saying I have no reason to think I'm the smarter one.
3. We announced our numbers out loud and each knows the other knows, so the common knowledge condition is satisfied.
4. Priya says 80% the bus lanes helped. I say 30%.
5. By (1) that combination is impossible for two rational people. So one of us is being irrational, or faking her number, or we didn't really have the same prior.
6. I can't tell which of those it is, and if the broken one is me, then the judgment I'd use to figure it out is exactly the broken part.
7. So the honest move is to treat her number as precisely as trustworthy as mine and land around the middle, call it 55%.
8. But if she does the same she also lands on 55%, and neither of us arrived at 55% by thinking about buses at all. We just split the difference.
9. Notice what (7) rewards too: the more extreme the number you shout first, the further your opponent's midpoint slides toward you. So the theorem's advice for winning any arguement is to open with 99%.
10. It also means Priya and I can never really convince each other with evidence, since whatever we end up agreeing on is fixed by the numbers we happened to announce, not by what the data showed.
11. A theorem about rational agreement can't be telling me that bluffing beats reasoning and that evidence doesn't matter.

Which step do I have to give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd really like to keep the theorem and keep counting myself as rational, and I'd rather not end up with "you can always escape by claiming you had different priors," since then the theorem doesn't seem to rule anything out at all.
