I cover language and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a piece on the territory between lying and merely failing to tell the truth — the space occupied by officials, employers, and perfectly ordinary people when everything they say is accurate and the impression they leave is not. I have enough Grice to know the machinery: what is said versus what is implicated, the cooperative principle and its maxims, and the claim that conversational implicatures are cancellable, or contextually defeasible, in a way that conventional implicatures and entailments are not. That last claim is the one I want to press, because it appears to smuggle in an assumption about timing — that a speaker can always append a clause withdrawing the implicature — and I would like to test it against a case engineered so that the timing assumption is precisely what gives way.

So: build me a thought experiment to specification. I want a short exchange set somewhere unremarkable — a school run, an office corridor, a kitchen table, a market stall — in which one speaker says something she believes to be true and thereby conversationally implicates something she believes to be false. The constraints matter. The implicature must be generated rather than asserted: no irony, no sarcasm, no metaphor, no hedge or particle doing the work, and nothing that depends on a tone of voice a transcript could not capture. It must arise from the maxims as ordinarily observed, not from a flagrant violation the hearer has to repair. The hearer must possess no independent evidence bearing on the point at issue, so the false impression is entirely the utterance's doing. And a straightforward lie must have been available to the speaker at greater cost to her, so the case cannot be waved away as someone with no better option. Please also spare me the well-worn furniture: no reference letters, no courtrooms, no parliamentary testimony, no product reviews. The exchange itself should run to roughly eighty words.

Then show me that the design works. State what is said, in truth-conditional terms; state what is implicated and which maxim, or clash of maxims, generates it; and confirm that the implicated content is false and believed false by the speaker, so that on Grice's own account of lying she has not lied. Now run the cancellation test: put the explicit cancellation clause into the speaker's mouth and report what becomes of it. I want to know which of two failures you have produced, and why you chose that one — either the clause is perfectly grammatical but pragmatically self-defeating, since uttering it would reveal the very belief the speaker is concealing, or the clause is unobjectionable and yet useless, because the hearer has already drawn the inference and retracting an inference is not a move available to the person who prompted it. Tell me which of these, if either, is a genuine counterexample to the cancellability of conversational implicature and which the framework can absorb; my own suspicion is that the second collapses into a confusion between withdrawing a commitment and correcting a belief, and I should like to be told plainly if I am wrong. Finish by identifying the single feature of the case doing the work, describing what the case becomes once that feature is removed, and stating the sharpest reply a defender of Grice could make.

Keep the whole thing under 550 words: the exchange first, then your analysis in numbered points, and then your bottom line — whether this is a real counterexample or only looks like one — in two sentences.
