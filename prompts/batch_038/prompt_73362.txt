I co-organize a small reading group on the foundations of computing — two working computer scientists, a philosopher of physics, and myself (a graduate student in logic) — and for two terms now we have been circling the same question without making an inch of progress: what kind of claim the Church–Turing thesis actually is. Every meeting ends in the same standoff, and I have come to suspect that the standoff is not about the thesis at all. Let me lay out the two positions as they are actually argued in the room, because I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a history of it. On one side are those of us who treat the thesis as conceptual, close to a stipulation: what is provable, on this view, is the extensional coincidence of the λ-definable, the general recursive, and the Turing-computable functions, and the residual "thesis" is just the claim that this common extension captures the informal notion of a procedure that can be carried out by rote, without insight or ingenuity. Since that informal notion has never been given an independent characterization, this camp holds that the thesis cannot be refuted by argument or experiment; a purported counterexample would have to be a procedure we could all inspect and agree is rote, and in ninety years nobody has produced one. Its most sophisticated members add that the interesting work lies in axiomatizing the notion — Gandy-style principles of boundedness, locality, and determinate state transition for discrete machines — and proving that anything satisfying them computes only Turing-computable functions, so that the thesis becomes a theorem relative to the axioms. On the other side are those who insist this makes the thesis empty, and that its content has always been empirical: either directly, as a claim about what physical systems in our universe can do, or via a principle to the effect that a universal computer can simulate any physically realizable process. On this reading the thesis is falsifiable in principle by a hypercomputer — a relativistic device exploiting a Malament–Hogarth-type spacetime, an accelerating machine performing successive steps in geometrically shrinking intervals, an idealized analog device with real-number states, or a machine permitted to revise its output indefinitely and read off a limit — and its unrefuted status is due not to conceptual necessity but to the fact that none of these devices is physically realizable, given unbounded energy, infinite precision, or spacetime geometries we have no evidence obtain. The conceptual camp replies that this slides illegitimately from a narrow claim about human rule-following to a sweeping claim about any physical system whatsoever; the physical camp replies that the axioms in the "theorem" version are themselves physical constraints wearing conceptual clothing, so the proof smuggles in exactly the empirical content it claims to dispense with.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept, is that this is largely a verbal dispute: the phrase 'effectively computable' is being used with two different extensions, one fixed by reflection on rule-following practice and one fixed by what physics permits, so that the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading, and the impasse is what you would predict from that. But I am uneasy about the hypothesis, because there seem to be residues that do not look verbal at all. One is the question whether the Gandy-style principles are conceptual or physical: if they are physical, the theorem version collapses into the empirical version; if they are conceptual, it is not obvious what work they are doing that Turing's own analysis did not already do. A second is the question whether the physical version is falsifiable in practice and not merely in principle — what observation would distinguish a genuine hypercomputer from a very fast ordinary machine, or from a broken one, given that any finite run of a purported oracle is consistent with its being an ordinary computer plus luck, and that the asymmetry between confirming and refuting such a claim may be unbridgeable. A third is whether the two camps even disagree about the same subject matter: one appears to be asking what follows from the concept of an algorithm, the other what the world permits, and it is unclear to me that any single sentence could be asserted by one and denied by the other.

So: diagnose this. Tell me whether it is a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or two legitimate questions travelling under one name, and support the diagnosis by reference to what each side actually says rather than to how the labels are usually glossed. Address the three residues above explicitly, and say which, if any, survives the disambiguation as a real disagreement. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction that dissolves it, a formal result that relocates it, a physical construction that decides it, a reformulation of the question that both camps could recognize as their own, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. As a concrete diagnostic, try to write down one formulation of the thesis that a member of each camp would accept as correctly stating their own view; if that cannot be done, explain precisely where the attempt breaks down, since I suspect that is where the real disagreement lives. Finally, give me your honest verdict on whether the question of the thesis's epistemic status is ill-posed, merely hard, or — as I have begun to worry — a proxy for an argument about the mind and mechanism that nobody in the group wants to have directly. I am not after a literature map: no citations, no reading list, no survey of who held which variant; argue from the content of the positions themselves, and say plainly where you think my hypothesis is wrong.
