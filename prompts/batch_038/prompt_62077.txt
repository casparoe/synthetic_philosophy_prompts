The question: In the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, what is Galileo's principle for reconciling Scripture with the Copernican system, and how far does it actually set him apart from Bellarmine? Does the letter subordinate Scripture to natural philosophy?

The student's answer: Galileo wrote the letter in 1615 to Christina of Lorraine, the mother of the reigning Grand Duke, after a friar attacked Copernicanism from the pulpit and the row spilled over into the court. His basic principle is that the Bible is not a science textbook: it was written for ordinary people and for their salvation, so wherever it appears to assert something about nature that science has shown to be false, we have to reinterpret it allegorically. He takes this from Calvin's doctrine of accomadation, the idea that God lisps to us the way a nurse talks to an infant, and he applies it to the passage where Joshua commands the sun to stand still. Read naively that passage says the sun goes round the earth, but Galileo points out it is really on the side of Copernicus, since on his account the sun's own rotation is what carries the earth around, so stopping the sun lengthens the day exactly as the text reports. On this basis Galileo says theology may be the queen of the sciences but she has no business dictating to astronomers about where the planets are; her domain is faith and morals, and everything outside that gets settled by demonstration and sense experience. That is the real gap between him and Bellarmine, who was a strict literalist and would not even consider that Scripture might be accomodating itself to its readers. The letter was enough to get Galileo condemned for heresy in 1616, and although the Pope had been friendly to him earlier the Dialogue of 1632 finished the job and he was made to recant. So yes, the letter does subordinate Scripture to natural philosophy, because on Galileo's account what the Bible means is finally decided by what the telescope shows.
