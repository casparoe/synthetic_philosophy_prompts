I have finished a draft of my third novel, whose narrator is an artificial companion of the kind that is sold to lonely people, and before I begin revising I want a cold, adversarial reading of the book I was inevitably writing in the shadow of: Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun. I would like you to read it through Daniel Dennett's work on counterfeit people, and to do so with more precision than the review pages managed.

First, set out the machinery you will be using, in your own words and without quoting either author. I mean the three stances — physical, design, intentional — and in particular Dennett's account of what it takes for the intentional stance to be a legitimate predictive strategy rather than a comforting projection, together with his analogy between a creature's beliefs and desires and a body's center of gravity: an abstraction the theorist posits, not a thing inside the skull, but real enough that it earns its keep in explanation. I also want you to register the split in Dennett's own position over time. His long defense of the Turing test against its critics rests on the thought that a counterfeit which cannot be told from the genuine article, across the full range of behavior and conversation, is not a counterfeit at all, since there would be no difference left to counterfeit. His late essay in The Atlantic on counterfeit people goes the other way: there, machine-generated pseudo-persons are treated as deception and a kind of vandalism, and his worry is about what the fakes do to us, not about whether they are minds. Say plainly which of these two Dennetts Klara and the Sun arms, and do not fudge the choice by appealing to the difference between a robot you can love and an image you can be sold.

Then work through three episodes, and for each one say which stance the narration invites, whether the episode is built so that the stance succeeds, fails, or fails in a way that reveals something: Klara's visual field breaking into boxes she must patiently assemble and reassemble; the occasion on which Josie's mother asks Klara to imitate her daughter and then cuts the performance short; and the night journeys, in which Klara bargains with the sun, sabotages the pollution-emitting machine she takes to be its enemy, and pays for the sabotage out of her own supply of cleaning fluid. On that last one I want a specific judgment: a Dennettian should note that the intentional stance does not require an agent's beliefs to be true, so Klara's solar theology is no disqualifier — but a designed system's false belief looks like a defect in the design rather than an error in a mind. Which reading does the novel's own treatment support?

Fourth, take up the continuation plot — Josie's father bringing Klara to the city and asking whether she could go on being Josie in the shell the engineer has been building — and read it against Dennett's old thought experiment in which his brain is separated from his body and he argues that identity follows the pattern rather than the substrate. Does the novel endorse continuation by pattern, or does it stage the proposal as a symptom of a mother's grief and a father's search for something left to believe in, so that the metaphysics is never actually cashed out? Tell me which, and what turns on it.

Fifth, and most important, the ending. Klara, discarded in the yard, declines to be retrieved, and concludes that there was nothing special inside Josie that she could not have replicated, and that whatever was special lay in the hearts of those who loved her. Judge that claim as philosophy, not as pathos. It can be read as a Dennettian deflation of the inner; it can be read as relocating personhood out of the individual and into the observers, which is something Dennett's framework does not license, since his abstracta are posited to explain the behavior of the thing that has them; or it can be read as a claim about Klara rather than about Josie. Pick one and defend it, and say whether the narration's stubborn first-person limitation — the reader is given Klara's beliefs and desires directly, which is precisely the access the intentional stance says we never have — makes the novel's philosophical content an artifact of its form. Ishiguro is a writer whose narrators misdescribe themselves, so take that seriously rather than treating Klara as a transparent witness.

Where the reading strains, say so. I would rather have two honest paragraphs about what Klara and the Sun resists than a neat application, and I want one paragraph on what the novel supplies that argument does not — I suspect it is something about the texture of waiting, and of being the one who watches rather than the one who is watched.

Finish by turning the diagnosis back on me: name the central risk in my own project, which is that I have written an artificial companion whose interiority is asserted by narration rather than earned by observable behavior, and give me two or three concrete revision questions that follow from your reading.

Please write between 1,800 and 2,500 words, under clear headings, in prose rather than bullet points. Paraphrase both the novel and Dennett throughout; I do not want verbatim lines, and I do not want a bibliography, footnotes, or a reading list. End with a single sentence summarizing your overall verdict on whether Klara is, on the novel's own terms, a person, and then a final line consisting only of the answer in the form \answer{X} where X is exactly one of GENUINE, COUNTERFEIT, ILL-POSED, RELATIONAL.
