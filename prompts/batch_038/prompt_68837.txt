I TA a third-year behavioural economics / decision theory seminar — 24 students, one 75-minute slot, and there's a second facilitator (the lecturer) helping me run it. Here's my problem: they've already had the Ellsberg lecture. They've all seen the two urns, they know the "correct" answer is that you shouldn't have a preference between red and black in the unknown urn, and they know the punchline about the sure-thing principle. So if I just wheel in a 50/50 jar and a mystery jar and hand out betting slips, I'll get textbook responses produced by demand characteristics and nobody will have felt anything. I want an activity that ambushes people who are already braced for the ambush — ideally one where the ambiguity is manufactured *by the participants themselves*, so they can't chalk the whole thing up to distrusting me as the experimenter (that's a huge confound in every classroom version I've seen; half the class just assumes the mystery urn is rigged against whoever bets on it).

The core mechanic I want you to build around, and improve on: every student blind-samples their own sealed cloth bag of chips from one big reference jar whose composition is public and known to be exactly even. So each person ends up holding a bag that is objectively drawn from a known distribution, that nobody — not me, not them — can see inside, and that is ex ante probabilistically indistinguishable from the "risky" urn. Then they price bets on red/black for their own bag versus bets on the public jar, using fake credits and a proper scoring rule (Brier or quadratic, whatever you think survives 24 undergraduates doing arithmetic under time pressure) so that what they report tracks what they believe. After that I want a trading phase: students can buy and sell each other's bet positions on an open floor, and I want the room to visibly discover that ambiguity-averse pricing lets someone else walk up and Dutch-book them — pairs of trades that both feel fine individually and jointly guarantee a loss. Finish with a public reveal of the bags so we can compare predicted vs actual compositions. Payoffs get converted into a small prize plus a couple of bonus points, nothing real-money.

Please give me the whole runnable thing: exact rules and round structure with a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 minutes for 24 people and two facilitators, a cheap materials list, the scoring-rule formulas written out so a non-maths student can use them without a calculator, the trading phase mechanics (how bids are posted, whether it's double auction or just shouting, how you settle), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific claims. The debrief has to confront the awkward fact that the bags were never genuinely ambiguous in the technical sense — their composition came from a fully known process — and use that to make them decide whether ambiguity is a property of acts or a property of an agent's epistemic relation to a source; I want them leaving unsure whether their behaviour was irrational or whether maxmin-expected-utility / imprecise-credence stories are just describing the same rationality more honestly. Also tell me what will predictably break the first time I run it: the showoffs who bet on the bag precisely to prove they aren't fooled, the market failing to clear or collapsing into two loud traders, leaks about bag contents, someone realising they can game the scoring rule, whatever else you foresee — and how to handle each without derailing. And be straight with me about which feature of the paradox this activity fundamentally cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking, plus one 15-minute fallback version in case the blind-sampling logistics turn out to be a disaster in a room with no prep time.
