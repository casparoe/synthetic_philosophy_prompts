Draft, working title "The Bishop Who Wanted a Public Bank":

§1 — Berkeley's money is a doing, not a thing.
Most of Berkeley's economic writing is a long list of numbered questions, The Querist, published in three parts in the mid-1730s while he was bishop of Cloyne, plus a couple of shorter tracts from around the same period. The through-line is that money is not wealth. He treats it as something like a counter or token used in keeping accounts, and its worth has nothing to do with what it's made of — he's writing about an Ireland that was short of coin and dependent on trade with England — but everything to do with what it does, which is set people to work. Wealth on this picture is really power, the power to command the labour of others, and a country is rich to the extent its people are busy, not to the extent it sits on a pile of metal. That's why he can be perfectly relaxed about paper money, as long as it's issued by a bank under public control for public ends, and why he's so hostile to hoarding, which withdraws the counters from circulation and therefore withdraws the spur to industry. Hence my claim that he's on our side: for Berkeley the only sensible questions about a currency are what it's for and who steers it.

§2 — money as sign is just Berkeley's theory of signs.
This is the bit I think is genuinely interesting and that I don't see anyone making. Berkeley's general view is that a sign needn't resemble, or even be caused by, what it signifies — that's the argument of his early work on vision, where visible things function as signs of tangible ones purely through custom — and in his later dialogues against the freethinkers he pushes further, arguing that plenty of ordinary and even technical terms do their work with no clear idea attached to them at all. So it costs Berkeley nothing to say money signifies power without being power, without resembling it, and without standing for any underlying stuff. And this fits his metaphysics instead of fighting it: since he denies material substance outright, there is no gold behind the coin for the coin to owe its value to. The token isn't a shadow of a substance, it's an institution — and institutions are precisely what he thinks signs are.

§3 — chartalism, and the thing I don't want to say.
That makes Berkeley, I want to claim, an early chartalist: money is a creature of law, its value is what the issuing authority makes it, and policy is entirely about who issues and to what end. Which puts him on the side of a public bank extending credit to employ idle hands and against private issuance for private gain. Two problems I half-handle. (a) Berkeley despised the speculative paper wealth of the South Sea era and saw stock-jobbing as parasitic, and he wanted credit tethered to actual productive industry, so he is not an unlimited-expand-the-money-supply guy. (b) His talk of employing the poor is also pretty plainly talk of disciplining them — idleness is a sin, the labouring poor should be kept busy and cheaply fed, and his picture of a good Ireland is closer to a well-run workhouse than to a free and prosperous society. So the piece ends by saying Berkeley gives us the right theory of money and the wrong theory of what money is for, and that we should take the first and refuse the second.
