So you know what you're working with, your resources include: the intrapersonal analogue (why is it obviously fine for me to accept a dull ache now to avoid a worse one next year, when the me-next-year is psychologically pretty remote from me-now?); the claim that the objection mistakes a metaphysical fact for a moral one; the non-identity problem for person-affecting views; and the charge that any connection-weighted aggregation is either arbitrary or collapses back into plain summing.

My opening: skip the trolley and the transplant surgeon, we've all done those. Here's one instead. Two patients in a pain clinic get hooked up to a neural link that lets each feel some slice of the other's nociception in real time. I have a dial. At zero, each feels only their own, and we're back to your familiar summing across strangers. Cranked all the way up, each feels the other's pain as fully and directly as their own — no confusion about which body it's in, just two streams carrying the very same hurt. So at that setting there's arguably a single subject of both pains, and your aggregation looks innocent. The question is what happens in between. If your answer is that the dial makes no moral difference at all because it's all just totals, you owe me a reason why the strength of the connection between two streams of experience does exactly zero moral work. If your answer is that it makes a difference by degrees, then you've conceded that boundaries matter on a scale — which isn't utilitarianism anymore, it's something else, and I want to hear you say what.
