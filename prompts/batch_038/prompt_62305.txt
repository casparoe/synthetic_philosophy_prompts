Consider Ruth Benedict's *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946) as a test case for two questions: whether national character is a coherent object of enquiry at all, and whether it is ever permissible to generalise about a people.

The facts I shall assume, so that you need not consult anything: the study was commissioned during the Second World War by the American agency responsible for information and morale, its purpose being to predict Japanese behaviour and to advise on the terms of surrender, including the fate of the emperor. Benedict had never been to Japan and had no Japanese. Her materials were literary and philosophical texts, film, press coverage, and informants, among them a second-generation Japanese-American colleague who had been born in America, raised in Japan, and spent part of the war in a relocation camp. The book opens with a catalogue of paired opposites, describing a people at once aggressive and unaggressive, militaristic and aesthetic, insolent and polite, submissive and resentful of being pushed around, loyal and treacherous, conservative and open to new ways. It popularised the contrast between shame cultures and guilt cultures. It sold modestly in America for decades, but after its Japanese translation in 1948 it sold in the millions in Japan during a period of acute national self-examination, and was largely absorbed into the postwar Japanese literature on national uniqueness. It was criticised early by Japanese scholars on the ground that it took a class ideology for a whole culture, a state of severe social dislocation for a normal condition, and an extraordinary historical moment for an unvarying norm.

For the analysis, please use a distinction I am stipulating rather than quoting. A national-character claim may be read **distributionally**, as an assertion about how psychological traits are spread across a population; **constitutively**, as an assertion about a self-conception that is partly sustained by being believed, so that uptake by the people described helps make it true; or **structurally**, as an assertion about the institutions, obligations and sanctions a population inhabits, from which behaviour can be predicted without positing any inner disposition at all. Call a generalisation **load-bearing** where withdrawing it would change the thing it describes, and call an enquiry's **corrigibility exposure** the degree to which its author stood to be corrected by the people described, and the interval before such correction could arrive.

With that apparatus, address the following:

1. Reconstruct the evidential position. What would each of the three readings require by way of evidence, and could evidence gathered at a distance, under wartime conditions, for a client with an operational interest in the outcome, in principle have satisfied any of them? Is the absence of fieldwork a defect of the conclusion or only of the confidence attached to it?

2. Take the opening catalogue of opposites. Under which reading is it informative rather than unfalsifiable? A conjunction of contraries attributed to a whole people is either a claim about variance within the population, a claim about dispositions whose manifestation depends on the situation, or a claim about conflicting normative obligations pulling the same agent in opposite directions. Argue which is most charitable, and then say whether it survives the situationist case against stable individual character: if behaviour tracks circumstances rather than traits in persons, what has to be true for a trait attribution to an entire people to be non-vacuous?

3. Consider the shame and guilt contrast. Is it a psychological thesis about which sanctions motivate, or a moral ranking of ethical cultures? Then the harder point: the distinction was taken up in Japan and entered ordinary self-description there. Does that uptake confirm the account, or does it merely convert a distributional claim into a constitutive one? What evidential weight, if any, can endorsement by the described carry?

4. Assess the reception. Does a generalisation that is true of a people at a moment but false of them as such fail because it is untrue, or because the notion it deploys has no temporal index? Is the Japanese scholars' objection best read as a charge of falsehood or of mistyping?

5. Address permissibility. Generalisations of the form *the X are Y* are readily accepted when the property is striking or menacing even at a low base rate, and rejected when it is unremarkable even at a high one. If national-character claims are typically generic in this way, does that explain their felt informativeness and their resistance to refutation independently of their truth? Then distinguish the harm of a false generalisation from the harm of being accurately described by an outsider in terms one does not recognise oneself in, and from the harm of having one's self-understanding shaped by an account composed in order to predict and manage one's conduct in a war. Can the latter two obtain where the generalisation is true, and does that show the acceptability of generalising about peoples is not a purely epistemic matter?

6. Conclude in both directions. Does the episode support national character as a coherent construct, or does it show that what travelled under that name was institutional analysis, comparative ethics, or morale assessment mislabelled? A method can predict well while its ontology is empty; say whether the defender of national character can take that instrumentalist reply, and identify what the theory leaves silent about the episode rather than forcing it to speak.

Do not quote the book, which you cannot consult, and do not offer references, a bibliography or a reading list. Argue from the facts stated above and from reasoning alone.
