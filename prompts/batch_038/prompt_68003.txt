I convene a final-year jurisprudence seminar and am setting the final paper. I need one essay question on the Hart–Fuller debate, worth 25 marks and answerable in 60 minutes, that cannot be answered by contrasting legal positivism with natural law, by reciting the separation thesis, or by summarising either thinker's general programme.

The question must be built on the following fact pattern, which you may tighten stylistically but not materially alter:

The jurisdiction of Varene has no written constitution. For forty years its courts have treated as law whatever the Minister of Justice has announced in a numbered circular published weekly in the Gazette; a later circular is held to displace an earlier statute of the legislature. Candidates should be told that: (i) the Gazette is available to anyone on payment of a modest fee, and circulars are drafted in precise, unambiguous language; (ii) no circular has ever been repealed, and conflicts between subsisting circulars are constant, but courts resolve them by applying the most recent, and where two issue the same day, the lower-numbered; (iii) no court has ever declined to apply a circular or treated one as invalid, and officials uniformly describe the practice as the law of Varene; (iv) some circulars are general in form, while others name individuals and direct the outcome of a particular pending case; (v) some circulars declare themselves applicable to conduct predating publication, and occasionally one imposes a duty that cannot be performed within the week it runs; (vi) courts decide exactly what the circulars direct, without deviation, favour, or quiet evasion.

The crux I want the question to force is this: the pattern satisfies Fuller's demand for congruence between official action and declared rule about as perfectly as a system can, while failing his demands for constancy through time, for generality, and against impossible obligations — so it tests whether the desiderata hang together as a package or are severable and unequally weighted, and whether the one Hart's readers most often treat as doing the moral work is in fact the one that does least work here. On the other side, Hart appears to have no difficulty at all: there is a rule of recognition, constituted by official practice rather than by any text, and it is efficacious. A strong answer should draw out what this shows about whether the two were answering the same question, and what it implies for the choice of the post-war informer cases as the battleground of the debate.

Please supply: (a) the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with any rubric or instructions to candidates, and with at most one short follow-up part that must not be independently answerable; (b) a model answer of about 800 words; and (c) a marking scheme out of 25 listing five or six must-hit moves, the marks each carries, and, for each, the specific mistake it is designed to catch — for instance, treating Fuller's internal morality as the classical natural law thesis that unjust enactments are not law, or holding that Varene lacks a rule of recognition because none is written down. State explicitly the ceiling for an answer that is fluent and accurate but purely expository.

Do not include quotations, page references, citations, or reading lists anywhere in these materials; the question must be answerable from the fact pattern together with the conceptual resources of the debate, and nothing in your deliverables should depend on consulting a source.
