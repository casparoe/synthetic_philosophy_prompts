I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently drafting a long feature on why well-funded attempts to change institutional norms so often leave the norms exactly where they were. The centerpiece is a large health system I've been reporting on for the better part of a year. About eighteen months ago its leadership decided to break the pattern in which junior clinical staff don't speak up when they think a senior colleague has made an error. They did close to everything a change-management consultant would recommend: a non-retaliation clause written into the handbook, a bonus line tied to the number of near-miss reports filed, an internal campaign built on video testimonials from surgeons describing occasions when they were glad a nurse had interrupted them, and a mandatory pre-shift huddle at which anyone is invited to voice a concern. Two rounds of internal survey work later, self-reported willingness to speak up is essentially flat; incident-reporting numbers rose for a quarter and then drifted back to baseline. My editors have asked me to give the failure a theoretical spine rather than only a narrative one.

The spine I've been building on is David Lewis's analysis in *Convention*. As I read it, Lewis holds that a convention is a regularity in behavior that solves a recurrent coordination problem: people conform because they expect the others around them to conform, each prefers to conform on condition that nearly everyone else does, at least one alternative regularity would have served just as well, and all of this is common knowledge in the population. That seems to give me a crisp diagnosis. If norms are equilibria of coordination problems, then this health system attacked its norm with persuasion and training instead of with payoffs: it announced a new regularity and hoped people would converge on it, but it never made conformity to the new regularity individually preferable given what each person expected everyone else to do, and it never produced common knowledge that expectations had shifted. On that telling, norm change is fundamentally incentive engineering plus publicity — you locate the coordination problem, you move the payoffs until the regularity you want is the equilibrium, and you make the change salient enough that the requisite common knowledge crystallizes.

I'd like you to help me sharpen that argument before I commit to it in print, working from the concepts and the reasoning rather than from sources: I don't want a reading list or a bibliography, and I'll handle names and dates separately. Three things. First, could you reconstruct the diagnosis at the level of detail Lewis's framework actually demands — what the recurrent situation is on that ward or in that theatre, who the relevant population is, what the candidate regularities are, and which expectations and conditional preferences would have to hold for the old pattern of silence to qualify as a convention in Lewis's sense at all, and for a new pattern to displace it? Second, Lewis insists conventions are arbitrary, in that a different regularity could have been the convention instead; my draft currently leans on that to argue that once a system moves the payoffs and broadcasts the move, the population should shift to the new equilibrium fairly briskly, because nothing in the old pattern gives it any intrinsic hold. Is that the right inference to draw from arbitrariness, and if not, what does arbitrariness license me to say about how fast and how hard such a shift is? Third, how much explanatory weight should the piece put on common knowledge? I'm tempted to write that the campaign failed chiefly because a poster and a handbook clause cannot generate common knowledge of changed expectations, but I suspect I'm using that notion far more loosely than Lewis would permit, and I'd like to know what would have to be true for common knowledge to be the binding constraint here rather than a plausible-sounding decoration. If any part of that architecture will not hold the weight I'm putting on it, I would much rather hear it from you now than from a reader's letter after publication.
