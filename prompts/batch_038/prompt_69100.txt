I sit on the curatorial advisory panel of a mid-sized natural history museum, and I need a decision memo I can circulate to the other seven members by Tuesday, ahead of Thursday's meeting.

We are rebuilding the permanent deep-time gallery. The timeline is fabricated rather than printed — routed into a stone-and-enamel wall, with the Phanerozoic compressed and the last few millennia blown up in a terminal alcove at eye level — so relabeling later is a construction project, not a reprint. The panel must decide what that terminal segment says. I've been asked to draft the recommendation.

The state of play, as I understand it: the working group of stratigraphers that spent well over a decade on the question proposed a formal Anthropocene epoch beginning in 1950, with a Canadian lake's sediments as the type section and the global fallout signature from thermonuclear weapons testing — the plutonium spike — as the marker. In 2024 that proposal was voted down by the subcommission that received it, reportedly on grounds having to do with how short and shallow the relevant record is and how recent the proposed boundary falls; the parent bodies then confirmed the rejection while noting that the term would remain in use as a descriptor of human impact on the Earth system. Meanwhile the Holocene is still the formally ratified epoch we live in, subdivided some years back into three ages, the current one of which began a little over four thousand years ago.

Half my panel reads the 2024 vote as geology's considered verdict that there is no such thing as the Anthropocene, and thinks putting the word on our wall would mean endorsing a failed campaign. The other half reads it as a technical dispute about where to drive the spike: the claim that humans have become a planetary force was never at issue, they say. Both sides are confident, neither will cite anything specific, and I cannot tell how much of the disagreement is verbal.

Two letters we received sharpen it. A Quaternary stratigrapher argues that a museum timeline is where the public learns what a geological unit *is*, and that printing an unratified epoch teaches them that such units are conferred by persuasive advocacy rather than by an identified marker in a type section. A historian argues that any date we print is a Schelling point with a causal story attached: 1950 attributes the change to humanity generically and locates it in the postwar industrial West, whereas the colonial-era alternative — a documented dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610, tied to the demographic collapse in the Americas and subsequent reforestation — was proposed precisely to contest that framing, and critics who prefer other coinages say a neutral-looking date is doing ideological work.

Having spent a lot of years on rationalist forums, I recognize the failure mode here: a motte-and-bailey in which the motte is "human activity now moves more sediment and carbon than natural processes do" and the bailey is "the timescale has been updated to say so," plus its mirror image, in which a failed boundary proposal gets retrofitted into a refutation of the descriptive claim. I'd rather the memo not fall into either.

The options as I see them: (i) follow the ratified chart exactly, Holocene and current age, with the Anthropocene confined to interpretive text; (ii) print "Anthropocene, 1950–present" as a unit label alongside the formal ones, treating the working group's boundary as the best available even though it wasn't ratified; (iii) print "Anthropocene" with no date, as a condition rather than an epoch; (iv) print "Anthropocene" with the popular late-eighteenth-century start, since that is what visitors have read elsewhere; (v) something else you can construct, provided a fabrication shop can build it and a volunteer docent can explain it without notes.

I lean toward (iii), but I worry it reads as fence-sitting, and the chair wants a date on the wall because visitors ask for one.

Please deliver a memo with: a bottom line in the first paragraph; exact proposed wording for the terminal label plus an interpretive panel of roughly one hundred words that a bright twelve-year-old can follow and that a stratigrapher would not consider misleading; the reasoning, including what the 2024 vote does and does not license, and whether deferring to the ratified chart is the same act as deferring to expert consensus about the Anthropocene — if not, say why not; a policy for how the rest of the gallery should handle competing candidate start dates and on what principle they should be ordered; a revision trigger specifying what would have to happen for us to relabel, and what choices now would make that relabeling cheap; and a closing paragraph stating the strongest case for the option you reject, written so that the panelists holding it would recognize it as their own.

No bibliography — nobody on this panel will read one. But if you are unsure of a fact you are relying on (which marker was proposed, who voted what, which dates are live), say so explicitly rather than smoothing it over; I would rather have a flagged uncertainty than a confident number I can't check.
