I write novels. Currently working on one with a character who has pain asymbolia after a stroke — she feels it, can localize it, will tell you it's sharp or burning or a seven out of ten, and then leaves her hand on the radiator to finish a sentence. Not stoic, not brave, she just doesn't mind.

I described that scene to a friend who's a physical therapist and she said "pain is inevitable, suffering is optional" — which I'd always assumed was some Buddhist line, my sister-in-law has it tattooed on her ribs and swears it's ancient — and then she used it to argue my character isn't realistic, on the grounds that nobody actually feels pain without the suffering part, its just an aspiration people repeat. Same week I saw the identical sentence on a poster in a hospital lobby, where it's obviously meant as encouragement to patients. So one sentence doing at least two, maybe three jobs that don't look compatible.

Can you take it apart for me? I want a menu, not a single favored reading you then spend a page defending. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making — what "inevitable" is doing in each (unavoidable in principle? certain to occur? invariant across people?) and what "suffering" is supposed to pick out (the felt distress, the badness of the pain, some second-order reaction to the sensation), and then sort them: which are true but empty, which are substantive claims about how minds work that a case like my character's would actually test, which are false, and which one my friend was leaning on when she called the scene unrealistic. Also tell me whether there's a reading where asymbolia is the counterexample to the slogan and a different reading where it's the best evidence for it, since I need to know which is which before I write chapter nine. And if any of the readings quietly converts into a way of blaming people for their own pain, flag that one too.
