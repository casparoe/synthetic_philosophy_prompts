I am trying to determine whether my behavioral criterion for mentality is jointly satisfiable, and what I want is the principle behind my verdicts, not a survey of the debate. The criterion I keep reaching for: a system has a mind just in case it can convince a suitably competent interlocutor, in unconstrained conversation, that it does.

My verdicts. (i) A system that handles five minutes of questioning flawlessly and then begins repeating itself verbatim: no mind — so duration of exposure is doing real work. (ii) A device holding a precomputed table of replies sufficient to match any hour-long exchange, with nothing behind the table: no mind, though its transcript would be indistinguishable from a genuine one; here duration seems beside the point, and something about the provenance of the answers is carrying the weight. (iii) A human actor delivering memorized lines in the same experiment: the performance is evidence of a mind, presumably the actor's — and I cannot explain the difference from (ii) without leaving behavior behind. (iv) A machine that denies being a machine when asked outright and so wins the judges' verdict: cheating, not passing. (v) A machine that announces that it is a machine and then out-argues the panel for an hour: a mind, although it convinced no one of the very proposition the criterion targets. (vi) Specialists taken in by fluent command of their jargon while a layperson sees through the thing in two exchanges: my verdict goes with the layperson, which sits ill with "suitably competent."

Please state the tightest principle, or set of clauses, that delivers all six, identifying which case motivates each clause; rule on two further cases of your own choosing in which those clauses pull against one another; and tell me which of the six is cheapest to surrender and what the criterion becomes without it. If the escape hatches I need — insufficient duration, an unsuitable judge, a degraded channel — render the criterion unfalsifiable, say so plainly and show me where.
