I'm putting together a session for a junior-officer staff course and I want the cadets to argue from what Plato actually decided rather than from vibes. The live proposal from our training command: three compulsory sessions inside basic training where recruits — mostly 18 to 20 — are given enough beer to get properly drunk, a sober NCO watches the whole thing, and there's a debrief afterwards. Two stated aims: practise self-control under pleasure, and screen out anyone who turns nasty or comes apart when drunk.

To set that up I've listed the calls Plato makes about drink across the dialogues:

- in the Laws he runs a thought experiment about a drug that would produce fear, and says it'd be a gift to a lawgiver, because you could test and train courage with it while keeping the test safe and free of serious risk;
- wine turns out to be the mirror image of that drug — it pumps up confidence and audacity — so he treats it as a cheap, comparatively harmless way of testing a man's disposition and of drilling people in holding themselves together amid pleasure, provided it's handled carefully. He even says getting to know a sour-tempered man over a drink beats testing him in a money deal at your own risk;
- but the same book sets age tiers: no wine at all before eighteen, because it's pouring fire on fire in body and soul; moderate wine up to thirty with intoxication flatly ruled out; from forty you're welcome at the symposia, and wine is Dionysus's medicine against the crabbedness of old age;
- also in the Laws: a drinking party is a benefit or a disaster depending almost entirely on whether a sober and competent leader is running it;
- in the Republic the guardians have to stay clear-headed like watchdogs on duty, drunkenness is especially unbecoming in them, and he says he'd go further than Sparta and Crete and copy the Carthaginian rule that no soldier tastes liquor on campaign;
- and in the Symposium thier best man drinks everyone else under the table and is never once seen drunk.

So: predict his verdict on the recruit drills. I want the candidate rules that all of the above are jointly consistent with (I think at least two or three fit), what each one says about this specific case, which single item on the list is the closest precedent and why, and whether the screening aim comes out differently from the training aim. If the rulings genuinely underdetermine it, say which principle we'd be adopting by deciding either way, and name the one further hypothetical you'd put to him to tell the rules apart. Then tell me how confident you are in the verdict you land on, and single out the claim in your answer you're least sure of.
