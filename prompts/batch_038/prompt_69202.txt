I'm TAing a first-year grad seminar on distributive justice — mostly philosophy students plus two economists who keep trying to turn every normative question into a mechanism design problem — and I've been handed the job of writing this week's in-class essay question on luck egalitarianism. 75 minutes, 25 marks, closed book, and honestly citations would be useless even if we allowed them, so I want argument rather than bibliography. For context on where I'm coming from: I arrived at this literature late, after something like fifteen years of reading and arguing on rationalist blogs and forums, where the default moral ontology is basically "sum welfare across agents, done, now go calibrate your priors." So I read the equality-of-what debate with a constant low-grade suspicion that I was being sold a motte-and-bailey. The motte is the completely reasonable claim that nobody should end up worse off through no fault of their own. The bailey is the claim that we can actually seperate what's yours from what the world handed you, and that institutions should track that boundary. I don't want to settle that in the exam, but I do want the question to make the students feel exactly where the pressure is.

Here's the seed I want built around — it's mine, invented for this exam, so please don't try to trace it to a published paper or "correct" it against one. A city runs a single-payer fund. Two residents end up with the same expensive chronic condition. Resident A got it from an activity they took up as a competent adult with full information about the base rates — let's say a decade of high-altitude free climbing plus a job doing roof inspection — and the fund charges them an actuarially-derived surcharge for the rest of their life. Resident B got it from a genetic draw and receives full treatment at no charge. Textbook verdict: option luck versus brute luck, surcharge A, compensate B, everyone goes home. The twist the question has to force them to sit with is twofold. First, A's risk appetite, A's discount rate on their own future health, and A's sense of what makes a life worth living were all installed early: A grew up in a household and a town where physical daring was the only available route to status, and stayed in it partly because leaving would have meant admitting the whole upbringing was a trap. Second, the surcharge revenue is what pays for B's treatment, so the scheme is self-financing only as long as A's disposition keeps being classified as "A's own." I've been calling the general shape of this the recursion worry: every time you say "they chose it," you can ask what made them the kind of person who chooses that, and there's no obvious floor. I'm sure I'm not the first person to notice this and I couldn't tell you who was, which is precisely why the question shouldn't turn on attribution. The essay needs to make students say whether the luck egalitarian can consistently (a) keep the surcharge, (b) identify what the unit is that counts as genuinely chosen rather than as circumstance, and (c) explain what happens to the theory if the answer to (b) turns out to be "the character, all the way down."

So the deliverables: the essay question as it would actually appear on the paper, phrased tightly enough that the case can be read in five minutes; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 organized around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it. Two constraints on the scheme matter a lot to me. One, the line between an A and a B cannot be "names the right people" — a student who never says a single philosopher's name but who correctly explains why the abandonment objection isn't really about harshness, it's about the standing of whoever is entitled to make the claim, should get the marks, while a student who invokes a hypothetical insurance market without explaining what work it's doing should lose them. Two, at least one of the five moves has to be about a cost the student pays whichever way they land: if they dissolve the recursion worry by expanding "circumstance" to swallow character, the theory collapses into straight equality of outcome and stops being distinctively luck-egalitarian; if they contain it by fiat, the boundary starts to look like a bare preference with an academic vocabulary wrapped around it. I want to see them notice the squeeze, not just pick a horn. Also tell me explicitly, after the scheme, which of the five moves a competent-but-unimaginative answer that just recites the brute luck / option luck distinction and then applies it would miss — I want that as a diagnostic for grading. And if there are standard dodges my case invites, fold the preemption into the wording of the question rather than letting twenty-five students spend the whole 75 minutes on them: the two I can already predict are "A was paid a wage premium so they were compensated ex ante" and "a surcharge is just a price, not a punishment, so no claim of desert is involved."
