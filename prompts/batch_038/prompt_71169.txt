Two friends of mine who do lettering got into a long argument I couldn't referee, and it started from a dumb observation of mine: the lowercase a comes in two shapes that seem to share almost nothing -- the one you learn to write as a kid, which is a circle with a straight stroke on the right, and the one in most printed text, which is a closed bowl with an arc over the top. I asked how those are supposed to be the same letter.

Dana said they're the same letter because there's some set of features they share, and that's all "same letter" could ever mean -- if you can't name the features you don't have a category, you have a mood.

Ruth said fine, name them. Dana couldn't. Every feature she offered died on a counterexample: stencil faces break the bowl into pieces, plenty of legitimate a's have no closed counter at all, and there are o's and d's carrying every feature she listed.

Dana said that only shows she hadn't found the right list yet, and that a notion of a-ness you could never get wrong isn't a notion at all -- if everything counts as an a, nothing does.

Ruth said her view isnt that anything counts. She said the two forms are joined by a chain of intermediate letterforms, each one only a hair from its neighbours, and you can literally draw the frames between them. So the relation is real and checkable, just gradual instead of sharp.

Dana said that proves too much: you can draw a smooth chain from a to o as well, so on that account an a is an o, and a relation that makes everything identical to everything isn't identity.

Ruth then said shape is the wrong place to look. What makes a mark an a is that it goes in the a-slot of a system of contrasts that readers actually use -- it's what you write where an a belongs, and what you'd flag as a mistake anywhere else. Shape only matters because it's what switches the right slot on.

Dana said that makes a-ness a fact about readers rather then about letters, so by that account a shape nobody can read is still an a as long as the designer filed it in the a-slot -- and besides, handwriting-recognition software sorts messy scrawls into letters purely by measuring resemblance, with no community and no slots involved, which is her original point all over again.

Neither budged. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, then say who comes out better overall? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are assuming that they shouldn't be.
