I'm a first-year doctoral student, and our weekly methods reading group has me presenting next Thursday on the epistemology of thought experiments in the natural sciences. My handout is organized around the question of how an argument conducted entirely in the imagination could produce information about the world that we did not already have, and my centerpiece case is Galileo's tied-stones argument against the Aristotelian rule of descent, as Salviati runs it in the Two New Sciences. I have reconstructed the case, then generalized it into a schema I'm calling the composition reductio, because I wanted the group to see what the epistemic claim amounts to in general rather than to quarrel about seventeenth-century mechanics. A friend who works on philosophy of physics read the draft and told me the generalization is indefensible: the schema, he says, refutes true theories just as readily as false ones, so whatever it produced in Galileo's case cannot have been knowledge, and the honest conclusion is that thought experiments of this kind tell us nothing we did not already have from observation. I can't tell whether he's right, and I can't locate the step where things go wrong. Here is the reconstruction exactly as it appears on the handout.

(1) Suppose, for reductio, the Aristotelian rule: within a given medium, the natural speed with which a body of a given material descends is fixed by its weight, so that of two such bodies the heavier descends faster. (2) Take a large stone whose natural speed is 8 and a smaller stone of the same material whose natural speed is 4. (3) Bind the two together and release them: the slower must partly retard the faster and the faster partly hasten the slower, so the bound pair descends at some speed strictly between 4 and 8, and therefore more slowly than the large stone alone. (4) But the bound pair is heavier than the large stone alone, since its weight is the sum of the two weights; so by (1) it must descend faster than the large stone alone. (5) (3) and (4) cannot both be true, so (1) is false: the natural speed of descent does not vary with weight. (6) Note what (5) was bought with. No observation was made, no measurement taken, no empirical premise introduced. The only inputs were (1), a target for reductio, together with two claims about parts and wholes: that a whole's weight is the sum of the weights of its parts, and that a whole composed of parts descending at speeds s1 and s2 descends at a speed between s1 and s2. (7) Those two claims are conceptual rather than empirical, since to possess the concepts of weight and of speed is already to grasp that they compose in this way; no course of experience could count as evidence against them. (8) Therefore (5) is known a priori: reasoning alone, operating on the content of the concepts, has refuted a substantive hypothesis about the natural world. (9) Generalizing, any physical theory whose commitments breach the composition constraints stated in (6) can be refuted from the armchair by the same route, and any theory that survives such an attempt has been shown a priori to be compatible with them; so thought experiments of this shape are a genuine source of information about nature, not merely a picturesque restatement of what we already knew from looking. (10) But (9) is too strong, and this is what I cannot fix. The premises contain no restriction to a vacuum, to a single shape, or even — as the move from (1) to (5) quietly drops — to a single material; and if the conclusion is genuinely a priori it inherits that absence, since nothing conceptual can supply an idealization. Run the identical argument on a lead ball and a feather of like shape, or on bodies of different materials, and the schema "refutes" the claim that shape, medium, and material affect descent — a claim that is true, and one Galileo himself accepted, since he allowed that air makes broad, light bodies lag. (11) Nor does composition behave as (7) says it must. Physics is full of wholes whose magnitude falls outside the range spanned by their parts: two identical springs joined end to end are less stiff than either alone, two identical resistors in parallel present less resistance than either, and the mass of a bound nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its constituent nucleons. So (7) cannot be a truth of mere concept, and (6) cannot be a purely conceptual premise either. (12) It follows, my friend says, that either the composition premises are covert empirical idealizations abstracted from ordinary experience with ropes, stacks, and pulleys — in which case (8) and (9) are false and the argument is empirical through and through — or the whole episode is a piece of legerdemain and we should stop calling it a source of knowledge at all.

What I need from you is a diagnosis rather than a survey. Which numbered step do you reject, and is your verdict that the local argument from (1) to (5) is invalid, or that it is valid but rests on a premise that is not what (7) claims it is? If your diagnosis is that some expression changes sense between steps, identify precisely the two readings in play and say which premise requires which one; if instead you think the argument is valid and the question is only which plausible premise is cheapest to surrender, say which and defend the ranking. Then explain why each of (3), (4), (6), and (7) looked entirely unobjectionable as I stated it, and what has to be added to each to make it actually true — marking explicitly whether each addition is empirical or merely a tightening of description. Next, price the two standing accounts of what thought experiments do: the view that a thought experiment is nothing but a compressed argument whose real content lies in tacit empirical generalizations, and the view that it involves a direct, non-sensory apprehension of a law of nature. Take the Galileo case as the test and say what each view can and cannot say about (8) and about the overreach in (10)–(11). Finally, address the restriction question directly: if I patch the premises by adding a vacuum condition and a same-material condition, do I recover a priori status for the conclusion, or have I conceded that the conclusion was empirical all along — and does the patched version still refute (1)? For what it is worth, I would much rather keep the upshot of (9), that a thought experiment can tell me something about the world that is not a repackaging of prior observation, than I would keep (7)'s claim that the composition principles are conceptual; but I want to know exactly what I am paying for that preference and whether it is a coherent position to hold.
