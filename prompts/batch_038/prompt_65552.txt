I'm the best man at my sister's wedding this summer. She's a metaphysician who defends Lewisian modal realism; I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, though twenty years of being lectured at over dinner means I know the vocabulary. Her fiancé teaches high-school chemistry, and between his family and ours, nobody in the room has ever heard of possible worlds. Please write the toast — about four minutes, one that lands as affection rather than as a lecture.

The bind is this. She asked me, half as a joke, to work in the line "in every possible world, it's you," and she would be the first to say it is false on her own view: nobody exists in more than one world, and her counterpart in some other world may never meet anyone remotely like him. She has also told me there is a technicality in her framework that would make the sentence come out true in the empty way — worlds in which she has no counterpart at all — and that a line made true like that is worse than no line. So the toast has to deliver the feeling she was asking for without the sentence, and find a version of the sentiment that is actually true given her view; my half-understanding is that it has to do with the fact that neither of them can be anywhere other than where they are, and that "actual" is just an indexical.

Keep the jargon to whatever a chemist's parents can follow, but make sure roughly a third of the room — her colleagues and former students — can hear that the philosophy is being got exactly right, including one moment they'll recognize and enjoy. Begin with the toast itself, with no preface and no explanation of your choices. Then add a short note separating what your rendering treats as settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, flagging anywhere you have leaned on a disputed reading of the doctrine or taken a liberty for the sake of the room.
