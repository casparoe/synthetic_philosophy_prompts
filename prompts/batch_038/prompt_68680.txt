I'm running a two-hour Saturday workshop on causation for about 18 people — half philosophy undergrads, half law students, and nobody's read anything on omissions beforehand. I want an activity where they run face-first into the problem of causing-by-not-doing before anyone says the phrase "causation by omission" out loud. By the end they should see that the counterfactual test they all instinctively reach for does some real work here, but leaves the case absurdly wide open.

Here's the scenario skeleton to build on: an 84-year-old woman dies alone in her flat during a three-day heatwave. There are six candidates, each of whom failed to do something — the daughter who didn't phone, the neighbour who didn't knock, the care agency that cancelled her home visit, the pharmacist who never flagged the drug interaction, the utility that cut her air conditioning over an unpaid bill, the city that didn't open the cooling centre. Nobody did anything positive. Everyone just didn't.

Build the game around a fixed compensation pot that the group has to divide, with rules that force them to treat at least some of those failures as genuine causes rather than as mere failures of duty. I need: a subgroup setup that makes different tables reach genuinely different splits of the same pot out of the same facts; a bargaining or scoring mechanic that doesn't need money or props I have to buy; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps the exact moments they got stuck onto the underlying issues — why admitting omissions seems to blow up the number of causes, and what has to do the narrowing work if it isn't the causal facts themselves.

Then be honest with me: which of the six will they instinctively rank as "really" a cause and why the game can't prevent that, what predictably derails the thing the first time you run it, and one feature of the philosophical problem the activity flat out can't reproduce, that I'll have to supply by hand.
