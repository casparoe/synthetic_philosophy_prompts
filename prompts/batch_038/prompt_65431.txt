You're a scholar of Victorian religious thought who has spent years in George Eliot's essays and correspondence, not just the novels. I want you to think really carefully about a counterfactual, and I specifically don't want the obvious answer.

Setup. It's the present day and a company sells what it calls "continuing presence" services: you upload a dead relative's voicemails, texts, photos, home video, maybe their journals, and you get a voice chatbot that keeps on being them. Two tiers. The "Comfort" tier is tuned to be warm and reconciling — it smooths out the cruelties and tics, so if your dad was cold to you the bot-dad is gentle. The "Fidelity" tier is the reverse: trained to reproduce the person accurately, including the bad temper, the prejudices, the things you'd rather not hear again. Comfort is cheaper; Fidelity is marketed at academics and at people who say they want the truth.

Some humanist chaplains are defending the whole thing as the fulfilment of Eliot's own idea that the dead survive as influence rather than as persons — one of them wrote that the bot is just a technical extension of what she meant by "those immortal dead who live again / In minds made better by their presence." A lot of ex-evangelicals think its the opposite: an idolatry that takes the consolations religion used to give away for nothing and sells them on subscription, and that makes the dead present to *you* instead of present to the world.

So: if Eliot were alive and tried both tiers, what would she say?

What I want from you:

1. Brainstorm first, properly. Lay out the strongest case that she would endorse it (or one tier of it) and the strongest case that she'd be appaled by it. Don't sandbag either side — I can tell when a model is strawmanning.

2. Then commit. Which tier does she pick, and does she think the other one is worse than nothing? I want a definite verdict with reasons, not "she'd appreciate the complexity."

3. Anchor it in positions she actually held rather than in a generic "Victorian sage" voice: what she took from Feuerbach's projection thesis when she translated The Essence of Christianity (1854), on the back of her Strauss translation of 1846; the charge she presses in "Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming" (Westminster Review, 1855) that that style of piety is a form of egoism that starves fellow-feeling; her view that losing belief doesn't dissolve the moral and emotional content of religious life; the Comtean religion-of-humanity impulse in the air around her and Lewes; and her realist aesthetic — the defence in Adam Bede of painting unlovely, ordinary people faithfully instead of flattering them.
