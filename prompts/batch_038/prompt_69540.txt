Context first, because what I want depends on it. I'm drafting the conceptual-framework section of a bid we're putting in with a city's transport and public-realm people. The awkward part is that whatever theory of conventional obligation we settle on is going to be handed to a mixed room — two moral philosophers, a transport planner, a community organiser, a couple of council officers — who will then have to apply it to cases they've never seen before and land on roughly the same verdicts. So adequacy here has a specific shape: it isn't "true and elegant," it's "operable by non-specialists under time pressure, on novel cases, with decent inter-rater agreement."

What I want from you is the spec, not a survey and not a defence of my favourite view. Write the desiderata: the full list of things any theory of *when a mere social convention binds you morally, and on whom* has to be able to do before I'd let it near that room.

Here's the apparatus I've been using, treat it as given and build against it. Four convention types: **coordination** (which side of the escalator, which side of the road — the value of my complying depends almost entirely on your complying), **positional/queue** (order-of-arrival norms over a scarce good), **signalling** (tipping, forms of address, what you wear to a funeral — where compliance communicates and noncompliance gets read as a statement), and **vestigial** (the practice persists but the expectation has died). Three pressure variables: whether the agent is a member or a visitor, and whether that's binary or graded; whether compliance is backed by *expectations about what others will do* (my plans depend on yours) or only by *approval and disapproval* (nobody's plans depend on it, they just think you're a creep); and whether a breach has a cost anyone can actually locate and point at.

The cases. Each requirement should say which of these it exists to handle.

- **A.** Bus stop, no painted line, one door, ten people in an informal order of arrival. Newcomer walks to the front.
- **B.** Same station, but ten windows open and no wait at all. The queue is now just a shape people happen to be standing in. Newcomer walks to a free window.
- **C.** Red pedestrian light, 3am, nobody in either direction as far as you can see. Cross.
- **D.** Same crossing, midday, you're holding a small child's hand, four adults waiting with you.
- **E.** Restaurant in a country where service is included and staff are salaried — the original rationale for tipping is gone — but half the diners still leave something and staff read it as a verdict on the meal. Visitor leaves nothing. *Same visitor*, separately, leaves 20% because that's what they do at home, and the staff now expect it off the next table.
- **F.** Clinic queue that is officially first-come, where everyone knows that people who greet the receptionist by name go first. Visitor follows the posted rule and waits, and is resented for it.
- **G.** Someone six years in the city, paying taxes, no intention of staying, who jaywalks routinely on the grounds that they never accepted the local arrangement and never will.
- **H.** Two norm entrepreneurs: the person in a jaywalking city who performs exaggerated patience at the green light to make a point, and the first person in a tipping town who refuses publicly and campaigns.
- **I.** A form of address in a workplace that every member of staff still uses out of habit and would be genuinely startled to see dropped, though nobody expects anybody to use it.
- **J.** Locals jaywalk freely and are visibly annoyed when visitors do it.

For each requirement give me: a short label I can say out loud in a workshop; what it demands of the theory; the case or cases behind it; whether it's non-negotiable or whether it's really the taste of one camp dressed up as a criterion; and what the theory loses if I drop it. Be ruthless about that last column — half of what people demand in this area is prejudice, and I'd rather find out which half from you than from a reviewer.

Also tell me which distinctions the theory is not allowed to collapse. I'd start with wronging someone versus doing wrong; having a reason versus having an obligation; the conditions for a norm's existing versus the conditions for its binding *you*; the convention's being a bad convention versus a breach of it being a wrong. Add the ones I've missed — there are at least three.

If you think some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, derive it for me in three or four sentences rather than asserting it. My own suspicion is that the trio of *content fixed locally*, *grounding in a locatable cost or unfairness*, and *the agent's own judgement kept out of it* can't all stand, but I might be wrong and I want the argument either way. That's the part I most want to see. Then say which single requirement, insisted on hardest, would clear the field fastest — and why I might not want to insist on it.

Finally, and briefly, a tenth of the whole thing at most: how the three obvious families score against your list — ground it in harm and cost, ground it in fair play and benefits received, ground it in what compliance and noncompliance express to the people around you. Two or three sentences per family, no more, I'll do the scorecard properly later.

House rules. No references, no reading list, no name-dropping, and please don't invent attributions. If you want to lean on an empirical result — say, the developmental finding that children treat some transgressions as holding even when an authority permits them and treat others as dissolvable by authority, or the evidence that psychopathic and autistic populations pattern differently on that split — describe the finding, don't cite it.
