I sit on our county historical society's committee and we're finally revisiting the old Confederate plaque out at the courthouse — the one the Daughters of the Confederacy put up a century ago. Every time somebody suggests the wording overstates things, the longest-serving member shuts it down with "history is written by the victors," then adds that this particular plaque is the rare account written by the losers, so replacing it is just the victors finally getting their way. I think that's a mess but I cant pick it apart in real time at a meeting. Can you lay out the different precise claims that one proverb could be making — about who controls the records, about what the defeated get to say, about whose account we should distrust, and about whether it's describing how things go or advising us how to read them — and for each say whether it's trivially true, substantive, or just false? Then tell me whether any of those readings actually gets him to his conclusion about our plaque, and whether the way the losing side's version of the war spread through school textbooks, monuments, and public memory in the decades after 1865 is evidence for the proverb or a counterexample to it. End with a line starting "Verdict:" naming in a few words the one reading he's really leaning on.
