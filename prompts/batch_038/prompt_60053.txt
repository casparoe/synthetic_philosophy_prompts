For the better part of fifteen years I have been a regular reader of LessWrong and of the comment threads it inherited from Overcoming Bias, and the argument that has recurred more often there than any other is the coherence argument for expected utility maximization: an agent with incoherent preferences can be pumped, rationality therefore requires being a utility maximizer, and a sufficiently capable artificial agent will accordingly look like one. Every few years the whole thing is relitigated, and the positions people actually take in those threads turn out to be far more varied than the standard summary (accept the theorem, or deny that it applies) allows for. I run a monthly reading group of eight — mostly working software engineers, one of whom maintains that no real organism is transitive and that this is beside the point — and for our next session I want a single-page diagnostic instrument instead of another evening of people asserting labels at one another.

Please write a decision tree that starts from the commitment that the money-pump argument is compelling — a broker who offers a sequence of trades to an agent with cyclic preferences can leave that agent strictly worse off, with the same holdings and less money — and that ends at a named position on what, if anything, the argument establishes. I am thinking six to eight branch nodes and nine to twelve leaves. Every node has to be phrased as a concrete choice situation or as a plain one-sentence claim that a member of my group could answer without knowing which axiom or which camp the answer sorts them into; where a node corresponds to a technical label, give the label in parentheses after the question rather than inside it. The nodes I think belong on the tree, though you may reorder, merge, or supplement them: whether the exploitability must be actual (a broker who really shows up) or merely possible; whether foresight and the standing ability to decline the first trade are resources the argument is entitled to assume away; whether the defect lies in the preference ordering or in the rule by which the agent selects from a menu, given that a chooser who plans ahead can often dodge the pump; whether utilities are permitted to take whole trajectories, policies, or decision problems as their arguments, and what normative force is left in the theorem if they are; whether an agent may rationally accept a small premium at each of indefinitely many steps; whether incoherence is bad because it is exploitable, or because it is something else of which exploitability is merely a symptom; and whether the conclusion is aimed at actual humans, at idealized agents, or at systems we might build.

The leaves must include at minimum: expected utility maximization as a straightforward normative ideal; satisficing or bounded rationality as a positive claim about what rational agents do; the view on which the value of a gamble depends on the shape of its probability distribution and not only on the weighted average of its outcomes; ambiguity-sensitive and imprecise-credence views; non-Archimedean or lexicographic utility; the deflationary view that any behavior whatsoever can be represented as utility maximization once utilities are defined over sufficiently fine-grained objects, so that the theorem carries no normative content; the view that coherence arguments are engineering predictions about goal-directed systems rather than norms binding on agents; and the dynamic-choice view on which pumps indict myopic sequential choice rather than the underlying preferences. For each leaf, one sentence on the commitment its adherents find hardest to swallow. Then, separately: list the cells in the logical space your nodes define that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or simply because nobody has worked it out; and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, since I would rather be told that than have the distinction faked. One combination in particular I cannot place and want addressed explicitly — someone who accepts the pump as showing that transitivity is required, while also holding that any externally observable behavior admits of an expected-utility representation over trajectories, so that incoherence is in principle undetectable from the outside. Please include no bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind; where a position has a standard name, the name is enough.
