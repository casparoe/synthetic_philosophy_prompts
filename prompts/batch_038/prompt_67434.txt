I sit on the programming committee of a regional archaeology museum. Each season we select eight speakers for our public lecture series, deciding by majority vote after discussion. We are not a peer-review panel: our stated purpose is to bring interesting and defensible claims about the human past to a general audience, many of whom encounter pseudohistory online and cannot readily distinguish it from scholarship. Our verdicts over the past four seasons have been as follows.

1. We accepted a talk by the excavator of a well-known prehistoric enclosure, arguing that its main occupation began roughly four centuries earlier than the standard handbook date. She had twelve accelerator mass spectrometry dates on short-lived samples from sealed contexts, gathered across three seasons; she set out the contamination risks herself; and she opened by explaining which of her own earlier claims the new data forced her to abandon.

2. We rejected a talk proposing that shared motifs across widely separated cultures are explained by a single drowned landmass civilization. When a member asked what finding would count against the thesis, the proposer replied that the absence of surviving remains is precisely what the drowning predicts.

3. We accepted an astronomer's debunking of the celestial alignments claimed for a celebrated Neolithic monument, in which he demonstrated that once one allows enough candidate targets in the sky and enough candidate points on the horizon, an impressive alignment can be manufactured at almost any site.

4. We rejected a talk arguing that the accepted chronology of several ancient and medieval states is inflated by many centuries, on the strength of statistical matches between patterns of reign lengths and repeated narrative sequences in the sources. The proposer had submitted to us two seasons earlier; when members objected that his matched sequences were generic narrative types found nearly everywhere, his resubmission added further matching parameters rather than narrowing the claim.

5. We accepted a talk by a researcher with no university affiliation who argued that a set of inscribed plates catalogued in our own collection as Victorian novelties are in fact ancient, on the basis of alloy composition and corrosion stratigraphy. He told us in advance that a modern smelting signature in the metal would refute him, and a second laboratory had replicated the analysis before the talk was given.

6. We rejected a talk on early metallurgy in which the chemical and metallographic analysis was competent and, so far as we could judge, accurately reported, but the conclusion drawn from it was that the techniques had been transmitted to ancient smiths by visitors from another world. No member could identify anything the speaker would have accepted as evidence against that conclusion.

Now this season's proposal. A researcher in archaeometry claims that a group of carved objects recovered from a peat bog — currently catalogued, and displayed by us, as Iron Age — are some two thousand years older, which would place a notational system in our region far earlier than any comparable example. Her evidence is a single radiocarbon date, obtained from the only one of the objects that preserved collagen. The sampling was destructive and the material is gone; the remaining objects are waterlogged and mineralized with no datable fraction; and the landowner has refused further excavation. Nothing resembling replication is therefore available to anyone, now or later. Two features count in her favor. First, she specified her criteria in advance and in writing: she stated that if the collagen fraction showed the contamination signature characteristic of ancient dissolved carbon in bog water, the date would have to be discarded; she ran those checks and they passed; and she has said publicly that a second early date from any other object in the region would confirm her claim, while its continued absence after further work would count against it. Second, three years ago she published an equally early date for a different site, which collapsed when other workers showed that the samples had been consolidated with a modern adhesive; she retracted the paper herself and subsequently co-authored a methods article on consolidant contamination that is now widely used. She has also offered to display her raw instrument data during the talk. One feature counts the other way: she wishes to title it "Two Thousand Years Earlier Than We Thought," and our own case label would have to change if she were right.

Two members have already staked out positions. One holds that we cannot lend the museum's name to a result no one else can check, however exemplary the researcher's conduct. The other holds that if unreplicable results are disqualified we shall never platform anything genuinely new, and that the point of the series is to model what good practice under uncertainty looks like.

Predict which way the committee's own record commits it, and then tell me what I ought to argue for in the meeting. Specifically: state explicitly the candidate rules that account for all six of our past verdicts — I suspect there is more than one, and I want them written out rather than assumed; say what each of those rules yields for the new proposal; identify which precedent is the closest analog and where that analogy begins to strain; and, if the record genuinely underdetermines the decision, say plainly what principle we would be adopting by voting yes and what different principle we would be adopting by voting no. Finally, describe one further hypothetical proposal — realistic, not a strawman — that would have forced these candidate rules apart at some earlier point, so that we would now possess a precedent settling this one.
