I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy working on the foundations of decision theory, and for the past year I have been seconded two days a week to the modelling unit of a national research-funding body. The unit produces the ranked shortlist of large-scale intervention programmes that goes to our scientific advisory committee, and its standard template reduces each programme to a single signed figure: probability-weighted net benefit, computed by summing over a discretised scenario grid of forty cells. The programmes in question — stratospheric aerosol field trials, autonomous ocean remediation fleets, gene-drive vector control — all have impact models in which scenario probabilities decay slowly (roughly like a power law) while payoff magnitudes grow geometrically, and in which the payoffs take both signs. For most programmes the template behaves; for a minority it does not. Refining the grid from forty cells to eighty flips the sign of the bottom line, and so does sorting the scenarios by descending probability instead of by index. In other cases the figure simply grows without bound as the grid is refined. I believe I recognise the pathology: the first is the Pasadena-game situation, where the series of probability-weighted payoffs converges only conditionally, so that its sum is hostage to an arbitrary ordering of terms and there is no expectation to report at all; the second is the St. Petersburg or Altadena situation, where the series diverges outright. Our unit has tried applying a concave transform to payoffs, which repaired some programmes and not others, and I suspect the relevant question is whether the transform renders the series absolutely convergent or merely smaller. There is also an internal convention — call it Rule 4 — requiring that any figure quoted to the committee be reproducible to within ten per cent by two analysts working from the same model with different software defaults, a condition an order-dependent sum can never meet.

What I need is a procedure the unit's analysts can actually run, not an exposition of the literature. They are trained in statistics and economics, not real analysis, they cannot install new software, and each programme assessment gets two working days before it goes into the shortlist paper. The committee's standing instruction is that every programme carries one expected net benefit figure, so the procedure has to tell an analyst, in order: what diagnostic tests to run on a model to establish whether its expectation is admissible (I would want the tests phrased as things one can do to a spreadsheet or a short script — grid-refinement stability, reordering or permutation sensitivity, a check on the sum of the absolute values of the probability-weighted payoffs, truncation-sensitivity curves, and whatever else you think is decisive); what verdict each test outcome licenses, distinguishing sharply between a well-defined finite expectation, a divergent one, a conditionally convergent and hence order-dependent one, an expectation that is finite but so poorly estimated that the point value is meaningless, and cases where the indeterminacy sits in the probabilities rather than in the payoffs; what to publish instead in each inadmissible branch — a bounded-utility figure with the transform disclosed, a truncated expectation with the cutoff stated, an interval spanning the range of permissible orderings, an appeal to stochastic dominance over some class of transforms, an optimism–pessimism weighting, a relative-expected-utility style comparison — together with the conditions under which each substitute is legitimate and the conditions under which it inherits the original defect; and where the procedure stops and hands the case to a named human because the remaining question is one of values or of institutional risk appetite rather than of arithmetic. Please also give the documentation format for a refusal, since telling the committee that a programme has no admissible figure is politically the hardest move the unit makes, and say explicitly how an analyst should respond to the objection that a concave transform makes the problem disappear.

Then run the procedure on three cases, with the numbers worked through rather than described. First, a solar geoengineering field-test programme whose model pairs modest, near-certain regional cooling benefits against small-probability outcomes of both signs — monsoon failure, terminated-shock warming, avoided heat mortality — with payoff magnitudes doubling across successive scenario bands. I should warn you that this is the head of analysis's own favourite programme and that I expect your procedure to block it; give me that result plainly, because a procedure that cannot deliver an unwelcome answer to a powerful person will not survive its first use. Second, two variants of a gene-drive programme for malaria vectors, one self-limiting and one self-propagating, where I suspect that neither variant has an admissible expectation on its own but that the difference between them is well defined; if that is so, the procedure needs a branch for comparative assessment that does not require absolute admissibility, and it needs to say when the comparison is licensed and when it is an artefact of the pairing chosen. Third, an ocean-remediation fleet whose tail is positive and divergent, so that the temptation will be to report an infinite benefit; explain what the procedure does with the ex-post regret objection, since a rule that approves such a programme can be guaranteed to look foolish in retrospect whatever it decided. Finally, mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure — the choice of transform, the choice of truncation point, the tolerance in the stability test, the decision about which reordering is "natural" — and state what the procedure cannot do: which questions it does not answer, which failure modes it will pass as admissible, and why the fixes that work for the St. Petersburg game do not automatically work here.
