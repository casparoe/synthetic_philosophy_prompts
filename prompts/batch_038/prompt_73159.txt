I am writing a term paper for a course on moral progress, and I would like to test a thesis I have not seen stated anywhere in our readings. It runs as follows: the beings who have actually acquired moral standing over the past few centuries track not the reach of sympathy or of impartial reasoning but of administrative legibility — the degree to which some institution can enumerate, register, locate and audit them. On this account the child, the wage labourer, the prisoner and the threatened species entered the moral circle because they first became countable, through birth registers, factory rolls, prison lists and population surveys. Please make the strongest case you can for this thesis, and show what it explains that the familiar expanding-circle story leaves out — above all, why beings we count extremely well (farmed animals, for instance) can remain near the moral margin while beings we cannot count at all (future generations, the dead, wild invertebrates) nevertheless attract a good deal of moral talk. Then confront the thesis with the counter-case you judge hardest for it. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the main claims you advance, and single out the one point of which you are least sure.
