Three-judge panel, breach-of-contract case: liability turns on two separate findings — call them "a duty existed" and "the duty was breached" — and liability is just the conjunction. Vote finding-by-finding and you can get a majority for both findings while a majority votes against liability; vote straight on liability and you get the opposite result. So far that's just the standard discursive/doctrinal paradox. What I actually want a number on is the List–Pettit upshot: roughly, no aggregation rule can simultaneously accept any possible pattern of individual judgments, treat the judges symmetrically, apply the same rule issue-by-issue independently, and guarantee the group's output is complete and consitent. Give me your credence that this impossibility is telling us something substantive about group agency — i.e. that there are real cases where "the group believes p" isn't just shorthand for an aggregate of what the members happen to believe — rather than it being a reductio that just says drop systematicity and live with path dependence.

But decompose it, don't hand me a bare figure. Which sub-questions does the number rest on — whether issue-by-issue independence is independently motivated or just technical convenience, whether the group-agency reading smuggles in a subject that's supposed to do the reasoning, whether the escape routes (a dictator, premise-based voting, restricted domains) already give the deflationist everything they want, whatever else you think belongs — with a rough probability and a line of justification for each, then how they combine into your final number. And tell me which of those the number is most sensitive to, plus what would actually change your mind about that one, given it's not the kind of thing you can go collect data on.
