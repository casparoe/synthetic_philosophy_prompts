So I'm a junior and I run the peer ambassador thing at my school, which means when the exchange students land in September I do a 40 minute orientation on how everyday stuff works here — not the paperwork, the unwritten parts. Last years version went badly. I told them you're expected to tip around 18% at a sit-down restaurant, that you don't cross against the light even when the street is completely empty, that there's an order people stand in at the bus stop even though nobody marks it out, and that you bring something when a family invites you to dinner. A kid from Rome said the crosswalk one is ridiculous if there's no car coming and nobody gets hurt, and a kid from Denmark said she has never tipped in her life and nobody back home thinks she's a bad person, and I couldn't answer either of them except "well, it's expected here," which is a weak answer and everyone in the room knew it. My sponsor's verdict afterwards was that I'd mashed together rules that are actually moral obligations and rules that are just local habits without ever working out which is which, and she said I can redo the session this year if I do the work first.

So — don't explain the philosophy to me, plan the session. I've got 40 minutes, a library room with a whiteboard, about 15 kids aged 15–18 from maybe 8 countries, and honestly half of them are still shaky in English.

Tell me the order the ideas have to go in and why, like what genuinely depends on what. My current plan opens with a list of do's and don'ts and I'm pretty sure that's exactly backwards.

Then pick the one example that should carry the whole thing and say what it has to have and what would wreck it. I was going to use tipping but I worry it's too tangled up with money and with people's actual wages to be a clean case. My candidates: the bus stop order, crossing against the light with no cars, bringing something to dinner, and answering "how are you" with "good" even when it isn't. Tell me which one and which ones I should just cut.

I also have this half-finished sorting thing in my head — some norms are about not hurting people, some are about being fair, some are just about coordinating so things run smoothly — and I can't tell whether that third bucket actually obligates anybody. Help me decide if it's real enough to teach, and if it is, how I explain the free rider point (that if everyone thought the way the kid from Rome did, the crosswalk thing stops working) without it turning into a game theory lecture they zone out of. And I need the honest version for the norms where he was right and it *isn't* morally required — how do I say "you're not a bad person if you skip this one" to a guest without it landing as "so your rules are fake."

Map the misconceptions: the ones they'll walk in with and the ones my session will create if I'm careless. I'm guessing at — it's not a law so it doesn't matter, Americans are just uptight, your norms are the neutral ones and mine are the weird local ones, and I never agreed to your rules so they can't bind me. Say where in the running order each gets headed off, including whichever one I cause myself.

End with something I can ask that shows whether the distinction landed or whether they just got politely quiet. And a short list of what you're leaving out on purpose at this level, plus what I say if someone asks anyway, because someone always asks whether there's a deeper answer.

Two more things. My sponsor wants a handout at the end with the house rules printed on it and I think that undercuts the entire point, so tell me what should actually be on that page. And 4 or 5 of our own students are going abroad for the year, so if there's a version of this about whether your obligations follow you to another country, tell me whether it fits in the 40 minutes or if I should flag it and do it seperately. No reading list or citations pls, I don't have time to read anything and neither do they.
