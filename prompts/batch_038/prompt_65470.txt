I run customer operations at a mid-size energy supplier: about thirty human advisers working off a hardship playbook, plus an automated assistant we switched on in the spring that handles much the same conversations (payment plans, back bills, vulnerability flags). Since then I've been having the same argument every fortnight, from two directions at once. Complaints come in that the bot 'just repeats itself' — a customer says something slightly off-script, like asking whether they can pay next Friday instead, and the thing apologises and cycles back through the same three sentences until someone re-words it — and my QA lead wants more flexibility, more escape hatches, more room for it to improvise. Our compliance signatory argues precisely the opposite: the playbook is there so that two customers in the same circumstances get the same answer and so there's an audit trail behind it, and she'd sooner have a wasp than a maverick. Both of them started using the word sphexishness at me after I mentioned Tinbergen's digger wasp trick at an away-day — you know the one, where the paralysed grasshopper gets nudged a few inches while the wasp is down its burrow, so the wasp comes out, drags it back to the lip, goes down to check again, and the ethologist can keep that loop running for as long as he likes — and I'm now fairly sure neither of them means the same thing by it.

What I actually want is one procedure my team can run, because this decision keeps coming up: every escalated complaint, every playbook revision, every release of the bot, and right now it gets settled by whoever argues hardest. So please write me a repeatable rigidity audit — the questions or tests to run in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each one, where it stops with 'this is fine', where it stops with 'this is broken', and where it stops being a factual question and has to hand the decision to a named human because it's really about how much risk of a wrong-but-flexible outcome we're willing to absorb. Build it out of whatever the idea of sphexishness genuinely gives you rather than out of vibes, and be explicit about which bits of the philosophy you had to keep and which you dropped to make it usable by an analyst who's never read any of it. A few things I specifically want it to handle: the wasp only shows up under repetition, so how many times do we have to re-run the same perturbation before we're entitled to call it rigid, and what do we do when we can't repeat it because the perturbation would harm a real customer; how to tell whether the rigidity is in the agent or in the scaffolding around it (the tool it's been given, the form fields, the IVR menu, the script a human has been handed) or in the tester, whose own repertoire of nudges may be the thing stuck in a loop; how to separate a performer that genuinely can't do otherwise from one that shouldn't, given that some of our rules are deliberately entrenched and are supposed to refuse to look at reasons that would otherwise be relevant; and the caution against judging a system by whether its flexibility looks like the kind we'd expect in a person, since the wasp isn't defective in the wasp's world and maybe neither is our bot in ours. I also want to know whether anything in the free-will side of this — the whole determinism-and-evasion debate the wasp example gets wheeled out for — actually changes what I should test for, or whether it's decoration. If a step is a judgement call wearing a procedure's clothes, say so on the step.

Then run it on three cases of ours. First, the bot's off-script loop above, which recovers as soon as a human re-phrases, generates something like forty contacts a month and has produced two ombudsman referrals. Second — and I suspect this is the one that's going to annoy me — our human advisers who decline a payment break for a customer whose situation isn't in the enumerated list, and who say afterwards that they knew the customer would default: is that sphexishness, is it entrenchment doing exactly the job it was designed to do, or is it a third thing, and what does the procedure tell me to do about it given that 'let them improvise' isn't an option I'm allowed to take to compliance? Third, our dunning letter sequence, a dumb workflow automation everyone assumes is rigid by design and nobody has ever tested: does the audit even apply to it, and if it doesn't, how am I supposed to know that in advance rather than discovering it after a complaint. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure can't do — the forms of rigidity it will miss, the cases where it will cry wolf, and the point at which it's really just telling me what I already wanted to hear. No reading list or sources needed, I'm not writing an essay, I need something I can put in front of two people who don't trust each other and get an hour's work out of it.
