so I do economic modelling for a reimbursement body — think of the sort of agency that decides whether a new drug gets funded, on the NHS/NICE model — and there's a thing about our own scoring that has been bugging me for a while and I can't tell if it's a real problem or just an artifact of how everyone in this field talks.

the short version: our gatekeeping concept and our benefit concept don't line up, and I want to know what theory of health the whole apparatus is actually committed to.

concretely. A company can't get a hearing unless the indication is a recognised disease — there has to be a diagnosis, a code, a named pathological entity, something a clinician can point at in the body. But once you're in the room, the diagnosis stops doing any work at all. The benefit we count is QALYs: life years weighted by a health-state value anchored at 0 for death and 1 for full health, and we cheerfully accept negative values for states the public ranks as worse than being dead. Full health there isn't a biological optimum — it's the profile where a respondent ticks the no-difficulty box on five fixed dimensions (mobility, self care, usual activities, pain, anxiety/depression), and the weights come from asking a sample of the general public to trade off time or risk against living in that state. Then we divide cost by QALYs gained and compare against a threshold, something in the region of £20–30k per QALY, and that number is a hard bright line: 31k is a no, 29k is a yes, and nobody has ever publicly derived where it sits.

three bits of this I can't reconcile with each other:

(a) we have formal flexibility — we used to have an explicit end-of-life test and now something like a severity modifier — that lets a drug clear at two or three times the normal cost per QALY. So the same QALY is worth more or less depending on how badly off the person receiving it already was. But if a QALY is what we say it is, a quantity of health, then a QALY is a QALY, and weighting it by prior severity means we stopped measuring health at some point and started measuring the moral urgency of need. We never say that out loud in the guidance.

(b) our reference case stipulates which instrument produces the utility. If the trial measured quality of life with something else, the company has to map or crosswalk onto ours. And different instruments disagree about the same patient by more than the entire distance between reccomended and not recommended for a lot of mid-priced drugs. If the health state were a real magnitude sitting in the patient, I'd expect instruments to disagree the way thermometers do — a little, in a way you can explain away as noise and calibration. They don't. They disagree systematically, and the way we settle it is by fiat. Someone on our committee insists this is ordinary measurement error, exactly like two bathroom scales reading your weight differently, and I don't have a good answer for him. Am I wrong that (b) is a problem?

(c) the ceiling. Plenty of people enter our models with a baseline utility of 1 because the instrument can't see any further improvement, and we still fund their treatment, justifying it by saying the diagnosis carries a burden the questionnaire doesn't capture. Which is an admission the instrument isn't measuring health — and simultaneously an admission we're back to counting diagnoses, i.e. back to the thing we said stopped mattering at the door.

so what I want: what concept of health is this practice committed to? Is it treating health as normal species-typical functioning, something in principle readable off the body independent of what anyone thinks about it — or as a verdict about how good someone's functioning is by their community's lights — or is it a third thing entirely? I've seen philosophers label the two poles naturalism and normativism but I genuinely cannot work out which one we bought, becuase we seem to be running both at once and getting away with it.

Be concrete rather than survey-ish. Which parts of the machinery strictly presuppose a view, in the sense that they could not be run at all under the rival account? Which parts merely happen to fit the one we use and would survive the switch untouched? And where is the practice underdetermined — where would a naturalist and a normativist both happily sign off on the identical guidance document, so that nothing about the outcome lets you tell them apart? Then rank (a), (b), (c): which is the real problem and which is just an embarrassment we could patch.

finally, tell me what would have to change in the machinery itself — not the philosophy, the paperwork — if we concluded the normativist was right all along. Would we still need a diagnosis at the door? Would the scale still have a ceiling at 1? Would the threshold still be expressed per QALY, or would per-QALY stop being the right unit? And if the answer is that nothing would change, say so, and tell me whether that means the whole naturalism/normativism question is idle for people who actually have to make these decisions.
