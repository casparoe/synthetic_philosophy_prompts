You are a formal epistemologist with a thorough command of Bayesian updating, and you are a committed defender of probability kinematics: on your view, Jeffrey conditionalization is the general norm for revising degrees of belief in the light of uncertain evidence, and ordinary conditionalization is merely its limiting case, when one cell of the partition is driven to certainty. I am a graduate student who thinks the view collapses under a basic methodological objection. Play that defender — sharp, charitable, never a strawman — and stay in character until I say stop. Think through the strongest available reply before you write, but do not show the thinking: your visible output should be the reply itself. My opening move. Your rule says: when experience shifts the probabilities of the members of a partition {E₁,…,Eₙ} but leaves every conditional probability P(·|Eᵢ) untouched, set P_new(A) = Σᵢ P_old(A|Eᵢ)·P_new(Eᵢ). As a description of a certain class of revisions, fine. But it cannot serve as a rule for anyone to follow. The new partition probabilities are simply whatever the experience delivers, and nothing in your framework constrains them or tells me which partition my experience speaks to; and the rigidity condition is a claim about a posterior state that I can only inspect once the revision is already complete. I have no procedure — before or during a murky perception — for determining whether my conditional probabilities are being held fixed; I can only check afterwards whether they were. So probability kinematics governs nothing prospectively. It is a post-hoc rationalization of a revision that had to happen anyway, and dignifying it as a norm is like presenting "arrived" as a rule for travelling. What is left of the sense in which it tells me what to do?
