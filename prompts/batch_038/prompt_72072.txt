I sit on the oversight committee for our hospital's neuromodulation program. Thursday we decide on a pilot that hands patients a controller for adjusting their own deep brain stimulation amplitude. One of the four patients (treatment-resistant OCD, 18 months in) has crept up to just under the safety ceiling and parked there — he's skipping meals and not returning his daughter's calls, but his symptom scores are the best in the cohort, and he says without any confusion or pressured speech that hes never felt better and doesn't want it turned down. Vendor engineering has proposed a locked controller with a clinician-set ceiling, plus an opt-in precommitment feature where the patient sets his own limits while in a state he endorses and the device holds him to them afterwards.

Draft the committee's decision memo: a clear call on the pilot (continue as-is, continue with limits, or suspend) and on the lock specifically, with reasoning the patient's side could actually respond to. I want two things handled head on — whether a preference the device itself produces can count as evidence of what he wants, and whether precommitment is genuine autonomy or just paternalism in nicer clothes. And give the strongest honest case for leaving him in control even if we think he's ruining his life.
