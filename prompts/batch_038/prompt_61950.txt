trying to pin down my view of well-being for a dissertation prospectus and I want to know where it falls apart before my committee tells me. here's the package:

1. Well-being is informed desire satisfaction — your life goes better to the extent that what you would want, having thought it through with full information and clear-headed reflection, is actually the case.
2. Nothing can be good or bad for you independently of your own conative attitudes. No objective goods, no list of flourishing that applies whether or not you'd ever care about it.
3. But well-being has to be experientially accessible — nothing outside the reach of your experience can make your life go better or worse. So the husband whose wife has been secretly unfaithful for twenty years isn't worse off if he never finds out, and what happens to your reputation after your death is no part of how well your life went.
4. I take the experience machine intuition at face value: plugging in really would be worse for you, and I don't buy the status-quo-bias debunking of that intuition.
5. Adaptation cases — the contented slave, sour grapes — are exactly why it has to be informed desires rather than actual ones.
6. Pleasure matters, but only derivatively: it's good because anyone thinking clearly would want it.

Which of these are genuinely in conflict, which only look like they are, and what's the cheapest revision? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what I'd lose by giving it up. I'm not attached to any particular item, I just want to know what each fix costs me.
