Been volunteering at a county historical society for about three years now, mostly typing up descriptions for boxes nobody has opened since the seventies. I've noticed something recurring that I don't have a word for, and I'd like to know whether the people who think about archives for a living have one.

The rule we follow, which I assume is standard everywhere: a collection gets described under whoever made it, and it stays arranged the way they arranged it. Makes sense, you don't want to tear the context apart. But here's what it means in practice.

We hold the papers of one planter family, maybe forty linear feet — ledgers, correspondence, a daybook, deeds. In an 1853 estate inventory, eleven human beings are listed by first name, approximate age, and dollar figure, filed between a mule and a lot of plough irons. Those eleven are the reason a good chunk of that collection matters to anybody alive today. And there is no way to reach them from their side. You can't look a person up. You have to already know the enslaver's surname, pull the inventory, read the whole thing, and then decide whether the "Suky, about 30" in line four is your Suky. The description I'm writing is going to say "estate inventory, 1853" and hang it under the family's name, and I keep feeling like I'm rebuilding the same wall in nicer prose.

More recent case, same shape. School board minutes from the fifties in which a Black community's fight to keep its school survives entirely inside the board's own vocabulary: the petitioners appear as "a group of residents," as "the colored delegation," and one man in full only because he happened to own a hardware store and the board needed to identify him for tax purposes. When I floated re-indexing those minutes under the community's name and the words they used for themselves, two people told me I'd be breaking the rule about keeping the records in their maker's order, and a third told me I'd be putting words into the record's mouth — which is fair, and is part of what's bugging me.

So the pattern I'm after is this. Nothing is missing. No fire, no purge, nobody hid anything, the boxes are in great condition and the cataloguing is honest. But the only handle on the record is the handle the powerful party installed, and the descriptive convention that's supposed to protect the record's integrity is precisely what makes the people inside it unreachable except by way of him. I know there's a lot of talk about gaps and silences in archives, but this isn't a gap — it's a full, well-kept record with the adress written on the wrong side. I've also come across the idea that a group can be wronged because there's no shared vocabulary for what happened to them, which is adjacent, except here the vocabulary exists, it's just filed under somebody else and you have to borrow it to get in.

Is there an established concept for this? I'm not after a bibliography or a reading list, I just want the carving done properly. Tell me what the nearest candidates are, what each one actually covers, and why none of them quite lands on this. And where are the edges — does it still apply if the creator wasn't powerful, just the one holding the pen? Does it lapse once somebody builds a parallel index alongside the original description, or does keeping both change nothing? Does it apply to a church register where the parishioners were perfectly happy to be recorded that way? And if there's no name for it, coin me one and define it tightly enough that I could tell a fellow volunteer when it's happening and when it's just ordinary description doing its job.
