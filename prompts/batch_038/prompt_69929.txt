In discussions of whether pleasure and pain are commensurable, consider four structures for the relationship between them. I state them explicitly so that the comparison does not turn on labels or on anyone's use of a technical term.

(I) Archimedean hedonism. Pleasure and pain are values on a single signed scale with a stable exchange rate: for any amount of pain, some sufficiently large amount of pleasure compensates it, and conversely.

(II) Unbounded exchange rates. There is still one signed scale, and every pair of outcomes remains comparable, but the rate at which pleasure compensates pain rises without limit as the intensity or duration of the pain increases. Any particular instance of suffering is outweighed by some finite quantity of pleasure; the quantity required simply grows without bound.

(III) Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. There is an intensity threshold such that suffering at or above it is not outweighed by any quantity of pleasure, for any number of beings, over any duration. Below the threshold, pains and pleasures trade off additively in the ordinary way.

(IV) Incommensurability. Above some intensity, pleasure and pain are values of different kinds, so the relevant comparisons admit no truth: the outcome containing the severe suffering is neither better than, nor worse than, nor equally good as the outcome containing the vast pleasure.

Compare (III) and (IV) in detail, using (I) and (II) as foils. Address at least the following.

1. Determinacy. (III) delivers a verdict in precisely the cases where (IV) goes silent. Is that determinacy a theoretical asset, and what would have to be true of value for the (III) verdict to be correct rather than stipulated? Conversely, does (IV) actually lose anything, given that both views rule out performing the trade?

2. Scale structure. In what sense, if any, does (III) deny the commensurability of pleasure and pain, and in what sense does it retain a single scale? How does (III) differ from (II): is (II) just (III) with the threshold pushed to infinity, or is the difference substantive? Identify a case in which the two give opposite verdicts, if one exists, and say whether any agent could face it.

3. Locating the cut. Both (III) and (IV) need a threshold, and the difference between just below and just above it is vague. Which view handles that vagueness better? Consider whether (IV) can absorb the borderline region as a region of incomparability without collapsing into (II), and whether (III) is committed to a sharp cut-off that no plausible account of the phenomenology of suffering supports.

4. Distribution and numbers. Take one person's hour of extreme agony against a billion people's hours of mild delight. Where do (III) and (IV) diverge in verdict, and where do they converge in practical recommendation? Then consider the population-ethical upshot: is either view committed to preferring the creation of lives slightly not worth living over the creation of lives that are very good, and is that consequence more or less damaging to (III) than to (IV)?

5. Compensation across time. Suppose a person's own earlier suffering is compensated by that person's own later pleasure. Do (III) and (IV) treat intrapersonal compensation differently from interpersonal compensation? If they treat the two alike, say what that costs them; if not, say what principle licenses the difference.

6. Explanatory power. Which of (III) and (IV) better explains the intuition driving the whole debate, namely that severe suffering has a moral status no quantity of happiness can match? Assess whether that intuition is evidence about the structure of value or evidence about the limits of our imagination in the face of extreme cases, and whether (III) and (IV) are differently exposed to that debunking possibility.

Close by stating whether (III) and (IV) are genuine rivals, notational variants of one another, or answers to different questions; defend the claim and say what would count as evidence against it.
