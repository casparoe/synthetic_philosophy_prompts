I am the TA for a third-year philosophy of mathematics course (mixed advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students). The final was closed-book, four questions in ninety minutes; the question below was worth 30 of the 100 marks and students were advised to spend about twenty-five minutes on it. I am grading a stack of these and want an independent assessment of one answer before I calibrate my marks against the rest of the pile.

**The question as it appeared on the exam**

Question 3 (30 marks).

(a) State the axiom of choice, and state two results that are provably equivalent to it over ZF. [6]

(b) Explain what is meant by the claim that the axiom of choice is independent of ZF. Identify the two results that establish this claim, and say what, if anything, independence shows about whether the axiom of choice is true. [8]

(c) State the Banach–Tarski paradox. Identify the point in the construction at which a choice principle is invoked, and explain why the result does not contradict measure theory. Does the paradox show that the axiom of choice is false? Argue for your answer. [10]

(d) For each of the following, say whether it is provable in ZF alone, provable only with the help of some choice principle (say which), or independent; justify briefly. [6]
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(i) The set of real numbers is uncountable.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(ii) Every vector space has a basis.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(iii) A countable union of countable sets is countable.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(iv) An arbitrary product of compact topological spaces is compact.

**The student's answer**

(a) The axiom of choice says that for any collection of nonempty sets there is a function that selects exactly one element from each member of the collection. Two equivalents over ZF: Zorn's lemma, that if every chain in a partially ordered set has an upper bound then the set has a maximal element; and the well-ordering theorem, that every set can be well-ordered. A third formulation, which I find the clearest: every surjection has a right inverse.

(b) To say that AC is independent of ZF is to say that neither AC nor its negation can be derived from the axioms of ZF, assuming ZF is consistent. Gödel established the first half: he defined the constructible universe L, showed that L is a model of ZF in which AC holds, and so showed that if ZF is consistent then ZF together with AC is consistent. Cohen established the second half by forcing, producing models of ZF in which AC fails, so if ZF is consistent then ZF together with the negation of AC is consistent. What this does not show is that AC has no truth value, or that the question of its truth is somehow dissolved; it shows only that the axioms of ZF are not strong enough to settle it. That is why the dispute about AC is conducted at a different level: people argue about whether AC belongs to the right conception of the set-theoretic universe, and about how much mathematics it buys once adopted.

(c) Banach–Tarski: a solid ball in three-dimensional Euclidean space can be divided into finitely many pieces which, using only rotations and translations, can be reassembled into two solid balls each congruent to the original. The construction runs through the rotation group. One takes two rotations generating a free group on two generators; this group acts on the sphere with only countably many exceptional points, and one selects exactly one point from each orbit of the action. No uniform procedure available in ZF can break the orbits up in that way, so this selection is where the axiom of choice enters; the remainder is bookkeeping, applying the paradoxical decomposition of the free group to redistribute the selected points. The pieces that come out of this are not Lebesgue measurable — they have no volume at all — which is why measure theory is untouched: Lebesgue measure is countably additive only over measurable sets, and no theorem of measure theory says that a ball cannot be rearranged into two balls when the pieces have no measure. So the result is paradoxical only in the loose sense of outrunning our spatial intuitions. But it follows that it supplies no evidence against AC. A theorem of ZFC that shocks us is still a theorem, and since Banach–Tarski contradicts nothing we know to be true, it cannot count against the axioms that imply it. Anyone who rejects AC on the strength of this result is mistaking surprise for refutation.

(d)
(i) Not provable in ZF alone; countable choice is needed. Cantor's diagonal argument shows that there is no bijection between the natural numbers and the reals, but the conclusion that the reals are bigger is a comparison of cardinalities, and comparing cardinalities presupposes that the sets compared can be well-ordered. Well-orderability of the reals is exactly what a choice principle supplies, so the diagonal argument cannot deliver uncountability without one.
(ii) Requires full AC — indeed it is equivalent to it. For an arbitrary vector space one takes the collection of linearly independent subsets, ordered by inclusion, and applies Zorn's lemma.
(iii) Requires countable choice, and this is a point that is often missed. Given countably many countable sets, their union is countable only if an enumeration of each can be selected; ZF guarantees that an enumeration exists for each set but not that the sequence of enumerations exists. Once the enumerations are in hand, the usual dovetailing argument goes through.
(iv) Requires full AC; Tychonoff's theorem for arbitrary products is equivalent to it. The standard proofs run through ultrafilters or through the finite-intersection property, and in the other direction one can recover a choice function for an arbitrary family of nonempty sets from a single point of a suitable product of compact spaces.
