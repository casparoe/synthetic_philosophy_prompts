Bit of background before the actual question: I'm 71, retired after thirty-some years in a solid-state chemistry lab (crystallography mostly, X-ray diffraction, arguing with postdocs about space groups). Since I stopped going in I've been reading philosophy on my own, badly, and twice a month I go to this wine-and-book thing at a neighbour's house. Last Tuesday one of the regulars — Ray, a retired engineer who listens to a lot of philosophy podcasts — announced that "Leibniz was right that no two things in the universe are exactly alike, and its not even philosophy any more, it's physics: Pauli proved it, no two electrons can be in the same state, so everything is uniquely individuated all the way down." Everyone nodded, we moved on to the cheese, and I've been chewing on it ever since, because I spent forty years around electrons and I don't think that sentence means what he thinks it means. I want you to help me sort it out, so: act as a metaphysician who genuinely understands quantum mechanics rather than one who gestures at it, and treat this as the careful job it deserves — brainstorm the space of possible readings first, including the silly ones, before you commit to a list.

The core ask is this. Take the slogan "no two things are exactly alike" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be standing for, running from whatever is true just by definition all the way up to the genuinely radical versions, as a menu I could bring back to Ray rather than a single favoured interpretation. Sort out at least these axes for me: whether it ranges over intrinsic properties only or also over relational ones like position; whether allowing a property like "is this very thing" makes it trivially true, and whether there's a non-trivial version that keeps haecceity-flavoured properties out; whether it's meant to be a necessary truth, an empirical generalisation about our universe, or a methodological rule; and whether the slogan people actually say is the same claim as "if two things share all their properties they're the same thing," since I have a nagging feeling those two aren't quite equivalent but I suspect I might be talking nonsense — tell me straight if I am. Give me a verdict on each reading: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me which reading Ray needs and whether the exclusion principle actually gets him there. I'll put my own hunches on the table so you can correct them: Pauli seems to me to be about state occupancy for identical fermions within a system, not about whether those particles are individuated by their properties; bosons pile happily into one and the same state, which is the whole condensate business, so whatever exclusion gives you it isn't a universal law of distinctness; and, most importantly, my memory of the quantum mechanics is that the state of two identical particles has to be symmetric or antisymmetric under swapping them, which looks to me like a reason to think such particles *lack* distinguishing properties rather than a reason to think they have them. Am I misremembering any of that? Please keep settled physics clearly seperate from contested philosophy of physics, and where it's contested, say it's contested instead of quietly picking a side. If there's a standard ladder of ways two non-identical things can nevertheless be told apart — absolute, relative, weak, whatever the labels turn out to be — set that out too, because I suspect Ray is collapsing all the rungs into one.

Two more things and then I'll stop. First, the philosophers' toy case that made me uneasy: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, symmetrically placed, nothing else in it. Tell me which of your readings that universe satisfies and which it violates, and what Ray would have to add to his slogan to rule it out — and be explicit about whether "each is five miles from the other" helps him or hurts him, since that one cuts both ways as far as I can see. Second, from my own working life: a good crystal is precisely the situation where you have an astronomical number of atoms sitting in identical environments, and nobody in my field ever thought that made them one atom, or even thought it was mysterious. Is that a counterexample to any of the readings, or is it just the difference between "can't be told apart" and "aren't different"? That distinction feels like it's carrying most of the weight in this whole conversation and I'd like it made explicit with a name I can use on Ray. No references, bibliographies or reading lists please — I can't look anything up and I don't much care who published what when — just the arguments. Define any technical term you need, think it through properly, and keep it readable for a scientist who's new to the subject.
