My flatmate N is four years into a dissertation on de re necessity and he uses me as a sounding board, mostly by announcing verdicts at me over breakfast. Current topic: whether a person could have had a different biological origin. Here's the running list I've kept, phrased as answers to questions about me specifically, because he insists on using real people rather than "Sue" and "Sam":

- Could I have come from a different one of my father's sperm — same egg, same mother, same night? No. Emphatic no.
- Same sperm, different egg — either another egg from my mother or an egg from a totally unrelated woman? Also no.
- Suppose someone took a cell from my cheek, put its nucleus into an enucleated donor egg, and gestated the result: an organism with my nuclear genome, carried by a stranger. Not me, and he gets anoyed when I push, because he says I keep trying to make the whole thing collapse into "but the DNA is identical."
- My actual egg and my actual sperm, except they meet in a dish and the embryo goes back into my mother two days later? That's me, no problem whatsoever.
- Same dish, same gametes, except the embryo is frozen and not transferred for eleven years? Still me, just born later.
- Same egg, same sperm, but a stray bit of radiation flips one base pair in the zygote's DNA right after fertilisation, so the genome is one nucleotide off from the one I actually have? Still me.
- A different sperm and a different egg which by wild coincidence fuse into a zygote with exactly the genome I do in fact have? Not me.
- And when I asked what happens if the zygote that became me splits at day three and both twins are born, he said that's a genuine possibility, that I'd be one of the two, and that there's simply no fact about which — and he seemed completely unbothered by that last bit, which I am.

The new case came up because I was reading about mitochondrial donation. Take the version where my mother's egg and my father's sperm are allowed to fertilise normally in the dish, and then the two pronuclei are lifted out of that zygote and dropped into a donor egg that has had its own nucleus removed but kept its healthy mitochondria. So the very gametes that actually made me do meet, exactly as they did; the child who develops carries the nuclear DNA I'd have had plus a few dozen genes of mitochondrial DNA from a stranger; and essentially none of the original zygote's cytoplasm comes along for the ride, since the pronuclei are basically bare. Is that child me? He owes me an answer Thursday and I want to know what to expect before he gives it.

I'm not after a survey of the literature, I roughly know the shape of it. What I want is: your best guess at his verdict on the mitochondrial case, with the reasoning that gets you there; the set of candidate principles that fit every entry in the list above, not just most of them — I keep oscillating between thinking he's a bog-standard origin essentialist, thinking what he actually cares about is the zygote rather than the gametes, and thinking he doesn't believe any of it and is really just making similarity judgements with a heavy weighting on origins, and I suspect there's a fourth option I'm missing that does better than all three of mine; which single further case would best separate the top two contenders; and if you think some of his verdicts are quietly in tension with each other, say which ones and how hard the tension bites, because I would really enjoy winning that argument once.
