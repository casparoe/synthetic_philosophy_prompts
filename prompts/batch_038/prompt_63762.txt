Having retired after thirty years in synchrotron beam diagnostics, I have taken up decision theory in earnest, and I find that I cannot follow the received verdict on the Pasadena game. As I read the setup, a fair coin is tossed until the first head falls on toss n, the payoff being +2^n/n dollars when n is odd and −2^n/n when n is even, so that the terms of the expectation are ±1/n. Taken in the order the game itself supplies, these constitute the alternating harmonic series, whose sum is ln 2. The literature nonetheless pronounces the game to have no expected value at all, on the ground that the series converges only conditionally and that its terms may accordingly be rearranged to yield any number one pleases. I cannot grant that inference. Rearranging the terms alters the order in which I choose to add the payoffs; it does not alter the coin, the stakes, or, one would have thought, the gamble — and in my own field it is entirely routine to evaluate an integral spanning the whole real line by letting symmetric limits run out together, whatever a re-ordering of the pieces might seem to imply. Is there, then, a principled reason why an expectation must be invariant under a re-listing of a gamble's outcomes, rather than fixed by the order in which the gamble delivers them? If so, I should like to understand what entitles a quantity of our own construction to that privilege.
