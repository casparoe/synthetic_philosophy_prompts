I'm TA-ing for an intro philosophy of biology course this semester and I'm leading next week's discussion section, which is built around a screening of Gattaca. Half the section has seen it and half hasn't, but the film's the easy part — the thing I actually need help with is getting the genetics right, because our course unit is on heritability and what it does and doesn't license you to say, and I'm worried the movie gets it wrong in a way that's too easy to wave at vaguely.

So: read Gattaca through the lens of heritability as a technical notion. I want you to be precise about the concepts before you apply them — heritability as a variance ratio rather than a property of an individual, broad vs narrow sense, the fact that an estimate is always relative to a particular population in a particular range of environments, why a high heritability number doesn't license individual-level prediction from genotype, why it says nothing at all about whether two groups differ because of genes, and how all of that sits with reaction norms and genotype-environment interaction. Then tell me where the film is actually in conflict with that picture, and — this is the part I care about — whether its error is the crude one people usually name ("it confuses heritability with genetic destiny") or something more interesting, like the screeners' probability readouts silently assuming an environment that contributes nothing, since predicting an outcome from a genome alone only works if you already know the environment isn't doing any of the work. If it's the more interesting error, say what the institution in the movie has to believe for its numbers to be actionable.

A few specific spots I'd like you to engage: Jerome, the elite-genome former swimmer who wins silver, throws himself in front of a car, and insists he was designed to be the best but wasn't; Irene, who counts as a valid and still has a cardiac risk that bars her from the deep space mission; Lamar's genetically selected son who turned out "not what they were promised"; and the two swimming races between Vincent and Anton, where Vincent's explanation for winning is that he never saved anything for the swim back. Is that last one a non-heritable factor sneaking in, or does the film just relocate the mystery — because if determination and risk tolerance are themselves partly heritable, then Vincent beating his brother with grit might be question-begging rather than a refutation of the screeners. Say which.

Here's my own take, and I want you to stress-test it rather than just go along with it: I think the film isn't really making a genetics claim at all, it's making a claim about institutions that convert a probability into a certainty — the valid/in-valid threshold is doing all the work, and the science is just set dressing. Does that reading survive contact with the scenes, or is it a way of letting the film off the hook for bad science? And is it compatible with the fact that the movie is pretty individualist — one exceptional man beats the system and the system stays completely intact, arguably vindicated by the fact that it usually works? I keep worrying the film endorses the sorting it's supposed to be criticizing.

Two deliverables for the section itself. First, build me a compact numerical example I can put on the board: a trait with heritability around 0.9 in some population where a uniform environmental change shifts everybody's outcome dramatically anyway, and then a second, separate example showing why a high heritability within two groups tells you nothing about a difference between them. Use your own numbers and set them up so a sophomore can follow; don't just hand me a stock illustration. Second, end with an honest paragraph on where the film resists this reading — where pushing heritability onto it distorts what it's doing — and on what the movie adds to the debate that the argument alone doesn't, something about how a statistic comes to feel like a fate.

No need to quote the film word for word and no bibliography or reading list, please — I just need the reasoning. American English throughout, since our style guide is annoying about it.
