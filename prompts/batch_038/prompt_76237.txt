I run an MA module that's half philosophy of science, half AI interpretability, and I need one essay question for the end-of-term paper. 45 minutes in class, closed book, worth 25 marks. The module's whole arc is that the new-mechanist stuff (mechanisms as organised entities and activities, the grain-sensitivity of any decomposition, multiple realisability, Craver's thought that you establish a component's membership in a mechanism by showing it mutually manipulable with the phenomenon) collides head-on with interpretability papers about circuits in transformer LMs, and the collision happens on one question: when is a mechanism found in one system the same mechanism as one found in another? Its the load-bearing question for mechanistic anomaly detection, because you can't flag something as anomalous unless "the normal mechanism" has determinate identity conditions, and I don't think the people building these detectors have ever said what those conditions are.

I want the question built on top of a concrete case the students have to reason with, so that reciting the multiple realisability literature gets them nowhere. Please invent the case and write it out in full as it would appear on the question paper. Here's the shape I had in mind — sharpen it, or change details, if that makes the case generate the philosophical pressure rather than just decorate it:

A fictional lab's technical report claims that three transformer language models all contain a circuit they call the priority gate: a small set of components whose output magnitude determines which of two competing continuation strategies the model adopts, with a fairly sharp threshold. Call them S (small, 140M params), L (large, 7B) and R (same as L but RL-fine-tuned against a reward model). In S and L the gate turns out to be three mid-layer attention heads. Their automated search, applied to R, returns something structurally different: one attention head plus a two-layer MLP, sitting in different layers, with a graded rather than sharp response curve. Benchmark behaviour is essentially unchanged across all three models. The trouble is the ablation numbers: knocking out the identified components costs 9.4% on the benchmark in L but only 1.2% in R. The lab explains this by saying R has a backup gate that partly compensates, so the priority gate is still there — same mechanism, differently implemented. A critic says R has no priority gate at all: the search procedure looks for gate-shaped things and found one, and the tiny ablation effect shows the behaviour in R is produced by a distributed, redundant process instead. The critic also notes that the lab's matching metric compares intervention signatures and is invariant under relabelling of components, so it cannot distinguish "same mechanism, different substrate" from "different mechanism, coincidentally matched signature". The lab wants to deploy the whole thing as an anomaly detector: flag any model in which the gate is missing or deformed.

The question should push students to do at least these things, though work out the phrasing yourself so it reads like a real exam question rather than a checklist: state explicitly the identity conditions on mechanisms their verdict depends on, and say which of the candidate criteria (same causal organisation, same functional role, same intervention signature, same computational algorithm, same origin in training) the lab is actually committed to; decide whether the lab's claim survives the critic's objections, and say what further evidence would discriminate between them; confront the backup-gate move and whether positing an undetected compensating mechanism to save the identity claim is licensed or ad hoc, and what would make it one rather than the other; and then handle the question I most want marks spent on — whether the lab/critic dispute is even a factual disagreement or a choice of decomposition, and whether an anomaly detector whose notion of "the gate" is fixed by the search procedure that finds it can ever return a result that isn't self-validating.
