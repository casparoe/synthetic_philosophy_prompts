Al-Ghazālī's fire-and-cotton example in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* is usually taken to make the point that the connection between what we call a cause and what we call an effect is nothing more than an observed sequence that God produces as a matter of settled custom, and could produce otherwise. I would like a new example constructed along the same lines, together with an analysis of it.

Please devise a case of your own that satisfies all of the following conditions. The regularity at its centre must be (a) quantitative — a measured rate, a dose-response relation, or some functional dependence, rather than a bare "this, then that"; (b) one that we bring about and manipulate ourselves rather than merely observe; and (c) one that can be made to fail by a further ordinary intervention, where the failure involves no miracle and no appeal to divine agency departing from its usual course. Fire and cotton are excluded, as is the familiar appeal to my willing and my hand's rising, and as is any example whose force depends on an unusual act of God. Note that this variety of occasionalism is normally paired with an ontology on which the world's qualities do not persist from one instant to the next but are recreated moment by moment; assume that background if it proves useful.

Then address three questions about the case you have built. First, give the occasionalist description both of what happens when the regularity holds and of what happens when your intervention makes it fail, and set it beside the description a realist about causal powers would give — attending in particular to the three-way distinction the realist has available between a power that was never present, one that has been destroyed, and one that is present but blocked from manifesting. Second, say whether the two descriptions could diverge in anything we might measure, or whether the dispute is confined to how a shared body of regularities is to be described. Third, given that your regularity is captured by an exact mathematical form, explain where the occasionalist locates that exactness, and whether attributing so precisely structured a custom to God concedes the very thing the realist wanted to find in created things themselves.
