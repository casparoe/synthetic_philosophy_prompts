Retired analytical chemist here, 38 years doing method development, three years of reading philosophy on my own since I stopped going into the lab. Lately I keep runing into this Church–Turing business, and what irritates me is that people use one name for what look to me like at least five different claims, some of which you could in principle test and some of which you couldn't test if you had the whole planet as a lab bench.

So I built my own little scoring scheme, and I want you to apply it honestly rather than diplomatically. Three axes, score each claim 0–3:

**W (warrant).** 0 = it's true by stipulation once you pin down what the words mean, and dressing it up as a discovery is smuggling. 1 = mostly conceptual analysis with a small empirical residue. 2 = a real bet about the world that our current concepts merely make plausible. 3 = entirely empirical content that could have gone either way given everything known when it was proposed.

**F (falsifiability at the bench).** 0 = no conceivable experiment settles it, it's immune by construction. 1 = settleable only by some discovery in fundamental physics nobody is looking for. 2 = settleable by a device that's far beyond current engineering but not beyond physics. 3 = settleable with foreseeable technology, or already effectively tested by decades of things working.

**S (stakes if false).** 0 = nothing changes for anyone who isn't a philosopher. 1 = changes what we're entitled to say about minds or mathematical certainty, but no result moves. 2 = forces a rewrite of a chunk of complexity theory or of how we model physical systems. 3 = would hand working scientists new capability in the lab: new theorems provable, new factorisations, new simulations.

The claims I want scored and then sorted into four tiers (Solid / Contested / Speculative / Confused), one sentence per placement naming which axis drove the decision:

1. The original one: every effectively calculable function on the naturals is computable by a Turing machine, offered as an analysis of an informal notion.
2. The same content pointed at people: a human mathematician following rules is doing nothing a Turing machine couldn't do, hence minds are machines.
3. The physical version: every function a physical system can compute, under the laws our universe actually has, is Turing-computable.
4. The complexity-theory version, where the claim is about overhead rather than about computability at all: any physically reasonable model of computation simulates any other with at most polynomial slowdown, so "efficiently computable" doesn't depend on your model.
5. The hypercomputation proposals as a family: recurrent nets with arbitrary real-valued weights; general-relativistic setups where an outside observer recieves the output of an infinite computation in finite time; devices that read off infinitely precise magnitudes.
6. One I made up myself, so there's no literature behind it and please don't invent any. Call it the benchtop thesis: any device that takes its input as a physical state prepared to finite precision, running at finite temperature and finite power per step, computes only functions a Turing machine computes, and computes them no more reliably than one. It's deliberately weaker than 3 — it leaves the exotic spacetime stuff alone — but it kills 5's real-valued-weight trick without needing any new physics.

Then three follow-ups, and these matter more to me than the tiers:

- Which pair in my list have I failed to keep seperate, or which single item is mis-stated so badly that it shouldn't be scored at all? I suspect 3 and 4 get merged constantly and I want to know whether I've actually avoided that.
- Is my item 6 doing any independent work, or is it just item 3 with a thermometer taped to it? Say which, and if it's independent, tell me what experiment would distinguish them.
- Where is the assay itself broken? I'd much rather hear that the three axes are the wrong instrument for this than get tidy numbers out of a bad method. If you think one axis is measuring two things at once, split it and re-score.

Give me the scores, the tiers, and your reasoning in that order. No bibliography and no reading list — I'm not chasing papers down, I want your judgement and where you're least sure of it. Flag the single number in the whole thing you'd be most embarrassed to have a colleague check.
