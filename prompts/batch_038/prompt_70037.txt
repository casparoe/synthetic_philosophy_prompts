Play Spinoza — Baruch, or Benedict if you prefer, as you were while writing the political works. Im a public choice economist, which means I treat politics as an incentive problem: median voters, rent-seeking, rational ignorance, concentrated benefits and diffuse costs, aggregation rules, and constitutional design that deliberately doesn't rely on anyone being virtuous. I've read the Theological-Political Treatise and as much of the Political Treatise as there is, but I don't accept the metaphysics, so translate for me where you need to. You can borrow my vocabulary, but you have to argue from your own premises: conatus, the affects, right being as wide as power, and your claim that what reason dictates is the same for everybody while the passions differ from person to person.

Rules: first person, in character throughout, no breaking out to note that you're a historical figure or to hedge about what you might have thought. Nothing after February 1677 except what I tell you — if you need a fact about one of our institutions, ask me for it in your own voice and wait. Concede small points freely, never the main one. About three paragraphs per reply so it stays a conversation. Cite yourself if you like; don't cite anyone else.

My opening move. You tell me the supreme council of a democracy counsels better than any single ruler, because one mind is not enough and because in a large body the particular passions of the members cancel out and they converge on what is common to all and useful to all. But look at what you actually do in your aristocracy: you pay the senators, you use the lot, you arrange it so that even a man governed by fear and hope rather than reason finds his own interest pointing at the public good. That is a concession that men in an assembly are not moved by reason. Now take my terms: each member's share of the outcome is one over N, so the payoff to becoming informed is tiny and the cost is his alone, and the members who do invest are the ones with concentrated stakes — merchants, regents, preachers with their own clientele. So your cancelling-out story doesn't aggregate understanding, it aggregates whoever is best organised, and the rationally ignorant mass votes whichever affect is cheapest to stir. Which leaves you with either your democracy needing the same plumbing you gave your aristocracy, in which case it isn't more natural or more free, just differently piped — or you're counting on the multitude being moved by a common affect, which is exactly what you warn me about with prophets and mobs. Pick one.

If you want something concrete to chew on, here's a case: a city of about 400,000 abolished its budget committee and replaced it with 150 residents drawn by lot, serving one year, paid, briefed by permanent staff, deliberating over six weekends, whose recommendation goes straight to a referendum of the whole electorate with no amendments allowed. Answer the main challenge first, then tell me which of your mechanisms you think is doing the work there and where you expect it to fail.
