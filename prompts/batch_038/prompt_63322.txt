I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy working on moral responsibility and autonomous systems, and I am trying to stress-test a case I have constructed for a chapter of my dissertation. I would be grateful if you would lay out the most important implications of the case for the responsibility-gap debate — above all, whether the gap here genuinely closes or merely relocates, and what has to be true for one of those verdicts to obtain. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I am interested in the argument, and I would rather you develop distinctions you think the case turns on than survey positions by name.

The case is stipulated in full below, so you need not speculate about any missing detail, and I ask that you treat the stipulations as fixed rather than proposing that the facts would probably be otherwise.

A logistics firm runs an automated system that assigns drivers, loads, routes, and rest intervals. The pipeline is divided among four legally separate entities: a data vendor that curates and labels inputs; a developer that trains and versions the assignment model; an integrator that deploys it against the firm's operational systems; and the firm's own dispatch unit, which receives assignments and executes them. Each entity's contractual duty is discharged. Each monitors a scoped metric — labeling accuracy for the vendor, validation performance and drift on its own benchmark for the developer, uptime and interface conformance for the integrator, on-time delivery for the dispatch unit — and every one of those metrics remains within specification for the entire period in question. No entity has contractual or technical visibility into the joint behaviour of the pipeline; the monitoring regimes are individually sound and mutually blind.

Taken together, the four components systematically compress rest intervals on a subclass of routes characterized by cold weather and tight delivery windows. A driver on such a route falls asleep at the wheel and seriously injures a bystander. A reconstruction conducted afterwards establishes that the failure arises from the interaction of the components and that no component is defective by any standard recognized in its own domain. No party at any of the four entities foresaw, or would have foreseen on reasonable inquiry within its own scope, this failure mode.

Two further stipulations matter. First, the firm's general counsel selected the four-entity division partly on the grounds that an arrangement of this shape reduces expected liability exposure; that consideration was explicit in the internal memorandum proposing the structure, though no particular harm was in view. Second, the firm holds a contractual right to demand an audit of any component at any time. It has never exercised that right, and no statute or regulation requires it to. Dispatch personnel also lack authority to override individual assignments; the only override available to them is to disable the system entirely, which would eliminate a substantial share of the firm's operating margin.

With that fixed, please tell me:

1. Whether this is a responsibility gap in the familiar sense — a case in which no agent meets both the control and the epistemic conditions for blameworthiness — or whether the deliberate structuring of the diffusion supplies the missing element and closes the gap. Say which of the two conditions you think fails, if any, and at which level it fails: the individual contributors, the firm as a collective agent, or the counsel who designed the division.

2. What the most important implications are for the division of labour between criminal and civil responses, and for whether strict or enterprise liability is best understood here as closing a moral gap or as abandoning the attempt to track one.

3. What the most important implications are for the claim that the gap is conceptual rather than merely evidential. In particular, if the reconstruction is available after the fact and identifies the interaction precisely, does the gap shrink to an administrative problem about ex ante monitoring capacity — and if so, what does that do to the more ambitious versions of the gap argument?

4. What the case implies for the propriety of the reactive attitudes: whom it is fitting to resent or to blame, and whether an answer that assigns resentment to the firm as such is a substantive answer or a redescription.

5. Most importantly, which of your conclusions in (1) through (4) survive if we strip out the counsel's stated motive — that is, if the four-entity division was adopted purely for procurement and specialization reasons and the liability-diffusion effect was never contemplated by anyone. Please treat this as a paired-case contrast and tell me explicitly what the higher-order intention contributes, if anything, given that in both variants the same diffuse structure exists, the same scoped monitoring obtains, and the same harm results. If you think the contrast is morally inert, argue for that; if you think it is decisive, explain why a difference at the level of a design choice about organizational form should carry that much weight when the harm itself was unforeseeable in both variants.

Please rank the implications you identify by importance rather than listing them as coordinate, and say briefly why each outranks the considerations you have set aside. Where you think my stipulations quietly beg a question against one side of the debate, flag that instead of working around it.
