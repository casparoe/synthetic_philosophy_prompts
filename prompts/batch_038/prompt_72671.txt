I am auditing a cross-listed graduate seminar that sits awkwardly between a philosophy of science department and a global economic history programme, and this week's session has left me genuinely unsure how to think about the object of inquiry itself. The historians in the room debate the merits of familiar first-order answers — accessible coal seams and New World imports as ecological relief, political fragmentation and competitive state formation, wage-cost structures that made labour-saving machinery profitable, imperial administrative capacity, or sheer contingency — while the philosophers insist that before any of this can be assessed we must know what the explanandum actually is. The question under discussion is a contrastive one: why did sustained, self-reinforcing industrialization begin in Britain in the last third of the eighteenth century rather than in the Yangzi Delta, which by many accounts had comparably commercialized agriculture, dense urban networks, sophisticated inland water transport, and highly developed craft production? Please write an essay on the controversy over whether that question — and its older relative, the question of why the distinctive form of early modern natural philosophy that emerged in Western Europe did not emerge in China despite China's long record of technological achievement — is well posed at all.

To keep the essay from sliding into a survey of the economic history, here is a scaffold you should engage with, refine, or reject. Notice first that the contrastive form admits of at least four distinct regimentations, and that they carry different commitments: (i) an idle-foil reading, on which "rather than the Yangzi Delta" is rhetorical ornament and the substantive question is simply why industrialization happened where and when it did; (ii) a narrow-foil reading, on which the foil determines a contrast space of regions that shared a common causal field around 1750 — advanced, commercialized, urbanized, coal-adjacent — so that a satisfactory answer must cite differences internal to that field; (iii) a wide-foil reading, on which the foil is a civilizational unit, which invites answers pitched at the level of political structure, property regimes, or knowledge institutions; and (iv) a modal reading, on which the question is not really explanatory but asks whether the Yangzi Delta could have industrialized around 1800, which is a claim about causal possibility rather than about actual causes. Then notice several worries that press on these readings. The outcome-selection worry: we choose the foil after knowing who won, and typically pick the most similar candidate that failed, which looks uncomfortably like selecting on the dependent variable. The comparability worry: a unitary island state of some millions and a continental empire of some hundreds of millions may not be tokens of a common type, and a contrastive question seems to require that they be. The null-difference worry: if the honest answer is that the two regions did not differ in any respect relevant to industrialization and the divergence was a matter of luck, does the question have a defective explanandum, a null answer, or a genuine answer of an unfamiliar kind? And the baseline worry: framing a question as the absence of something Europe possessed may treat non-European trajectories as deviations from a European norm rather than as histories in their own right, which is a charge frequently levelled at absence-formulated questions in the history of Chinese science and technology. It may help to test your account against two control cases, one intuitively well posed and one intuitively not: why did the same influenza strain produce catastrophic mortality in some populations and comparatively mild illness in others, versus why did Europe and not Australia dominate the nineteenth-century world economy.

Your deliverable should be an essay of roughly two to three thousand words that takes a stand rather than cataloguing positions. Work through the regimentations and the worries above, saying explicitly which worries are fatal to which reading and which can be absorbed, and finish with a determinate verdict: state whether the question is well posed, ill posed, or well posed only under a specified regimentation, and defend that verdict against the strongest objection you can mount to it. A qualified verdict is acceptable provided it is precise about the qualification — for instance, that one half of the contrast survives while the other is defective, or that the question is well posed only relative to an explicitly stipulated contrast space — but do not end on a note of studied agnosticism or split the difference for its own sake. Please also address a reflexive point I could not settle with the seminar: whether a scholar's favoured first-order answer tends to dictate which regimentation of the question they adopt, and if so, whether that undermines or merely complicates the project of settling well-posedness in advance of the history. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, page references, or verbatim quotations; I have no access to the literature this week and would rather have the argument reconstructed conceptually, so paraphrase any position you draw on and flag as merely illustrative anything you are not certain of. Naming well-known theses or schools of thought is welcome and will help me situate the discussion, but the weight of the essay should rest on your own analysis of what the contrastive form commits one to.
