I'm working through Benjamin Constant's 1819 lecture on ancient and modern liberty, and the passage in which he defines modern liberty has me stuck. Here it is, with some of the list elided:

> First ask yourselves, Gentlemen, what an Englishman, a French-man, and a citizen of the United States of America understand today by the word 'liberty'. For each of them it is the right to be subjected only to the laws, and to be neither arrested, detained, put to death or maltreated in any way by the arbitrary will of one or more individuals. It is the right of everyone to express their opinion, choose a profession and practice it, to dispose of property, and even to abuse it; to come and go without permission, and without having to account for their motives or undertakings. … Finally it is everyone's right to exercise some influence on the administration of the government, either by electing all or particular officials, or through representations, petitions, demands to which the authorities are more or less compelled to pay heed.

Later he summarizes: "The aim of the moderns is the enjoyment of security in private pleasures; and they call liberty the guarantees accorded by institutions to these pleasures."

Three things I want help with. First, what is the phrase "the arbitrary will of one or more individuals" doing in the first sentence — is it characterizing the *manner* of an interference, its *source*, or the absence of a legal check on it, and how much work does the preceding clause "subjected only to the laws" do that this phrase does not? Second, I have been using a rough diagnostic I'll call the substitution test: a conception is republican rather than negative to the degree that it counts as unfreedom an interference that is regular, predictable, and law-bound but issued by a will that need not track the interests of the person interfered with. Does Constant's list pass that test, or does "arbitrary" here just mean "lawless," leaving him a non-interference theorist? Third, the final clause puts something recognizably ancient — influence over administration — inside the modern inventory. Is it meant as one liberty among the others, or as an instrumental guarantee securing the rest, and does Constant's own summary about "guarantees accorded by institutions" settle the question either way? Please also flag anywhere my framing imports distinctions the 1819 text has no use for.
