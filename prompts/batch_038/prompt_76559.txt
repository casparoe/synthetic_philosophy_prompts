So I'm assembling a small zine of philosophy fiction and I need one more piece — something built on philosophical zombies, but not the usual "is my friend secretly a zombie" thought experiment dressed up as a story.

Here's the premise I want you to work from. In this world a clinic chain offers a procedure that permanently removes phenomenal consciousness while leaving behaviour, memory, speech, tastes and personality completely intact. It's marketed to people with chronic pain or unbearable grief: you keep doing everything you currently do, you just won't be home for any of it. Because the outcome is by definition undetectable from the outside — and because whoever walks out the other side reports exaclty what a successful outcome would sound like — there is no test that could ever show the procedure failed, and equally no test that could show it worked. Regulators shrugged and let it be sold as an elective lifestyle service.

The narrator is an outcomes auditor at one of the clinics, the person who signs off for the insurer that a given client's procedure "took." Everyone inside the company knows the job is pure theatre; the reports are filed, the premiums get paid, nobody has ever caught a single failure, and nobody could. Something has to happen that makes the narrator treat the question as a real question anyway — a spouse, a client, a coworker, a whistleblower, whatever you like.

Two constraints that matter more than the plot. First, the story should turn on the fact that no possible observation could settle it, not on somebody finally finding evidence that does — I want the reader to fell the specific dread of a claim that's insulated from every test anyone could run. Second, the narrator has been quietly wondering about themselves, and the ending should leave their own status genuinely open without cheating by tipping the reader off either way.

Aim for something like 1200–1800 words, first person, past tense is fine. Keep the philosophy load-bearing — let it drive what happens — but don't have anyone lecture about qualia and don't name-drop Chalmers or any real philosopher. Also please don't make the clinic cartoonishly sinister; the horror works better if it's a normal, well-run business doing something perfectly legal.

Start straight in with the story. No title, no preface, no note about your choices, and no afterword explaining what it means.
