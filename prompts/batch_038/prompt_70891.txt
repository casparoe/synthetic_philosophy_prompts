**Question (final exam, 25 points).** State Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem: give its assumptions and its conclusion as precisely as you can. Then answer two questions about its philosophical reach. (a) What, if anything, does the theorem establish about utilitarianism? (b) Harsanyi also offered a derivation of an equal-weighted social welfare function from the reasoning of an impartial observer behind a veil of ignorance. What assumptions does that further argument require beyond those of the theorem, and which of them are the contestable ones?

**Student's answer.** Harsanyi's theorem concerns a society of n individuals facing a set of lotteries, that is, probability distributions over outcomes. Suppose each individual's preferences over these lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so that each is representable by an expected utility function u_i unique up to a positive affine transformation, and suppose the social preference over the same lotteries likewise satisfies those axioms, so that it is representable by an expected utility function W, also unique up to positive affine transformation. Suppose finally that the social preference respects Pareto indifference: whenever every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, society is indifferent between them as well. Then W is a weighted sum of the individual utilities, W = a_1u_1 + ... + a_nu_n, with each a_i strictly positive and the weights unique up to a common positive multiple.

The theorem escapes Arrow's impossibility because Arrow confines the social welfare function to ordinal preference information and rules out interpersonal comparison, whereas Harsanyi works with cardinal utilities over lotteries. It also shows that interpersonal comparisons are achievable without any ethical assumption whatsoever: the linear form is forced by the Pareto condition together with the individual and social expected-utility representations, and nothing has been used beyond the rationality of each agent and of the society. That is the answer to the complaint that the theorem merely presupposes comparability.

On (a): the theorem does not by itself establish utilitarianism in any full-blooded sense. The weights are not fixed by the Pareto condition — nothing in the theorem says they must be equal — so the equal-weight version requires a further symmetry assumption, which Harsanyi justified ethically rather than derived. And the quantities being aggregated measure preference satisfaction, not well-being or happiness; so even the equal-weight version yields only the maximization of total preference satisfaction, which is utilitarian just in case preferences are good guides to welfare. Still, once the weights are equal, the social ordering is exactly the ordering by the sum of individual utilities, and that is the characteristic utilitarian claim.

On (b): the veil-of-ignorance argument is essentially Rawls's original position with expected-utility maximizers placed in it. An impartial observer does not know which person he will turn out to be, assigns each person an equal chance of being him, and maximizes expected utility. He therefore ranks lotteries by average utility, so the weights come out equal and the conclusion of the theorem is recovered by decision theory rather than by axiom. Since the observer's calculation is a matter of arithmetic, the only assumption genuinely open to contest is whether equal probabilities are the right ones; Rawls's maximin rule is simply irrational by the theorem's own lights, and so the original position yields utilitarianism after all.

Finally, because the aggregation is over lotteries it applies at every stage of the decision: once the state of the world is known, the same weighted sum ranks the outcomes that actually obtain. The theorem therefore delivers an ex post rule and not merely an ex ante one.
