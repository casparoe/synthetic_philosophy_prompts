I'm a third-year PhD student in political science, mostly on the policy side, and I audit a political economy reading group over in the econ department. Last week we worked through a stylized case one of the participants had put together: a midwestern state weighing a package — property tax abatement, site prep, expedited permitting, and a direct grant — to land a battery plant that would otherwise go to a neighboring state, where the package is worth something like 40 percent of the present value of the payroll and sales taxes the plant would generate over twenty years. Afterwards two people in the group, Noor (a fifth-year in econ) and Gabe (a fellow over at the law school), got into a long argument about whether this is a real multipolar trap / race to the bottom, and whether the states involved are doing anything irrational or wrong. I wasn't taking sides, I was mostly just trying to keep track, and I typed the exchange up afterwards because I couldn't tell who had actually won what. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Gabe said it's a textbook race to the bottom: nationally, where the plant sits is close to fixed-sum, so the incentives just shuffle the plant across a line while every state pays for the shuffle; each governor's dominant strategy is to bid, and the equilibrium leaves all of them worse off than a mutual no-bidding agreement would, even though every one of them would sign that agreement tomorrow. (2) Noor said that's sloppy — the bidding is an auction, and auctions don't destroy value, they transfer it. The firm captures some of the surplus, sure, but the winning state can still come out ahead as long as the local spillovers (wages, suppliers, tax base) exceed what it paid. Calling that a trap just confuses losing an auction with being exploited. (3) Gabe said the auction analogy fails on exactly the point that matters: in a normal auction the losers pay nothing, but here every state spends real money on site prep, consultants, and deal costs whether or not it wins, and that spending is pure dissapation, the same as in rent-seeking contests. Add that firms re-shop the deal after it's announced and play the states off against each other, and whatever surplus the winner thought it captured gets competed away. (4) Noor granted some dissipation but said it doesn't make the individual state irrational: if the plant is worth X locally and the package costs 0.8X, bidding is correct even though everyone bidding makes everyone worse off. She said its the definition of a coordination failure, which means the criticism should be aimed at the missing coordinating institution rather than at the state — and such institutions demonstrably exist, since the EU prohibits this kind of selective aid by member states and actually enforces the prohibition. (5) Gabe said the EU example proves his side: it works there because of a supranational commission and court that can order the money clawed back, plus structural funds that compensate the poorer members for giving up the bidding weapon, and nothing comparable exists between US states. He added that "wait for an institution" is precisely the move that lets traps persist indefinitely, since the institution has to be built by the same actors making the same individually rational choices, and he claimed there's a unilateral option with real bite — a state that publicly and legally commits to never bidding builds a reputation for dealing on fundamentals, and the reputation is credible exactly because it's costly. (6) Noor said the reputation strategy is only available to a state with enough market access and amenity value to attract firms without writing checks, so it's a luxury position that can't be generalized (if the low-amenity states refuse too, they just get nothing), and that one state's refusal doesn't lower anyone else's bidding, so the externality is untouched. She said the virtuous-opt-out idea is a feel-good move with no mechanism behind it.

Please adjudicate this move by move instead of giving me a global verdict up front.
