Please describe the most important implications of Scanlon's contractualist rejection of aggregation for a public health authority choosing between two uses of a fixed budget. The first option is a screening programme that imposes a small levy on every taxpayer and prevents a handful of premature deaths each year; the second is a broad preventive campaign that gives almost everyone a large number of individually trivial benefits — a few extra symptom-free days — while preventing no deaths at all. Work through what the contractualist procedure of comparing the strongest individual complaint on each side actually licenses in this case, and then address three further points. First, does the conclusion survive the fact that the deaths prevented are statistical rather than identified, so that no particular person can point to the programme as the thing that saves them, and if not, what kind of complaint do the people exposed to the residual risk hold? Second, may the authority still justify its decision by ordinary cost-per-QALY reasoning, and if not, what can it say to the taxpayers who bear the levy and to the relatives of those who would have been saved by the alternative campaign? Third, does anything change if the trivial benefits fall disproportionately on people who are already considerably worse off than those who would die — that is, does contractualism's insistence on addressing each person separately let distributive facts do work that aggregation was supposed to do?
