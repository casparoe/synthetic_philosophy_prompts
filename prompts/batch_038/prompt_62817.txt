I run a small reading group on the bootstrapping/easy knowledge stuff — me and two grad students, roughly 18 months before one of them has to go on the market, no lab, no grants, purely conceptual work. Everyone in the group already knows the standard setup cold: Cohen's basic-knowledge structure and the table-looks-red case, Vogel's gas gauge and why bootstrapping is supposed to be absurd there, the tension between closure and the no-easy-knowledge intuition, plus the usual moves (deny basic knowledge, deny closure, add a no-defeater clause, go pragmatic about warrant). So please don't recap any of that, and don't give me a bibliography — I've got the stack of papers, what I want is judgement about where to point 18 months of three people's attention.

Here's the wrinkle that got me interested, and I'd like the shortlist to actually engage with it rather than just stay inside the 2000s literature. Our target cases aren't ones where the source's reliability is a fixed independent fact about the world that the subject is trying to discover. They're cases where the source's track record is partly manufactured by the loop itself: an instrument whose calibration record was written using the instrument's own earlier readings and then trusted as an independent check; a model that scores its own outputs and whose scores become the labels for the next round of training; a self-reporting patient whose questionnaire answers set the baseline that later answers get compared against; a committee that keeps citing its own past reports as evidence that it was right. Call it endogenous reliability. The bootstrapping worry stops being "can you get warrant for free" and starts being "can a source's apparent reliability be an artifact of the very reasoning that produced it," and I'm not convinced the existing toolbox handles that.

So: give me four or five open questions in this area that are worth the 18 months once endogenous reliability is taken seriously, in priority order. For each one, say what makes it upstream of the others (which questions change their answer depending on how this one goes), whether it's tractable with tools analytic epistemology already has — counterfactual and process-individuation analyses, closure principles, probabilistic modeling of confirmation, the usual defeater machinery — or whether it needs something genuinely new, and what would count as real progress rather than a re-description. Then tell me what you'd deprioritize: where the literature is just spinning, generating variants that nobody's intuitions actually discriminate between. Be explicit about the criteria you're ranking by and the tradeoffs between them, and say what evidence or argument over the next year or two would shuffle the order. If you think one question swamps the other four, say so and defent it rather than padding the list — I care way more about the reasoning than about having five items.
