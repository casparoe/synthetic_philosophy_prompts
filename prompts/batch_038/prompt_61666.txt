The exam question was:

**Q3 (20 points).** (a) State Lewis's Principal Principle as precisely as you can, including whatever proviso the statement needs. (b) It's sometimes objected that the Principle could be made true by stipulation: just define objective chance as whatever an ideally rational agent's credences are, and the Principle stops being a substantive claim. Explain what this objection is driving at and why Lewis thinks the Principle is nonetheless a real constraint rather than a definition. (c) Suppose, with Lewis, that chances supervene Humeanly on the total pattern of particular matters of fact across the whole history of the world, future included. What difficulty does this create for the Principal Principle?

The student's answer:

(a) The principle says: let X be the proposition that the chance, at the relevant time, of A is x, and let E be any body of admissible evidence; then a rational agent's credence in A conditional on X and E together should be exactly x. So Cr(A | XE) = x. The proviso is doing real work. Admissible means roughly that E can't smuggle in information about how the chance process actually comes out — knowing the coin has already landed heads is not admissible evidence bearing on whether it lands heads, whereas knowing the complete history up to the toss, and knowing the chance hypothesis itself, is fine. What the principle accomplishes is to make chance a practical notion: it's the bridge from an objective feature of the world to the degrees of belief that are supposed to guide action, and with the bridge in place, other evidence becomes irrelevant to the agent, since the chance screens off everything else that might bear on A. One thing worth noting is that this doesn't require certainty about the chances. If your evidence leaves you uncertain among competing chance hypotheses you just average over them, so the principle still yields a definite recommendation when you don't know exactly what the chance is.

(b) The objection is that if the Principal Principle were the only thing we knew about chance, we could equally well introduce "chance" as a label for ideal rational credence, and then the principle would be analytic rather than a discovery about the world. Lewis's reply is that chance isn't fixed by that role alone. It's a theoretical posit that also has to earn its place in the best systematization of what actually happens — the laws are the deductive system that best trades off simplicity against strength, and chances enter where the best system puts them. So the principle constrains a notion that is independently pinned down, which is why it's substantive. In effect it's the sharpest available version of the principle of induction: it tells the agent that the world will keep going the way the chances say it will, and that's the whole of our reason for relying on chances rather than on the bare frequencies we've observed so far.

(c) The difficulty is epistemic. If chances depend on the entire mosaic, future included, then which chance hypothesis is true depends on facts that haven't happened yet, so no agent is ever in a position to know the complete chance hypothesis. That means the antecedent of the principle is never actually satisfied for us, and the principle can't be applied in practice even though it's true. Lewis seems aware of the problem, and I think it pushes in the direction of making the chance hypothesis something indexed to the agent's own evidential situation rather than a claim about the world taken as a whole.
