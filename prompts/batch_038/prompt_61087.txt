Consider lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LNU): the view that there is a threshold T in the intensity of suffering such that no quantity of pleasure, however large and however many lives it fills, can outweigh a single episode of suffering at or above T, so that worlds are ranked by a lexically ordered comparison in which above-threshold suffering takes absolute priority over any amount of sub-threshold good. The standard complaint against the view is that this requires pleasure and pain to be incommensurable in a way that is mysterious and unmotivated: LNU simply posits a cliff in the value scale where nothing seems to license one, and then borrows its intuitive appeal from that cliff.

Please argue for the following position. The complaint misidentifies where the lexicon comes from, and LNU's defender should ground it not in a claim about value but in a claim about determinacy. Specifically: intensity comparisons across the threshold — questions of the form "how many episodes of mild pleasure would outweigh this episode of agony?" — have no determinate answer, and once that is granted the lexicon follows as the correct treatment of the indeterminacy rather than as a further substantive postulate. An aggregation rule that treats the comparison as determinate is committed to answering a question that has no answer; a rule that refuses the trade instead treats agony as never determinately outweighable, and therefore never determinately permissibly traded away.

In making the case, please do the following:

1. State the position precisely enough that it could fail. Distinguish the thesis that the relevant cross-threshold comparisons are indeterminate from its neighbours — that they are unknowable to us, that the predicates involved are vague, that pleasure and pain are incomparable in the sense of being different in kind rather than degree — and say which of these LNU needs, which it can do without, and which it should affirmatively reject.

2. Give the derivation explicitly: from "there is no determinate number N of sub-threshold goods that outweighs one above-threshold bad," through whatever bridging principle you rely on (a maximin rule, a prohibition on acting where permissibility is indeterminate, a supervaluationist treatment of the aggregation, or something else), to "above-threshold suffering is lexically prior." Flag every step at which you add a premise not already contained in the indeterminacy claim, and name the added premise as such.

3. Handle the two cases that do the most damage. First: the intuition that a world containing one agony together with a billion blissful lives is better than a world containing one agony and nothing else. Does the position deliver that verdict, contradict it, or leave it indeterminate, and is the result a cost or a vindication? Second: the charge that indeterminacy is symmetric, and therefore licenses the opposite lexicon just as readily — if it is indeterminate whether a billion blissful lives outweigh the agony, it is equally indeterminate whether the agony outweighs them, so why does the lexical priority come out on the side of the suffering rather than the side of the pleasure, or on neither side?

4. Identify the strongest objection you cannot answer, and say what it would take to answer it.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled among people who work on welfare aggregation from what is genuinely contested. Mark which of your premises a competent critic would grant without argument, which they would deny outright, and which remain matters of live dispute even among people who agree on everything else. Do not present the position you are arguing for as the consensus — you are arguing for it, and I want to see exactly where the ground beneath it is soft.

Close with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing the one premise on which the argument most depends: the premise whose falsity would do the greatest damage to the case, stated in a sentence or less.
