Background, since it explains why I'm asking: I ran a trace-metals lab for thirty-odd years — water and soil samples, mostly ICP-MS — and I've been retired eighteen months, and of all the things to fall into headfirst I've fallen into philosophy. I'm auditing an epistemology seminar at the university here and doing far too much of the reading on the bus. The bit that's hooked me is the regress problem, the old foundationalism / coherentism / infinitism three-way. In the lab I always assumed this was a non-problem: a concentration is what it is because the instrument said so, the instrument was calibrated against a standard, the standard traces back to a better standard, eventually you hit a definition and everybody goes home. But the seminar has me half-convinced that the *shape* of that chain is precisely what the three theories are fighting over, and I want to find out whether it's a real fight or three different questions hiding under the one word "justified." So here's the thing I'd like you to build me. One subject, one belief, three scenarios that are as near-identical as you can make them — same person, same content, same stakes, same amount of reasoning on hand — differing only in the structure of the reasons behind the belief. Target is a diagonal. Scenario one: the chain bottoms out in a non-inferential ground, but the belief sits horribly with everything else the subject holds, and only the foundationalist calls it justified. Scenario two: the reasons form a wide mutually-supporting web with no bottom and no loose ends, and only the coherentist calls it justified. Scenario three: there's an endless supply of fresh non-repeating reasons, never a stopping point and never a loop back, and only the infinitist calls it justified. Exactly one "yes" per scenario, a different theory each time.

Constraints. Working-professional and realistic — a lab, a field survey, a bridge inspection, a clinic, a water board, whatever suits you — but no demons, no vats, no sci-fi. You may use ordinary technology, including something like an indefinitely extendable archive or a chain of people handing reasons along, provided nothing in the story is smarter than a shrewd human and nothing is supernatural. Keep each scenario to about 150 words. Crucially, the scenarios have to *generate* the verdicts rather than assert them: don't write "and of course there's no basic belief here," make it true of the situation that there isn't one and let that do the work. After the three, run the check as a nine-cell grid — every theory against every scenario, a couple of sentences each on what that theory's own criterion says and which feature of the case triggers it. And flag anywhere you had to lean on a stipulation to get the verdict you wanted, because that's the cell I care about most.

Then the honest part. My layman's objection to scenario three is that a finite skull can't have walked through infinitely many reasons, and I assume the infinitist answers that walking isn't required, only that the reasons are there to be asked for. Tell me straight whether your scenario three survives that or whether you've quietly smuggled in a traversal — a finite person who has actually collected, or could recite, a lot of them. If you've concluded the third scenario can't be built at all out of a real human being, don't fudge it: say so, give me the closest achievable thing, and tell me what the failure costs infinitism. Finally, pick whichever of the three theories comes out worst across the grid and give me its best counterattack — where it would say I've described the situation unfairly, or that one of my scenarios isn't really the shape I claim it is — and close with your own read on whether the diagonal demonstrates a genuine three-way disagreement about one property or three parties grading different properties under one name.
