Write a poem of four sestets that enacts the multiple drafts model rather than describing it from the outside. The occasion should be a single ordinary perceptual moment — a light you are certain changed color halfway across the room, though nothing you had yet received could have told you it would — and each sestet should give a complete, self-consistent version of what happened, with a different ending from the others. Two of the four must be composed so that they can be transposed without changing a word of the speaker's report, the point being that the order of near-simultaneous events is not a fact available to be gotten right; the remaining two should correspond, without naming them, to the Orwellian and Stalinesque alternatives, memory doctored after the fact versus the moment manufactured before it was ever lived. One hard constraint throughout: no line may serve as an arrival point, a place where the parallel strands converge and the show begins for a single watcher, and the final sestet must not resolve the others so much as add to their number. What the poem should leave a reader holding is the sense that the missing finish line is the argument rather than the loss — that a content becomes conscious as its purchase on speech, prediction, and action spreads across various times and places, never all gathered at once. Someone who knows the debate should be able to identify which sestet is which and why the transposable pair is transposable; someone who doesn't should simply find a good poem about being sure of something one may not have seen.
