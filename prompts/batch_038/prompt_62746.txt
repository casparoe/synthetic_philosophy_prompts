I am drafting the conceptual groundwork for a research program that wants to make claims about long-run moral and political progress — the abolition of slavery, the widening of the franchise, the retreat of cruelty in punishment — in a way that a skeptical historian must take seriously rather than dismiss as Whig interpretation. Before we commit to any account of what progress consists in, I want the specification. Write out the desiderata: what a theory of historical progress must deliver in order to be usable for this purpose.

For each requirement, state the case or consideration motivating it and mark it as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp. Address at least the following, and add any I have missed: whether the account can say in advance what a reversal would look like, or can only recognize improvement after the fact; how it handles the worry that the standards by which we judge the past worse are themselves products of the history being judged; what it owes to those who bore the costs of a process whose aggregate looks like improvement; whether it requires an endpoint or can dispense with one; and what unit of analysis it adopts — humanity, a civilization, an institution — and why that choice is not arbitrary.

Then tell me which single requirement eliminates the most contenders, and assess a conjecture of mine: that the demand for standards not endogenous to the history under evaluation and the demand that the account guide present policy cannot both be met. If some subset of the desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, that is the result I most want. Close with a few sentences on how the broad families of account — progress as the accumulation of solved problems, progress as the enlargement of the circle of moral concern, progress as the unfolding of a direction built into the process itself — fare against the list.
