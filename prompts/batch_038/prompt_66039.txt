I'm three-quarters of the way through a novel and I've hit a wall that's philosophy-shaped, so bear with me. Two of my characters are stuck in a car in a snowstorm for forty pages: she's a hospice chaplain who came to panpsychism late in life and defends it with the ferocity of a convert, and he's a materials engineer who thinks she's just smuggling God back in through the basement window. I want their arguement to actually be good, not the strawman version you get in bad fiction where the smart character only looks smart because nobody answers her.

Here's the specific thing I need. I want to make the case that the combination problem isn't a problem about consciousness at all but a bookkeeping artefact — that it only looks fatal because we assume experience comes in countable units that could in principle be added up, and that once you drop that assumption there's very little left to explain. In my current draft she raises this image I invented, which I've been calling the ledger problem: if every micro-subject keeps its own ledger of experience and the macro-subject just totals them, then my inner life should be flooded with the raw feels of my own liver cells, and it plainly isn't, so panpsychism fails. I want her to turn that around and claim the ledger image is precisely the mistake, that the objection smuggles in the very thing under dispute. And I need her to deal with the old worry that a hundred little minds sitting side by side don't automatically add up to one big mind, rather than just strolling past it.

So: argue that position as hard as you can. I don't want five seperate defences of panpsychism bolted together, I want a single argument that builds move by move across five steps, each one doing work the previous one didn't, and each one phrased so a smart non-philosopher could say it out loud in a freezing car without sounding like she's reading from a textbook. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no intro paragraph and no summary at the end. And don't be precious about hedging — I'd much rather get an overconfident argument I can soften than a cautious one I have to sharpen.
