> Ranke, Carr and White are all routinely described as making claims about objectivity in history. Explain what Ranke took himself to be doing, what Carr took him to be getting wrong, and whether White's account of narrative in historiography radicalises Carr's critique or changes the subject. Be explicit about whether Carr should be counted a relativist.

The answer:

> Ranke's ambition, stated in the preface to his 1824 history of the Latin and Germanic peoples, was to show how things actually were. The historian should suppress his own views and let the documents speak: primary sources, ideally eyewitness accounts, and then a disciplined reconstruction of the past on the basis of what those sources say. Ranke was not simply a naive compiler, though. He also held that each epoch is immediate to God, that is, that every period has its own value and should be understood in its own terms rather than judged against what came after it. That already puts pressure on the idea that Rankean history is a story of progress towards the present, and it is part of why he objected to the philosophical historians who imposed a scheme on the past from outside.
>
> Carr's attack, in his 1961 Trevelyan lectures, is usually summed up as an attack on the fetish of facts. His point is that the past is infinite, that only a tiny fraction of it left any trace, and that what did survive was preserved for reasons that had nothing to do with future historians; the historian then selects from that residue according to what interests him now. So the facts do not speak for themselves — they speak when the historian calls on them, and which facts he calls on is determined by his own situation. Carr concludes that history is a continuous interaction between the historian and his facts, and, because that interaction is always situated in the present, that history is in the end contemporary history: it is what the historian makes of it, and each generation writes the history it needs. This is essentially Croce's position, and it means Carr is a relativist about historical knowledge. There is no standpoint outside the historian's own age from which to judge one account better than another, so objectivity can only ever mean the agreement of historians among themselves. Once you accept that the facts are chosen by the historian according to present interests, it follows that there is no fact of the matter about the past independent of the historian's framing; the past is constituted by the questions put to it.
>
> White goes further. In *Metahistory* he takes a set of nineteenth-century historians and philosophers of history and shows that each of their works can be classified by the kind of plot it tells — romance, tragedy, comedy or satire — together with a matching mode of argument, a mode of ideological implication, and one of the four tropes: metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche and irony. The choice of plot is not determined by the evidence. Nothing in the sources tells you that the French Revolution is a tragedy rather than a satire, and equally competent historians choose differently. Historical writing is therefore a verbal artefact of the same kind as a novel. Since the historian constructs the events he describes in the very act of narrating them, the distinction between history and fiction collapses, and White's position is simply the radical version of Carr's: where Carr says the historian selects the facts, White says the historian makes them, and the whole notion of objectivity is exposed as a rhetorical effect.
>
> So all three are making claims about objectivity, but of quite different kinds. Ranke thinks it is achievable through self-effacement, Carr thinks it can only ever be the historian's situated interaction with an inexhaustible past, and White thinks the question was misconceived from the start because historical writing is literature. White radicalises Carr rather than changing the subject: both are denying that the past itself determines the account, and White only draws out the consequence Carr hesitated to draw.
