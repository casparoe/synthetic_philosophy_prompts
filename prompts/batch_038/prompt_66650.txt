I am writing the framing section of my doctoral chapter on the common prior assumption, and every draft I produce collapses into a summary of two camps that have not moved relative to one another in thirty years. The critics hold that a common prior is a substantive empirical claim about where beliefs come from: since agents have had different information and different lives, their genuine priors differ, so the assumption is not a requirement of rationality but a false idealisation — and Aumann's agreement theorem then rules out persistent disagreement by stipulation rather than by argument. The defenders reply that the prior is no claim about the world at all, but part of the descriptive apparatus, Harsanyi's way of coding what the players know about the game, so that it is neither true nor false and the critics have simply misread the formalism. My own hypothesis, which I would rather have tested than confirmed, is that the two sides are answering different questions — one epistemological, about the justification of belief, one methodological, about what a model represents — and that this is precisely why each argument is met with a shrug. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive disagreement buried inside it: for instance, about whether the assumption is really a coherence requirement capable of failing, as the betting-based characterisations of when a common prior exists suggest? I want the diagnosis argued from what each side actually says rather than a survey, and then an account of what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a reformulation of the question — or of why none should be expected. Please answer in British English.
