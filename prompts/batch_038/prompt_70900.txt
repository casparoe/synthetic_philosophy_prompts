Third time I've taught the Strawson week and third time it's gone flat. Fourteen second-years, one 100-minute slot, chairs that move, a whiteboard, and a postgrad TA I'm happy to use as a stooge. They'll have read the paper beforehand. What happens in seminar is that someone says resentment is just a feeling so the whole issue is psychology not philosophy, everyone nods, and we never get anywhere near the actual mechanics. So this time I want them to do something instead.

What I want them to come out having felt, roughly in this order:

First, that the difference between being excused and being exempted is not a difference in how much causation is involved. I've got a half-formed idea here and I want you to make it workable or tell me it won't: engineer a real small wrong inside the room, something with a bit of bite to it — a put-down delivered in front of the group, somebody's contribution quietly written over — and then hand out two versions of the backstory which contain exactly the same causal facts about how it happened but frame them differently, so one half of the room receives it as an excuse and the other half as a sign the person isn't one of us in the relevant sense. Keep the two halves seperate until the debrief. If it lands, they discover that identical causal detail produced completely different responses depending only on framing, and that's the point made without me saying it.

Second, that holding the objective attitude is easy for a minute and impossible for an hour. So a round where everyone is instructed to deal with their partner purely as a case to be managed — no thanks, no apologies, no visible annoyance, nothing that isn't assessment and handling — and they find within a few minutes that they keep slipping into the forbidden moves and that the task itself starts to feel unbearable.

Third, that their own resentment is data rather than a conclusion drawn from a premise. I want them to catch themselves being moved by considerations that never mentioned determinism once.

Constraints: paper and whiteboard only, no money, no phones. Nothing theoretical read aloud before the debrief, and the phrases reactive attitudes, objective attitude and exemption don't get spoken until near the end. The rules and the cards have to carry it — they should run into the problem before anyone names it.

Two places where I need you to be straight with me rather than optimistic. One is ethics: the first phase depends on a genuine grievance my students didn't consent to, and I'm uneasy about that in a way I can't quite articulate, so please articulate it for me — how much sting the planted wrong can have before the activity stops being defensible, and how to run the reveal so the stooge isn't socially wrecked for the rest of term. The other is the honest note: which feature of Strawson's view this simply can't reproduce, so I know what I have to argue for by mouth afterwards.

Deliverables: full round structure with timings across the 100 minutes; the cards written out verbatim so I can just print them, including the stooge's briefing and both matched backstories; a response measure that isn't a 1–10 how cross are you scale — I'd rather have something behavioural I can tally on the board, though a couple of self-report items alongside is fine; debrief questions each tied to a specific moment in the activity; and a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it plus what to do about it. On that last one please cover at least: someone who flatly refuses to play along, a pair who finish in three minutes and then sit there, someone who gets actually upset rather than experimentally upset, and the case where one of my students has a diagnosis that puts them in the exemption category and takes the second round personally — with fourteen of them I'd bet money it happens and I have no idea how to prepare for it.

No bibliography and no reading list, please. I know where to look.
