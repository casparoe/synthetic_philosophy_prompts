You are an expert in early modern monetary thought — particularly George Berkeley's *The Querist* (first published in 1735, with the later parts following) — and you are also a competent satirist of technical-financial genres. Think carefully before writing: the plan matters as much as the prose, and the humor must come from getting the economics right rather than from any joke about crypto in general.

**The document to produce.** A decentralized project has posted a governance proposal, and a delegate writing under the handle "Querist" has responded not with a comment but with a formal submission in Berkeley's manner: a short "Advertisement by the Delegate," followed by a long sequence of numbered queries. Write that submission.

The project, AUREUS Protocol, is entirely invented (as must everything in your piece be — no real tokens, firms, custodians, exchanges, standards bodies, or living persons). Per its own whitepaper, AUREUS is a stablecoin each unit of which is redeemable for 0.5 grams of vaulted gold held with a custodian in Zurich and a second in Singapore, attested quarterly by an audit firm; it is marketed as the first money with no counterparty risk, on the ground that the bearer holds a claim on metal rather than on a promise; 60% of supply sits staked in the protocol's vault contracts, earning 4% annually paid in newly minted AUR; an oracle network marks the gold price on-chain; the founders hold 15% vesting over four years; and a separate governance token, VOTE, confers proposal rights and no claim on the gold whatever.

**Form, length, and voice.** The Advertisement runs 90–120 words and explains, without irony-signaling, why the delegate offers questions rather than assertions. Then 26–32 numbered queries, roughly 900–1,100 words in total. Every numbered item must be grammatically a question, and the querist must never answer one: the argument is carried entirely by the ordering and by which questions get asked. The diction should be Berkeley's own eighteenth-century idiom with DeFi nouns dropped in unexplained — the vault, the oracle, the stakers, the peg, the vesting schedule. Ban the vocabulary of later macroeconomics: no "velocity," "fiat," "seigniorage," "monetary policy," "counterparty risk" in the querist's own mouth, no equations. Where Berkeley's thought needs a modern concept, the querist must render it in his own idiom — the swiftness with which counters pass from hand to hand, money that lies idle, the treasure of a nation.

**Content.** The queries must actually run Berkeley's arguments, not generic skepticism. Draw on, in your own paraphrase: money useful only so far as it stirs up industry and lets people participate in one another's labour; wealth being the industry of the people, which is what makes even land and silver wealth, neither having value except as means and motives to industry; the proper use of money being to circulate; less money swiftly circulating equivalent to more money slowly circulating; the denominations being retained although the bullion is gone, with things still rated, bought and sold; no virtue in gold or silver other than that they set people at work; a national bank properly meaning a bank in the hands of the public, with no shares, of which the public alone is proprietor and reaps all the benefit; the creating of wants being the likeliest way to produce industry in a people; and the body politic as an animal body that lives and moves under various indispositions, perfect health being seldom found.

**Structure — three movements.** First, the general theory, with AUREUS barely named. Second, the application: the vault, the redemption promise, the boast of no counterparty risk, the yield paid to those who leave their tokens unmoved, the oracle, the audit, the founders' locked allocation, the governance token that claims nothing. In this movement at least two queries must concede something real to the bullionists — that a token redeemable in metal disciplines its issuer, that a promise nobody believes will be honored cannot hold its price — before the querist denies the inference. Third, the turn: the querist's own alternative (a public bank issuing tokens with no shares, secured on the industry of the island) is subjected to the same questions he has been asking, and his own principle that whatever quickens buying and selling enriches a nation commits him to granting that a token backed by nothing but a picture of a dog does more for a country's wealth than a tonne of gold in a Swiss vault. At least one query in this movement must be one the querist plainly cannot answer.

**Two queries from the real *Querist*, quoted only to fix the voice.** Do not lift either into your piece, and do not attribute query numbers to anything, since the numbering differs between editions:

- "Whether there ever was, is, or will be, an industrious nation poor, or an idle rich?"
- "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"

**Process.** Begin with a planning section of no more than eight bullets: which doctrines each movement advances, where you will place the two concessions, which query will be the dog-token query, and which query the querist will be unable to answer. Then write the document. Do not follow it with a gloss, a bibliography, a list of references, or any explanation of the jokes, and never use words like "satire" or "ironic" in the piece itself.

**Bar.** A reader who knows *The Querist* should find the economics right and the satire fair to the bullionist position; a reader who knows DeFi should recognize the design flaws as real ones; a reader who knows neither should still find the document funny.
