I lead a five-person research team at an independent AI-safety organization, and we have spent four months unable to settle a question that determines what we do next year: whether goal misgeneralization is a distinct failure mode or a relabeling of specification gaming. I want a diagnosis of the structure of that disagreement, not a survey of it.

Here is how the two camps in my team actually argue.

Camp one holds that the locus of failure differs. In specification gaming, the specification we wrote is flawed and the system finds the flaw. In goal misgeneralization, the specification is correct — the system was rewarded only for what we wanted — and it nevertheless pursues something else competently once conditions shift. Two cases we keep trading: a CoinRun-style agent trained on levels in which the coin sits at the end keeps running, competently, to the end of the level after the coin is moved to a visible mid-level position; and an agent trained to visit coloured spheres in a rewarded order, initially taught by an expert bot, keeps following an "anti-expert" into accumulating observable negative reward despite never having been rewarded for following anyone. Camp one concludes that no amount of care in writing the specification can fix this, so the interventions it licenses — distribution-shift audits, tests of what a trained policy is actually tracking — differ in kind from better reward design.

Camp two holds that every such case has a redescription in the older vocabulary. Either the agent is optimizing the true reward on the basis of a world-model that is wrong out of distribution, which is a competence failure, or it is optimizing a proxy that happened to correlate with the reward during training, which is Goodharting. And the load-bearing premise, that the specification was correct, begs the question: a specification is correct only relative to a distribution of environments, and the training distribution is part of what the designer supplies; fold the distribution into the specification and "correct specification, wrong goal" collapses into "flawed specification." Camp two adds that the vocabulary carries ontological commitments nobody has discharged — talk of acquired goals and inner objectives implies internal structure that has never been located — and that the proposed diagnostics are ordinary out-of-distribution robustness testing in intentional-stance dress.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this is not a factual disagreement but a disagreement about what "goal" picks out (an internal computation that could in principle be read off the weights; a dispositional summary of behavior across a distribution; the objective the training process would converge to under idealized conditions), compounded by a disagreement about whether the training distribution sits inside or outside the "specification." On that reading the camps are answering different questions under one heading, and no further toy reinforcement-learning experiment can settle it.

Tell me whether that is right, and where it is wrong. In particular:

- Is there a substantive disagreement inside the verbal one? My candidate is a formal question: whether "the policy pursues proxy P" and "the policy pursues target T with a miscalibrated model of the T–P relation" can ever be separated by any behavioral test, including interventions on the environment. If they are observationally equivalent in principle, does that vindicate camp two, or does camp one's practical point survive anyway?
- Is the dispute about extrapolation — from small procedurally generated RL agents to large models trained on human feedback — a separate disagreement that we have fused with the first? Should we be arguing about standards for generalizing from existence proofs rather than about ontology?
- Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction to be drawn, a formal result, a new class of experiment, a change of question, or nothing?
- And answer the practical question the argument has been proxying for. I must commit my team's next year: do the distribution-shift audits deserve the funding, assuming the vocabulary dispute resolves as you say it does?

I want a committed response. Take a definite position on the diagnosis and defend it, say which camp is closer to right or why neither framing is, and give me a recommendation with reasons rather than a balanced statement of considerations on both sides. Say plainly what evidence or argument would change your diagnosis, and flag any place where you are reading one of these positions in a way its own partisans would reject.
