I hold a chair in philosophy at a university where the medical school's neuroscience program and the computer science department have just jointly endowed an annual lecture series, funded by a gift from an AI firm whose founder will sit in the front row at the inaugural event. The occasion is a one-day symposium on von Neumann's last book: the manuscript he prepared for Yale's Silliman Memorial Lectures and never delivered, published posthumously in 1958 as *The Computer and the Brain*. I have been asked to give the opening remarks — ten minutes, roughly twelve hundred words, before the first panel and before coffee.

The room will contain three constituencies with incompatible expectations, and the speech has to satisfy all three without lying to any of them. First, working neuroscientists, who have spent their careers being told by outsiders that von Neumann proved the brain is a computer, and who will walk out if I say it and will roll their eyes if I say he was simply wrong. Second, computer scientists and the donor's people, whose company's stated position is that intelligence is computation and that computation can be realized in anything. Third, undergraduates and alumni who know the name only from popular accounts. There will also be four or five philosophers who will know exactly how much I am smoothing over.

What I want the remarks to be built on is the view von Neumann actually held, which is neither of the two he is usually given credit for. As I read him: he took the nervous system to look, at the level of the neuron, like a digital device — pulses that either occur or do not, with thresholds — while insisting that analog processes are in the mix too, and that neither fact settles what code the system uses. He thought its components carry something like two or three significant digits of precision where the components of the machines he built carry far more; that it runs thousands of times slower than the vacuum-tube machinery of his day and compensates by doing vastly many things at once; that its memory is nothing like a machine's — no fixed addresses, no exact retrieval, but something statistical, layered, and reconstructive, with an enormous ratio of stored material to active elements. Above all he suspected that whatever language the nervous system speaks, it is not arithmetic: that its logical structure may differ in kind from the notation we use to describe a computer, so that the analogy might hold as engineering and fail at the level that matters, and that finding out would require inventing a new formalism rather than applying the old one. He also had the idea that the complication of a thing should be measured by the length of the shortest description of it, that this measure depends on the descriptive scheme adopted, and that below some critical complexity the notion stops being useful.

The remarks must be organized around that last idea, because it is what licenses the constraint I most want enforced: the speech may not assert, or imply, either that the brain is a computer or that it is not. Both formulations are banned. The reason must be audible to the non-philosophers in the room without turning into a lecture, and the words "functionalism," "multiple realizability," and "substrate" are not to appear at all.

Specific requirements:

- Build the whole thing on a single non-computational analogy of your choosing — translation between languages, a map projection, a score and its performance, whatever you judge best — and return to it at least three times, so that it carries the argument rather than decorating it. The room is saturated with computational analogies; the point is that a good analogy is a claim about notation.
- Say one gracious and specific thing to the founder in the front row that a donor would be pleased to hear, without endorsing the company's position on what intelligence is.
- Allude, once, to the fact that von Neumann wrote this from a hospital bed and died before the lectures could be given. That may not be the emotional center, and there is to be no speculation about his state of mind.
- Exactly one joke, and it must land for both the neuroscientists and the AI people. It may not concern his death, his accent, his personality, or any film about him.
- Do not quote him. I will supply any verbatim lines myself from a checked copy. No citations, no bibliography, no named secondary literature, no dates beyond the ones I have given you.

Write it as speakable prose, with the paragraphing that indicates where I should pause. End with a last line beginning "Gist:" followed by a single sentence stating the one claim the talk asks the room to accept.
