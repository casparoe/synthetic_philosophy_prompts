I am preparing a graduate seminar session on the first book of the Politics, and I have run into a disagreement between two colleagues that I cannot settle to my own satisfaction. I would like your help auditing the analogy at the center of it.

The session concerns Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery, and in particular the comparison that appears to carry it. As I read the argument, Aristotle sets up a proportion: as the soul stands to the body, so the natural master stands to the natural slave; and as intellect stands to appetite, so the statesman stands over free and equal citizens who rule and are ruled in turn. The first pairing licenses despotic rule, the second political or kingly rule, and the household that results is held together by instruments that are alive — the slave being a possession prior to other possessions, the kind of tool whose function Aristotle illustrates by saying that if the shuttle wove at a command and the plectrum struck the lyre of its own accord, the master craftsman would have no need of assistants. He adds that nature does not act like the smith who fashioned the Delphian knife for many purposes: she makes each thing for a single use.

Colleague A maintains that this analogy is load-bearing, and that Aristotle's softer interpreters are wrong to treat the natural-slavery passages as a concession to Athenian practice that can be excised from the system. On A's reading, once you grant that foresight and directive capacity belong to one element while bodily execution belongs to another, and once you grant that such a pair has a single interest, the rest follows: some human beings are slaves by nature, and their enslavement is both expedient and just for them. A leans heavily on Aristotle's remark that master and slave have the same interest, arguing that the claim is imported directly from the soul-body case, where the good of the body simply is the good of the whole they compose.

Colleague B maintains that the analogy is expository scaffolding. On B's reading the work is done by two quite different claims: a developmental one about the household arising from pairs that cannot exist without each other, and a psychological one about who possesses the deliberative capacity and who possesses it only so far as to apprehend it in another. B says that if the soul-body comparison were struck from the text the conclusion would survive unaltered, and that the seminar therefore should not spend its hour on it.

A third possibility, raised by one of my students last year, complicates both. Her suggestion is that the comparison is not an argument from similarity at all but a piece of hylomorphism transferred into politics: the household and, behind it, the city are the primary substances, and master and slave are matter and form within them. On that reading the proportion does not assert that one person's relation to another resembles a soul's relation to its body, but that parts of a single whole stand to one another as matter stands to form — and the obvious objection that a slave is a separate human being who could exist perfectly well without a master loses its grip, since the parts of a substance are not separate beings either.

What I want is an audit of the analogy precise enough to decide between A and B. Specifically:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly; a table is welcome. There seem to be at least three comparisons running in parallel — soul over body, intellect over appetite, craftsman over instruments — and I want to know which is doing which piece of work, and whether Aristotle is entitled to treat them as instances of a single form of rule or is sliding between incompatible ones. Note also whether the "one thing, one function" premise transfers from tools to human beings, or whether it is precisely the premise the slave case needs and cannot get.

2. Identify what the soul-body source quietly imports into the master-slave target: inseparability, unity of substance, the absence in the ruled element of any good that is not the ruler's good, the silence of the ruled element, and the further complication that for Aristotle the soul is the form and actuality of the body rather than an agent acting on a patient. Then sort the disanalogies into those that are fatal for the intended use and those that merely look embarrassing from a modern point of view.

3. Assess my student's hylomorphic rescue. If master and slave are matter and form of one substance, what is gained and what is lost — in particular, what becomes of Aristotle's own insistence that the slave shares in reason enough to understand commands though not to originate them, and of his claim that the slave has a virtue of his own? Does the rescue make the analogy coherent, or does it make it coherent at the price of the conclusion A wants?

4. Say whether A's conclusion survives the audit: that there are human beings whom nature has fitted to be the property of others and whose enslavement serves their own good. Please keep this separate from the diagnostic problem Aristotle himself raises — that nothing in the comparison tells you who the natural slaves are, and that the slavery actually practiced around him rested on law and on capture in war, which he concedes may be unjust. I want to know whether the analogy is supposed to supply a criterion of application, and whether it can.

5. Give me your verdict on the A/B dispute: load-bearing, expository, or something else — for instance, load-bearing for the claim that slavery benefits the slave while doing nothing at all for the claim that anyone in particular is a natural slave.

6. If the analogy is defective, propose a better one: a relation in which directive and executive capacities are genuinely asymmetric, the ruled element has no independent good of its own, and yet both parties are the sorts of thing Aristotle's argument requires them to be. If no such relation exists among his own examples, say so plainly and tell me what that shows about the argument.

Please work from the text and from the reconstructions I have given you rather than reaching for the secondary literature; I am not asking for attributions or references, and I would much rather you mark a point as your own conjecture than supply a confident provenance for it. Where my summary of A, B, or the student is itself tendentious or garbled, tell me so — part of what I am after is whether the disagreement as I have framed it is a real one.
