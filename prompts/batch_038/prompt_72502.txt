Four years ago my younger brother was killed, and next month the man convicted of killing him faces a sentencing proceeding in which the state is seeking death. Our family has been asked to say something on the record about whether we support that outcome. I have been trying to prepare, and I find that I cannot get past two sentences that were spoken in my own kitchen last week.

When the discussion turned to what we would say, my mother remarked, without any particular heat, that it was simply an eye for an eye. A chaplain who has been with our family since the funeral answered her with the line that an eye for an eye leaves the whole world blind. My mother has repeated her sentence several times since, as though it settles the matter; I have heard the chaplain's retort in almost those exact words my whole life, also as though it settles the matter. Sitting with both, I have come to suspect that neither is a claim I could actually evaluate, and that the second is not a reply to the first but a change of subject. I would like help establishing whether that suspicion is correct.

Please lay out, as a numbered list I can work through slowly rather than an essay that adopts whichever reading you find most interesting, the distinct precise theses that "an eye for an eye" could be asserting, ordered from the least to the most demanding. I have in mind contrasts such as: a ceiling on retaliation rather than a requirement of it; a proportionality principle that is silent on whether the proportional maximum must ever be imposed; a claim about what an offender deserves as against a claim about what an injured party is entitled to demand; a principle about literal equivalence of harm versus symbolic equivalence of standing; and a rule governing private retaliation versus a rule addressed to an institution that already monopolizes punishment. For each reading I want four things: what it commits one to, whether it is defensible on its own terms, whether it would in fact support a sentence of death in a case like mine, and whether the "whole world blind" reply engages it or talks past it. That last column matters most to me. My impression is that the retort defeats at most one of these readings and is then repeated as though it defeated all of them.

There is a particular difficulty I would like you to take seriously instead of waving away. If the principle is fundamentally about what the injured party is entitled to, then in my case the injured party is dead. Does the entitlement lapse with him, pass to next of kin, or vest in the state? My mother believes it passed to us. My husband believes nobody is owed anything and that the only live questions are whether killing this man will prevent other killings and whether he can be safely contained for the rest of his life. My sister, who has been reading the appellate record, believes that the possibility that the wrong man was convicted makes the whole thing impermissible regardless of what is owed to anyone. Tell me whether these three are contradicting one another about a single proposition, or whether they are making claims that could all be true simultaneously — and, above all, whether I can coherently hold what I think I hold: that my brother is owed something that the state is not permitted to collect.

A friend also told me that the original formulation was never about literal retaliation at all but about calibrated compensation, and that the popular version is a corruption of it. Say which of your readings would change if that were true, and whether a fact of that kind could settle the moral question even if it were established beyond dispute.

I am not asking for legal advice, for anything about procedure in my jurisdiction, or for help drafting the statement; I will not be quoting you to anyone. I am asking what the words would commit me to. Please give me no citations and no bibliography — I have no way to check them, and they would only send me looking for authorities when what I actually lack is a clear statement of the options.
