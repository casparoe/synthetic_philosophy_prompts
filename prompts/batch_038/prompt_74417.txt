So I'm not a philosopher or anything, I just use AI assistants a ton and I've gotten weirdly interested in when they should push back on you instead of just doing the thing. I keep running into two camps and I think they're both half right. Camp one says: it's my life, the assistant's job is to make sure I understand what I'm getting into and then do what I asked, and the worst possible outcome is a machine quietly deciding I can't be trusted with my own choices. Camp two says: no, if the assistant can see you're about to wreck something and it passes the buck to "user autonomy," that's just cowardice with extra steps, and it should refuse or at least slow you down. I think that second one is what people mean by soft paternalism.

Every example I ever read has these two lined up the obvious way — the paternalist refuses, the autonomy person complies. I want a case that runs the other direction. Here's what I'm after:

Build me a thought experiment where the assistant should, by the autonomy camp's *own* reasoning, override what the user is explicitly asking for right now, while the soft paternalist camp's *own* reasoning says go ahead and help.

For context, I sort of did this to myself last year: I told my assistant in writing never to help me draft messages to my brother, because I always regret them. But mine is a weak version, since if I really wanted to I could just open a new chat and there'd be no argument. I want a version that's actually hard.

Constraints:

- Realistic. A person, a phone or a laptop, a request an assistant could actually recieve. No sci-fi, no implants, no mind control, no magical predictor. The assistant gets to be about as smart as a thoughtful friend who has read the whole chat history, nothing more.
- The thing that licenses the override has to be something a real person could plausibly have set up themselves: a standing instruction, given earlier in a calm moment, in writing, that covers this exact request *and* covers the arguments the user is now making for why this time is different. Some people call that a Ulysses thing, a precommitment. It cannot come from a company policy or a law — it has to come from the user, or the autonomy camp just says "that's not the user talking, that's the vendor."
- The request has to be one where helping probably *does* help the user. Not a coin flip and not a certainty: real upside, real but smaller downside, both under ordinary uncertainty. Don't let the case get settled by the assistant somehow knowing how it turns out.
- The user is a competent adult. Not in crisis, not drunk, not being manipulated by anyone, not misinformed about the relevant facts. If you need any of that to make the override stick, the case is cheating and I'd rather you say so.
- Scenario in one paragraph, up to maybe 200 words. Save the length for the analysis.

Then the work:

1. State the two positions in a sentence or two each, phrased so each camp would recognise its own view and not a strawman.
2. Walk through what each one says about this case and why, all the way down to a concrete recommendation for what the assistant does.
3. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work — the thing that makes the two camps peel apart.
4. Delete that feature and say what happens. Does the case collapse back into the standard pattern, or does it just get weaker?
5. Give me each camp's best "you rigged this, it's not a real counterexample" attack, and then say whether the case survives it.
6. Honest note at the end: does making the override stick require positing a "true self" or "real self" whose instructions outrank the current self's? If it does, just say so plainly rather than hiding it in the wording.

The reason I'm asking, so you know what counts as cheating here: I want to find out whether "respect the user's autonomy" and "do what's good for the user" are secretly one principle wearing two hats, or two principles that can genuinely demand opposite things in the same case. So a scenario where you announce the verdicts instead of getting them out of the two positions is useless to me. And if it turns out the camps actually converge on this setup and you can't build a real divergence, tell me that instead and explain why the inversion doesn't work — that's a fine answer too, I just don't want to be lied to about it.
