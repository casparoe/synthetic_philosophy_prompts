I'm in my last year of high school, and since my school doesn't offer philosophy I've been running an informal reading group with two friends: we meet on Sunday afternoons, and each of us has to arrive with one objection to whatever the previous week's reading was, or admit we have none. We've just finished our unit on moral responsibility, and the centerpiece was Strawson's paper on freedom and resentment. I think we've absorbed the vocabulary well enough to stop being impressed by it — reactive attitudes, the participant stance and the objective stance, the distinction between the personal reactive attitudes and the impersonal or vicarious ones, the way Strawson replaces the question of whether determinism is compatible with responsibility by the question of whether it could make sense for us to hold these attitudes, and his remark that a life conducted wholly in the objective attitude would be one of human isolation, too impoverished for anyone to seriously contemplate. My objection, which I expect my friends will call unfair, is that this last move quietly converts a philosophical claim into an empirical one, and that the empirical claim is not nearly as safe as Strawson assumes.

Here is the case I want to hang it on. My aunt spent eleven years as a juvenile probation officer, writing reports about teenagers whose histories she had memorized — the abuse, the placements, the diagnoses. She describes the job as requiring her to see each of them as something to be assessed, managed, and treated rather than as someone to resent or forgive, and she says she got very good at it. What unsettles her, and what she now describes as the reason she left, is that the stance stopped switching off. At home she found herself producing resentment and gratitude toward her own children in something like the way she produced them in court: on schedule, in the right register, as performances she could recognize and endorse but no longer feel arriving. She insists she wasn't depressed, or numb, or being cynical; she says she simply ran the participant stance down, the way you can run down a language you no longer speak, and that what she had lost didn't come back when she changed jobs. I want to argue that this is not a sad anecdote but a counterexample in principle: it suggests the participant stance is not a fixed point of human sociality that we can't coherently imagine abandoning, but a depletable capacity with a maintenance cost. If it can be exhausted locally in one person's life, then whether it can be exhausted globally is a contingent question about psychology and social organization, not a question settled by what our concepts permit. And that is exactly the concession the incompatibilist wants, because it means the determinist does not have to convince anyone that determinism is true — only that the reactive attitudes are optional and expensive.

So please argue that position, in earnest rather than as a survey. Assume I already know the paper; don't rehearse its structure for me. I'd like the argument to do three things. First, show why treating the impossibility of a universal objective stance as a fact about our motivational economy, rather than as a fact about what we can coherently will, is the natural reading of Strawson's own appeal to impoverishment and to how thoroughgoing our commitments are. Second, defend the general step from local depletion to the possibility of global depletion — I suspect this is the weakest link, since one could say a single burned-out officer tells us nothing about a whole species, and I want the inference made as carefully as it can be. Third, explain what exactly Strawson loses if the step goes through: the reactive attitudes were supposed to be insulated from metaphysics, and I want to know whether the insulation was ever doing more work than the contingent claim that nobody can actually live without them. Please also engage the three objections I already expect from my friends and say which one you think is most damaging to my case and why: that attitudes and their affective tone come apart, so my aunt may have been resenting perfectly well all along and merely not feeling it, which would make her case no threat at all; that what she describes is occupational burnout, a pathology to be treated rather than a discovery about the contours of the possible; and that Strawson's claim is about what we can seriously contemplate, which concerns reasons rather than stamina, so a person who is too worn out to hold the participant stance hasn't shown that we could rationally give it up. If you think one of those objections actually wins, say so plainly and explain what remains of my argument afterward. Around twelve hundred words, and no reading list at the end, please — I can't get hold of most of the secondary literature anyway.
