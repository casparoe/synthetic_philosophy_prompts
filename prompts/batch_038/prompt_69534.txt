I am auditing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics this term, and I have come away with the impression that the debate between platonism and nominalism has settled into a small number of rehearsed exchanges: the indispensability argument and its critics, Benacerraf's challenge about how we could ever have cognitive contact with causally inert abstract objects, the Field-style project of showing that science can in principle be reformulated without referring to numbers, functions, or sets. I understand the dialectic well enough, but I find it hard to see what could ever settle it, since each side seems to have a standing answer to everything the other says. So I would like to test the two positions against a concrete case that neither position was designed with, and I would like you to write the result as a dialogue rather than as an essay.

Here is the case, which I have constructed myself; please treat every detail of it as stipulated fact within the dialogue rather than as something to be checked against the historical record. Call it theorem T, a result in algebraic combinatorics. T has been machine-verified: three independent formalizations, written by different groups and checked in three proof assistants whose kernels share no code, all pass. The decisive step is a case distinction that no one designed; it was produced by a search over a configuration space of roughly ten million objects, and the resulting verification artifact is tens of thousands of lines long. No living mathematician has followed more than perhaps five percent of it, and the informal write-up that accompanies it is a suggestive sketch that has itself never been machine-checked, though experts regard it as broadly faithful. Within a generation, T is cited constantly and used as a black-box lemma in further work. One more stipulation, which matters: T has turned out to have no application whatsoever to empirical science, so nobody can argue that we ought to believe in mathematical objects because our best scientific theories quantify over them.

Please write a dialogue between two characters who meet in a department common room after a seminar talk about T. The first is a platonist in a broadly Gödelian vein: she holds that T was true before anyone proved it, that its truth consists in something other than our verification practices, and that we have a fallible, perception-like rational access to mathematical structure — but she must defend that last claim without making her faculty of intuition sound mysterious or infallible, and she must say what her ontology adds in this particular case. The second is a fictionalist or nominalist of the sort who treats mathematical claims as true only within a useful fiction and who believes the fiction is in principle dispensable from serious ontology; he must explain what, on his view, gives the machine-checked artifact its authority if not a relation to abstract objects, and he should resist the familiar suggestion that computer-assisted proof simply turns mathematics into an empirical science, since that concession would cost him more than it gains him. Both characters should be fully competent advocates of their views, neither a strawman nor a pushover, and the dialogue must be a genuine back-and-forth in which each responds to the specific move the other just made rather than delivering alternating mini-lectures. In particular, I want them to press hard on a worry that the case of T seems designed to raise: that here the two ontologies issue in exactly the same verdicts, the same practices, and the same confidence levels, which would make the disagreement merely verbal — and I want each of them to try, with real resources rather than bluster, to show that the worry does not go through. Each character should concede at least one non-trivial point to the other, and each should end up articulating a position recognizably modified by the exchange, but the dialogue should not conclude with a truce, a synthesis, or an admission that the question is meaningless. Give the two characters names and lightly drawn personalities, keep stage directions minimal, aim for roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, do not open with either character restating the indispensability argument, and do not put any words in the mouth of an actual philosopher or quote from any real text.
