I spent thirty-eight years as a condensed-matter physicist and retired four years ago; philosophy has been my occupation since. I have been working through the recent literature on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality — chiefly Kit Fine and Jonathan Schaffer, and the secondary discussion around them — and I have become suspicious of the order in which I am reading it. In my working life, "levels" was never a metaphysical doctrine but a practical discipline. One described a metal in terms of quasiparticles with effective masses, then in terms of electrons moving in a periodic potential, then in terms of a many-body state; each description was treated as holding in virtue of the one beneath it, and as owing consistency to it, while remaining the correct description for the question actually being asked. Nothing about the Fermi surface appears in the Hamiltonian, and yet nothing about it conflicts with the Hamiltonian either. The grounding literature looks to me as though it were built to make that structure precise, but it is developed almost entirely on toy cases — the scarlet ball, the singleton of a thing, an act and its corresponding omission — and I cannot yet tell what it is being offered as a theory *of*. So before I read further I want the specification rather than the candidates.

Please write the desiderata: what any account of grounding and fundamentality must deliver in order to be adequate for the purpose I have in mind, namely serving as the general framework in which the relations among physical theories at different scales, and between physics and the special sciences, can be stated exactly. I would like the list organised under headings something like these, though you should add what I have missed and discard what is idle. First, the phenomena to be accounted for: the explanatory work actually done by "in virtue of" claims, and the fact that such claims are meant to be stronger than any claim of mere necessitation — say why, with a case. Second, the formal properties the theory must either vindicate or explain away — irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, well-foundedness — giving for each both the consideration that motivates it and the style of case that is thought to threaten it, including the familiar worry that some facts (negative, general, or essential ones) may be incapable of being grounded by anything. Third, the distinctions it must not collapse: grounding against causation, against supervenience, against reduction and against analysis; and the question of whether there is one primitive relation or a family of specific ones, with the burden that choice imposes. Fourth, the constraints from my side of the fence: the architecture of effective field theory, in which every level is derivative with respect to one below and authoritative with respect to one above, in which the ultraviolet completion may be permanently unknown without the low-energy theory being deficient, and in which nobody's practice depends on there being a bottom layer. Fifth — and I suspect this one is underweighted — an epistemological requirement: if grounding is primitive and unobservable and not a matter of conceptual analysis, the theory must say how its claims are known at all, and how disputes about them are to be settled rather than traded. Sixth, whatever must be said about approximation, idealisation, and whether grounding admits of degree, since scientific explanation plainly does.

For each item, mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and say so explicitly where a demand that looks neutral is in fact a partisan one. Then do the part I most want: identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, since if there is one, the list is itself a result rather than a shopping list. My own suspicion is that insisting both on a single primitive relation grasped by intuition and on a substantive logic strong enough to decide hard cases is too much to ask, and that well-foundedness may be incompatible with taking the actual structure of physics seriously. Tell me plainly if I am wrong. Also tell me which single requirement, if pressed, eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive views. A short closing paragraph — no more — indicating how the main camps fare against your list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria. I have no use for references: where you rely on a named argument or example, describe it rather than cite it.

Finally, a request about manner. I should like a committed answer, not a survey that presents every tension as an open question of equal weight; where you think a desideratum is bogus, say so and say why. And end by telling me what would change your mind: which argument, if it went through, would cause you to drop or reorder the list; which of the items you hold most weakly; and where in your own reasoning above you think you are most likely to have gone wrong — for instance, by reading too much into the English idiom, or by assuming that the structure of a successful physical theory is a reliable guide to the structure of the world.
