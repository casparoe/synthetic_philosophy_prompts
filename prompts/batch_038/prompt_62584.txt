Six weeks into a job as the in-house science writer at a mid-size natural history museum (two permanent halls, a traveling exhibit program, about 400k visitors a year) and I've already inherited a problem. My background is local-news journalism, not biology — one intro bio course, twenty years ago. Label text here has always been written by whoever was free, so the halls are full of function claims: organs that are "for" things, traits that "exist to" do things, DNA that "does a job." Last month a regional paper covered a school visit and quoted our appendix panel, and the letters that came in were not nothing. My director has now made the wording of every function claim my problem.

My brother-in-law is a philosophy grad student, and when I complained about this over dinner he said there's an actual debate about what biological function even means — two main camps, something like "etiological" versus "causal role," i.e. whether a trait's function is what it was selected to do or what it currently contributes to some bigger capacity the organism has. He said the difference bites exactly on cases like the appendix. That's about as much as I retained.

So: I need you to turn that into a procedure I can actually run, not an essay. Something I can tape inside a cabinet door and hand to a temp. For any trait, organ, behavior, or stretch of DNA I've got to write about, I want the questions in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each given that I get maybe 25 minutes per label and I'm working from museum reference books and general-audience sources rather than primary literature, and which verb constructions each path licenses — "evolved to," "helps," "is used for," "may have been co-opted for," "does X, though nobody knows that it's for anything," or just cut the sentence. It needs to say what I do when the science is genuinely split or when I simply can't find out, and it has to mark the spots where it isn't really the procedure deciding but a curator's judgment, because those go up to my senior curator who signs off on everything and hates being handed a fait accompli.

Then run it on four things we have panels on right now: the human appendix, wisdom teeth, goosebumps, and non-coding DNA (there's a whole case built around the phrase "junk DNA" and I'm fairly sure the current text is indefensible). Labels run 60–90 words at roughly an eighth-grade reading level, and our docents read them out loud to groups of eleven-year-olds, so hedge-stacking has a real cost — the procedure should account for that rather than defaulting to weasel words.

I want to see the actual wording it spits out for each of the four, at least one case where it tells me to kill a line visitors love, and a straight answer on what the procedure cant do — where it'll misfire, what it's silently assuming, and where I should expect to get burned again.
