At the Q&A after a public lecture on dissent during wartime, an audience member asked whether citizens who publicly oppose their government's ongoing military operation wrong their compatriots by weakening them in the middle of a fight. The lecturer replied only with "my country, right or wrong," and treated that as settling the matter. Half the room found this decisive; I left unsure that it was a claim at all.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing — for instance as a thesis about what patriotic attachment requires, about what one may say in public, about how compatriots' interests weigh against strangers', about how much deference is owed to the country's own judgment of its conduct, or about the country as a constituent of one's identity such that criticism becomes a kind of self-betrayal — and say for each whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or simply false? For each reading, also say what work the words "right or wrong" are doing, and whether that reading can avoid presupposing a standard of rightness independent of the country itself. Please note where the popular extension, to the effect that a right country should be kept so and a wrong one set right, departs from the bare slogan, and whether the extension changes the verdict on any of the readings.

Finally, tell me which reading, if any, would actually license the lecturer's conclusion about mid-operation dissent. I would like the readings as a numbered list I can bring back to my reading group.
