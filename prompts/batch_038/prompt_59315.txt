Our reading group is doing Zamyatin's *We* in three weeks, and I got roped into doing the 20-minute intro. The wrinkle: one of the members is an econ PhD student who's been loudly claiming for a month that the book is "obviously the socialist calculation debate, 15 years early," and I want to either give him his point properly or take it away from him properly, rather than let him just assert it over the samosas. Nobody else in the group has any economics at all. So I need notes I can actually talk from.

Please put yourself in the shoes of someone who teaches both philosophy of economics and 20th-century dystopian fiction and has thought hard about both — start with a short brainstorming pass where you list the candidate mappings before you commit to any of them, then write the thing up. The spine I want: (a) a careful mapping of what the One State's economic setup actually is in the novel — the glass apartments, the synchronized Table of Hours, the pink checks, the Benefactor, the Green Wall, D-503 as the builder of the Integral and a mathematician who thinks in equations — onto the pieces of the debate as it actually ran: Mises's claim that without genuine market prices for capital goods there's no way to compare alternative production plans, the Lange/Lerner reply that a planning board can just set prices by trial and error and force managers to follow the marginal rules, and Hayek's shift onto dispersed and tacit knowledge and the competitive process as discovery. Be disciplined about which features of the novel bear on which argument, and say which features are decoration that do no work at all. (b) Then the real question: if One State comes apart, is it coming apart because of an *information* problem or a *motivation* problem, and does the novel actually care which? I have a hunch that what Zamyatin has his finger on is neither Mises nor Hayek exactly — it's something about imagination and unpredictability as the thing that can't be planned for, which is adjacent to Hayek's tacit-knowledge point but not the same claim. Test that hunch for me and tell me if it's wrong. (c) Then the honest pushback on my econ friend: is this really a novel about calculation, or is it a novel about Taylorism, Fordism, and the wartime state that Zamyatin saw firsthand in the English shipyards — correct me if I've garbled that bit of biography, it isn't load-bearing — and does reading it as socialist economics actively flatten it? (d) Finally, what does the novel give the debate that the arguments on their own don't? If there's a scene, or something about the form itself — the numbered records, D-503's voice breaking down as he writes — that makes a point the literature can't make, name it.

Two ground rules. Don't turn it into an allegory with a decoder ring: where the mapping strains, I want you to say "this doesn't hold" out loud, that's the part I trust most. And no citations or reading lists — nobody in the group can look anything up and I'd rather you flag uncertainty than manufacture a reference. If you're not sure about a detail of the novel or a date in the debate, mark it with brackets and keep going.
