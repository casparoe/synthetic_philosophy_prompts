Ok so I've been arguing about the Popper tolerance footnote with a friend for going on three years — he drops it in every time some platform bans somebody, I tell him it's being asked to do way more work than it can hold — and we've agreed to settle it with a case study instead of another 400-comment thread. I want you to analyze the Skokie business, the 1977–78 one, through the paradox of tolerance, but using a lens I'm going to hand you, because the naive version of this debate is just people shouting past each other. I already know the standard First Amendment story about content neutrality and counterspeech, and I know the ACLU's after-the-fact self-congratulation, and I don't want either of those.

The facts, so you don't have to guess at them: Frank Collin's National Socialist Party of America was a Chicago outfit that had been demonstrating out of Marquette Park until the city shut that down by demanding a $350,000 public-safety insurance bond and banning political demonstrations in the park. In October 1976 Collin wrote to the park districts of a bunch of North Shore suburbs asking for permits; most ignored him, Skokie answered. Skokie had a large Jewish population including a lot of Holocaust survivors. The mayor and village council initially wanted to let them come and simply ignore them, on the theory that no publicity was the worst outcome for the Nazis; the Jewish community found that unacceptable, held meetings, and the council then ordered the village attorney to seek an injunction and passed three ordinances — no military-style uniforms at demonstrations, no distribution of material containing hate speech, and that $350,000 bond. A Cook County judge granted an emergency injunction barring the uniforms and the swastikas. The ACLU took the Nazis' side and lost below, the Illinois appellate courts wouldn't stay or expedite, and the Supreme Court reversed per curiam 5–4 on the narrow ground that an injunction restraining speech has to get immediate appellate review. The Illinois Supreme Court then said the NSPA could march and display swastikas, the federal courts struck the three ordinances, and the Supreme Court declined to take that up. They never actually marched in Skokie — Collin offered to march in Marquette Park instead. Two more details I want you to keep in view: Collin had run for Chicago alderman in 1975 and pulled 16% of the vote, and his rivals in the other neo-Nazi organization published evidence that his father was born Max Simon Cohn in Munich, that his grandparents were murdered in the Holocaust and that his father was a Dachau survivor who'd changed the family name, which Collin denied; then in 1979 his own people handed evidence of child molestation to the police, he was convicted and went to prison, and the group came apart. If you think any of that is off, say so at the top rather than building on it.

Now the lens, which is the part that's actually mine. I work in insurance analytics, and the framing I keep trying to make my friend take seriously is this: treat a society's tolerance of an intolerant movement as an unhedged exposure to a tail risk, and treat supression as the premium you pay to cut it. The premium is near-certain, immediate, and — here's the bit everyone skips — paid by a particular subgroup rather than by the polity that makes the decision. The payoff is uncertain and catastrophic, and its probability may be correlated with whether you pay: cracking down can martyr people, legitimize the grievance, and hand the movement a narrative, so the premium can raise the very thing it's supposed to lower. On that reading Popper's footnote is an argument that the premium is sometimes worth it, and his caveat — only suppress those who refuse to meet you on the level of rational argument and answer argument with fists or pistols — is really a claim about who counts as a live threat. Run Skokie through it. I want the mapping first and I want it disciplined: who were the actual decision-makers at each node (village council, the community organizations pushing them, the ACLU's national leadership and its Illinois affiliate, the state courts, the Supreme Court, Collin), what were their real options at the time rather than in hindsight, what was the premium and who was going to eat it, what was the tail event, and what was a defensible estimate of it in 1977 given 30–50 announced marchers, a 16% ward-level vote share two years earlier, and no movement anywhere near power nationally. What would have counted as evidence that the tail risk was real, and did anybody have any? Then: what does the framework say each actor should have done, what does it predict about the aftermath, and how does that compare to what happened — the NSPA didn't march, got an enormous amount of press, and died within a couple of years, but died from a scandal its own leadership turned in to the cops rather than from anyone refuting them. Does the counterspeech story survive that, or is it an unfalsifiable piety?

Then run it backwards, which is what I actually care about. Does Skokie confirm the paradox of tolerance, is it an anomaly, or is it a case the paradox was never about? Is there any version of the paradox that survives contact with a forty-person group with no path to power, or does it quietly presuppose something like Weimar in 1932 and therefore say nothing here at all? And is the "refuses rational argument" test even usable, given that the NSPA's whole strategy was to work the tolerant system's own procedures — permit letters, lawsuits, an appeal to the Supreme Court — and to win on a procedural due-process point? That looks like the test certifying them as tolerable, which either means the caveat is doing all the real work and the "paradox" is really about who accepts the rules of argument, or it means the test is gameable by anyone who can retain a lawyer. Pick a side and defend it. Also tell me where the risk framing simply can't represent what happened: my suspicion is that the premium-bearer asymmetry is fatal to it, since the people asked to absorb the cost of being tolerant were the ones least able to, and "the survivors stay quiet and the streets stay empty so the tail risk stays low" is a demand that one specific group eat a specific harm for an aggregate benefit that mostly accrues to other people. But argue it, don't just agree with me — if you think the framing handles that fine and my worry is confused, say that instead. Where Popper is silent, say he's silent; don't recruit him into answering questions he never asked. Finally, name at most two rival models of the same episode — something like a side-constraint or rights-as-trumps view that refuses to aggregate at all, a speech-act view on which the swastika isn't a proposition but a hostile act done to somebody, or a signaling view on which the whole point of letting them march is that the liberal order visibly extends its own procedures to its worst enemies — and say whether either does better on these same facts, using the same discipline. Call it 1500–2200 words, mostly prose, headers are fine, and please no bibliography or reading list and no verbatim quotes of Popper or the opinions, since I can't check any of it and I'd rather you paraphrase than guess.
