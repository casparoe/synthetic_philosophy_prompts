Our philosophy-of-physics reading group has a resident slogan. Whenever the discussion turns to the direction of time, one of the senior members says, flatly, that entropy always increases, and then puts that single sentence to work three times over in the same argument:

1. It shows that the difference between past and future is a feature of the world rather than an artifact of our experience, because the asymmetry is already there in the physics.
2. It explains why there can be records and memories of earlier times but not of later ones.
3. It disposes of a suggestion made at an earlier meeting — that the second law is only a theorem we derive from time-reversal-invariant statistical mechanics — on the grounds that any such derivation must be smuggling something in, since the conclusion is asymmetric and the premises are not.

A second member objects on two counts: that fine-grained entropy, in either its classical or its quantum form, is exactly conserved by the dynamics, so nothing whatsoever always increases; and that whatever coarse-grained quantity does increase can be shown to increase only by first fixing a direction in which to run the equations, which makes the argument circular. I suspect the two of them are attacking different claims and that nobody in the room has separated the sentence into its components.

I want a disambiguation I can hand around before the next meeting. Please give me a numbered list of the precise claims the sentence could be standing for, varying at least: which entropy is in play (Boltzmann, Gibbs/ensemble, fine-grained); which system it is being applied to (an isolated system, a closed one, an arbitrary subsystem, or the universe as a whole); what the modality of "always" is (strict necessity, overwhelming probability, typicality with respect to some measure, long-run average); and whether the claim is offered as a law of nature or as a consequence of a law plus a boundary condition about the early universe. For each item, state the claim precisely enough to be evaluated, assign it a verdict — trivially true, true but not what the argument needs, substantive and defensible, contested, flatly false, or not even well-defined — and then say which of the three jobs above, if any, it could actually perform.

Then add a short closing section covering: which reading, or combination of readings, jobs 1 and 3 would require, and whether that reading is available to anyone who also accepts the time-symmetric underlying dynamics; whether the fluctuation-theorem results about transient entropy decreases in small systems over short intervals speak against any item on the list or only against a popularized version of them; whether the fine-grained-entropy objection and the circularity objection target the same reading or different ones, and if different, whether either survives once that is clear; and which readings, if any, license the customary gloss of entropy as disorder — and whether that gloss does any work in the argument or merely makes it sound intuitive. I am not after the history of the debate or a list of sources: just the claims, stated exactly, with verdicts attached.
