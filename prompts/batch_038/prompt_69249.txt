I teach a philosophy of mind seminar and I'm building a case study around the fMRI work on patients diagnosed as vegetative — the 2006 Owen et al. study where a woman who'd been given that diagnosis was asked to imagine playing tennis and lit up her supplemenatry motor area, and asked to imagine walking around her house and lit up the parahippocampal gyrus and posterior parietal cortex in patterns basically indistinguishable from healthy controls, and the follow-up a few years later where the same group got a man who'd been behaviourally unresponsive for years to answer yes/no questions just by switching between those two imagery tasks. Analyze that episode through the access-vs-phenomenal consciousness distinction, and through global workspace theory in particular.

Here's the reason I'm asking rather than assigning a textbook chapter. GWT keeps getting accused of being a theory of access rather than of phenomenology, and the tennis case sits right on that fault line. Either her imagery involved global ignition and she simply couldn't report it — in which case behavioural scales built on report are measuring something other than consciousness, and GWT owes an account of what ignition looks like when the motor outputs are disconnected — or there was no ignition at all and what the scanner saw was local, task-appropriate recurrent processing of the kind people like Lamme would be happy to call non-accessed (and in his framing, precisely where phenomenal character lives). Work through that fork explicitly and say which horn a global workspacer should take and what it costs them. Also bring in the higher-order views that deny you can have phenomenology without some access, since they make a different prediction about this patient. And tell me which way the dissociation is supposed to run here: is she a candidate for A-without-P, a cognitive zombie obediently generating motor imagery, or for P-without-A — and whether the case gives us any grip on that question at all when all we actually have is a BOLD contrast.

Then be hard-nosed about the inference itself: what has to be true for "she was conscious of the tennis imagery" to follow from "the expected region activated in the expected pattern," and what the sceptical replies are — semantic priming, learned motor preparation, stimulus-driven rather than willful activation, the base-rate and false-positive problem once you scan dozens of patients.

Please use these sections, in this order:

1. **The episode** (~150 words) — what was done and what was measured. Flag anything you're reconstructing rather than remembering cleanly.
2. **The machinery** (~250 words) — A-consciousness, P-consciousness, the two dissociation directions, the ignition claim, the local-recurrent-processing rival, the higher-order denial. Only the claims that will do work below; no history lesson.
3. **The mapping** (~400 words) — theory by theory: what each says about this patient, what each would have predicted before the scan, what result here would have embarrassed it.
4. **The fork** (~300 words) — the ignition-or-not dilemma, the choice, the price.
5. **What the case can't settle** (~250 words) — where the imaging underdetermines the philosophy.
6. **Verdict** (~150 words) — does the episode confirm GWT, strain it, or just bypass it?

Throughout, keep settled seperate from contested. Where the field is genuinely split — e.g. over whether covert command-following licenses any inference to phenomenal consciousness, or over whether P-consciousness can outstrip access at all — say that it's split and roughly who sits where, and don't dress up your preferred camp as the consensus. Same on the empirical side: if you're unsure of a detail or a percentage, mark it as unsure instead of stating it confidently.
