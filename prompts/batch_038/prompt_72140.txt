Last night I spent four hours arguing with a friend — an economist, precise-Bayes to the marrow — about credal states represented by sets of probability measures rather than by a single one. He concedes that such representations are coherent, and that they describe agents facing ambiguity better than an arbitrarily chosen prior does. What he denies is that they earn their keep. His charge has two prongs. First, that under any decision rule worth the name an interval-valued agent either collapses back into a precise agent in disguise or refuses to act, so the representation buys only paralysis — and that the literature has made a virtue of the paralysis. Second, that the phenomenon in which learning something strictly widens your interval is not a discovery but a bookkeeping accident of declining to commit to a prior; on his account any honest agent turns out to have had a determinate credence all along, the moment they are made to put chips on the table. I defended the view badly. I could gesture at cases but could not make him feel the difference, and further argument seems a waste of both our evenings. I want to run it as a game instead, with him in the room.

The setting: an interdisciplinary reading group I convene, eighteen people, one two-hour evening session, a room with a whiteboard, a printer, and a bag of poker chips. No real money. Roughly half have graduate training in statistics or econometrics and half are philosophy graduate students; exactly one has read anything in the imprecise-probability literature, and none have met the term dilation. My friend will attend and will be playing.

Design an activity in which the group runs into both prongs of his objection — and into whatever resources exist for answering them — without my naming the point until the end. I need:

- Roles and materials. At minimum: agents whose credal states are assigned rather than chosen (some precise, some interval-valued, and at least one holding a precise marginal together with an indeterminate conditional), a bookie or market-maker, and a scoring ledger anyone can read at a glance.
- A payoff scheme in chips or points. Abstention must carry a real cost, or refusal-to-bet becomes dominant and the exercise proves my friend right by construction; but the cost must not be so steep that imprecision is simply punished, or it proves me right by construction. Tell me where you have set that dial, why, and what verdict each alternative setting would have manufactured.
- One round in which a participant learns something that strictly widens the interval for a quantity they had previously pinned to a single number, and in which the widening is visible to the whole room rather than only to the person it befalls. I would rather this be built out of a correlation whose sign is unknown than out of an urn of unknown composition: half the room has seen the Ellsberg urns and will recite the expected response instead of having it.
- One round in which two agents hold the same credal state, are directed to apply different decision rules to it, reach different verdicts, and are scored differently for it. The divergence must be attributable to the rule and not to any difference in belief, or nothing is shown.
- If you can find one, a mechanism by which the precisely committed agent is picked off over repeated rounds in a way the interval-valued agent avoids — plus an honest statement of whether what your mechanism demonstrates is a fact about imprecise credence or a fact about your scoring rule. I have no interest in a game rigged in my favour; I would rather have one I can lose cleanly.
- A debrief mapping specific moments of play onto: the difference between a set of measures and an unspecified single measure; the choice among decision rules over one and the same set; widening-under-evidence and what it does and does not license; and the paralysis charge in its strongest form. Close on the question the room should still be arguing about on the walk home, not on a summary.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I expect two failures in particular: participants quietly adopting a uniform prior over the unknown parameter and reporting a single number when the design requires otherwise; and a player assigned an interval-valued state being unable to keep the arithmetic up with the clock. Handle both. Also handle my friend — he plays well and loses badly, and I need a role for him that neither lets him derail the session nor converts him through an artifact of the rules.
- A frank statement of which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I suspect it is this: any arrangement a facilitator sets up imports background structure — a known generator, a room, someone who chose the setup — that silently restores the determinate prior he insists was there all along. If that suspicion is correct, say so plainly, and tell me what I ought to concede to him in the debrief rather than pretending the game settled the matter.

Finally, tell me what to cut if I only have seventy-five minutes.
