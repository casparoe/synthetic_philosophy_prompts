I've been going down a rabbit hole on mechanistic anomaly detection lately — the family of approaches where instead of policing a model's outputs you try to build a detector on its internals, some circuit-level or feature-level signature that shouldn't light up in the deployment distribution. The bit I can't get past is the identity question it silently assumes: your detector has to decide whether the mechanism firing in this weird new context is the *same* mechanism you characterized in training, just misfiring, or something else wearing its clothes. Nobody says what "same" means, and the choice looks load-bearing — loose criteria and everything is a false alarm, tight criteria and you wave through a genuinely novel mechanism as "ah that's just the usual one in a new situation." I'm prepping a talk for a small reading group and I don't want to do it with toy models, so I want to work the problem out on a real episode where the same structural mistake happened with actual consequences: the Boeing 737 MAX and MCAS.

The facts I'm leaning on, so you don't have to guess at them (and tell me if you think I've garbled any of this): the MAX's bigger engines sat higher and further forward, which gave the airframe a pitch-up tendency at high angle of attack, and certification rules about stall characteristics basically require stick forces to keep increasing smoothly as you approach a stall. MCAS was the software patch — a function in the flight control computer that trims the horizontal stabilizer nose-down — chosen partly so the MAX would handle enough like the old 737NG that airlines wouldn't owe their pilots simulator differences training. It started out scoped to a high-speed pitch-up case and got broadened during flight test to cover low-speed, high-AoA flight too. As built, it triggered off a *single* AoA vane, only with flaps up, autopilot off, manual flight; it could command up to about 2.5 degrees of stabilizer over roughly ten seconds, pause about five, and then re-trigger as long as the condition persisted, with the trim cutout switches as the only real way to stop it. It was not described in the flight crew manuals, so pilots on Lion Air 610 and Ethiopian 302 didn't know it existed while it repeatedly drove their noses down after takeoff on bad vane data — 346 dead, global grounding in March 2019, FAA ungrounding in Nov 2020 after a software fix. The fix kept the name MCAS but changed the behavior a lot: both AoA sensors have to agree, one input per elevated-AoA event, less authority, column force can override it, plus disclosure and simulator training. And the cockpit annunciator that would have told the crew the two vanes disagreed — the AoA disagree alert — was an optional paid feature bundled with the AoA indicator, so a bunch of fleets simply didn't have it until Boeing made it standard after the crashes. Boeing's own safety analysis reportedly assumed a pilot would recognize and counter an unexpected activation in something like three seconds. Separately there's the 2016 simulator session that only surfaced years later, where a Boeing technical pilot watched MCAS repeatedly push the nose down on a false AoA input and decided not to pass it along to the FAA; treat that one as fuzzy, I don't want the analysis hanging on it.

Here's what I actually want. First, do the mapping honestly and explicitly before you argue anything: in this story, what is the mechanism, what is the characterized/certified context versus the deployment distribution, what plays the role of the anomaly detector, and what counts as an anomaly. Then take a set of candidate identity criteria and adjudicate them on the case rather than in the abstract — I've been scribbling these down: implementation identity (same code path, same subroutine, same weights), input–output identity (same function from inputs to activations), contextual-role identity (same job relative to the system's goals, i.e. still smoothing stick forces toward the stall), causal-contribution identity (same upstream causes and same downstream effects in the mechanism graph), and lineage identity (descended by revision from a common ancestor). For each: is MCAS firing right after takeoff at low altitude on a bad vane the same mechanism as MCAS in the wind-up-turn regime it was certified for, and is post-2020 MCAS the same mechanism as pre-crash MCAS? I especially want the places where the criteria come apart, and then a diagnosis of which criterion Boeing and the FAA were implicitly using when they treated this as a minor modification that didn't need to be mentioned to pilots or retrained for. Then run it backwards: what does the episode do to the MAD picture? Does it show that mechanism identity is a real, load-bearing problem, or is the MAX story actually about something else — single sensor with no redundancy, envelope expansion without re-doing the safety analysis, disclosure and human factors — such that the MAD framing obscures more than it reveals? There's at least one datum that seems awkward for the whole enterprise, which is that here the mechanism was hand-written, fully known, and completely interpretable, and the monitoring still failed; sit with that. And I want your take on the paid-option annunciator as an analogue of a detector that exists in principle but was never deployed by default — is the absence of the monitor part of the mechanism-identity problem or a separate failure mode? Be disciplined about where the analogy has no counterpart at all and where MAD is simply silent instead of forcing it to speak, and don't give me the standard regulatory-capture-and-greed narrative, I've read that version. No reading list, no citations — I just want the analysis.
