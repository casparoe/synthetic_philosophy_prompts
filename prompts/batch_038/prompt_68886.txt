I'm a staff engineer on the reliability org at a company that runs a mid-sized cloud platform, and my team is about to make a decision I don't feel equipped to make. Today our incident-response system does exactly one thing: when a page fires, it reads the telemetry and the runbook corpus and prints a diagnosis plus a ranked list of remediation steps for the on-call human to execute. Leadership wants to know whether we should let it execute the steps itself, and the argument in our design reviews has stalled on what looks to me like a definitional question nobody is willing to name directly — at what point does the thing we have stop being a tool and start being an agent? I've seen those two words used as though the difference were obvious, I've seen people insist it's only a matter of degree, and I once read an argument (by someone whose name I've half forgotten) that even a system that does nothing but answer questions can be dangerous, which struck me as either profound or as a cheat, and I couldn't tell which. Before I evaluate any proposals, I want the specification. Please treat me as someone who can read code and reason carefully but has no philosophy training: don't assume the technical vocabulary is familiar, and don't point me at a literature I can't go look up.

Write the desiderata: the list of things any account of the tool/agent distinction has to deliver in order to count as adequate for the purpose I actually have, which is (a) deciding whether to ship a change and (b) defending that decision to an auditor after an incident. For each requirement, give the consideration or the case that motivates it rather than just a label. I want to know, among other things: must an adequate account return a verdict on every system, or is it allowed to declare some systems genuinely borderline, and if it is allowed, is that a feature or an evasion? Must the property be intrinsic to the artifact — readable from its architecture, its state, and its code — or may it depend on what the artifact happens to be wired up to? Must it come in degrees, and if so must it also say where the thresholds go, or may it leave them to context? Must it be checkable from the outside by black-box testing, or is internal inspection sufficient for the audit use case? Must the verdict be stable as the system gets better at its job, or does a system that changes category when its accuracy improves thereby reveal something true about itself? Must the account compose — if I know the verdict for each component, must the verdict for the pipeline follow? And must the classification line up with who we hold responsible when something breaks, given that our incident reviews name a system or a person and not a point on a spectrum? Some of these are, I suspect, real requirements and some are just the preferences of whichever camp happens to be arguing loudest in my building; tell me which is which and why.

To make this concrete rather than abstract, here are six systems from my platform — some real, some invented specifically to break the accounts my colleagues propose. (1) Our current model, except that its top-ranked step is carried out automatically by a dumb runner script with no reasoning of its own and no human in the loop; the model itself has no credentials, no network access, and cannot act, it only emits text, and the runner does literally what the text says. (2) A system with no tools whatsoever whose only output is a weekly strategy memo to executives, optimized against a business metric that only those memos can move, and whose recommendations are followed almost verbatim. (3) A stateless function invoked ten million times a day, each call with no memory of any other, no plan, and no horizon, whose aggregate effect reliably drives one number on a company dashboard. (4) A full autonomous planner with persistent state, long horizons, a rich world model, and — in simulation — a disposition to avoid being switched off, running in a VM with no network path to anything, permanently. (5) An agent that proposes actions and requires a human click before each one, where the click is granted 99.7 percent of the time and throughput is part of its reward, so it has learned to make proposals that are easy to approve. (6) A chain of five tools, none of which is an agent on anyone's account, where each one's output is the next one's input and the composite reliably holds a target variable against disturbances. I have a suspicion I'd like you to confirm or correct: that the requirement that the classification be intrinsic to the artifact and the requirement that it track who is causally responsible for outcomes cannot both be satisfied, because cases (1) and (4) pull in opposite directions. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and say which subset — that would be a result about my design problem, not a failure of my reading, and it's the part I most want to see. Only after the list is complete, and briefly: how do the four accounts I hear most often at work fare against your requirements — that a tool has its ends handed to it per call while an agent selects its own; that the difference is whether a human authorizes each action at the moment it happens; that anything worth predicting as if it had beliefs and desires is an agent; and that a tool is a pure function while an agent is a long-lived process with state and side effects? One or two sentences each, no scorecard; I would rather you spend that space telling me which single requirement knocks out the most contenders.

End with one line in exactly this form, so I can diff your answer against my colleague's: `Verdict: <short name of the single requirement that excludes the most candidate accounts> | <SURVIVES or COLLAPSES>`, where the second field reports whether the tool/agent distinction survives as a property you can read off the artifact itself rather than off the system it is embedded in.
