In the graduate seminar I am running this term on Aristotle's account of wealth-getting, I want to set my students a problem that forces the ancient theory to do real work, and I should like your help drafting the assignment in a form I can hand out. The subject is money engineered to decay.

Here are the materials, which you may take as given rather than research. First, an interwar Austrian municipality issued labour scrip that forfeited a small fraction of its face value each month unless the holder purchased and affixed a stamp; the notes circulated with unusual velocity until the courts suppressed the scheme. Second, prepaid gift cards, on which unspent balances lapse after a fixed period and revert to the issuer as revenue. Third, the several major central banks that over the past decade set policy rates below zero, so that very large balances held at the central bank shrank in nominal terms for as long as they sat idle. Against all of this, consumer-protection statutes in many jurisdictions have moved in the opposite direction, restricting or prohibiting expiry dates on stored-value cards. The result is a legal order that at one end forbids money to die and at the other end requires it to.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit philosophy, not a survey of monetary economics. Begin by asking what a decaying currency presupposes about what money is: what it must be for, who it belongs to, and what holding it amounts to. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely sits comfortably with — the Aristotelian thesis that money exists by convention rather than by nature, and so can be unmade or fall into disuse, is obviously congenial to demurrage, but tell me whether demurrage is unintelligible without it. Say which rival conceptions the practice quietly rules out: money as a commodity with a use of its own, money as an item of property the holder may keep indefinitely, money as the token of a credit relation. Then address the apparent inconsistency between the retail prohibition and the monetary authority's practice. Is a single legal system committed to two incompatible theories of money, or does a distinction — between who holds the balance, in what capacity, and for what purpose — dissolve the tension? What does each stance presuppose about whether idle holdings are a private affair or a loss to the whole?

Finally, bring Aristotle to bear in both directions, paraphrasing him rather than quoting. He holds that money arose to serve exchange, that it is conventional and not natural, and that taking interest is the most unnatural mode of acquisition because it makes money breed from money instead of serving the end for which it was contrived. Does decay-money vindicate that framework, or is it the mirror image of the vice he condemns — another device that treats money as having a generative life of its own, only running in reverse? Does his functional account license compelling circulation at all, or is it silent on how long money may be held? Note where the silence is real: he has no theory of the price level, no account of a monetary authority, and no conception of money as a store of value across time in a credit economy. Tell me whether that silence leaves the case underdetermined or whether it decides something. Conclude by saying what would have to change in the practice, and in our legal notion of owning a balance, if the instrumental view were adopted consistently.
