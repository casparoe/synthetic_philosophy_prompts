I facilitate deliberative processes for a county government — land use, a proposed group home, school consolidation, budget priorities — and roughly ten or twelve times a year a session deadlocks in the same way: the two sides are not disagreeing about the evidence so much as about the extension of a loaded predicate, and every attempt to settle the matter by definition leaves somebody feeling dismissed. On my own time I have been reading Nāgārjuna and the Madhyamaka treatment of the tetralemma — the four-cornered analysis in which a claim is examined as holding, as not holding, as both, and as neither — together with the associated doctrine that things are empty of intrinsic nature and exist only as dependently arisen and conventionally designated. I want to know whether any of that can be turned into something my teams could actually run in a room, or whether the attempt corrupts it past the point of usefulness.

Please write the procedure itself rather than an essay about the philosophy behind it. It should be a sequence of questions a facilitator asks in order, with the available moves at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, the conditions under which the procedure halts and refuses to proceed, and the points where the decision is a judgment call that cannot be written down. Design to these constraints: two-hour sessions; twelve to forty participants with no philosophy background and mixed literacy; a co-facilitator who is openly skeptical that any of this is more than a parlor trick; a program director who needs a one-page handout and a written record of how each session concluded; and no Sanskrit or Pali vocabulary in anything the group hears. Then tell me which distinctions in the doctrine must survive the simplification for the procedure to still be doing what Nāgārjuna was doing — I suspect the difference between negating all four corners and asserting the fourth, the fact that his arguments function as reductios rather than counter-theses, the dependence of any designation on causes and conditions, and the insistence that emptiness is itself empty all have to stay — and which can be dropped without loss, such as the soteriological framing, karma and rebirth, and the monastic setting. I specifically want the last of those built in as a closing move, so that a group cannot walk out having installed "it's both and neither" as a new fixed answer to replace the old one. Include an explicit prohibition on using the procedure to declare a disagreement about observable fact somehow both true, and say how a facilitator recognizes that case in time to bail out.

Run it on three situations I have actually sat through: a neighborhood association split over whether a supervised consumption site made their block more or less dangerous, where both sides have incident counts and the counts do not match; an alumni group and a new parent council arguing about whether the school formed by merging two buildings is still "our school"; and a volunteer board arguing over whether a longtime member's habit of steering newcomers toward particular projects was welcoming or gatekeeping. For at least one of these I expect the procedure to refuse the case, or to reach a conclusion I will not like — say so plainly rather than bending the tool. Finally, two requests about epistemic honesty. First, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on Madhyamaka: whether Nāgārjuna advances any thesis of his own, whether the fourth corner is ever a position rather than a refusal of all positions, whether emptiness is a metaphysical claim about how things are or a claim about our concepts, and whether the tetralemma licenses anything like true contradictions — and if the procedure's design quietly depends on one side of any of those disputes, flag that dependence rather than presenting your preferred reading as the consensus. Second, tell me where I may be fooling myself: whether reducing a practice aimed at liberation from suffering to a technique for getting a county working group to stop shouting is a distortion, a legitimate secular borrowing, or a question nobody is in a position to settle.
