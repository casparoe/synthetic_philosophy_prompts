I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — decision theory, rationality, prediction, that beat — and I'm trying to build a sidebar graphic for a piece on whether you can be argued into coherence. The hook is the Dutch book business: if your betting prices don't obey the probability rules, a clever bookie can sell you a package of bets that loses you money come what may. Every person I've talked to agrees the arithemtic works. What they don't agree on is what it proves, and that's the part I have no handle on, which is a problem because the graphic has to be the part readers actually take away.

So what I want is a decision tree that starts a reader at "fine, I accept the Dutch book theorem, the math is fine" and walks them down to a named position about what the theorem is worth. Call it eight to twelve branch nodes and somewhere between six and nine leaves. It has to fit on one page next to the text, so keep the node questions short.

Ground rules I care about:

1. Every node should be a question a reader can answer honestly without knowing which camp it sends them to. Not "do you accept the standard Bayesian reply" — I want the concrete situation, with the labels attached only at the end.

2. Please route the tree through these specific cases, or tell me if one of them can't actually do any sorting work:
   - the package case, where someone cheerfully accepts each of three bets separately and only loses once they own all three — is the loss an indictment of the credences, or of their readiness to buy a bundle?
   - the unbettable belief, something you'll happily put a number on that no bookie could ever actually offer you odds on (a hard mathematical claim, whether you're a brain in a vat). Is that number still criticizable, or has the argument just lost its grip?
   - the case where the bookie offers bets in sequence and re-quotes after seeing what you've learned. Does the same money-loss argument then say something about how you should update, and if so why should I believe it says that rather than something else?
   - the infinite/unbounded one. I gather there's a result that if payoffs are allowed to grow without bound you can book an agent whose finite credences are perfectly coherent. I may be garbling this, so correct me if I am — but I'd like the tree to branch on whether the reader is willing to live with that.
   - sleeping beauty, where my understanding is that people on both sides have each claimed a Dutch book for their own camp. If that's right it seems like a great node, because the two camps presumably answer some earlier question differently.

3. I'd like a few leaves to definitely show up, whatever the field calls them: the view that the theorem is really a representation or definitional result about the connection between prices and beliefs rather than a premise-to-conclusion argument; the view that it survives only once you stop talking about beliefs and talk about actual choices; the view that the betting framing is a historical detour and the real content is about one incoherent set of attitudes being dominated by a better one; and some flavour of "coherence is only coherence relative to the bets you're actually offered, so the argument proves much less than advertised." Feel free to add or subdivide.

4. Build it out of the logical space before you build it out of names. That is: first tell me what axes your tree is secretly using (real money vs. representation? beliefs vs. choices? all beliefs vs. bettable ones? one-shot vs. sequential? and what exactly is supposed to have gone wrong in the booked agent's head?), then roughly how many combinations those axes permit, then where the named positions sit in that grid. And for the combinations nobody occupies, say why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. That last part is honestly what I most want, because it's the bit an encyclopedia entry won't have.

5. Where your tree can't actually separate two leaves, flag the pair instead of faking a node that sorts them.

On epistemics, one favour: I need to know what's settled here versus what's genuinely a live fight among people who work on this. So mark each leaf as best you can — broadly the default view, respectable minority, or a one-or-two-person position — and if there's a spot where two of my sources would tell me flatly opposite things, say that's contested rather than smoothing it into a consensus. Two of the people I interviewed gave me very different pictures and I don't want to print one of them as fact. Relatedly, if you're not sure who defends a given leaf, just describe the view; I'd rather have an unattributed leaf than a confident wrong name.

No reading list and no citations, please — I'll chase the papers myself once I know which questions to ask.
