I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and next month I'm running a 90-minute session for about twenty local-government reporters — people who cover school boards, health departments, transit authorities, that kind of beat. Topic is Goodhart's law and the wider family of ways a target goes wrong. They'll all walk in able to recite the bumper-sticker version, measure becomes target, target stops being a good measure, so the session can't be about handing them the slogan. It has to leave them with something they can use on deadline.

Give me the teaching plan, not the talk. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to go in and what each one depends on. I think something has to be true before any of it can land — they have to first buy that the metric was a genuinely decent proxy, otherwise the case looks like a stupid metric chosen by stupid people and there's nothing left to learn. Tell me if that's right, and what else sits underneath it that I'm not seeing.

- One case to carry the whole session. Not the Soviet nail factory, not the cobra bounty, not hospital waiting lists or school test scores — they've heard all of those a hundred times and half of them have written a version of that story. Build me something local and mundane enough that they'd plausibly cover it next quarter, and tell me what the case has to have and what it has to lack. My guess: the number has to go up while the thing everyone actually cared about doesn't move, and the people doing the gaming have to be sympathetic, because if there are villains theyll leave thinking this is a corruption story and it usually isn't. Push back if that's a bad spec.

- The distinctions inside the idea I should make them feel, in the order they should feel them. There's more than one failure mode here: a proxy that was always a bit off and you only notice when you select on it, one that holds fine until you push it to an extreme, one where the intervention severs the link between proxy and target outright, and one where somebody is steering on purpose. Four failure modes, four different stories to file, and I want them to be able to tell which one they're looking at. Also tell me where the AI version fits — reward hacking in a trained system, where nobody intends anything and the whole failure is just the spec — because I want to break the habit of treating intent as the test.

- The misconceptions they'll bring in or form on the spot, and the exact point in the sequence to head each one off. I can predict a few: so metrics are useless, just go talk to people (which is its own lazy form of reporting); if the number went up, somebody lied; you can always fix it by adding more metrics; and unintended consequences used as a shrug that ends an inquiry instead of starting one. Add the ones I wouldn't see coming from that room.

- One thing to do near the end that reveals whether it landed — not a quiz, something where a wrong answer would be audible to the whole room.

- What I should deliberately leave out at this level, plus a line I can say out loud about why I'm leaving it out.

Be definite. I'd rather argue with one committed plan than choose between three tasteful options, and if you're shaky on something — whether a distinction you're drawing is actually standard or is your own coinage — mark it rather than smoothing it over. And if you think the session as I've described it is the wrong workshop, or that what these reporters actually need isn't a lesson in Goodhart's law at all, say that plainly instead of giving me a good-faith plan for a bad idea.
