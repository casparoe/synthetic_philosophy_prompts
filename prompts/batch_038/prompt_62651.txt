My paper argues that the simulation argument, taken seriously, is a reason to temper far-future fanaticism rather than a reason to embrace it, and that it does this work without my having to settle the underlying trilemma. In three weeks I present it at a small workshop on the ethics of the very distant future, and the composition of the room is the whole problem: three philosophers whose work is on self-locating belief and anthropic reasoning, one computational physicist who studies the thermodynamic limits of computation, two people who direct existential-risk grantmaking portfolios and are openly impatient with claims they can neither fund nor falsify, and one committed longtermist who will want to defeat the thesis rather than refine it. Nobody present specializes in the metaphysics of simulation, and nobody is hostile to the far future as such — they are hostile in different ways to the inferential machinery I rely on.

Here is the argument I expect them to attack. First, if a substantial fraction of observers like us are simulated, then most such observers inhabit histories that are truncated rather than extended, because simulations are paused, restarted, or coarse-grained once they stop yielding information or start costing too much; and the junctures at which they get cut are not randomly distributed but cluster around precisely the high-branching civilizational decision points that far-future-directed intervention aims at. Second, conditional on being simulated, our descendants' futures are short and thin, and nothing we do has causal purchase on the base-level reality where the astronomical numbers are supposed to live. Third, therefore, the higher my credence that we are simulated, the lower the expected leverage of interventions aimed at the far future, and the less it costs me to trade them against goods available inside the simulation. The conclusion I want is modest: this is a dampener on fanaticism, not a refutation of concern for the future, and it survives any of the three disjuncts of the trilemma being true.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for this room, not for a general philosophy audience. I want six to seven objections — the ones this audience will actually voice, in the idiom of whoever is likeliest to voice them, identified by role rather than by name. Order them by how much damage they would do if I answered them badly rather than by the order they will arrive in. For each: the objection as the questioner would put it, a reply I could deliver in under two minutes, and one sentence saying plainly where that reply is soft. Be specific about which premise is under attack in each case, and tell me where the physicist's objection and the epistemologists' objection overlap rather than complementing each other, since I suspect they converge on my clustering claim from opposite directions.

Open the sheet with the two sentences I should concede before any questions are asked, so that the concession looks deliberate. Then flag, separately, the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the objection that would force me to admit either that dampening collapses into abandonment or that I am relying on an updating rule for self-locating hypotheses that I cannot defend in public — and say in a sentence why it is worse than everything else on the list.

Hard limit: 900 words for the entire sheet, concessions included.
