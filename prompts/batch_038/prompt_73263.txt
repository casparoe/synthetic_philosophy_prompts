Hofstadter's typogenetics exercise in Gödel, Escher, Bach is usually presented as a warm-up for Gödel numbering: strands over a four-letter alphabet are read in triplets, each triplet stands for one member of a small set of "amino acids," so that a strand's meaning is the enzyme it spells, and a strand counts as self-replicating when the enzyme it spells can bind and copy the very strand that spelled it. The intended parallel is with a quine, and behind the quine with the Gödel sentence, since in each case a fixed mechanical coding scheme supposedly lets an object pick out itself.

I am trying to pin down where that parallel is not merely loose but structurally misleading, and my suspicion turns on a difference in what the coding scheme does in the two cases. In arithmetic the coding is not only used but represented: the theory can express, as a function on numbers, the operation that takes the Gödel number of a formula and substitutes a numeral into it, and that expressibility is what the diagonal lemma exploits to produce a sentence that names itself. In typogenetics the codon table is only ever used — strands are copied and translated, but no object inside the system carries a manipulable description of the table, so a replicating strand never seems to pick itself out at all; it is merely recognized causally by machinery that already knows how to read it. Actual cells look the same way: the genetic code is implemented in synthetases, tRNAs and ribosomes, every one of which is itself a product of the code, yet the code nowhere exists as data the organism could inspect or rewrite.

With that setup in view, explain the conceptual issue: what precisely is the extra ingredient that allows a formal system to contain its own coding scheme rather than just be governed by one, and why does possessing that ingredient matter for whether the resulting self-reference is self-reference at all instead of self-recognition by an interpreter supplied from outside? Then say whether the ingredient is available in principle to a replicating system — whether anything recognizable as a cell could survive carrying a complete, internally readable, internally rewritable copy of its own translation rules, or whether acquiring such a copy would dismantle the very arrangement that makes replication possible. If you think my diagnosis is the wrong one — that the two cases are structurally identical after all, or that the real difference lies elsewhere, for instance in whether the fixed point is proved or merely instantiated — argue for the better account instead. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no preamble of any kind.
