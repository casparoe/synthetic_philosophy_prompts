I spent some forty years in comparative physiology, mostly on the energetics and morphology of vertebrate limbs, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than my former colleagues would have predicted. A reading group I joined is working through medieval rationalism alongside contemporary philosophy of biology, and one strand of Maimonides has left me genuinely perplexed in a way I cannot resolve on my own. In the third part of the Guide, as I read him, Maimonides argues that God did not simply abolish the sacrificial cult to which the Israelites were habituated, but redirected it to Himself — because human nature cannot abruptly abandon deeply entrenched custom, much as a newborn cannot pass at once from milk to solid food. He presses the point by noting that a prophet who today commanded us to abandon prayer and fasting in favour of purely mental worship would meet the same resistance. Yet in his legal code, in the section on kings and the messianic age, he maintains that the Messiah will rebuild the Temple and that all the statutes will be reinstated as in former times, sacrifices included, together with the sabbatical and jubilee years. I have the impression that elsewhere he distinguishes the general aim of a commandment, which follows from divine wisdom, from its particular details, which he treats as not further explicable; whether that distinction helps here is part of what I want to understand.

What puzzles me is not the historical question but a structural one about the notion of function, and here I suspect my own training has left me with a vocabulary that could be applied in a way I have not seen done. In biology we distinguish at least two accounts of a trait's function: the causal-role or dispositional account, on which a trait's function is whatever it currently contributes to the capacities of the system containing it, and the etiological or selected-effects account, on which a trait's function is fixed by the history of its retention — what it was selected for. The two come apart in familiar cases: vestigial structures, by-products of other features, traits whose current contribution differs entirely from the one that secured their retention. Artifacts complicate this further, since we usually treat an artifact's function as relative to the designer's intention, and yet in Maimonides' account the intention seems to be fixed partly by the recipient — by the cognitive habits and historical situation of the people addressed — rather than by the maker alone, which strikes me as a strange hybrid. So: when Maimonides explains the sacrificial cult by reference to its occasion, which account of function is he actually invoking? And what is the status of the practice's function under the messianic restoration, when the original occasion has by hypothesis vanished but the practice is nonetheless to resume? Does the restoration require a causal role available in that future age, does it make the cult a vestigial practice preserved without function, or is he committed to a third category — a function fixed by a contribution that is designed but not yet realized, rather like a trait whose expression is developmentally deferred?

There is a further point that I find sharper than the exegetical one, and I would like you to explain it as a conceptual matter rather than as a matter of who said what. In my own field an etiological finding is normatively inert: establishing that some structure originated as a by-product, or that its current use differs from its historical one, tells us nothing against it, because traits make no claim about themselves. In ethics and religion, by contrast, an etiological explanation of a norm — showing that it arose as a compromise with conditions that no longer obtain — is usually felt to count against the norm's authority, as though the origin contaminated the obligation. I have been calling this the etiological asymmetry, and I want to know what, if anything, really accounts for it: whether it follows from the fact that norms, unlike traits, purport to justify themselves and so carry a standing demand for reasons that outlive their occasion; whether it depends on a conflation of the reason a rule was adopted with the reason it remains binding; or whether the asymmetry is itself an artifact of applying a biological intuition where it does not belong. Then please bring the analysis back to Maimonides and tell me which of the following you think he is committed to: that the concession account undermines the perpetual obligation of the sacrificial cult, that it is perfectly compatible with it because the concession explains the timing and form of the institution rather than its warrant, or that his position can only be held together by an unstated account of what sacrifices will do in the age whose inhabitants, on his own diagnosis, will no longer need them. I am not after a survey of the secondary literature — I have no access to it and would rather you argue from the structure of the problem. I would simply like the conceptual issue laid out clearly, with the places where the analysis could reasonably go in more than one direction marked explicitly.
