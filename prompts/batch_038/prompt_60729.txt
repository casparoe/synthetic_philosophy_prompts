I spent the greater part of my working life in a national metrology laboratory, where my days were given over to calibration, traceability, and the rather unglamorous business of ensuring that when somebody wrote down a number, the number meant what everybody agreed it meant. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more enthusiasm than method: I sit as a lay member of the independent monitoring board at a Category B prison in the north of England, and I attend a reading group in which we have recently been working through the comparative literature on penology — Halden in Norway, which opened in 2010 to a capacity of some two hundred and fifty places in a village-like layout with unbarred windows; the Danish facility at Storstrøm, opened in 2017 on similar lines; and closer to home HMP Berwyn at Wrexham, opened in February 2017 with a capacity of a little over two thousand, which the British press habitually described in Scandinavian terms. We have also been reading John Pratt's two-part account of Scandinavian exceptionalism in the British Journal of Criminology in 2008, and the material on the housing unit at a medium-security prison in Pennsylvania which officers and visiting researchers came to call Little Scandinavia: begun in 2018, staffed partly by six men serving life sentences who were brought in as mentors in 2020, with single cells, a communal kitchen, Nordic-style furnishings and a planted outdoor area, later joined by twenty-nine further residents. What strikes my metrologist's eye is not the architecture but the endpoints. Every one of these schemes is justified by recidivism figures that are not measured the same way in the two jurisdictions, over the same follow-up window, or against the same denominator, and every one of them imports the visible parts of a system whose invisible parts are a welfare state.

I should like you to write a piece of satire for me to read aloud at the group — a mock client-facing brochure, of roughly one thousand to one thousand two hundred words, issued by a wholly invented American consultancy that licenses Scandinavian penal design to county jails, state departments of corrections and private custodial operators. Let us call it something flat and plausible; invent the firm, its trademarked programme names, its tier structure, its personnel and its testimonial-givers, and invent every number that appears. Do not name a real prison, a real company, a real jurisdiction or a living person anywhere in the piece, and do not report any real statistic: the joke is not that the figures are wrong but that the firm has produced its own. The register should be that of an architectural product specification spliced onto an investor deck — headers, footnotes, one boxed sidebar, a specification table if you like — and it must remain absolutely deadpan. No winking, no raised eyebrow, no sentence in which the brochure appears to know that it is absurd. The target is a practice and a style of discourse, namely the treatment of Nordic penality as a fit-out that can be procured, and not any individual or nationality.

The content must be built out of real features, exaggerated rather than fabricated, so that a specialist recognises every item on the list. The tier structure should make the reader do the work: a modest entry package of sightlines, timber, single cells, unbarred windows, planted courtyards and the removal of the dining hall; a middle package adding officer–resident interaction, shared meals, a two-year university-level officer qualification with its associated salary consequences, and the substitution of dynamic security for static security; and a flagship package offering the principle of normality itself — whose prerequisites, the brochure should note in passing footnote after footnote, are all supplied by parties outside the firm's control: municipal health, education, library and clergy providers under the import model, a housing market, a labour market, a benefits system, sentence lengths with an upper limit, and a political culture that will not claw the whole thing back the week after a single incident. Give the firm a trademarked internal metric, set out in the boxed sidebar with apparent rigour, whose function is to ensure that no amenity inside the walls exceeds what the poorest free households in the catchment enjoy; the brochure should present this as prudent risk management rather than as cruelty, because it believes that. Include an FAQ entry explaining, in technically impeccable and entirely honest-sounding language, why the Scandinavian outcome figures cannot be reproduced on the client's estate — different follow-up windows, rearrest against reconviction against re-imprisonment, who counts as released, what counts as a technical violation — so that the real reason is displaced by a measurement dispute of exactly the kind I used to have at work. Include two testimonials, one from an invented warden and one from an invented county commissioner, both sincere, both concerned with per-diem rates and with local press reaction. Close with a list of known limitations and matters not covered by warranty. Above all, let the firm be right about at least two things — it should have correctly understood that the building is the cheap component and that officer training and caseloads are the expensive ones, and it should say so plainly — because a fool would make this a strawman, whereas a competent consultancy makes it an indictment. By the end a reader who has never heard of less eligibility, the normality principle or the import model should be able to reconstruct all three objections on their own: that normality is a social settlement rather than a specification, that dynamic security is a labour-market question, and that any humane upgrade survives only so long as the endpoint measure is not quietly redefined. Keep the staff and the prisoners out of the line of fire throughout; the mockery belongs entirely to the discourse.
