I'm drafting a chapter for a collection on rights and social choice, and one of my co-contributors — an economist, very sharp, zero patience for philosophy — has read the draft and wants me to rewrite the section on Sen's 1970 impossibility-of-the-Paretian-liberal result. His claim is that the whole thing is just a Coase problem wearing a moral costume, and that I've been treating it as a deep puzzle when its really a footnote about missing markets.

Let me restate the case as I have it, so you can check my work too. Two people, one copy of a salacious novel, three possible outcomes: nobody reads it, the prude reads it, the lewd one reads it. The prude ranks them: nobody reads, best; then himself reading it (in disgust); then the lewd one reading it (and enjoying it), worst. The lewd one ranks: the prude being made to read it, best; then reading it himself; then nobody reading it. Give each person a protected pair — the prude is decisive over (nobody reads vs. the prude reads), the lewd one over (nobody reads vs. the lewd one reads) — add Pareto, and you get a cycle: lewd reads beats nobody reads by the lewd person's right, nobody reads beats prude reads by the prude's right, and prude reads beats lewd reads by unanimity.

My colleague says: treat each protected pair as an entitlement, exactly like a property right, and notice that Sen has simply banned trade. Allow the two to bargain and the lewd one pays the prude to read the book, which is the Pareto-best of the three outcomes anyway; and by the usual invariance reasoning it doesn't matter which way you initially allocate the entitlements, you get the same result. So the cycle is an artifact of a no-trading assumption, not a discovery about rights. He adds a second, seperate move: Sen modeled a right as decisiveness over a social ranking when a right is really a move in a game form, a protected choice, and once you model it that way the cycle doesn't even get going.

Here's what I want from you. First, the correspondence table for his analogy: what plays the role of the entitlement, the externality, the transaction costs, the bargaining, the efficiency benchmark, the invariance claim — and where each of those goes missing or changes character in the target case. Second, sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten his conclusion and the ones that are merely untidy. I suspect at least three are load-bearing but I can't get them straight: whether the prude's disgust at the lewd one's reading is compensable at all without the prude becoming a party to it; whether a trade presupposes a right to alienate rights, and if so whether that meta-right is itself exposed to a Sen-style paradox one level up, ad infinitum; and whether the efficiency his bargaining delivers is being measured by the very Pareto criterion that helped generate the cycle in the first place, which would make the reply circular rather than dissolving. Third, tell me whether his two moves are one objection or two — I have a feeling the game-form point survives even if the trading point collapses, and if so which of them I should actually engage with in print. Fourth, run his frame against two variants I cooked up for the chapter: (a) the two people never meet and no communication is possible, so bargaining is off the table physically rather than legally — does the paradox still bite, and does that strengthen or weaken Sen? and (b) the prude's objection is moral rather than aesthetic — he thinks the lewd one's enjoyment would be a wrong, not just an annoyance — so a payment would buy his complicity. Does the Coasean picture have anything to say there, or does it just fall silent? Fifth, I want to use the property rule / liability rule / inalienability rule trichotomy here: is a Sen-style protected pair a property rule or an inalienability rule, and if it's the latter, does labeling it that explain anything or just restate Sen's assumption in law-and-economics vocabulary? And sixth, if the analogy fails, is there a better one — something that captures why giving people decisive power over small personal pairs can produce collective incoherence without being a trading problem?

No citations needed, please — I'll chase the literature down myself. I want the argument.
