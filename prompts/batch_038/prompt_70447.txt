I have been building an argument for a paper whose question is whether Scandinavian trust or American suspicion is the better-supported attitude toward one's own government, and I can no longer tell whether the argument is sound or whether it proves far more than I can accept. It is meant to show that a familiar picture of high-trust societies is unstable. Here it is.

1. Trust in an institution is rational for a citizen only insofar as it is proportioned to that institution's trustworthiness. Trust that outruns the evidence is credulity, not a civic virtue.

2. An institution's trustworthiness depends on the scrutiny and sanctions its agents expect. Officials behave well when defection is likely to be detected and expensive; officials who expect to be taken on faith have one fewer reason not to defect.

3. Trust displaces vigilance. The more a citizen expects an institution to behave well, the less she invests in checking on it — reading the audit reports, attending the hearings, following the procurement notices, turning out to punish incumbents. Monitoring effort is costly, and trust lowers the expected return on paying that cost.

4. Therefore higher aggregate trust lowers the expected cost of official defection, and so lowers trustworthiness.

5. Since trustworthiness is what makes trust rational, higher trust makes trust less warranted. Trust consumes its own warrant.

6. So trust is self-limiting: a society's trust must erode through its own operation until it settles at a level low enough that the vigilance still being supplied suffices to keep trustworthiness at the level the trust presupposes. That equilibrium should sit well below what the high-trust democracies report.

7. Hence the Scandinavian pattern cannot be what it appears to be. Those societies report both the greatest trust in government and the least corruption. If the argument holds, one of the two must be an artifact: either their citizens are not as trusting as the surveys suggest, or their officials' good behavior is produced by something other than expected scrutiny — smallness, homogeneity, resource wealth, career civil services, or the way the corruption measures are built — in which case their trust is not tracking trustworthiness at all and is not evidentially warranted.

Two things leave me uneasy rather than confident.

My colleague points out that the same steps, unchanged, appear to show that a good marriage is irrational: you stop auditing a spouse you trust, trustworthiness falls, and so the trust was unwarranted all along. He treats this as a reductio of the argument. I am not sure whether it is a reductio, or simply an unpalatable truth about reliance in general that I should swallow.

Separately, the argument seems to entail a policy I find simultaneously attractive and repugnant: that the way to deserve a citizenry's trust is not to behave better but to make behavior cheap to inspect — publish every contract, meeting, and expense in real time — since on this account trust is only the residue left where verification is too expensive. I cannot decide whether to treat the resulting prediction as a refutation or as a sign that the surveys are measuring something other than what I assume. On the substitution reading, the most inspectable governments should generate the least reported trust, because their citizens would need the least of it; the surveys appear to run the other way, but I am not confident that the trust items and the corruption indices are even measuring the same layer of the phenomenon, so I do not know how much weight that mismatch can bear.

I want a diagnosis, not reassurance. Which step or steps fail, and what precisely goes wrong at each one you reject — an equivocation, a slide between levels of analysis, an unstated assumption about the direction of causation, a suppressed ceteris paribus, a fixed point being mistaken for a monotone decline? For each step you keep, identify the nearby true claim that makes it look acceptable; for each step you reject, say what giving it up costs, since I am especially loath to abandon (1), which looks to me like nothing more than the demand that trust be a response to the world, and (3), which looks like an ordinary fact about how individuals allocate attention. Then tell me whether the argument can be repaired — what must be added or weakened for it to survive contact with the cases in the previous two paragraphs — and whether the repaired version retains any bearing on the question I started with, namely whether a citizen of a high-trust, low-corruption democracy is better warranted than a citizen of a low-trust, high-scrutiny one, or whether the second is simply making a different and equally defensible bet.
