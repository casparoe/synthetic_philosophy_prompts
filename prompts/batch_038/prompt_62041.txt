> **Question 3 (20 marks).** In *Surfaces and Essences*, Hofstadter and Sander argue that analogy is not one cognitive ability among others but the core mechanism of thought itself. Explain what this thesis commits them to. Address: **(a)** the relation they posit between concepts and categories; **(b)** what they mean by the "essence" of a category, and how essence is supposed to differ from surface resemblance; **(c)** how, on their account, a particular act of analogising actually proceeds; and **(d)** what the thesis implies about the boundary between ordinary literal thought and figurative or metaphorical thought. Be concrete: include at least one example of your own.

> **Student's answer.** Hofstadter and Sander's central claim is that analogy is the core of cognition rather than a special-purpose skill occasionally deployed by poets and physicists. On their view, to understand anything new is to see it as an instance of a category one already possesses; thought is a continuous, lifelong process of category construction, and there is no separate faculty of "reasoning" sitting above it that manipulates symbols according to rules.
>
> (a) This requires them to collapse the usual distinction between concepts and categories. A concept just is the category of things it applies to, together with the history of its construction in a particular mind; concepts are not mental definitions subsequently applied to a pre-sliced world. It follows that concepts have no crisp boundaries: membership is graded, and which items fall under a concept shifts with context, because a given situation can activate different facets of the same concept.
>
> (b) What they call the essence of a category is not its surface appearance. Two situations can look nothing alike and still belong to the same category — a rocket lifting off and a squid jetting backwards through water, say — while two things that look nearly identical may be essentially unrelated. To grasp an essence is to grasp the fixed, defining core that makes a thing what it is beneath its incidental perceptual features. Once that core is in hand we have a stable criterion: an item either instantiates the essence or it does not, and the halo of borderline cases surrounding it is a defect in our knowledge rather than a feature of the concept itself.
>
> (c) An act of analogising, on their account, proceeds by retrieving a familiar source domain from memory and projecting its relational structure onto a less familiar target domain, preserving one-to-one correspondences between the relations in each. Retrieval fixes which domain plays the source; the target is then read in the light of that source, and the more systematic the resulting correspondence, the stronger the analogy.
>
> (d) Metaphor therefore does not constitute a distinct mode of thought requiring its own explanation. When we speak of a flood of complaints or an icy reception we are activating the same categories in a less customary context. The difference between literal and figurative use is a matter of how familiar and entrenched the categorisation is, not of two different mechanisms. My own example: in reading that a firm is bleeding money I am doing nothing special — I am placing it in a category whose essence I built up over years of encounters with wounds and with loss.
>
> One further implication is worth flagging. Their position makes cognition essentially non-computational: because the essences we grasp are fluid and open-ended, no fixed program of symbol manipulation could reproduce the process, which is why the authors insist that analogy is a matter of high-level perception rather than of rule-following. This, I think, is the boldest part of the thesis.
