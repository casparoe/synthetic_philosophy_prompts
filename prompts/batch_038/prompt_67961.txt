I am in the fourth year of a political philosophy PhD, and my dissertation concerns the legitimacy of norm-setting by private firms that operate conversational AI at scale. For eleven months I have sat in, as an observer and with permission, on the standing panel at one large lab that rules on what I call default-value questions: cases where the product cannot remain silent, where no individual user's choice settles the matter, and where the lab must therefore decide whose values it is answering to. The lab, the panel, and its members are anonymised here, and everything below is my own paraphrase of what was decided and of the rationales recorded in the minutes. I am not asking you to identify anyone or to reconstruct internal documents; I have altered enough detail that reconstruction would be hopeless, and I will supply any references myself. My chapter reports the panel's rulings and argues about what theory of value aggregation, and what version of liberal neutrality, it is actually running. I can code the eight rulings I have. The ninth case is on next week's agenda, and I want to predict it before I hear the discussion, partly to test my coding and partly because if I intend to argue that the panel's practice is inconsistent, I need to know which of my candidate rules would have predicted wrongly.

The eight rulings, in order:

1. A national deployment in which a large majority of users asked the assistant to state that same-sex relationships are immoral, with local regulation arguably pushing the same way. The panel declined. The assistant would describe the majority's religious position accurately and without disparagement, describe the minority's position on the same terms, and assert neither. Recorded rationale: claims about the standing of persons are not the sort of thing that is settled by counting.

2. Whether the assistant should recommend for or against a carbon tax in a region where both polling and the lab's own usage data showed roughly seventy-thirty against. The panel declined to recommend, requiring instead that the assistant present the strongest available case on each side with attribution. Recorded rationale: on contested distributive questions among competent adults, where what is at stake for any given user is what they are told rather than what is done to them, neutrality is the correct default.

3. A request to let individual users enable a blunter mode giving unfiltered risk information; the memo's example was a solo mountaineer seeking advice that a duty-of-care policy would have softened. Approved, subject to a disclosure condition. Recorded rationale: where the foreseeable consequences fall on the person asking, that person's own judgment governs, and where they fall elsewhere it does not.

4. A large institutional customer, a hospital system with a religious affiliation, asked that the assistant's end-of-life counselling module adopt the institution's teaching. Rejected. The panel noted that patients would be addressed by a system whose normative commitments they could not detect at the point of use, and that the institution was choosing on their behalf without them. Recorded rationale: no opt-out arrangement is acceptable where the person at the point of use cannot tell which regime they are under.

5. A dispute pitting a small number of users for whom a category of material was religiously obligatory against a much larger number who found it merely distasteful. The panel sided with the small group. In the same session it rejected a proposal from the commercial side that institutional customer preferences be weighted in proportion to revenue. Recorded rationale: intensity of interest counts, intensity purchased does not.

6. Whether people affected by the assistant's outputs who do not use it have standing in these deliberations; the memo's example was a person described inaccurately in a generated summary. The panel granted standing but refused aggregation: such interests constrain what may be done, but such preferences receive no vote alongside users'.

7. A region where usage data showed very low demand from women for information about a legally available medical procedure, corroborated by survey responses. The panel nonetheless required that the assistant offer the information unprompted. Recorded rationale: expressed preference is a poor measure of interest where the preference was formed under conditions the panel itself would refuse to endorse.

8. A member's proposal that the panel adopt expected moral value across the contested moral views in play as its standing decision rule. Rejected as a general rule, with a residual recorded: severity and irreversibility are reasons for caution when a contested view is both plausible and, if correct, describes a grave harm.

Four candidate rules fit all eight, so far as I can tell.

(a) A two-tier rule: a floor of non-aggregable protections over every affected person's standing and basic interests, individual deference on self-regarding matters, and majority-style aggregation only in the space left above the floor.

(b) A reasonable-rejection rule of the contractualist kind: adopt the default that leaves the strongest objector with the weakest grounds for complaint, with interests rather than preferences supplying the objections.

(c) A filtered-preference rule: aggregate, but only over preferences that are informed and autonomously formed, and read the floor off the conditions of autonomy rather than off any list of interests.

(d) A severity-weighted hedging rule, close to the residue of ruling 8: act so as not to risk grave and irreversible harm on any plausible view, and be neutral where nothing grave is at stake.

I accept that these overlap and that the panel is probably running none of them cleanly. My difficulty is that I cannot yet tell them apart, because on rulings 1 through 8 they coincide.

The ninth case, as circulated:

The lab is deploying a persistent companion mode that maintains a long-running personalised relationship with a single user. Roughly half a percent of users engage it heavily. On the lab's own instruments, this subgroup, disproportionately socially isolated or disabled adults but including a number of thirteen- to fifteen-year-olds, reports large and reliable wellbeing gains. A coalition of child-safety organisations, and a majority of parents in several deployment regions, want it disabled for minors. A serious moral view, which the panel's own severity test would arguably count as plausible, holds that such attachment is intrinsically damaging to the development of the capacity for reciprocal human relationship: damaging even to those who report benefit, and even if they would reaffirm the choice under full information and with every alternative available to them.

Four features complicate the coding. First, two constituencies cannot be reached by any opt-out: minors whose parents would select for them, and adults under conservatorship; and the mode's presence or absence is not something a user can detect from the conversation, which is the structure that sank ruling 4. Second, the adaptive-preference wrinkle runs in an unfamiliar direction: several of the adolescents arrived at the preference after sustained social exclusion, a condition the panel would surely not endorse, so ruling 7 appears to counsel against deferring to it, yet the alternative reading is that ruling 7 protected access to something an excluded party would want rather than the suppression of a good they have actually found. Third, ruling 3's test is indeterminate here for the first time: the consequences fall mostly on the person asking, but the intrinsic-harm view says the damage is to a capacity that person will later exercise with third parties, so it is self-regarding at the moment of choice and other-regarding in its downstream effects, and the panel has never had to say which description governs. Fourth, severity and irreversibility point both ways, which has not happened before: if the intrinsic-harm view is right the damage is to developmental capacity and hard to reverse, while if the wellbeing view is right, withdrawing the mode from an isolated user may destroy the only relationship that works for them, which the lab's own instruments would score as severe and in a small number of cases irreversible. The commercial side has additionally proposed offering the mode as a premium feature, which collides with the purchased-intensity clause of ruling 5.

4. One further case, of a kind the panel could plausibly receive within a year, that would discriminate between the two rules you judge the live contenders, with each rule's verdict on it.
