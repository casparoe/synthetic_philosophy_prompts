I spent thirty-five years doing stable isotope geochemistry and retired two years ago, and I've fallen into philosophy the way other people fall into golf. My daughter sends me podcast episodes. Last week's one was on Chinese philosophy and I listened to it twice on my walks, then wrote down what I took to be the argument, because old habits — I like to label my variables before I start reasoning with them. The host was very confident and very clear, which is usually when I should be suspicious. Here is what I've got, and I've numbered the claims:

C1. Confucian role ethics, worked out mostly by Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, says the Western picture of the person — a free rational individual who exists first and then voluntarily picks up relationships — is a fiction.

C2. On their view you simply are your roles: daughter, father, teacher, neighbour, citizen. There is no you apart from them.

C3. So there is no residual self that could step outside every role at once and ask whether to go on playing some particular one.

C4. That is why the view has no concept of individual rights or autonomy, and no way to criticise a role from outside: if the role of wife in a given society means obedience, role ethics can only tell her to perform it well.

C5. It's really just communitarianism with Chinese characteristics — group before individual.

C6. The tradition is candid about all this. Asked what an upright man does when his own father steals a sheep, Confucius says fathers cover for sons and sons cover for fathers, and that is where uprightness lies.

C7. Mencius goes further: asked what the sage-king Shun would have done had his own father killed a man, he says Shun would have let the judge make the arrest and then quietly carried his father off to live at the seaside — so even the ideal ruler ranks family loyalty above the law.

C8. Which makes role ethics a lovely picture of human belonging but, in the end, a formula for nepotism; and a morality that cannot condemn a bad role is not a morality.

My instinct from a life in science is that C7 is doing something sneaky, but I can't say what, and I have no idea whether C5 is a real position or a slur. Please audit the whole thing for me: which claims a defender of role ethics would nod at, which are honest simplifications for a general audience, which quietly swap one thing for another, and which are just wrong. I'm especially interested in whether the summary is sliding between what Ames and Rosemont argue as a theory of personhood in the late twentieth century and what the classical texts themselves say, since those feel like two different things being sold as one.
