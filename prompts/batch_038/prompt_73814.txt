I'm one of five maintainers of a mid-sized open-source database library, and I'd like an adjudication of an argument that ran for three days in our public governance channel between two other members of the steering committee. I'll call them S and T. I was reading along and didn't take a side, and I've tried not to write this up in a way that leans toward either of them. I'm an engineer with no formal philosophy training, so please don't assume I know the vocabulary — gloss any term you need in a clause.

Undisputed background:

- Donations arrive through per-maintainer tip pages, so each donor picks whose page they fund. One maintainer, R, receives roughly 90% of the flow. He wrote the query module most users come for, and he gives the conference talks.
- The proposal on the table folds all donations into a single pool, distributed half in equal shares and half by hours logged in the issue tracker. Under it R would receive about 35%.
- A fact that surfaced only in passing and was never argued about: R had roughly a two-year head start on the rest of us, during which his employer let him travel and present on company time. Nobody else on the committee had anything like that.

The exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can:

1. **S:** People chose to send that money to R through a page with his name on it. Reassigning it afterward substitutes our judgment for the choices that produced the current split in the first place. There's a philosopher's example I read about years ago — a basketball star whose fans each paid a quarter on top of their ticket to watch him, so that a million small voluntary payments made him enormously rich — and the moral was that a distribution built out of free choices can't be second-guessed just because the result looks lopsided. Lopsidedness isn't an injustice.

2. **T:** The donors' choices settled where the money landed, not who gets to keep it on whatever terms he likes. Once it's R's, the only live question is whether R agrees to the pool — and R sits on this committee and votes on it, so his agreement is precisely what we're deciding. If the proposal carries over his no, what follows is that he stops maintaining the module. That's something he's free to do, and it isn't anyone reaching into his account.

3. **S:** That freedom is nominal. The module is worth what it is only inside this project — its users, its release cycle, its docs, its tracker. Handing him 'take 35% or walk away from the thing you built' is handing him a choice in which one of the two options isn't real. Consent extracted against that option set isn't consent, and you'd say so yourself about a contract signed at that price under those circumstances.

4. **T:** Then look at what the money is actually for. R's module is valuable because the rest of us triage the issues filed against it, review his patches, write the docs users arrive through, and keep CI green — none of which is compensated at all. The donations are payment for a jointly produced thing that happens to have one recognizable face on it. Splitting the pool isn't imposing a pattern onto free exchanges; it's an accounting of who made what's being paid for.

5. **S:** Two problems. First, that accounting begs the question: calling everyone else's unpaid work a claim on R's donations is already an entitlement principle, and then you say the pool is legitimate because it tracks entitlements. Second, you've chosen logged hours as the measure, and hours has nothing to do with what any donor chose. It's a formula. You could have picked need, or seniority, or lines of review, and each would give a different split — which tells you the formula isn't discovered, it's selected by us.

6. **T:** Hours is a proxy and I'll grant it's crude; I'd rather argue about better proxies than about principles. But your first objection, if it worked, would rule out every distributional justification including the one supporting the status quo, since 'the person whose name is on the module made the thing' is also somebody's implicit accounting. And the example you're leaning on doesn't do the work you need. It shows that free transfers will drift you away from whatever split you start with, and so that you can't hold a split fixed without continuously interfering with people's choices. It doesn't show the starting split is the right one, and it doesn't show that a single re-pooling — adopted by vote, in advance, under a published rule — is unjust.

7. **S:** In advance, by rule, and then again next year, and the year after, because the drift you just described never stops. A rule that has to be reapplied every cycle to keep its outcome in place is continuous interference with a calendar attached. If you're now claiming only a difference of degree, let's discuss degree: the reset runs against the one person the donors actually selected, every single time. A rule with that property isn't neutral about whose choices count.

What I'd like from you:

(a) Go through moves 1–7 and rate each one on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating. Where a single move does two things — one defensible, one not — split it and rate the parts separately rather than averaging them.

(b) S invokes the basketball example and T reinterprets it. Neither of them named the philosopher or the book, and I haven't gone looking. Tell me whether each of them is using that argument in a way its author would recognize, and specifically whether S is claiming more for it than it can bear and whether T is claiming less.

(c) Say who is more right overall, and then give me the strongest version of the losing side's case — the argument they should have made and didn't.

(d) Say what both of them left on the table. I'm especially curious whether the two-year head start matters to the dispute at all, and whether there's some arrangement of the donation system that would dissolve it rather than settle it.

(e) Mark your confidence as you go: tell me which of your ratings you'd defend under pressure and which are genuine coin flips, and end by naming the single point in your answer you're least sure of and what would settle it.

Please don't give me a reading list or citations; I can't chase them and they aren't what I'm after. I want your judgment on this particular argument, not a survey of the positions people have taken on it.
