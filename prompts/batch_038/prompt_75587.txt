I am in the third week of a graduate seminar on minds and machines, and this week's reading was the Gödelian anti-mechanist line: Lucas's original paper, and Penrose's later book-length versions of it, including his claim that the relevant notion is soundness rather than consistency. After class two of my classmates argued for the better part of an hour in the coffee room. I wrote the exchange down as accurately as I could, because I found it hard to follow and harder still to referee, and our convenor has asked each of us to submit a written adjudication of some argument from the week. Neither participant is a strawman: A is a second-year working on philosophy of mathematics, and B did formal logic as an undergraduate and now writes software for a living. I would rather not hand in my own reconstruction of who was right, since I suspect I have absorbed A's framing more than I realize, so I am asking you to do the adjudication. The two of them also coined a bit of seminar-local vocabulary along the way, which I have preserved.

(1) A: Suppose my mind is a Turing machine M. Then there is a sentence G(M) which M cannot prove but which I can recognize as true, since it says of itself that M does not prove it, and if M is consistent that is exactly right. So I can see something M cannot. Hence I am not M; and since M was arbitrary, no machine is me.

(2) B: The step from "M is consistent, so G(M) is true" to "I can see that G(M) is true" requires you to know that M is consistent. But if you are M, then knowing that is knowing your own consistency, which is precisely what a consistent system cannot do for itself. The premise is unavailable to the one person who needs it.

(3) A: Penrose anticipated that, which is why he shifts the ground. Drop consistency for soundness. Suppose I am a sound algorithm F. Then I can reflect on F and see that whatever F asserts is true — that is what makes F a candidate for me in the first place — and from the soundness of F I can infer the truth of F's Gödel sentence. If it turns out that I cannot run that reflection, I have refuted the supposition that I am a sound algorithm, which is still anti-mechanism. Either way the mechanist loses.

(4) B: That is a disjunctive argument with one empty horn. Either you can see that you are sound, in which case you have established your own consistency by another route, which is impossible; or you cannot, in which case you have shown only that self-transparent soundness is not on offer, not that mechanism is false. A consistent machine need not be able to certify its own soundness, and neither need you.

(5) A: Then retreat to the weaker claim we were calling the 'identification problem'. Mechanism is not the bare claim that I happen to be some machine or other; it is the claim that someone could tell me which one and hand over the specification. For any candidate M that is actually exhibited to me, program in hand, I can construct G(M) and, provided it is consistent, see that it is true — something M cannot do. So every machine anyone can ever hold up as me is refutable, and that is all the anti-mechanist needs.

(6) B: But no working computationalist holds that thesis. I can be implemented by a process whose code no one, including me, can effectively enumerate — that is exactly the epistemic situation of a large trained network with respect to its own behavior. And if my set of arithmetical assertions is not effectively enumerable, there is no M to diagonalize against in the first place. Your argument now refutes a claim about introspective transparency, not a claim about implementation.

(7) A: There is an idealization asymmetry you are skipping. If you idealize me far enough to recognize G(M) reliably, you may say I have become superhuman. But idealize the machine the same way and you get a machine that recognizes its own consistency, which Gödel's second theorem rules out. So the two idealizations are not parallel: the human can be idealized coherently, and the machine cannot be idealized into knowing its own soundness.

(8) B: The human idealization is incoherent too. Grant me the power to recognize every true arithmetical statement and you have proved a theorem about arithmetically omniscient beings, which tells you nothing about me. Grant me only finite, fallible powers and I make arithmetic errors, at which point you can pad the machine's program with exactly the same error model and the asymmetry vanishes. You get to choose the idealization only if you get to choose it twice, in opposite directions.

(9) A: Then note what is really at stake. Nobody disputes that some physical process in my skull computes; obviously it does. The question is whether I am identical to a computation. The Gödel move is a probe for the difference between executing a procedure and understanding one, and it is the only probe on the table that is actually sharp.

(10) B: It is a diagnosis, not a refutation — which is the other phrase we kept reaching for. Run it on a toy program that enumerates the theorems of a weak arithmetic by bounded search. That program has a Gödel sentence it cannot see. Nobody concludes from this that the program understands nothing, because we already knew it understood nothing. Conversely, no program anyone actually proposes as a mind is ever presented to you with its code, so the probe never fires where the question is live. Your argument yields something about formal systems in general and nothing about minds in particular, and it is compatible with everything computationalists claim.

Please go through (1) to (10) one at a time and rate each move — sound, partly sound, or mistaken — saying in each case what exactly turns on it. I am especially interested in the places where one of them has mis-stated the logic rather than merely overclaimed; I suspect (3) and (7) each smuggle something, but I am not sure what, and I would rather you found the problems yourself than confirmed my hunch. In particular, be precise about (7): say whether an idealized machine could be extended so as to prove the consistency of the system it started from, and what that does to the asymmetry A is claiming. Then tell me which concessions each of them made that they should not have, whether there is a move neither of them made that would have settled the exchange, and which single premise the loser would have to abandon in order to stop losing. I want the technical verdict to be correct rather than diplomatic: if one of them simply wins, say so plainly.
