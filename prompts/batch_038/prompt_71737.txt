I am a second-year graduate student in archaeology. This term I hold a curatorial assistantship in my department's teaching collection while also taking the required method-and-theory seminar, and the two have collided in a way I would like help with.

The collection is small — roughly four thousand catalogued objects from a single multi-period site in the Near East that the department excavated between the 1970s and the late 1990s — and every year we reinstall one case and rewrite its interpretive text. Next month we open a case of about thirty objects whose labels have not been revised since the 1990s. The current drafts were written by three different people: a retired faculty member, an undergraduate on work-study, and me.

The difficulty is that the drafts make claims of wildly different ambition, and the department has no shared standard for what a label is permitted to assert. One draft describes a basalt object with a worn depression as a quern. Another says of the same object that it shows household grain processing was women's work. A third calls a clay figurine recovered from a domestic courtyard evidence of a household fertility cult. A fourth argues that a set of near-identical small stone weights found on three successive floors demonstrates a regulated market. Our supervising curator signs off on the final text, and she has told me plainly that she wants a written procedure she can apply consistently across the case rather than settling each label by argument in a meeting.

Please write that procedure. It should be something a person can run on a single draft label in about twenty minutes, with no specialist training beyond one introductory course, and it should decide: whether the claim is one the object together with its excavation record can support; at what strength it may be stated; and what must be cut or hedged. Specifically, I would like it to do the following.

1. Sort claims into types according to what they assert. I have in mind something like the differences between how an object was made, what it was used for, what it was deposited alongside, what it meant to the people who handled it, what those people believed, and what institutional arrangements it implies. For each type, state what evidence would be required to license it. If you think my list carves the space badly, replace it — but explain what is wrong with mine.

2. Handle the analogy problem explicitly. Many of the drafts lean on comparison with an ethnographic or later historic case. The procedure needs a test for when such a comparison is doing real work and when it is merely decorative, and it should say what the user is supposed to check.

3. Build in the excavation record itself: findspot, association with other finds, the matrix, whether the object is in primary or secondary position, and whether the record comes from a recent season or from a 1970s trench whose field notes may be all that survives. A substantial fraction of our objects have thin or ambiguous records. The procedure must tell the user what to do in that situation rather than assuming a clean context.

4. Say what to do when several readings are equally consistent with the evidence — whether the label should advance one, present all of them, or decline to interpret.

5. Mark the steps where the procedure is in fact asking the user to exercise judgment and cannot be made mechanical, and identify where it will err systematically. My expectation is that it will be too conservative in some places and will wave through things it should not in others; I want both failure modes named rather than discovered later.

Then run the procedure on three of the drafts described above — the worn basalt object, the courtyard figurine, and the stone weights — showing the intermediate answers at each step, not only the final verdict. For at least one of the three, the procedure should yield a result I would be unhappy with. I would far rather learn now that it licenses the fertility-cult language, or alternatively that it forbids us to call the basalt object a quern, than learn it in front of the curator. Please do not invent excavation details I have not given you: wherever the procedure needs a fact I have not supplied, have it stop and issue a specific request to the site archive, and state what it would then do with each possible answer.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. Our collection contains objects that no amount of contextual rigor will ever make interpretable, and cases where the honest text is simply uninformative. I need to know whether adopting this procedure will drive us toward a wall of agnostic labels, and whether you regard that as a cost worth paying or as evidence that the procedure has been misdesigned.
