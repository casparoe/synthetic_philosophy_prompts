I'm a staff writer at a magazine where I mostly cover psychology and neuroscience, with the occasional detour into the measurement-obsessed end of self-improvement. A few years back I was reporting a piece on people who track everything about themselves, and in the interests of method I built my own tracker and then, when the piece got spiked, just kept going. Three years now. The format isn't original to me — I lifted it from an old memoir I read in college and couldn't tell you whose it was if you asked. Thirteen rules I wrote for myself, each with a one-line gloss, and a few of the glosses came from the book too: one about not spending time on pointless conversation, one about giving every object its place and every task its hour, one about never pretending to be something you're not. The last one, about not thinking too well of yourself, I only added after a friend told me I'd become insufferable. The rules run down the side of a little grid, days of the week across the top. One rule gets my full attention for a week; every evening I go back through the day and put a black mark beside any rule I broke. Thirteen weeks to a cycle, four cycles a year, and I've done maybe ten cycles.

Here's the trouble, and its three seperate troubles and I can't tell which ones are real. (1) My marks are way down since year one — like sixty percent fewer — but I've come to suspect that's because I've stopped being anywhere I could collect one. I don't do the late dinners anymore, I left the group chat, I skip the bar thing after deadline, I mostly work alone. My wife has a less charitable version: you're not more patient, you just don't talk to people. She also says a person with no visible faults is exhausting to be married to, and I've never known how to answer that. So the ledger measures how often I fail, and the cheapest way to fail less is to never get tested. Is a clean record I bought by shrinking my life worth anything? (2) The rule about places and hours — the organizational one — was the one I broke most by a mile, and somewhere in year two I quietly stopped marking it, on the reasoning that being a mess isn't a moral failing, it's logistics, it doesn't belong on a list like this. Except it was load-bearing: when my calendar is a disaster I blow four of the other rules that week. So did I make a legitimate discovery, that one item on my list was never a virtue to begin with, or did I do the single most classic thing a person with a dashboard can do, which is delete the metric you're losing on? (3) The self-audit problem. One of the rules is about not misrepresenting things, which means the rule about honesty applies to whether I honestly record honesty violations, and I've caught myself at eleven at night re-deciding whether something counted, sometimes in my favor, sometimes against. Worse, I've started performing the rules for the grid. I'm pretty sure you can't mark yourself as not thinking too well of yourself without voiding it on the spot. Also, there was a philosopher I interviewed for the killed piece who said something that hasn't left: that you can't install character one item at a time, like apps, because these aren't separate capacities — get one right and most of the rest come along with it, get one badly wrong and the rest are cosmetic. If he's right, the whole weekly-rotation design isn't a slow method, it's a category mistake, and I've spent three years doing it very diligently.

Can you help me think this through? Put yourself in the shoes of a moral philosopher who's also spent real time in the behavioral-science and habit-formation literature — someone who'll take a self-tracking rig seriously as a piece of ethics rather than as a productivity hack, and who won't just tell me to be nicer to myself. Please actually think carefully rather than answering off the cuff: start with some brainstorming about what's genuinely at stake in each of the three problems — what each one assumes about what a virtue is, what a record of behavior is even evidence of, and whether an unreachable target can be worth pursuing — and only then give me your verdicts. Then rank the three problems by severity: which is a real defect in the thing, which is a fixable design flaw, and which is me being precious about a spreadsheet, and for each say what would actually have to change. Finally answer the practical question — keep it as is, restructure it, or quit — and if restructure, be specific about what the new version looks like. One constraint: I can't look anything up (I'm on a train to moderate a panel about measurement in psychology, which feels pointed), so no reading list, no bibliography, no see-also section. Just your own reasoning, though obviously invoke whatever names, cases, or thought experiments you need to make the points land.
