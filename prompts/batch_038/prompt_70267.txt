Please answer as a working philosopher of causation and moral responsibility, and think the argument through carefully before you reply. I have no academic background in any of this; I built it myself over a few months, originally to settle whether I ought to feel guilty about not giving to charity, and it has turned into something I cannot get out of.

Suppose omissions can be causes, in the ordinary way:

1. Causation is difference-making. If E would not have occurred had C not occurred, then C caused E.
2. This morning I did not transfer five hundred pounds to a famine-relief agency.
3. Had I transferred it, some child who in fact died of malnutrition last month would have been fed and would be alive today.
4. So my not transferring it caused her death.
5. Causation is transitive: her death caused her younger brother's abandonment, so my not transferring it caused that too.
6. Nothing in steps 1–5 mentions me in particular. By the same reasoning I caused the deaths of every patient who died last year waiting for an organ I was not a match for, and Queen Victoria caused them too, by not having been a microbiologist.
7. On a view where everyone, and equally no one, causes everything bad that happens, "cause" has stopped marking any distinction.
8. Therefore 1 is false: omissions are not causes.

I would very much like to keep 1 — it is the only handle I have on the concept. So I assume 3, 5, or 6 must go, but I cannot say which, or what I lose by dropping it.

Diagnose it for me. Begin with the step or steps you reject and state in one line what is wrong with each; then explain why each of them looked perfectly safe when I wrote it down; then price the alternatives — what a view costs if it keeps that step and gives up 1 instead. If you think the argument is sound and 7 is mere squeamishness about a promiscuous causal relation, say so plainly and show me how such a view can still tell me anything about this morning.
