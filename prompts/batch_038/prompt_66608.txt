I've been rereading Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" — the piece where he takes every stock defense of generic "man" and generic "he" and re-runs it with race slotted in instead, so the paragraph you'd have nodded along to becomes unbearable while not one logical step changes. Honestly it's the mechanism I care about rather than the politics: the arguments about tradition and elegance, about how context makes the inclusive sense obvious anyway, about hypersensitive objectors, about confusing the word with the world — they all survive perfectly well right up until you swap which word is sitting in the slot.

Could you write me a short story that runs the same operation on a fresh pair of terms? Nothing gendered, nothing racial, nothing Hofstadter already used. Invent a word that in your story-world everybody reaches for when they mean person, where one group is technically covered by it but clearly wasnt what anyone pictured, and put your protagonist on the defending side — usage panel, translation desk, standards committee, corporate style guide, whatever — someone whose actual job is explaining to complainers why the word is fine. The defending arguments should be genuinely good and symapthetic, not strawmen: the traditionalist is right that the inclusive sense really is understood, and the objector is right that understanding it isnt the same as being pictured, and I want both of those visible in the scenes rather than announced.

Somewhere in the middle I want the in-fiction version of Hofstadter's trick: the protagonist gets assigned the parallel usage note for some other word and discovers the argument they've been making all week doesnt transfer. But dont hand it over as a speech or a realization-in-the-shower — make it a work task with a deadline and someone watching over their shoulder.

Two more asks. The transposition should cost the protagonist something real (Hofstadter gets to stop when the essay ends; a character who has to say the loaded version out loud, over and over, to prove a point doesnt), and I dont want the word fixed and everyone relieved at the end. Leave it unresolved. Also, no essay-in-disguise please — scenes, dialogue, roughly 1500 words.
