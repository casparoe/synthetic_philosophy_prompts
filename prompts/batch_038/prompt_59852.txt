I'm a TA for a second-year module that's roughly "games, information and disagreement" — a mix of econ and philosophy students, 24 of them — and I've got my own 75-minute seminar slot in week 6. They've had one lecture on conditionalisation and they've had Aumann's agreement theorem stated at them, but in the last tutorial they basically treated it as a bit of formal trickery: nobody really believes that two people who've pooled everything have to end up agreeing, and one of them said something like "surely you can just have different starting points and that's that." Which is... actually the whole question, and the lecturer has asked me to make them feel that the common prior assumption is doing real work rather than being bookkeeping. I don't want to lecture at them or write the theorem on the board a third time. I want them to run into it.

So design me an in-class game that does this. My rough idea — improve on it or bin it — is two rounds. Round one, their priors genuinely are common because they come from a public device we all watch together (a bag, a dice roll, whatever you pick), they get private signals, and then they have to post their posteriors and try to make bets with each other; I want them to discover that a bet each of them was privately sure was favourable keeps collapsing the moment it gets announced. Round two, they arrive with genuinely different starting points, implemented not as a stipulation from me but as different learning histories — each subgroup was raised on a differently biased sample of past cases, and they know which subgroup they're in — and now bets go through and they happily agree to disagree. The debrief I actually care about is the bit afterwards, where someone asks whether round two can be redescribed as a common prior plus the extra evidence "which subgroup you're in", and if it can, whether the assumption is restrictive at all or just unfalsifiable, and if it can't, what the difference is supposed to consist in. Don't hand me a resolution of that — I want the questions staged so the class has to sit in it for a while.

I need it to be actually runnable: rules I could print and hand out, materials that cost basically nothing (we have a whiteboard, phones and a printer), a scoring scheme that isn't real money — small participation marks or something equally cheap is fine — and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 minutes with 24 people in whatever group structure you want. Also tell me what will predictably break the first time I run it. My suspicions: they'll botch the arithmatic on the posteriors, someone will treat posting their posterior as a competition and bluff, and risk aversion will kill bets that should have gone through even in the common-prior round, which would wreck the contrast I'm trying to set up. Tell me how to design around each of those, or tell me I'm wrong that they matter. And be straight with me about which part of the idea the game just can't reproduce — I already know the awkward one, which is that in my activity the common prior is something I manufacture in front of them while in real life nobody knows whether there is one, so I'd like you to say how much of that gap I can close inside the debrief and how much I just have to admit out loud at the end.
