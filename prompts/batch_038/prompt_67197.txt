I am revising a novel in which a disproportionate amount of the emotional work is done by characters who say things about their own minds that ought, strictly speaking, to be unsayable. My editor, who read philosophy as an undergraduate, has twice written "Moore's paradox — cut" in the margin, and I have reached the point where I need a criterion of my own rather than her authority or my ear alone. I understand the general shape of the difficulty: these sentences are not formal contradictions, so whatever is wrong with them must be wrong with the saying of them, or with the mind that could produce the saying, rather than with the proposition asserted; I know enough of the literature to be aware that accounts differ over whether the culprit is a norm of assertion, the transparency of belief, or belief's characteristic orientation toward truth. What I want is a principle precise enough to adjudicate lines I have not yet written.

Here are my verdicts, arrived at by reading the passages aloud and by watching two friends' faces. "It's raining, but I don't believe it is": cut — no character can utter this sincerely and remain intelligible. "It's raining, but I don't believe that it is raining": cut, and somehow worse, though the content seems the same and the only difference is that the second clause spells out what the second clause is about. "It rained last night, but I didn't believe it at the time": keep, entirely natural. "He's out in the garden, but I don't believe it": keep — a person reporting distrust of testimony. "My husband is seeing another woman, but I don't believe it": keep, and this is the line my editor most objects to; it is structurally the first line with a substitution, yet it is precisely what a self-deceived person says, and readers recognise themselves in it. "It's raining, but I don't find that I believe it": keep — the introspective phrasing seems to drain the absurdity. "If it's raining, then I don't believe it": keep, and I would defend it as the remark of a numb character rather than as a solecism. "It's raining, but I believe it isn't": cut, though differently — the first line sounds like nonsense and this one sounds like a confession of derangement, and I should distrust any principle that called these the same failure. "There's milk in the fridge, but I don't believe there is", written by a character alone in a journal with no reader in view: still cut, which suggests to me that the presence of an audience is not doing the work. My machine character's "The seal will fail, but I don't believe it will": keep, and readers accept it, apparently because a device can hold a record without holding a belief. "The letter will come tomorrow, but I shan't believe it when it does": keep, and indeed striking; the same person has both attitudes, merely at different times, yet the line is unobjectionable where its present-tense cousin is not. "It's raining, but I don't entirely believe it": keep — hedging the degree seems to rescue it.

So: is there one principle, or a short list of candidates, that delivers these verdicts, stated clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause? I want to know in particular whether the defect, where there is one, is a property of the sentence, of the speech act, or of the psychological state the speech act betrays — because that decides whether my editor is right that the lines are flawed, or whether they are instead accurate reports of flawed minds, which is a thing a novel is permitted to be about. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts the simplest adequate principle forces me to abandon, and whether a principle that saves all of them buys them at a complexity I ought to refuse. Finally, tell me what your account predicts for two lines I have not yet decided: a dying man addressing his own reflection — "You are going to die, but you don't believe it" — and a character who recites, as an actor on a stage within my book, words another man wrote.
