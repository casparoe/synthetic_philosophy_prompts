Consider two stylized societies, offered here as a deliberate abstraction of the contrast people usually have in mind when they set Nordic political cultures against the American one. Neither is meant to be a faithful description of any actual country; both are constructed so that a feedback loop is visible.

In Society A, citizens assume by default that public officials will roughly do what they have said they will do. Officials, aware that their word is being taken, find that keeping it is cheap: reputations accumulate, commitments made today are believed tomorrow, and so the machinery of governance can run on assurances instead of verification. Citizens, seeing that machinery run smoothly, take their default assumption to be vindicated, and pass it on.

In Society B, citizens assume by default that discretion will be abused unless it is watched. Officials, aware that every exercise of discretion will be audited, litigated, leaked, and interpreted as evidence of capture, respond by narrowing their own commitments, saying as little as possible, and building records designed to survive hostile reading. Citizens, seeing officials who will say nothing plainly and who keep large reserves of unexercised authority, take their default assumption to be vindicated, and pass it on.

Both loops are self-stabilizing. In each, the attitude that citizens hold generates much of the behavior that appears to justify it. I want to press on what it could mean to call one of these attitudes more reasonable than the other.

Please take up the following four questions in order.

First: distinguish relying on something from trusting it. One can rely on a bridge because it was built to a code and inspected on a schedule, and one can rely on a stranger's self-interest, and in neither case does betrayal seem like the right word for what happens if things go wrong — disappointment, or miscalculation, is closer. Explain what has to be added to reliance to get trust, and why the addition matters for the comparison above. In particular, consider this possibility: that what makes Society A work is not trust in the goodwill of particular officials but well-calibrated reliance on durable procedures, and that Society B's citizens are not making an error about the same object but rather declining to extend that kind of reliance. Relatedly, explain why the ordinary survey instrument for this topic — a question asking whether, generally speaking, most people can be trusted or whether one cannot be too careful — may not be measuring the attitude at issue at all, given that it asks about people rather than offices, and given that an affirmative answer from someone living inside a functioning system may report nothing more than the absence of salient evidence to the contrary.

Second: in both societies, a citizen's evidence about officials is partly an artifact of the stance the citizen adopted before the evidence arrived. Explain the difference between a belief that is confirmed by evidence and a belief that has helped produce the evidence said to confirm it, and say whether that difference is sufficient to render the Society B citizen's distrust unjustified. In doing so, be careful to say whether this is anything more than the familiar phenomenon of a self-fulfilling prophecy. There is a reason to think it is: a prediction about the weather cannot be addressed to the weather, whereas an attitude about officials can be registered by them, and they can respond to the fact that they are being trusted or being distrusted, not merely to the incentives that the attitude happens to create. If that is right, ask whether the two directions are symmetric. One influential suggestion is that being trusted supplies the trusted party with a reason to prove trustworthy — a reason grounded in not wanting to be the kind of person who disappoints that estimation — and that distrust supplies no mirror-image reason of the same kind. Evaluate that suggestion: does it hold, and if it does, does it give the trusting attitude in Society A an epistemic or practical advantage that survives the fact that the attitude is itself self-confirming?

Third: consider whether reasonableness is even the right shape of evaluation here. If trust in government is closer to a standing policy of reliance than to a belief about how officials are, then it may be assessed practically — does adopting this stance produce the arrangements one wants? — rather than epistemically — does it fit the available evidence? Those two assessments can diverge, because a stance that is worse at tracking how things currently are may be better at producing how they will become. Explain that divergence, and say what you think follows for the original question. If the attitudes in A and B are each self-sealing and each practically productive of what they predict, is there any non-question-begging standpoint from which one can be judged more reasonable, or does the judgment collapse into a prior disagreement about what one wants government to be?

Fourth: introduce a complication that cuts against the framing so far. The American constitutional order is often defended on precisely the ground that it does not require citizens to trust officials — that dispersing authority, setting competing ambitions against one another, and demanding public justification for the exercise of power are substitutes for trust rather than symptoms of its absence. On this reading, a well-designed system converts distrust into a productive check, so that low interpersonal confidence in officials is not a civic deficiency but the input the machinery was built to consume. Explain the difference between an institution that expresses distrust of officeholders and a citizenry that feels distrust of officeholders. Say whether a society can coherently maintain the first while its citizens retain the second, what happens when the two come apart — for instance, when monitoring institutions are themselves staffed by officials and so fall inside the circle of suspicion they were meant to close — and whether the existence of the first can serve as evidence about the second.

A few constraints on the response. You do not need to cite sources, name authors, or supply a reading list, and you should not attempt to; argue from the concepts and from the two stylized cases. Introduce no term of art without defining it in a clause. Where you think the ordinary framing of the Nordic-versus-American comparison is itself confused — for instance, where it treats a difference in institutional design as a difference in national character, or treats the absence of vigilance as a virtue — say so directly rather than reproducing the framing for its own sake. Aim for a sustained, well-organized discussion of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, and end with a short statement of what you take to be the single most consequential ambiguity in the question I have asked.
