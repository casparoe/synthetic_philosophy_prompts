I am a third-year PPE student and, alongside my lecture course on democratic theory, I run one of its weekly tutorial sections: twenty-four students, ninety minutes, a room with a long table, two whiteboards, and no budget for printing, though I can bring index cards and sticky notes and about two-thirds of the students have a phone with them (I would rather the activity not depend on that). In week nine we cover the referendum as an instrument of direct democracy, and each year the same thing happens: the hour dissolves into an abstract exchange between students who defend popular votes on principle and students who recite Arrow's theorem as though it were a knockdown objection, and nobody's view moves. I have also tried a warm-up in which the class votes over three flavours of ice cream and discovers a majority cycle; it fell flat, because one student spotted the cycle in about four minutes and the rest of the session was spent congratulating ourselves for having beaten a theorem. What I want instead is an activity in which the students run into the underlying problem three separate times, from three different directions, before anyone tells them what the problem is — and then realises, in the debrief, that the three failures are not the same failure and that they compound.

The three directions I have in mind are as follows. First, the option set itself: a Brexit-shaped menu of at least four proposals, voted on in an order I fix in advance, in which the alternative that a majority of the room ranks above the eventual winner nevertheless loses — the point being that the loss is produced by how the question was put and how the votes were sequenced, not by anyone's incoherence. Second, the premise-versus-conclusion split: the room votes separately on each of three premises that nearly all of them will accept and then on the conclusion those premises entail, and discovers that the majority assembled premise-by-premise and the majority taken directly on the conclusion disagree about whether the proposal passes. I have a half-formed idea here about premises concerning whether a majority supports a proposal, whether that majority is competent to judge it, and whether competent support is sufficient for adoption, but I am not attached to it; what I need is a premise set whose individual acceptability is high and whose conjunction is contentious, together with the exact acceptance pattern required for the divergence to fire, so that I can check the room's numbers before I commit to it. Third, a double-majority rule of the Swiss kind: the same ballots produce a different winner once approval must be registered both across individual voters and across pre-existing subunits into which I have sorted the class, with one thinly populated subunit able to block an outcome almost everyone else wants. Throughout, none of the students should hear the words cycle, doctrinal or discursive paradox, or double majority until the debrief begins, and there should be no lecture beforehand — the only notice they get is a one-line announcement that week nine involves voting and that the result will bind us. I want the votes to be about something real: the machinery will decide, with finality, what we do in week ten (a student-led session on a topic of their choosing, a screening, or an extra reading), so that the frustration in the room is genuine rather than hypothetical.

Please give me the whole thing in runnable form: the exact rules and ballot wording for each stage, the materials I need to prepare the night before, how I allocate the twenty-four students into subunits of deliberately unequal size and on what plausible-sounding pretext, and a minute-by-minute timeline with the facilitator's transitions written out — including what I say when someone asks whether the option set is fair, since my aim is for that accusation to arise and to become the payoff rather than something I defuse. Build in a fallback for the case where the room's preferences turn out to be single-peaked and no cycle emerges, and tell me what to do with the one student who reads comparative politics, has followed the 2019 indicative votes in Westminster, and will very likely foresee the vote-splitting effect within the first ten minutes: how to keep her from spoiling it for the others, or how to use her. Then a debrief that maps specific moments from the session onto each of the three failures, shows where they interact rather than merely coexist, and explains why a design that produces one failure in isolation is not enough to make the point.

Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply by talking. I am thinking of at least the following, and I want you to tell me which of them are fatal and which are merely limitations: preferences in a real referendum campaign are formed and reshaped over months rather than revealed on a ballot, so my students are not deciding anything they have been persuaded about; the intensity of what is at stake in a room of peers choosing a week-ten activity is nothing like the intensity of a constitutional vote; a twenty-four-person class cannot exhibit the aggregation problems of a whole electorate, only their shape; and the activity can generate a result that everyone finds procedurally unsatisfying without generating the thing real referendums claim to generate, namely a mandate that the losing side is expected to treat as settled. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — where students will vote strategically once they sense what is happening, where the subunit sorting will provoke charges of gerrymandering that I should welcome rather than rebut, and where the timing will overrun — and how to handle each of those without abandoning the design.
