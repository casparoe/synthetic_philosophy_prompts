ok so I'm in this seminar on evidence in mathematics — half phil of math, half number theory, very weird room — and this week we read the paper about formalising the presumption of independence. The setup as I understand it: you want a 'heuristic estimator' that, when it has no specific information about the correlation between X and Y, defaults to estimating E[XY] as E[X]E[Y]. The paper lays out a list of coherence properties you'd want such a thing to have, notes that none of the obvious candidates satisfy them, and leaves as its main open problem whether there's an estimator that captures the legitimate uses of that default without also accepting spurious arguments.

Our seminar leader talks about this like it's a hard engineering problem someone will eventually crack. My term paper is due friday and I've already committed publically to arguing it can't be cracked in that form, so now I need an actual argument. Thesis I want you to make the case for:

Having no specific information about the correlation between X and Y isn't a property of X and Y. It's a property of the way X and Y are written down, together with how much reasoning the estimator is permitted to spend on noticing the correlation. Which means the three things the project wants at once — an estimator that's a well-defined function of the expressions it's handed, one that falls back on independence whenever correlation-information is missing, and one that never accepts a spurious argument — are jointly unavailable, and not for want of cleverness.

Build that case properly, don't just assert it. I want the pressure point constructed: find me a concrete estimation problem where the default is fine, and an equivalent way of writing down the very same problem where it's catastrophically wrong; say precisely in what sense the two are the same problem; and explain why an estimator of the kind described can't tell them apart. Two things from seminar you can lean on, both of which I'm confident about: the Hardy–Littlewood k-tuple guess is the naive independent-estimate times a product of local factors, one per prime, recording how far the tuple's residues mod p deviate from what independence predicts (for twin primes that's the ~1.32 correction); and a tuple like n, n+3 has a vanishing local factor at 2, which matches the fact that they're essentially never both prime — only n = 2 works. You'll also want a case where the correlation is real but only visible if you do more work than the estimator is allowed, so think about what happens when you change how the predicates are expressed without changing what they pick out.

Then handle the two objections I'm bracing for. (1) Working number theorists never apply the default raw: the singular series *is* the correlation-detection step, behaviour mod p is completely computable, and the fact that sieves run into a parity barrier shows the limits sit in our proof methods rather than in the heuristic — so a decent estimator just internalises the local machinery and there's no mystery left. (2) My equivalent-reformulation move is cheating, because a respectable estimator should be compositional over the way its expressions are built up, and the opaque reformulations I'm leaning on wouldn't be well-formed in its language at all.

If either of those genuinely sinks the thesis, say so and tell me what weaker claim is left standing — I'd rather hand in an honest paper than a fake win. Around 1200 words. And no references or bibliography please, I'm not citing anything, I just want the argument.
