I TA an upper-level seminar this term, "Collective Reasoning and Social Choice" — 18 students, mostly philosophy majors plus three poli-sci people who already know Arrow's theorem cold and seem to think that means they're done. I've been handed the final to write (three hours, closed book, they get one double-sided sheet of notes but no texts). I want a single essay question worth 25 marks on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma, and I want it built so that reciting "premise-based vs conclusion-based, List and Pettit, group agency, done" gets you a C at best. Here's the fact pattern I sketched on a napkin — please use it, and tell me if my arithmetic is off anywhere.

A nine-member environmental licensing board has to decide whether to revoke an operating permit. The statute says revoke if and only if all three of: P — the facility exceeded its emissions limit in the relevant year; Q — the exceedance caused measurable harm to the downstream wetland; R — the operator failed to take precautions a competent operator would have taken. Every member is individually consistent, and they fall into five blocs:

- three members: P yes, Q yes, R yes
- one member: P yes, Q yes, R no
- one member: P yes, Q no, R yes
- one member: P no, Q yes, R yes
- three members: P no, Q no, R no

So premise by premise you get 5–4 for P, 5–4 for Q and 5–4 for R, and the conclusion follows: revoke. Voted on directly, revocation loses 3–6. That's the textbook paradox, but it's only the setup — I don't want the question to stop there. What I want it to turn on is four "remedies" that board members propose afterwards, where the students have to actually work out which ones do anything: (a) drop the reasons and just take a majority vote on revocation; (b) keep voting premise by premise and let the conclusion fall out; (c) vote the premises in a fixed order, P then Q then R, skipping any premise whose vote can no longer change the outcome; (d) require a 6-of-9 supermajority on each premise while leaving the conclusion at simple majority. My own guess is that (c) is completely inert here and (d) only "works" by luck, but I want them to show that rather than assert it. On top of all that, the legislature "fixes" the statute by demoting R from an element of the test to something going only to the penalty, so the operative test becomes revoke iff P and Q — and the students have to redo the tally to see whether the paradox disappears (I'm fairly confident it doesn't, and that the outcome actually flips, but they can't just assume that).

The crux I most want them to reach, and the thing that should seperate the A's from the B's, is that none of this is a fixed feature of the case: the result depends entirely on how the legal test is chopped up into premises. If you lump "exceeded the limit" and "caused harm" into one composite premise S and vote on {S, R}, you get a different answer than voting on {P, Q, R}, and there's no neutral argument for which chopping is the real one. I'd like part (c) of the question to drive at that and then ask what it does to the idea that the board has a view at all — whether the premise-based outcome is the board's judgment or a bookkeeping artifact.

So: could you write the question itself in three parts with the marks shown against each part, with the whole fact pattern and the bloc tally inside the stem so it's fully self-contained and needs no outside reading; a model answer of about 700 words that actually does the arithmetic and displays the tallies instead of gesturing at them; and a marking scheme out of 25 organised around five must-hit moves, saying what each is worth plus a one-line note on how students typically botch each one. Then add a short paragraph describing the fluent-but-hollow answer — the one that names the discursive dilemma, mentions the epistemic versus authoritative reasons for premise-based voting, and never once touches a number or the premise-individuation problem — so I can calibrate where to put the B/C boundary. No citations anywhere: the model answer shouldn't cite literature or pretend the students have read any, and it shouldn't ask them to.
