So me and my roommate Dan got into a proper row last night, three hours of it, started over something dumb and then turned into whether a person can lose the right to call someone else out. Background: Dan has been consistently two weeks late on rent for like six months, and last week he tore into our other flatmate Priya for being unreliable about doing the dishes. His argument was that Priya's fault is real and Dan's fault is irrelevant to that, so the dishes thing still stands and Dan gets to say something about it. My argument was that it doesn't work like that, that the person doing the blaming matters as much as the fact being blamed, and once you're in the same mess you've forfeited your position. Neither of us budged and now I want to sort out which of us was less wrong in a more general way.

I've tried to write down the full list of ways someone can lose their standing to blame, and I've deliberately included a few categories I made up myself because I couldn't find a name for them, so please don't assume they're standard terms — I've defined each one so you can work purely off the definitions:

1. **Same-fault hypocrisy** — you blame them for X while currently doing X yourself.
2. **Complicity** — you helped cause, fund, or cover up the very thing you're blaming.
3. **Meddling** — your fault is clean, but this business simply isn't yours; you're a near-stranger with no stake or relationship.
4. **Asymmetric noticing** — you flag this fault in rivals and excuse the identical fault in allies, so your blame is really a political move.
5. **Laundered standing** — you know you can't say it, so you engineer things so that someone with clean hands delivers the blame on your behalf, and then you loudly endorse it.
6. **Silent beneficiary** — you knew about the fault while it was happening, said nothing because you were quietly profiting from it, and only speak up now that the benefit has run out.
7. **Borrowed grievance** — the actual victim is present, doesn't want you involved, and you blame on their behalf anyway, claiming their complaint as your own.
8. **Committed self-reform** — you're doing X too, but you've publicly announced you're changing and you use blaming others partly as a way of holding yourself to it.
9. **Intensity mismatch** — your hands are clean and you're entitled to speak, but you blame at a volume wildly disproportionate to the fault.
10. **Time-decay** — the fault was years ago, both people have genuinely changed, and you're digging it up now anyway.

Here's what I actually want from you: rate each of the ten on a 0–10 scale for how much it defeats standing to blame, where 10 means the blamer has completely lost the right to say anything and 0 means it's not a standing issue at all, and then rank them best to worst. Don't just give me the numbers — for each one, a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. Then answer two follow-ups, because these are the bits Dan and I actually fought about. First, tell me which of my categories you think I've misfiled, i.e. which ones aren't really about standing at all but about something else entirely, like proportionality or just epistemic access to the facts, and say what they're really about instead. Second, give me a combining rule: if someone is guilty of two categories at once, say complicity at a 7 and asymmetric noticing at a 7, does that make a 9 or a 14 or something else — I want an actual formula or heuristic I could apply, plus your reasoning for why the defeaters should stack in that particular way rather than another. And finally settle the flat dispute as directly as you can: was Dan right that his lateness is a separate matter from Priya's dishes, or was I right that it disqualifies him? You can hedge if you have to but I'd rather have a verdict.
