I'm preparing a twenty-five-minute talk for a graduate workshop on transitional justice, and the assigned theme is the retroactivity objection to the postwar tribunals — the charge that Nuremberg and Tokyo punished individuals for conduct that was not criminal under law binding on them at the time they committed it. My own view is that the objection is real but survivable; the convenor's view, which she has stated more than once, is that it is fatal and that everything else offered in reply is decoration. To stop the two of us talking past each other, we've agreed on a single exercise, and I'd like your help running it properly before the talk.

The exercise is a portability test. Take each of the standard replies to the objection and ask how much of its work survives being lifted out of 1945–48 and transplanted into a hypothetical present-day tribunal: convened not by victors but by a broad coalition including several states that were not parties to the underlying conflict; operating under a charter drafted in advance and opened for accession; staffed partly by judges whose states have no stake in the outcome; and trying senior officials of a state that remains intact, undefeated, and in possession of its own functioning courts. The charge is a deliberate, years-long policy of severing food, water, and medical supply to a besieged civilian population — lawful under the domestic law of that state when it was adopted, never adjudicated as criminal under international law either, and matched within living memory by closely analogous practices of several members of the prosecuting coalition. The point of the transplant is to strip away the two things that quietly carry most of the 1945 replies — the defendants' total defeat, and the fact that the world has since converged on the monstrosity of what they did — and to see what is actually left standing.

Here are the seven replies on my list, stated as I understand them:

1. The treaty-notice reply: aggressive war had already been renounced as an instrument of national policy by multilateral treaty between the wars, so the defendants had notice that the conduct was legally condemned even though no court existed to enforce the condemnation.
2. The general-principles reply: what was in fact punished was mass murder and systematic cruelty, and those were crimes under principles shared by the domestic legal systems of every civilized state; international law needed no special code to reach them.
3. The structural reply: *nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege* is a constraint that makes sense inside a legal order with a legislature that can be told what to enact; an order with no legislature and no criminal code cannot be held to that standard, and insisting on it mistakes a domestic rule of law for a universal one.
4. The threshold reply in the spirit of Radbruch: there is a point at which an enactment violates elementary justice so grossly that it forfeits the character of law and can shelter no one, and defendants who hid behind such enactments cannot complain when the shield is refused.
5. The pre-existing-obligation reply: international law had long imposed duties directly on individuals rather than only on states — piracy being the standing case, together with the interwar prosecutions for violations of the laws of war — so the premise that only states can bear liability under international law was already obsolete before the charters were drafted.
6. The lesser-evil reply: even granting that the proceedings were legally defective, the alternatives actually on the table were summary execution or nothing at all, and a flawed public trial that gives the accused counsel, a forum, and an evidentiary record beats both.
7. The forum-and-personnel reply: whatever turns on who sat on the bench and who staffed the prosecution — victors' jurisdiction, the presence of a judge whose own state had massacred Polish officers it then tried to charge to the defendants, the decision to assess no punishment on the submarine-warfare count once the defence produced written answers from the Allied naval commander in the Pacific about Allied practice — is an objection to the administration of the tribunal rather than to the law it applied, and imperfect administration does not make a substantively correct verdict unjust.

For each of the seven, give me three things. First, a portability score from 0 to 10, where 10 means the reply does exactly as much work in the hypothetical as it did at Nuremberg and 0 means it is emptied out entirely once the defendants' defeat and the settled moral consensus are removed. Second, two or three sentences of justification that identify what inside the reply is doing the work and say precisely where it breaks under transplant — I want the diagnosis, not a restatement of the reply. Third, a verdict on whether the reply absorbs the objection or relocates it: absorbing means it shows the objection misdescribes the legal situation; relocating means it concedes the objection's premise and then argues the concession is tolerable. Please note explicitly that I regard these as different achievements and that a high-scoring relocating reply should not be treated as answering the same worry as a high-scoring absorbing one.

Then, on top of the seven assessments: tell me which of the replies collapse into one another or are parasitic on each other, so that the list is really shorter than it looks; give me a single ordered ranking from most to least portable, with any ties broken and the tie-breaking reason stated; and tell me which one reply you would stake the thesis of the talk on if you had to commit to one, together with the strongest counterargument available to someone in the convenor's position. As a final check, say whether the dissenting opinion delivered at Tokyo — which pressed the retroactivity objection hardest and would have acquitted across the board — gains or loses force in the hypothetical, and why. I'll supply all the references myself, so don't give me a bibliography or a list of further reading; what I need is your judgment on the seven scores, the ranking, and the reasoning behind them.
