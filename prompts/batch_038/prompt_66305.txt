For six years I have run an informal monthly discussion group for software engineers at my company — eleven or twelve people, a basement room in a coworking space, one whiteboard, a strict no-homework norm. Most of them know what a reward function is and roughly how a policy gets optimized against it; none have read philosophy and none have read the technical alignment literature, though two have watched a few conference talks. Next month I have seventy-five minutes, and there is exactly one idea I want them to leave with: that telling an agent to avoid side effects does not give us a way around the problem of specifying what we value, because any account precise enough to be implemented has already settled which differences between states count as significant, and settling that is a value judgment — while at the same time it falls far short of a full specification of value, so the live question is how much we can get away with fixing and which choices we are forced into. Plan how I should get that across. Do not write the talk and do not give me the argument in essay form; I want the architecture — the dependency structure among the pieces, and a map of the ways the idea will be misheard. No readings, please; I want nothing that depends on their reading anything or on my distributing a list.

Concretely, four things. First, the order the components must come in and what each presupposes. I suspect they cannot feel the force of the side-effect proposal until they already sense that a competent agent pursuing an unremarkable goal is hazardous in the first place, but I do not know how much of that machinery — subgoals, resource acquisition, the fact that the default disposition is to keep pursuing — has to be built explicitly before the impact question is even intelligible to them, or whether I should assume it and spend one sentence. Tell me which piece is load-bearing and which I can compress. Second, the running case. I have been assembling one: a household robot told to tidy a living room before guests arrive, where the room contains a chessboard left mid-game by two of the residents, a stack of one resident's work papers whose disorder encodes something, and a spilled glass of water on a rug. Assess whether that case can carry the whole session — what it must exhibit in order to do so (the ambiguity about what the agent's behavior is being compared to, the question of which differences count at all, whether reversing a change makes it not a change, and the collision between the penalty and the goal the agent was actually given), and what it must conspicuously lack. My own suspicion is that it must contain nothing the room would immediately recognize as harm to a person, or they will resolve the case with ordinary moral instinct and never see the specification problem underneath it; tell me whether that is right and what else I should strip out. Third, the misconception map, with the point in the sequence at which each is headed off. My list so far: that impact is a distance in state space and the only open issue is picking the right metric; that this is reward shaping, so add a penalty term and tune the coefficient; that if we count only physical changes we remain neutral about values; that an agent carrying a strong side-effect penalty is thereby a cautious and safe agent; and the reflexive leap to autonomous weapons. Tell me which of these will actually block the idea, in what order they surface, which two collapse into each other, and — most usefully — which one I have failed to list. Fourth, a single closing question that separates understanding from agreeable nodding. The one I drafted was to ask them for an example of an action with a large side effect, but I now think someone who has learned only the slogan passes that; give me something that cannot be answered correctly without noticing where the value judgment entered.

Finally, tell me what I should announce as out of bounds at this level, and why. I expect the formal proposals for measuring impact, the competing choices of counterfactual baseline, the alternative of sampling at random among good-enough actions rather than optimizing, and the neighboring problem of whether the agent will permit itself to be switched off all belong in that category — but I want your judgment on which of them earns ninety seconds of honest refusal (here is what turns on it, and here is why I am not going to explain it tonight) and which should simply be omitted. Plan also for the moment, which in this group always arrives around minute fifty, when someone sharp says that all of this merely relocates the specification problem instead of solving it and asks whether the whole approach is therefore empty: where in the sequence should I already be standing so that I can concede exactly the right amount, without overselling the project and without letting the room conclude that nothing can be said. Two bits of logistics to factor in. One member considers the entire field premature and will steer toward machine consciousness and moral status; tell me whether that gets thirty seconds of engagement or gets cut off before it starts, and at what point in the sequence a diversion there would be most damaging. And I have a single working marker, so the plan should not depend on any diagram I could not redraw in twenty seconds.
