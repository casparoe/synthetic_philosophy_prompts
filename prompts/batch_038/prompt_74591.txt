I TA a second-year metaphysics seminar and roughly half the section showed up convinced that "fragile" just means "would break if struck," full stop, and that anythign more complicated is metaphysics-brained overthinking. I've given the standard counterexample talk three years running and it goes in one ear and out the other, so this term I want to hand them something they'll actually read for fun instead of another set of slides. Could you write me a mock product-safety recall notice, in the exact voice of a national consumer-protection agency posting to its website, recalling a line of ceramic mugs?

The target isn't the mugs and isn't any real philosopher or living person — it's the practice of trying to define what a property *is* by listing what a thing *would do* under test conditions, and the associated practice of rescuing the definition by bolting on one more proviso every time a counterexample turns up. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and absolutely no closing paragraph that turns around and explains the joke. Around 650–750 words, formatted like the real genre: heading with a recall number and issue date, then Product / Hazard / Remedy / Incidents-and-Injuries counts / What Consumers Should Do, plus a boxed appendix of definitions and a short FAQ at the end. I'd also like the manufacturer's official response statement included, and that's where the proviso-piling should be at its funniest.

Here's the technical content I want the jokes to run on, so it lands with someone who knows the literature — please keep the distinctions precise rather than vaguely gesturing at "weird counterexamples":

- A mug can be genuinely fragile and still fail to break when struck, because something extrinsic interferes: say it ships permanently bonded inside a shock-absorbing sleeve that soaks up the entire impact. The disposition is still there, only the manifestation is blocked. The agency's drop test therefore records a false negative, and the piece should treat that as a *regulatory* catastrophe, not a metaphysical one.
- A mug can be fragile and yet have its fragility switched off at the moment of impact by a built-in monitor that anneals the ceramic the instant a blow is incoming — so it wouldn't have broken even though it really is disposed to break. The conditional comes out false while the disposition stays real.
- A mug can fail to be fragile at all and still pass every drop test, because the device runs the other way: a thoroughly non-brittle mug is shattered on contact by something external. This is the mimic case, and the manufacturer should absolutely point out, with some heat, that its mugs have never once failed to break under testing — which is precisely what a mimic would say.
- The agency's fix should be a new clause requiring the mug to retain some intrinsic character of its own through the interval between stimulus and manifestation, so extrinsic interferers stop counting. Then have them get quiet about it, because the clause never says *which* intrinsic character, and the only available way to specify "canonical" or "ideal" test conditions turns out to appeal back to dispositions — so the definition goes around in a circle. Bonus points if the agency tries to outsource the list of canonical conditions to the manufacturer's marketing department and is told that's a conflict of interest.
- Work in the untested stock: a pallet-warehouse of mugs that have never been struck and never will be. Whether they fall inside the recall depends on counterfactuals about them, and the agency should be visibly uneasy about certifying a batch it cannot test without (possibly) changing the answer it gets.

The comedy should come from the metaphysics being right and from bureaucratic register being hilariously unfit for the job — an agency legally obliged to state a hazard it cannot state without making it false. Don't cite anything, don't invent fake journal articles or attach views to named philosophers, and don't reproduce any real text; keep it all self-contained and invented. Hold the flat officious tone to the last line — the FAQ is the one place you can let it slip slightly unhinged, e.g. a customer asking whether their mug is fragile if it has never been dropped and the agency being forbidden from answering yes.
