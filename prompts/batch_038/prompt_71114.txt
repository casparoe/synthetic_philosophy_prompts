I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for close to fifteen years, and in the recurring argument about how a vastly more capable system ought to talk to us, the proverb "honesty is the best policy" reliably turns up as a conversation-ender. A thread I follow did exactly that last month. Someone had described a system that answers a user's question about whether to accept a job offer using nothing but sentences the user would agree are true, and yet the user's decision is, for practical purposes, made for them: the system chose which true considerations to raise, in what order, at what confidence and with what affect, out of a set of true considerations far larger than any human interlocutor could have surveyed. The reply was that this is not a problem at all, because the system deceived no one — it was honest, and honesty is the best policy. In our reading group we've taken to calling that setup frame-setting, though I'll admit the term doesn't yet mean anything precise, and part of what I want from you is whether the candidate readings differ on whether frame-setting counts as a violation.

I suspect several distinct propositions are being smuggled through that one sentence, and I want them separated before I respond. Could you lay out the precise theses the proverb might be standing for when it is used this way? I'd like the axes made explicit rather than collapsed: what honesty is being taken to constrain (assertions the system believes to be false, the beliefs it causes in the listener, what it omits when directly asked, what it reports about its own goals and uncertainties, the selection and ordering of truths); whose policy it is (the system's, the designer's, the user's, the lab's); and in what sense "best" (instrumentally optimal for the speaker over a repeated relationship, morally obligatory regardless of consequences, welfare-maximizing for the listener as the listener currently judges welfare, or robust to the listener's values changing later). For each resulting reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, straightforwardly false, or incoherent, and say briefly why. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually license the dismissal quoted above, and which one the people repeating the proverb probably have in mind without being willing to state it outright.

Please give me the menu as a numbered list I can paste into the thread, and close with the single case that would most cleanly separate the reading I take to be true from the one that was doing the work in that reply.
