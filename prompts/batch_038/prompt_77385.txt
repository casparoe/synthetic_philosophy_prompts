I run a small graduate seminar in non-classical logic this term, and I need help designing the final examination question. Some background, since it explains the constraints: I came to this material the way a lot of people of my generation did, through years of reading rationalist blogs and comment threads, where paraconsistent logic turns up every eighteen months in threads about how an AI agent might safely hold contradictory beliefs. My students have absorbed that folklore rather than the logic. Last year I set a question on the Liar paradox asking candidates to compare the three standard responses — classical with gaps, dialetheist with gluts, and revision-theoretic — and it was a near-total failure as an assessment: eleven of twelve scripts reproduced the same three-column summary, used the phrase "true contradiction" without once explaining why a glut is not a triviality, and still earned high marks because the summary was accurate. I want this year's question to be impossible to answer from a summary, and I want it to force candidates to do something with a three-valued semantics rather than gesture at one.

Please take the role of a philosopher of logic who has taught and examined this material at the graduate level and who has set exams that discriminate well. Before drafting anything, spend a short paragraph brainstorming three or four candidate cruxes in the dialetheism literature — for instance the exclusion problem (what stops the slide from "some contradictions are true" to trivialism), the classical recapture strategy and whether it is question-begging, the cost of giving up a detachable conditional, or the revenge problem generated by operators that purport to say "true and only true" — and state in a sentence or two why you are choosing one and rejecting the others. Then write a single in-class question worth 25 marks, answerable in 100 minutes without any resources, in two parts. Part A should be a self-contained formal exercise: you print in the question itself everything the candidate needs (the truth values, which are designated, the clauses for negation and conjunction, a naive truth schema, and one stipulated new one-place operator of your own devising, together with its semantic clause), and the candidate must determine by an explicit case analysis whether a specified self-referential sentence admits any consistent assignment at all. The shape I had in mind is a determinacy- or "just true"-style operator, but design it yourself and please check your own construction by hand before including it — I would much rather have a slightly different operator that actually works than my suggested one that does not. Include at least one contrast sentence in Part A that does admit a stable assignment, so that a candidate cannot collect marks by answering "no model" to everything. Part B should be a short essay asking what the dialetheist can say in response to the result of Part A and what the response costs her. Nothing in the question may depend on a citation, a reading list, or prior familiarity with any particular author's terminology; every technical notion must be defined on the page.

The deliverables are: the question as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of roughly 900 words in which the Part A derivations are shown valuation by valuation, and a marking scheme totalling 25 marks that a teaching assistant who is not a specialist could apply mechanically. The marking scheme is where I most want rigour: allocate marks to named moves rather than to topics, and state explicitly what separates an A from a B answer. Please build in the errors I expect my students to make and say what each one costs — declaring the Part A sentence "just another glut" without noticing that a glut is itself an assignment and that the trouble is the absence of one; conflating non-designation with falsity; inferring that the whole theory is trivial without identifying the step that licenses that inference; assuming without argument that the object language must be able to express the operator; and treating the resulting expressibility problem as a difficulty unique to dialetheism rather than as something any non-classical treatment of naive truth has to face. Finally, think carefully and double-check your formal work as you go: if there is any step in your own case analysis or in your stipulated clause for the operator that you are less than fully confident about, say so in a brief note after the marking scheme rather than papering over it.
