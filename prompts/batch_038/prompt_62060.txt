So I write novels, and I'm stuck on a chapter that I love and that a friend of mine insists is literally empty. Not empty as in dull. Empty as in it doesn't say anything at all. He used to be a philosophy grad student before he burnt out and went to work in a bookshop, which is somehow worse for me, because he does things like write out his objections numbered.

The chapter: a dying forger leaves a letter behind, and the letter's one sentence amounts to a claim that everything asserted in this letter is false. I've built the scene around it so there's no clean way out — no second letter, no unreliable narrator hatch, no "he was confused, bless him" reading the reader can retreat into. The text forces both directions at once, on purpose.

His argument, which I can't get out of my head:

1. What's true in a story is what the text prescribes the reader to imagine.
2. My text prescribes imagining the letter's sentence as true, and it also prescribes imagining it as false.
3. So it prescribes imagining that the sentence is both true and false.
4. From a contradiction, in whatever logic readers are actually using when they read, anything follows.
5. Readers do imagine what follows from what they're prescribed — that's the only way they get the stuff that isn't said outright, like that a character has a liver or that it's the same Tuesday as last chapter.
6. So in my chapter everything is true: the forger died, the forger never existed, the letter is one page and four hundred pages, I am not the author.
7. A story in which everything is true gives the reader nothing to go on, and deserves exactly as much attention as a blank page.
8. So my chapter is a blank page.

Then the bit that actually scared me, because it generalises past my book: the same argument swallows every continuity error in every novel ever written. Mine has one — a brother who dies in chapter four is standing at the back of the funeral in chapter nineteen and nobody comments on it. So either that book is also a blank page, or one of the steps above is bad, and I can't tell which, because they all look like things I already believe.

I want to keep the chapter. I also don't want to be told that readers switch off their logic the moment they open a novel, because their logic is the machinery my plots run on. So: which step breaks, why did that step look innocent going in, and what do I owe once I've broken it? Price each of the repairs you think are live, including any that would force me to rewrite the letter. And be concrete about whether the deliberate contradiction and the accidental brother are one problem or two — its not obvious to me that step 2 is even saying the same thing about both of them.
