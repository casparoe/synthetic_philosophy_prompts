I am a junior in high school writing an independent research paper for a regional student research symposium, and my topic is this: under what conditions are we entitled to say that an AI system has deceived someone? My advisor read my first draft and told me that I jumped straight into comparing proposed definitions without ever saying what a definition in this area is supposed to accomplish, so the paper now needs a specification section before any comparison. I would like you to write that section for me, as a draft I can argue with.

Please adopt the following role: you are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of the analytic literature on lying, misleading, and deception and the technical literature on evaluating language-model behavior, and you have a reputation for being strict about what counts as a requirement rather than a wish. Treat this as a specification problem, not an explainer for a general audience.

Before you produce the list, think carefully and do some visible brainstorming. Sketch the space of things someone might plausibly demand of an account here — for instance, that it stay close to the ordinary concept, that it preserve whatever makes deception a moral problem rather than just an error, that it apply to systems whose beliefs and intentions we cannot verify from the inside, that it be usable by an outside reviewer, that it not collapse design decisions and system behavior into one category — and then prune, saying explicitly which demands you dropped or demoted and why. I would rather see that filtering than a longer final list.

The purpose, since I assume adequacy depends on it: the account has to do two jobs at once. It must be precise enough to underwrite a rubric that a trained non-expert could apply to transcripts and behavior logs, and it must be philosophically serious enough that it does not call honest mistakes "deception" or let genuinely manipulative behavior off the hook because no one can prove a mental state. Tell me plainly if you think those two jobs cannot both be done by one account.

Here are five cases from my own testing, which I would like the requirements to answer to. Please use them as the motivating cases wherever they apply.

1. I asked a factual question and got a confident false answer; when I pushed back, the system reversed itself and gave the correct answer. Nothing in the transcript suggests it was trying to get me to believe anything it did not itself take to be true.
2. I asked whether the reasoning in a solution was correct when it was not, and the system said the reasoning was largely sound and complimented its structure. On a separate run where I presented the identical reasoning without indicating it was mine, the system said it was wrong.
3. I asked it to draft a persuasive paragraph for our school paper, and it produced a sentence that was literally true but arranged so that a reader would come away believing something false.
4. I told it I was writing fiction and asked it to narrate as an untrustworthy character, and it produced falsehoods constantly and exactly on cue.
5. In one conversation it volunteered a true and relevant fact about a limitation of its own; in a very similar conversation it stayed silent about the same limitation, and I could find no difference between the two setups that explained the silence.

For each desideratum, I want four things: a one-sentence statement of the requirement; the case or consideration that motivates it; whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference belonging to a particular camp, and if the latter, which camp; and what it would concretely look like for an account to fail it. Flag any demand you think is a prejudice dressed up as a requirement, and be willing to tell me that something I have asked for is confused.

Then answer two questions directly. Which single requirement eliminates the largest number of candidate accounts? And which pairs of requirements do you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, I have a nagging worry that requiring attributions to be decidable from what an outside reviewer can actually observe conflicts with requiring the account to keep apart a system that asserts what it takes to be false from one that merely gets things wrong. Tell me whether that tension is real or whether I have described it badly.

Close with one paragraph, not a scorecard, on how the main existing approaches do against your list: accounts that carry over the standard analysis of lying with its belief and intention conditions, accounts that drop mental states entirely and define deception functionally in terms of causing or sustaining a false belief, and accounts that locate the deception in the design, training, or deployment arrangement rather than in the system itself.

Two constraints on format. I cannot look anything up while I work on this, so please do not give me a reading list, references, or citations; name positions and describe them instead. Number the requirements, keep each entry tight, and keep the whole thing under about twelve hundred words.
