I've followed the debates about the moral weight of small animals for a decade, and I've grown dissatisfied with the way both camps wield neuron counts as though the numbers settled the question rather than raising it. I'd like a dialogue that gets into the weeds of what those numbers are supposed to be doing.

Two characters, both fully competent, each arguing the best version of their view:

- **The scaler**: moral weight comes in degrees and tracks the quantity of neural tissue doing integrative work, so a creature with a thousand times more neurons has, other things equal, roughly a thousand times the capacity for welfare. Aggregate welfare is therefore dominated by large-brained animals.
- **The threshold theorist**: what matters is whether a nervous system is organized so as to support the relevant states — a unified valenced field, flexible learning, something like a self-model — and once that organization is present, additional neurons buy resolution or additional separate experiences rather than more welfare per experience. Aggregate welfare can therefore be dominated by vast numbers of small minds.

Give them a decision. A city is consolidating two contract farms of identical cost and identical protein yield, and exactly one must close; closing either ends the lives of every animal in it. Farm A holds five million laying hens, Farm B three billion crickets. Stipulate 1.5 × 10⁹ neurons in each hen's integrative structure and 10⁶ in each cricket's. Stipulate further that both species show conditioned place aversion, nociceptive trade-offs, and opioid-reversible analgesia, and that neither shows the late, all-or-none, broadcast-style ignition that some theories treat as the mark of conscious perception. These are stipulations: argue about what they mean, not about whether they are right. Note that on these numbers the two theories rank the farms in opposite orders, and have the characters fight explicitly over that flip.

Constraints: both accept that experience is what matters and that we aggregate totals rather than applying a person-affecting restriction; neither may win by denying sentience to the other's animals or by retreating into species-typical behaviour; and each must concede at least one substantive point and visibly revise a claim mid-exchange. The scaler should press on what fixes the shape of the neuron-to-welfare function if not its slope; the threshold theorist should press on whether a fainter experience and a lower-resolution experience are really the same thing.

Please structure the response as follows:

1. **Stipulations** (~100 words): the case and the shared assumptions, naming the single quantity actually in dispute.
2. **The dialogue** (~1,200 words): speaker labels, no winner, ending on a disagreement sharper than the one it began with.
3. **Crux** (~150 words): the one proposition the whole exchange turns on, and the cheapest observation or experiment that would settle it.
4. **Cost of losing** (~100 words per side): what each character must give up — in this decision and in general — if the other turns out to be right.
