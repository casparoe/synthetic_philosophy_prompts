Novelist here, third draft of a book about a clockmaker who decides to finish an infinite list of repairs before noon — one screw, then a smaller one, intervals halving, the works. A friend with a philosophy degree sent me a paragraph so I dont make a fool of myself. It runs:

"In Thomson's lamp the switch is thrown at one minute, then again half a minute later, then a quarter, then an eighth, so an infinite number of throwings is over before the two minutes are up. Thomson showed the lamp can't be on at the end, since it was only just switched off, and can't be off, since it was only just switched on — but it has to be one or the other, so the description is contradictory and the whole thing is logically impossible. Which settles it: no infinite task can ever be completed, and that's also why Zeno's runner never reaches the line. The people who say the answer is 1/2 are just noting that 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 … can be summed to 1/2, which is no help, because there's no such thing as a lamp being half on."

How much of that survives? Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher who works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what isn't what Thomson argued at all — I half suspect he's compressing too much somewhere but couldn't say where. Two things I need most: whether the "only just switched off" move actually does any work at the final instant, and whether one broken lamp licenses banning every infinite task. Then tell me whether his conclusion holds up once the summary is fixed, because my last chapter turns on wether there's an honest sense in which the clockmaker finishes at noon rather than being cut off by it.
