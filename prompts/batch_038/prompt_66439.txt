I edit Wikipedia on the side, mostly articles about obscure hardware protocols and companies that went under in the 90s, and I've been in enough edit wars that I've internalized the sourcing rules. But the longer I follow them the wierder they look, and I want someone to spell out what theory of knowledge the whole thing is built on, because I doubt anyone on the project noticeboard could articulate it.

The rules, as I'd summarize them to a coworker: what goes in an article is settled by what has already been published, not by what editors know or believe. If I'm certain something is true — I worked at the company, I was in the room when the decision got made, I have the internal design docs sitting on a drive somewhere — it still cant go in until some reliable outlet has printed it. Even combining two properly sourced facts to state a conclusion neither source states is banned; people get reverted for synthesis over an inference a ten-year-old could make. Eyewitness and first-person material counts as a primary source, which is treated as about the weakest thing you can offer. And if no reliable independent sources were ever published about a topic, there's no article at all — not a weak article, no article, as though the topic never happened.

Here's the part I can't square. When the actual subject of a biography shows up on the talk page and says the article has her name or her employment history wrong, the standard answer is: go find a published source that says otherwise, and also you have a conflict of interest. The record beats the person the record is about. Meanwhile on the same site, every internal decision — which articles get deleted, which outlets count as reliable, whether a given publisher is known for fact-checking — gets made by consensus of experienced editors appealing to past decisions and their own accumulated feel for the place, and the no-original-research policy says explicitly that it doesn't apply to talk pages and deletion discussions. So community memory and judgment run everything procedural and are worth precisely zero on content. Same people, same afternoon, opposite rules.

What does this apparatus presuppose? Is it committed to something like a claim being assertible only once an institution with a checking process has vouched for it in print — which would make social authorization the whole story and truth beside the point? Is it a theory of testimony, where a stranger's memory is worthless and a newsroom's is fine? Given the notability rule, does it presuppose that the published record constitutes what happened rather than merely being evidence about what happened? And which rival views does that quietly rule out — I'm thinking of views where the person who was there has the best standing to correct the record about her own life, or where knowing something is true is already enough to entitle you to say it.

Then draw the line for me between what the practice strictly requires and what it just happens to fit nicely with. My suspicion is there's a much more boring story available — thousands of anonymous volunteers, no way to audit any of them, legal exposure around living people, so outsource the judgment call to institutions — and that story licenses the same rules with no epistemology attached. Tell me if that's right, and if it is, which specific rules still need the fancy story and which ones don't.

Last thing: if you swapped in a view that gave real weight to first-hand memory, including the subject's own, what would have to change on the site? Not a slogan — the machinery. Who adjudicates between two people who were both there, what happens when memory and the record conflict and the memory is more likely correct, how you keep it from collapsing into anyone asserting anything about themselves, and whether the notability rule could survive the change at all.

Please no reading list, I'm not going to read books, I just want the reasoning.
