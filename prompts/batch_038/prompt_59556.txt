Every autumn the same question resurfaces in a research seminar I attend on the Late Bronze Age Aegean — whether there was ever a Trojan War — and every autumn it is argued for six weeks and then left exactly where it started. I am not asking for an answer to the question. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, and I want you to take seriously the possibility that the impasse is not a failure of scholarship but a symptom of something about the question.

Here is the dispute as I see it. One camp, whom I will call the reconstructionists, treats the epics as degraded memory and builds its case from three directions. There is a destruction horizon at the mound of Hisarlık, dated broadly to the last decades of the thirteenth century or the early twelfth, containing scorching, sling bullets and bodies interred in haste. There are Hittite diplomatic texts of the thirteenth century referring to a northwestern Anatolian polity they identify with the Homeric city, to a ruler of that polity bearing a name they equate with a Homeric one, and to a rival power across the sea they equate with the Achaeans. And there is the general claim that raiding, hostage-taking and quarrels over tolls and anchorages on the Dardanelles were routine enough in the period that a war of the sort the tradition describes requires no special explanation. The opposing camp, whom I will call the reductionists, concedes most of these facts and still refuses the conclusion: the destruction horizon is one of many in a century of general collapse and exhibits no attacker; the Hittite identifications rest on phonetic resemblance plus a geography that can be drawn several ways; the epics took shape four or five centuries after the events they narrate, in a register saturated with anachronism, and no channel of transmission preserves the identity of a particular war as opposed to a motif; and the tradition is better explained by the processes that generate siege narratives generally — the fall of a famed city, an abducted woman, a ruse of entry, a roll-call of heroes — than by any single occurrence. A third group in the room says the question is being asked badly, but has never been able to say what a better-formed question would look like.

My hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that the two main camps are not in fact contradicting one another: that the sentence "the Trojan War happened" is being read by each as a claim with different satisfaction conditions. The reconstructionists appear to assert something existential and thin — that some conflict at some site matching the tradition's outline occurred, such that the tradition is not ex nihilo — while the reductionists deny something specific and thick — that a war answerable to the narrative the epics tell, with its cause, its duration and its protagonists, took place. On that reading both sides can be entirely right about everything they assert, and the seminar stalls because nothing either says bears on the other's claim; the apparent contradiction is an artifact of scope, and the live question is not whether the war happened but how much determinacy a tradition-object can carry before we are entitled to call it the memory of an event.

Assess this hypothesis rather than restate it to me. In particular:

1. Is the scope diagnosis correct, and what exactly turns on it? If the two positions can be re-expressed so that they genuinely contradict each other, perform that re-expression and say whether the resulting disagreement is empirical, methodological or conceptual.

2. Consider whether a substantive hidden disagreement is doing the work behind the surface one — for instance about what excavation can in principle deliver concerning a single event, about how much information survives a given number of centuries of oral transmission, or about whether arguments from the silence of the Near Eastern archives are symmetric between the camps or whether the party making the positive claim bears a heavier burden. Identify which of these, if any, is the real crux, and whether it is decidable in principle.

3. Say what would count as progress. I do not want a wish-list of future excavations; I want to know what kind of thing could move the dispute at all — a sharper analysis of the claim being made, evidence with the right logical shape, an explicit model of transmission with stated parameters, or a deliberate change of question — and for each candidate, whether it is realistically available. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, defend it and explain why the dispute is nonetheless worth maintaining rather than merely interminable.

4. Address the third camp. Suppose their objection is that the question is ill-posed, because it demands a determinate yes-or-no answer about an object that is determinate only as a narrative and indeterminate as an event. Is that position coherent and genuinely distinct from reductionism, or does it collapse into it? And can it be stated without conceding everything to scepticism — that is, is there a version of the claim that the legend has a historical core which survives your diagnosis, and what does accepting it cost?

Argue from the characterization of the positions given above; you may refine it, but do not substitute a survey of the scholarship or a verdict on the war's historicity. Where you disagree with my hypothesis, I want to know precisely where and why.
