For the last couple of years my day job has been profiling and untangling our CI/build pipeline, and the finding that keeps repeating is this: when you make one stage 5x faster, wall-clock time barely moves, because the machine queue, the test shard scheduler, and the human review at the end just soak up the gains. You spend a month optimizing the compiler and nothing happens. You spend a week on the merge queue and suddenly everything ships faster.

That got me into the AI takeoff debate from a weird direction, and I've landed on a position I now want stress-tested rather than defended.

**The position:** there won't be a discontinuity in AI progress, because an intelligence explosion is a throughput problem, not a speed problem. Even if AI systems get dramatically better at generating research ideas, writing code, and designing experiments, end-to-end capability gain is capped by whatever fraction of the pipeline can't be parallelized or automated — chip fab lead times, datacenter power and hardware delivery, the wall-clock cost of training runs and evals, capital, and the verification step where a human still has to check that the output is actually good. So: real capability gains, sure, but wall-clock progress stays a smoothish curve with at most mild step changes, spread over a lot of years. Basically Amdahl's law — the explosion is bounded by the serial fraction, and the serial fraction is big.

What would count against this? I want a map of where the view is exposed, and I'm fine with the answer being that it's badly exposed.

Some things I specifically want addressed:

- Which observations, results, or arguments would actually defeat it, versus which would just be annoying for it? Rank them, most decisive first.
- What would have to be true for a genuinely fast takeoff anyway? I want the coherent stories where the serial fraction shrinks or gets routed around — e.g. AI work shortens the fab and datacenter timelines too, or verification gets automated, or capability jumps come from using existing compute much better so the bottleneck never binds. Say which of those routes are live and which are just conceivable.
- Stuff that has arguably already happened and cuts against me. Be concrete about what happened and why it counts as evidence, and honest about how much weight it really carries — I'd rather have three good items than fifteen weak ones.
- What does my view predict that the fast-takeoff view doesn't? Give me the discriminating ones, where the two would visibly diverge over the next handful of years, not the ones where both sides can claim any outcome.
- The worry I cant shake: this might be unfalsifiable, because whenever a bottleneck I predicted fails to bind, I can just say fine, the bottleneck relocated, there's always a serial fraction. Is that fatal, is it a normal feature of a conservation-style claim, or somewhere in between? And what would the position have to give up to be genuinely risky?
- Which of these would the fast-takeoff people themselves concede counts against them — I want their own standards, not just what critics throw at them.

No citations or reading lists please, I'm not going to chase anything down, I just want the reasoning.
