**The student's answer** (transcribed as written, typos are theirs):

> The logical problem of evil says that evil is just flat-out inconsistent with an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God. That's the version Mackie pressed, and it's pretty much considered dead now, because Plantinga showed that God and evil aren't logically contradictory. Rowe accepts this. So what he's doing instead is an evidential arguement: even if the two are compatible, the actual pattern and amount of suffering in the world makes theism much less likely, and Rowe wants to show that it's unreasonable to keep believing.
>
> His example is a fawn trapped in a forest fire, horribly burned, that dies in agony days later where nobody ever sees it. He says there's no good we can think of that would justify God in letting that happen — it doesn't build the fawn's character, it doesn't teach anybody a lesson, and an omnipotent being could have prevented it without losing anything. So the argument is:
> 1. There are instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.
> 2. An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent any such suffering it could.
> 3. Therefore there is no omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being.
>
> Really the whole thing is an argument from ignorance: we can't think of a reason, so we conclude there isn't one. Rowe is upfront that this makes it inductive rather than deductive.
>
> Plantinga's free will defense is that a world containing genuinely free creatures is much better than a world of automata, and God can't cause someone to freely do the right thing, so any world he actualizes with real freedom in it may contain moral evil. Plantinga shows that God's morally sufficient reason for permitting evil is human freedom, and that's what makes the answer work — it doesn't matter that we don't know the reason for each particular evil, because we know the general one. What it clearly doesn't cover is natural evil, like the fawn: the fire and the pain aren't anyone's free choice, so Plantinga has nothing to say about that, and Rowe's central example survives the free will defense untouched.
>
> Skeptical theism is the more general response. Wykstra's CORNEA says that since God is infinitely greater and wiser than we are, we shouldn't expect to be able to understand his reasons, and so we should accept that these evils do serve some purpose we can't see. On that view Rowe's premise 1 is unjustified and the argument fails.
>
> Is it costless? No. If we can never rule out that some apparent atrocity serves a greater good we can't grasp, then it looks like we can never know that any action is wrong — the person torturing a child might be doing what God has a reason for. That's the main cost I see, and skeptical theists tend to say it's just the price of taking God's transcendence seriously.
