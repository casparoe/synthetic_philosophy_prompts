I have spent twenty years in process-safety engineering. My working vocabulary is fault trees and event trees, minimal cut sets, bow-tie diagrams, barriers and the Swiss-cheese picture of accident causation, and root-cause analysis with its distinctions among root causes, contributing factors, and background conditions. Philosophy is new to me, but I have been reading about metaphysical grounding — the claim that some facts obtain in virtue of others, and that a fact is fundamental just in case nothing grounds it — and I keep feeling that this is my day job with the time axis deleted. I would like that intuition tested rather than flattered.

The assignment has two parts.

First, restate the grounding debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Take each of the following claims and render it in terms of gates, edges, cut sets, barriers, and root causes, preserving the technical content rather than the flavor of it: (a) grounding is a strict partial order — irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive; (b) the grounding hierarchy terminates in ungrounded fundamentals, versus the alternatives of infinitely descending chains and of structures with no minimal element at all; (c) grounding differs from causation in being constitutive and synchronic rather than productive and diachronic; (d) grounding differs from entailment or necessitation, because the same necessary connections can hold without the same priority ordering; (e) whether grounding is one uniform relation or a family of specific ones with only a family resemblance; (f) whether claims about grounding are themselves grounded, or whether the framework must exempt them from its own discipline; (g) the deflationary option on which "because" is a device for offering explanations rather than the name of any relation out in the world.

Second, and more important, the residue report. For each of the seven, state whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion — and if so, name precisely the step where you had to fudge — or has no expression at all in fault-tree terms. Then answer the two questions I actually care about. Is the residue a defect in my vocabulary, meaning that there is a genuine notion of non-causal in-virtue-of dependence that a diagram of events cannot hold? Or is it evidence that grounding talk is less substantive than it looks — a redescription of entailment, of causation, or of not much at all? And run the translation in the other direction as well: which of my own instruments have no counterpart in the grounding debate — barriers and redundancy, probabilistic gates, minimal cut sets, and above all the action-relative character of a root cause, which is the node you can intervene on — and does their absence mark a limitation of the metaphysics, or simply a difference in what the two enterprises are for?

Write for a reader fluent in reliability engineering and untrained in philosophy: define every philosophical term of art at the point of use. No references, no reading list, no author names — describe the positions rather than attributing them, though you may give a position's standard label alongside a gloss in my terms. Where two philosophical views cannot be told apart by anything in my vocabulary, say so explicitly instead of forcing a mapping; those cases are the interesting ones. The output should read as a report I could hand to a colleague in the safety group.
