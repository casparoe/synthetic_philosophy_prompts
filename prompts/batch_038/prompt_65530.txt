You are a philosopher of biology with a working knowledge of comparative anatomy and evolutionary developmental biology, and you have followed the adaptationism debates that grew out of Gould and Lewontin's 1979 critique of the adaptationist programme. I am asking you to do case-work, not to summarise that debate. Work carefully and commit to definite verdicts.

**The material.** Below is a wholly fictional genus, *Paludomys*: six species of small omnivorous mammals, four of them stream-dwelling and two fully terrestrial, plus a fossil outgroup. Every fact you need is stipulated here; treat the stipulations as exact and as the complete evidence base. Do not look for real-world counterparts, and do not supplement the descriptions with background you think you remember about analogous animals. Where I have supplied a number — a slope, a correlation, an experimental result — assume it is precisely measured.

- **T1. The palatal keel.** In all six species the two halves of the palate form as separate bony plates that fuse along the midline late in fetal development. Wherever the plates meet at a shallow angle, a raised keel of bone is left along the seam; keel height across individuals and species is an almost perfectly monotonic function of fusion angle and of nothing else. Four species use the keel to strip bark. A fifth has come to rasp it against its lower incisors to produce a courtship sound, and in that species males producing louder rasps sire more young. The sixth species eats only soft fruit, never uses the keel for anything, and has the tallest keel in the genus.
- **T2. The flank pocket and its gland.** The geometry of the hindlimb girdle against the torso leaves a shallow triangular pocket of skin at the hip in every species. In two species a scent gland has developed inside the pocket and is used in territory marking; individuals with functioning glands hold territories longer. In the other four the pocket is empty, hairless, mildly prone to abrasion, and has no measurable effect on survival or reproduction.
- **T3. Hind-foot webbing.** In the three most aquatic species the extent of webbing sits at an optimum measured in a flow tank. The same degree of webbing is present in the two fully terrestrial species, which never enter water, and in the fossil outgroup, whose own ecology is not recoverable from the remains. Experimentally reducing webbing during development impairs swimming measurably and leaves every other trait unchanged.
- **T4. Relative liver mass.** Liver mass against body mass falls on a single line across all six species and across forty related genera, with a slope matching what is predicted if liver mass simply tracks the volume of the abdominal cavity. Species that shifted diet secondarily lie on the same line as species that never did. Residual deviation from the line is uncorrelated with diet, activity level, or any fitness proxy measured.
- **T5. The tail tuft.** A terminal tuft of stiff hairs occurs only in the two species that sleep at exposed burrow mouths and use it to brush off flies. It is absent in all congeners and in the outgroup. It develops late from a distinct follicle field, and within those two species tuft size predicts parasite load and number of sleep interruptions.
- **T6. The blue eye-corner tint.** Two species, which are not each other's closest relatives, carry a bluish tint at the inner corner of the eye. It is produced by a single amino-acid change in a transporter protein that has no other detectable role. In experiments the tint makes no difference to vision, to ultraviolet screening, or to how conspecifics respond to the bearer. Both species occupy shaded pools in which several untinted relatives also live, and reconstructed population sizes at the time the tint arose were very small.
- **T7. The supernumerary molar cusp.** All six species retain a small extra cusp on the last molar that plays no part in chewing. It is present in the fossil outgroup, where wear patterns indicate a hard-food diet that no living species practises. It develops from the same enamel-knot sequence as the functional cusps, and no manipulation has eliminated it without also eliminating those. It costs measurable mineral and slightly raises the risk of crown fracture.

**Task 1 — diagnostics first.** Before you rate anything, spend one short paragraph setting out the diagnostic questions you will apply to each trait. They must be capable of actually separating a trait that selection built for its present effect from a trait that the organism's construction throws off as a side-effect from a trait that is merely functionless, and from a trait that was once selected for something else and is now carried along. State in a sentence why those categories are not interchangeable, and note explicitly that a trait may score high on more than one of them at once.

**Task 2 — two-axis ratings.** Score each trait on both axes below, using the whole range rather than clustering near the middle. Score with respect to the species in which the trait is most developed, and add a clause if the score would differ for other species. If a trait's classification turns on something the stipulations leave silent, say so instead of filling the gap with a guess.

- *Axis A — Adaptation (0–10):* how strongly the stipulated evidence supports the claim that natural selection shaped this trait for the effect it currently has. 0 means the evidence positively rules that out, 5 means genuinely underdetermined, 10 means the evidence is as strong as such claims ever get.
- *Axis B — Architectural byproduct (0–10):* how strongly the evidence supports the claim that the trait's presence is explained by the geometry, the developmental sequence, or the inherited construction of the organism as a whole, rather than by any selection on the trait itself. Same anchors.

Present these as a table: trait, Axis A, Axis B, a classification label drawn from *{adaptation, exapted adaptation, byproduct later co-opted, byproduct still unused, neutral variant, phylogenetic legacy, indeterminate}*, and one sentence of justification naming the specific stipulation that does the work.

**Task 3 — tier list.** Arrange the seven traits into tiers according to how much damage each would do to a colleague who insists that every trait of these animals is an adaptation for the role it now performs. Top tier for the case they have no good answer to; bottom tier for the case they would cheerfully absorb into their own framework. One sentence per trait saying why it lands where it does.

**Task 4 — rating the argumentative moves.** The 1979 critique is usually reconstructed as four distinct moves, paraphrased here. Rate each from 0 to 1 for how much of its original force you think it retains today, where 0 means it survives only as a historical curiosity and 1 means it is still correct and still needed as a corrective. One or two sentences of reasoning per move.

1. That a trait's present usefulness tells you nothing by itself about how the trait arose, and that confusing the two questions has systematically inflated the number of traits the field is willing to call adaptations.
2. That an organism is not an assemblage of independently optimisable parts: the geometry and developmental sequence of the whole channel and limit what form the parts can take, so any account that never mentions that architecture is incomplete rather than merely terse.
3. That the field's default procedures are asymmetric — selectionist hypotheses are accepted on plausibility while rival explanations are seldom even formulated — so a standing presumption in favour of alternatives is needed as a corrective to practice, not as a claim about the world.
4. That the architectural metaphor itself is a good instrument for teaching these lessons. On this last one, weigh the following objection: the decorated surfaces in the Venetian basilica are more accurately pendentives — transitional surfaces carrying a circular dome onto a square base — than spandrels, and they were deliberately designed as decoration and could have been left as blank wall, so the analogy's architectural premise is technically wrong and its rhetorical force depends on a space that was, in fact, meant to be filled. Judge whether a metaphor's technical inaccuracy in its source domain weakens the biological argument it is borrowed to make.

**Task 5 — two closing judgements.** Name the trait whose classification you are least confident about, and identify the single further observation that would move your scores most, saying in which direction and by roughly how much. Then name the move from Task 4 that you consider least defensible as things now stand, and say concretely what would have to be true to rehabilitate it.

Do not cite sources, list readings, or pad the answer with history; I have deliberately given you everything you need. Be decisive: where the evidence is evenly balanced, say which way you lean and what tips it.
