You are a philosopher of science with a working command of the truthlikeness literature and, separately, real experience sitting in on model-evaluation meetings inside engineering and environmental agencies. Treat what follows as a design problem rather than an expository one, and think carefully before answering: the difficulty here is not knowing the literature but making it survive contact with a committee that has deadlines.

I chair the modeling panel at a national environmental regulator. Several times a year we must designate one process-based model of subsurface contaminant transport as the reference model behind an exposure standard. We never choose between candidates one of which is true; all of them are known to be false, and everyone in the room knows it. What the panel currently does is indefensible: we rank by out-of-sample fit against the historical monitoring record, then settle disputes with a show of hands on "physical plausibility." The problem is that the designated model is then used for questions no data can settle — plume behaviour under an injection regime nobody has run, barrier integrity thirty years out, what a remediation order must achieve — and fit to the historical record is close to irrelevant to those questions. A model can win the fit contest and be the worse guide exactly where the decision lives.

I want a written procedure the panel can run on a pair of candidate models in a single two-day workshop, staffed by hydrogeologists and transport modellers who have never read a page of philosophy. Two things I need you to engage with rather than wave away:

First, my own half-formed idea, which I want pressure-tested and then either built on or dismantled: I suspect the defensible question is not which model is closer to the truth outright, but which model's errors are least entangled with the specific question we need it to answer — something like decision-relative truthlikeness, or error-orthogonality to the query. If you think that is a confusion, say so and tell me what the panel should be asking instead.

Second, an alignment problem I cannot see past. Our candidates do not share a vocabulary. One is written in terms of effective saturation and capillary-pressure curves; another partitions the domain into mobile and immobile regions; a third carries a distributed set of first-order rate constants. As I understand it, the accounts of closeness to the truth come in two families — one measuring likeness to the way things actually are, one measuring how much of a theory's content is right against how much is wrong — and both appear to be defined over statements in a fixed language, with a standing worry that the ordering shifts when the vocabulary does. If that is right, tell me plainly whether the machinery is simply inapplicable to us, or whether vocabulary alignment is a legitimate first step in a procedure, and if so what the stopping rule for that step is and how a panel member would know an alignment was dishonest.

Structure the deliverable as follows. Begin with a short brainstorming section: at least four candidate framings of what the panel is actually trying to decide, with one line each on why you reject the ones you reject, then a commitment to the framing you will build. Then give the procedure itself — the questions asked in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which it halts with a finding that the candidates cannot be ranked; and the points at which the decision is handed to a person because what remains is a matter of regulatory values rather than of truth. Define every term as you introduce it, and flag explicitly which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume.

Four further requirements:

1. Do not let the procedure quietly assume a contested account of closeness to the truth. Wherever a step depends on choosing between the two families above, or on the difference between how close a model is to the truth and how close we can estimate it to be, say so, and say what the panel would do differently under the other choice.
2. Handle these hard cases: two models that fit every available dataset equally well and differ only in structure; a model that is measurably more accurate on the record but whose residuals are correlated with the intervention variable we must predict; and a pair for which no honest alignment between vocabularies exists.
3. Run the procedure on two or three worked cases of your own construction, specific and realistic, and include at least one in which it rejects the model I would personally have favoured. I need to see that it can bite me.
4. Add a back-test: how the panel would audit the procedure against past designations whose consequences we now know, and what result would count as evidence that the procedure is worthless.

Close with what the procedure cannot do, and the two or three questions on which I should expect the panel to deadlock however carefully it runs. Then give a one-page version suitable for handing to a member who has read none of the above.

I will supply the references myself, so produce no bibliography and invent no citations; naming positions, arguments, and known negative results is welcome and expected.
