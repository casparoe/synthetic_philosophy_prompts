I have settled into a fairly definite version of the Downsian position on voting, and I want it stress-tested rather than summarized. It has two parts. The first is an arithmetic claim: in a large-scale election the probability that any single ballot is decisive is so small that, multiplied by the difference the outcome makes to the voter, it falls below the cost of turning out, so instrumental rationality counsels abstention. The second is an interpretive claim: the fact that most citizens vote anyway is evidence about their motives — duty, expression, sociability, plain error — and not evidence against the calculation, since the calculation concerns what rationality requires rather than what people happen to do.

Map the exposure of that position. I want to know what would have to be true for it to be false, with the candidates separated into those that would be decisive and those that would merely be awkward. Take up each of the following pressure points and tell me how much weight it actually bears:

- The decisive-probability estimate itself. Whether the familiar order-of-magnitude intuition is the right one, how the probability of a tied outcome differs from the probability of casting the decisive vote, and how much the estimate moves once it is conditioned on genuine competitiveness rather than averaged across all elections. What empirical finding about close races would force a revision rather than earn a footnote?

- The specification of the stakes term. If the voter's concern extends beyond himself, the benefit attached to his preferred outcome scales with the number of people affected, and that number grows with the size of the electorate. Is the paradox therefore an artifact of egoism rather than of scale, and what must be assumed about the magnitude of other-regarding concern for that escape route to work?

- The causal structure of large numbers. Cases in which each of many acts makes no difference on its own though together they make a great deal; the hitchhiker-style case in which each vote genuinely matters; and the further suggestion that making a difference is not a sharp matter admitting a yes-or-no answer. Which of these are objections to the arithmetic, and which are objections to an ethic erected on top of it?

- Evidential accounts, on which my ballot is evidence about how voters like me will vote. Whether these are a live alternative, and what their reductio costs them: the same reasoning appears to license voting against my own preferences whenever that would be diagnostic of others doing likewise.

- The addition of a term for the intrinsic satisfaction of voting. Whether this is a legitimate extension of the utility function or an immunizing strategy that leaves the position compatible with every possible turnout level. If it is the latter, is that fatal, or is it defensible given that the target of the position is rationality rather than prediction?

Then tell me where the position is confirmed rather than merely unfalsified — small committees, tied votes, referendums, low-salience local races — and whether its failure to generalize upward is the real content of the objection. Treat the comparative statics separately from the level: turnout does appear to respond to expected closeness and to the cost of voting, and I want to know whether that corroborates the framework even while its absolute prediction fails. Say what the position predicts that its rivals — expressive accounts, civic-duty accounts, evidential accounts — do not, and where it risks collapsing into one of them. For each defeater, state whether a sophisticated defender would concede it as counting against the position or dismiss it as beside the point, and close with a frank verdict on whether the view is well insulated and, if so, whether that insulation is a defect for a claim of this kind.
