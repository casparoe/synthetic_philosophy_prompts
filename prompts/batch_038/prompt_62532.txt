Im slowly working my way through Cicero's Academica — the second book, the one usually called Lucullus — in the Rackham translation, and I've hit a bit about the sorites that I only half understand. My sense of the context: they're arguing over whether any "presentation" can be so clear that it guarantees its own truth, and the skeptics keep dragging in dreams and madmen. Then Lucullus (I think it's Lucullus objecting here, but honestly I lose track of who's attacking whom in this dialogue) says:

"And as a first point one must criticise them for employing an exceedingly captious kind of argument, of a sort that is usually by no means approved of in philosophy – the method of proceeding by minute steps of gradual addition or withdrawal. They call this class of arguments sōritae because by adding a single grain at a time they make a heap. It is certainly an erroneous and captious kind of argument – for you go on mounting up in this way: 'If a presentation put by the deity before a man asleep is of such a character that it is probable, why not also of such a character that it is extremely like a true one? then, why not such that it can with difficulty be distinguished from a true one? then, that it cannot even be distinguished? finally, that there is no difference between the one and the other?' If you reach this conclusion owing to my yielding to you each successive step, the fault will have been mine; but if you get there of your own accord, it will be yours. For who will have granted you either that the deity is omnipotent, or that even if he can do as described he will? and how do you make such assumptions that, if it is possible for X to resemble Y, it will follow that only with difficulty can X and Y be known apart? and then, that they cannot even be known apart? and finally, that they are identical? for example, if wolves are like dogs, you will end by saying that they are identical. And it is a fact that some honourable things are like dishonourable ones and some good things like not good ones and some artistic things like inartistic ones; why do we hesitate therefore to aver that there is no difference between these?"

Things I cant get my head around:

- The argument he names a sorites isn't about grains or heaps at all, it's a run of questions about how much a god-sent dream can resemble a true presentation. Is that just the heap argument with a different predicate? What's playing the role of the single grain being added — resemblance, or distinguishability?

- The bit about "the fault will have been mine … it will be yours" feels like the crux and I don't get its force. Is he claiming the conclusion doesn't follow, or only that he shouldn't have kept saying yes? It reads like he's granting that each individual step is harmless and blaming himself for the pile-up, which seems a wierd thing to say if he thinks the whole procedure is bogus. And is going quiet at some point in the series being floated as the right response, or as a dodge?

- The wolves and dogs line: is that a real counterexample that breaks the chain, or just an insistence that the world has joints? If you think resemblance shades off gradually with no sharp cut anywhere, what do you answer him with?

- Which makes me wonder if he's commited to a sharp line between "can with difficulty be distinguished" and "cannot even be distinguished." If he is, is that the weak point of his case or the entire point of it?
