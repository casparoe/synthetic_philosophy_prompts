**Question 3 (35 points).** A hospital deploys a machine-learning tool to rank patients for ICU beds. The tool keeps updating itself on incoming data, so its later rankings differ from anything its developers tested. Six months in, it systematically deprioritizes a group of patients, and several of them die — patients a reasonable clinician would have admitted. Nobody chose that outcome and nobody saw it coming. Some philosophers call this a "responsibility gap." State the argument that such a gap exists. Then evaluate the most common reply: that the gap is only apparent, because responsibility can be picked up forward-looking (duties to monitor, audit, and repair) or collectively (by the institution rather than by any person). Be explicit at each step about which notion of responsibility you are using.

**Student's answer:**

The responsibility gap argument says that when a machine learning system produces a harm, there is nobody left to hold responsible. Responsibility normally requires control and knowledge: you can only be blamed for something if you could have done otherwise, or at least if you could have foreseen it. In the ICU case the developers can't be blamed, because the tool kept learning after they handed it over, so the specific rankings it produced six months later weren't in anything they tested and couldn't have been predicted. The clinicians can't be blamed either, since they were following the protocol and had no reason to distrust the ranking — the tool had been validated by the hospital. The patients obviously did nothing. And the system itself isn't the kind of thing that can be responsible, because it has no intentions, can't be punished, and can't feel guilt. So the harm is real but there's no one it can be attributed to. That's the gap: a wrong without a wrongdoer.

The usual reply is that we don't have to look backwards at all. The hospital has a forward-looking responsibility to monitor the tool, to audit it regularly, and to compensate families when it goes wrong. There's also a collective responsibility belonging to the whole group that designed, bought, installed and used the system — the many hands problem doesn't mean the hands aren't there. On this view the gap is just an artifact of assuming responsibility has to be blame for a past act. Once you allow a forward-looking notion, the hospital is clearly on the hook: it has a duty of care toward its patients, and duties of care are what negligence is based on. So there is no gap, just a different way of assigning things.

I think the reply mostly works, and it shows the deeper point that retributive blame is the wrong tool for machine learning systems. Where the harm comes out of an accumulation of small, individually blameless decisions by programmers, procurement officers, nurses and administrators, the right response isn't to hunt for a culprit but to spread the cost — say through an insurance or compensation fund — so that the people who benefit from the technology also carry its harms, which are unpredictable one at a time but predictable in aggregate. The one place the reply doesn't cover everything is the developers who chose not to build in a monitoring step. That was a decision they made knowingly and it traces back to them, so they are blameworthy. But that's a failure about the process, not about the rankings themselves.

So the gap is narrower than it first looks. There may be nobody to blame for the particular deaths, but there are plenty of people with responsibilities going forward, and that's what actually matters for policy.
