I chair the scientific panel of a private certification scheme for farmed aquatic animals, and our draft standard on crustaceans has been blocked for fourteen months by a dispute between two camps on the panel. Rather than let it stall, I have asked both to produce a single document they can each sign, to be published alongside the draft standard. Write that document.

The two camps are these. Call them the Sentience-Precaution camp and the Validation-First camp. The Sentience-Precaution camp holds that the available comparative evidence — prolonged protective behaviour at injury sites, learned avoidance of shelters paired with noxious stimuli, motivational trade-offs in which animals accept a noxious exposure to obtain a resource, and opioid-sensitive modulation of nociceptive responses in decapods — already justifies treating farmed decapods as presumptively capable of negative experience, and that a standard may not wait for species-by-species demonstration before requiring changes to handling, stocking, and killing. They note that at least one jurisdiction has already recognised the sentience of certain decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs in statute on the strength of a commissioned scientific review. The Validation-First camp holds that most welfare indicators in aquaculture were developed and validated in salmonids and that transferring them across this phylogenetic distance is an inference nobody has yet earned; that "insensible," "aversive," and "stress" are being used as if they had settled crustacean referents when they do not; that mandates resting on unvalidated indicators can actively harm animals — for instance by pushing production to jurisdictions with no standard at all, or by requiring an intervention that reduces survival or fitness without reducing suffering — and that precaution is a two-directional principle, not a one-directional one.

The deliverable should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. I expect some of these to be non-trivial — about the comparative anatomy and physiology of decapod nociception, about what the behavioural studies do and do not show, about the absence of any agreed operational test for consciousness in invertebrates, about which current practices (eyestalk ablation of broodstock, live air exposure and desiccation, ice-slurry chilling, spiking or segmentation, electrical stunning) are candidates for restriction and on what grounds. Where a claim both can sign is weaker than either camp's private view, say so in a marginal note rather than inflating it.

2. A disagreements table. For each contested issue: the issue; a formulation of the disagreement that the Sentience-Precaution camp would accept as a fair statement of its own position; a formulation the Validation-First camp would accept as a fair statement of its own; a classification as empirical, normative, or verbal; and whether it is a crux. Do not let either column be a strawman or a caricature.

3. The cruxes proper — the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one camp. For each, name the study, measurement, or argument that both camps agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance, specific enough to be wrong. At least one crux must concern whether stress physiology in decapods tracks welfare the way cortisol-based measures are taken to in teleosts; at least one must concern whether the sheer number of individual animals farmed annually, including postlarval stages, should shift the evidentiary threshold that a mandate must clear, and whether larval animals belong in that count at all; at least one must concern whether a method's efficacy can be demonstrated for a group rather than a species.

4. Falsifiers. What each camp agrees would count against its own position, and whether there is anything both agree would move the debate as a whole.

5. A worked clause. Our draft says that animals must be rendered insensible before killing by a method whose efficacy has been demonstrated for the species concerned, and must remain insensible until death. The two camps read "demonstrated" and "species concerned" incompatibly. Show both readings, then produce a single wording both can sign — or, if no wording exists that both can sign, state the default rule and the review trigger they can jointly accept instead, so that an auditor on a farm can act next quarter either way.

Two constraints on the prose. No sentence may consist of a hedge such as "more research is needed" unless it is attached to a specified study and a specified expected result. And wherever you judge part of the dispute to have been about words rather than about the world, say which part, what the two camps meant, and what is left over once the verbal component is removed — I suspect something is left over, but I do not want the document to assume it.
