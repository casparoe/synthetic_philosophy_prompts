I have no training in philosophy. This is an argument I worked out for myself over several years of disagreeing with a friend about whether there are any moral rules that hold without exception, and I have come to distrust it without being able to locate the fault. I would like it taken apart properly.

(1) A rule guides my conduct only if I can tell that it applies to the situation I am in.
(2) Telling that a rule applies is itself a judgment about what is to be done in that situation. The rule does not announce its own instances; if I needed a second rule to fix which cases the first one covers, I would need a third to fix which cases the second covers, and so on without end.
(3) So I can follow a rule only in a case where I could already have settled what to do without it.
(4) What I can settle without a rule is not settled by the rule. At most the rule records the outcome of a judgment I was able to make on my own.
(5) A record of past cases cannot settle a new one, because whether the new case resembles the old ones is itself a judgment, and by (2) no rule supplies it.
(6) Therefore no rule guides conduct. Moral principles do not: they are summaries, and the actual work is always done by the judgment that reads them off against the case in front of it.

(7) I cannot accept (6), because the reasoning that produces it produces things that are obviously false. My daughter cannot do long division, and being taught the procedure is precisely what makes her answers correct rather than lucky. The rules of the road tell a driver what to do at an intersection she has never approached. Someone who learns that a knight moves in an L has been given something he did not already possess, and can then play a game he could not have played before. In each of these cases the rule settles a question the person could not settle without it, which is exactly what (3) through (6) deny is possible.

(8) So the argument proves too much: the premises that dispose of moral principles dispose of arithmetic, driving, and chess along with them, and I am not prepared to pay that price.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it comes down to a choice, I would rather abandon (6) than (2), since the regress in (2) looks to me like a plain fact about how rules work and not a claim anyone could argue against. But I want to know what I am buying by keeping it.

I also want to know whether the cases in (7) are genuinely parallel to the moral case or whether the analogy gives out somewhere. If it gives out, tell me exactly where it gives out and what turns on the difference: whether that difference rescues moral principles, or only leaves them neither refuted nor explained, which is not much of a victory for either side.

Finally, once the diagnosis is in, tell me what each camp in the disagreement I started from has to give up. I mean the people who think there are principles admitting of no exceptions, and the people who think there are no such principles, or that every principle someone proposes has some case that defeats it. I am not asking for names or books, only for what each position is left holding and which of those holdings is the more expensive.
