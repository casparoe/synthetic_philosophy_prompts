I should say at the outset that I have no academic training in philosophy. What follows comes from a few years of reading on my own, mostly in translation, and from watching one film far more often than is entirely reasonable. I would like a reading that is willing to tell me where I have got things wrong, rather than one that flatters the question.

The film is Christopher Nolan's Memento. The Borges I have in mind is the essayist rather than the fabulist: specifically the essay usually titled in English as a new refutation of time, which as I understand it builds on the British idealists and on Schopenhauer to argue that there is no such thing as an objective succession of instants — that each moment of awareness is a complete, self-sufficient presentation, that "before" and "after" are something we impose on it, and that the persisting self which supposedly carries these moments along is a construction rather than a fact. I am aware that Borges is writing as much to perform an argument as to win it, and I may well have flattened him into something more like a thesis than he intended. If my paraphrase is wrong in a way that matters for what follows, please say so first and correct it, because the rest of my question depends on it.

My own hunch, which I cannot quite get past, concerns the film's form. Memento runs two strands: black-and-white material that proceeds forward in time, and colour material that proceeds backward, so that each colour scene shows the effect before the cause. The strands meet at the moment the black-and-white turns to colour — the shooting in the derelict building. I want to suggest that this convergence is doing more than hiding information from the audience. It is the one point in the film where the two temporal orders coincide, and if the film's shape is a single point at which a forward order and a backward order touch, then the film is not really a picture of a life with a broken memory in it. It is a picture of an instant that keeps arriving. On that reading, Leonard is not a man who persists and has lost his continuity; he is a series of momentary selves, each of which wakes up in possession of an archive — tattoos, annotated photographs, a story about a man called Sammy Jankis — that it did not write and cannot verify, and each of which takes that archive for a past. The tragedy would then not be memory loss at all, but the absence of succession, which is exactly what Borges's essay claims is always the case and which we normally fail to notice because our archives are consistent.

I can see three places where this reading looks fragile, and I would like you to press on them rather than rescue me. First, the warning Leonard writes on the photograph of the corrupt cop, telling his future self not to trust the man, appears to be an act of cross-temporal intention: a present self binding a self that does not yet exist. Second, the ending, where Leonard has been told the truth and deliberately destroys the evidence and misdirects himself toward a new target, seems to require precisely the continuing agent who can deceive himself over a year. Third, the cop's accusation is that Leonard has been manufacturing his own purpose for a long time, which only means anything if there is someone who has been doing it. A momentariness reading looks like it has to explain these away, and I suspect that explaining them away is what makes the reading dishonest.

I would also like to know whether I have chosen the wrong Borges. The story I keep reaching for is the one about the condemned writer granted a whole year of subjective duration inside the single objective instant of his execution: a self given time without succession. That seems closer to Leonard's situation than the essay does, and if so, my whole framing may be built on the wrong text.

So: is the reading available, and if it is, what does it cost? Please work through the specific scenes I have described rather than summarising the plot, and do not put any lines in quotation marks, since neither of us can check them and I would rather have accurate description than invented dialogue. Finish by saying what the film contributes to the question of whether selves persist that the essay's argument on its own could not deliver — or, if your honest verdict is that it contributes nothing and that the pairing is only a pleasing coincidence of two works about disorientation, say that plainly. Please do not end with suggestions of further things to read; I am not looking for a syllabus, only for your judgement.
