I cover science and ideas for a British newspaper, and I am writing a long read on compulsory voting that uses the Australian machinery as its case study. What I need from you is not a survey of the arguments for and against compulsion, but a reconstruction of what that machinery quietly assumes about what a vote is for. The details I am working from are these.

Voting is a legal duty. An elector who fails to attend a polling place is sent a penalty notice for twenty Australian dollars, and well over a million such notices are issued after each federal election. The Act describes the duty as including the marking of the ballot paper, but because the ballot is secret the electoral commission can only ever establish that someone attended, had their name marked off and was issued a paper. No one is prosecuted for what they did with it: an elector may hand in a blank sheet, or one defaced with a drawing, and the paper is set aside as informal, uncounted and unpunished.

In the House of Representatives, a ballot on which the voter has not numbered every candidate in consecutive order is informal and discarded. There is no way to record indifference between two candidates, no way to rank only those one considers tolerable, and no way to decline to place a candidate one detests anywhere in the order at all. In the Senate, since 2016, the same voter on the same day may stop ranking whenever they wish.

The participation figure the commission publishes, and the one cited when the health of the democracy is assessed, is calculated by dividing the sum of formal and informal votes by total enrolment: blank and spoiled ballots count towards it.

The Act treats a religious duty to abstain as a valid and sufficient reason for failing to vote. The courts have held that a sincere, carefully worked-out non-religious moral conviction against voting is not one.

Against that background, I have six questions. First: what theory of the value of voting does this bundle presuppose — that a vote is a small causal contribution to an outcome, that it is a statement of judgement, that it is a ritual act of membership in the demos, or that what matters is the mandate and legitimacy of whoever wins? Please distinguish what the rules strictly require from what they merely happen to sit comfortably with. Second: is the state's indifference to content evidence that only the aggregate matters, or can an expressive theory absorb it? And what does the combination of compulsory attendance with unverifiable marking presuppose about whether the vote is addressed to anybody, given that secrecy means no one else can ever read it? Third: where do the parts pull against one another — mandatory ranking all the way down in one house against optional ranking in the other, costless blank ballots against blank ballots counting as participation, religious conscience excused while moral conscience is not? Does each pair rest on a different theory, and which one is the practice actually committed to? Fourth: what does a twenty-dollar penalty, issued en masse and frequently waived on explanation, presuppose about the status of the duty it enforces? Fifth: where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would license exactly the same rules? Sixth: what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a purely expressive one, or by a purely outcome-based one — which feature would go first, which would survive untouched, and what would the lesser-evil judgement that a House ballot forces on every voter become?

You may name thinkers where it genuinely helps, but I want the reasoning rather than a reading list, and I want it pitched at an intelligent general reader without sanding off the philosophy.
