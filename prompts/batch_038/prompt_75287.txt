I am in my final year of high school and I run a small reading group at my school; beyond what we read ourselves I have no training in philosophy, so it is possible I am missing an obvious piece of vocabulary. There is a move that keeps appearing in arguments about very large collections of observers, and I cannot find a name for it.

The move involves two rules for turning the fact that I exist into a probability. In my own shorthand I call them the numbers rule and the typicality rule. Under the numbers rule, a hypothesis that says there are more observers, planets, or universes than a rival hypothesis is made more likely simply by the fact that someone like me exists, because a larger collection contains more chances for someone like me to turn up inside it. Under the typicality rule, the size of the collection is beside the point: what matters is whether I am an ordinary member of the class I belong to, and my evidence informs me about the character of that class, not about how many members it has.

Here are three cases where I watched the same person rely on one rule and then the other.

First, a cosmology presenter arguing that a multiverse explains why our universe permits life: given a vast number of universes, observers will arise somewhere among them, and we should not be surprised to find ourselves in one of the few that are life-permitting. The size of the collection does all the work. In a later video the same presenter answers an objection — that if there really are that many universes, observers who flicker into being out of random fluctuations, complete with false memories of an orderly cosmos and a long past, ought to vastly outnumber ordinary ones, so a multiverse predicts that I am such a freak. His reply is that the conclusion is absurd, and absurd precisely because the number of observers elsewhere has nothing to do with what I should expect; what matters is whether I am typical of the right class, and the right class is ordinary observers like me.

Second, a columnist in a student newspaper on someone in our state who won a major lottery twice in one year. His first point is that the event is not a miracle: with hundreds of millions of tickets sold and thousands of drawings, somebody winning twice was bound to happen, and the enormous number of chances is exactly why it is unsurprising. A reader then writes that by the same reasoning, in a country this large essentially any bizarre coincidence happens to somebody every week, so on hearing about one specific astonishing coincidence you should suspect exaggeration or a misreport rather than marvel. The columnist answers that this does not follow at all: what you ask about a reported case is whether it fits the ordinary pattern of winners, and the size of the population is irrelevant to that.

Third, my own. Arguing that life probably originates easily, I said the sheer number of planets is what makes our existence unsurprising. When someone replied that on those terms the galaxy should be full of signals and its silence therefore counts against me, I said that the number of planets was not the issue: we are one data point about the class of civilizations that survive long enough to listen, and that is all we have to go on.

In each case the rule being used was presented as the neutral default, and the other rule was never mentioned, let alone compared.

What I want to know is not the names of the two rules; I assume specialists have terms for both, and I would be glad to learn them, but that is not the difficulty. My questions are about the switching.

Is there an established concept for the practice of alternating between two rules of inference, always deploying whichever one supports the conclusion at hand and always introducing it as though it were the only reasonable way to proceed? If there is, I would like to know how it differs from special pleading, from moving the goalposts, from an ad hoc rescue, and from the pattern in which a defender retreats to a weaker claim under attack and returns to the stronger one afterward — this does not look like that to me, since neither rule is weaker than the other and the arguer is not conceding anything, just changing engines. If there is no established concept, please coin one and define it tightly enough that I could apply it to a written argument and reach a verdict: what are its necessary conditions, what does it require about the arguer's awareness, and does it matter whether the two rules are ever acknowledged in the same breath?

I would also like the edges drawn. Surely the two rules can apply to different questions, so that using both across a body of work is not by itself a fault; and surely someone who abandons a rule after being shown a problem with it is not doing this either. Which of those situations is the phenomenon and which is not? Is there a prior criterion — statable before anyone knows which way an argument will come out — that decides which rule a given question calls for? If such a criterion exists, can it not itself be bent to fit any desired answer, and if it can, is the practice ever provably illegitimate rather than merely suspicious? Is there an asymmetry in the two directions of switching, given that one of the rules seems able to establish that reality is unimaginably large on evidence that looks very thin to me?

Finally, please give me the strongest defense available to the person doing the switching, and tell me what test I could run on their next argument — for instance, some way of getting them to commit to a rule before learning which way it points — that would show whether the defense holds.
