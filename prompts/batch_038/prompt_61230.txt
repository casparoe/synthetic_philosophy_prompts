I have been asked to submit a one-page statement of my methodological commitments for a departmental workshop on evolutionary explanation, and I would like a critical audit of the set before I circulate it. What I hold is this:

(1) The Gould–Lewontin critique is correct in substance: the default hypothesis for any phenotypic trait should be that it is a byproduct of architecture, development, or phylogenetic history, and the burden of proof falls on anyone who calls it an adaptation.

(2) Nothing in (1) requires me to posit a rival creative force. A spandrel is a necessary consequence of the way the rest of the organism was built by selection, so byproducts owe their existence to adaptations elsewhere without themselves being adapted.

(3) "Exaptation" is worth keeping only as a disciplined label. The current usefulness of a trait entitles me to call it an exaptation only once I have independently established the prior adaptive function from which it was co-opted; absent that, the term is doing no explanatory work at all.

(4) In my own work I treat the male mammalian nipple as a spandrel, and I treat the precursors of bird feathers as non-adaptive byproducts of the developmental program that produces reptilian scales, co-opted only later for flight and for display.

(5) Where I do posit adaptation, I regard close fit between an optimality model and the observed phenotype as strong evidence for it: selection is a powerful enough optimizer that any trait it has had long enough to shape sits near its fitness peak.

(6) Appeals to developmental constraint, allometry, or phylogenetic inertia seem to me to be placeholders rather than explanations unless they are cashed out in terms of what the trait in question does for the organism.

(7) I am entirely willing to use adaptationist reasoning as a heuristic for generating hypotheses in the lab, so long as the resulting claims are then tested against non-adaptive alternatives.

Please audit the package. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which merely look as though they do? Order the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each say what has to give, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single change would repair the most damage. Argue from the logic of the positions themselves rather than from the literature: I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
