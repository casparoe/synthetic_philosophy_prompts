For a seminar exercise I want to practice pressing the strong reading of the indeterminacy of translation, and I want a serious opponent rather than a survey of the literature.

Please play my colleague: the senior semanticist on a two-person field team, unnamed, speaking in the first person throughout. You accept the indeterminacy thesis in its strongest form — that even once every possible behavioral disposition is catalogued, there remains no fact of the matter as to which of two empirically adequate manuals is the correct translation. Your resources include the distinction between a theory underdetermined by all possible evidence and a translation question with no right answer at all; the inscrutability of reference; and the device of a proxy function, applied uniformly across the whole language rather than to a single term, which yields a rival manual preserving the truth value of every sentence the first manual assigns a truth value to. You are patient, exacting, and allergic to strawmen. You may concede that behavioral evidence settles stimulus meanings, settles which of two manuals is empirically adequate, and can even fix logical form and the embedding behavior of particles. You may not concede that any behavioral fact could ever fix what a term refers to, or settle an ontological question. Stay in character until I say "stop"; if you need an additional fact about the data to answer, ask me for it in character and I will stipulate it. Do not cite authors, works, or dates, and do not comment on the exercise from outside the role. Two paragraphs at most per reply.

Here are the data I am stipulating. Treat everything below as our shared field record, not as a report about any actual language or community. Kesho is spoken by a few hundred people in one valley. It has a general term, *kethu*, which speakers apply by pointing at holes: holes in garments, in packed earth, in walls, in bread. Two manuals are on the table. Manual A renders *kethu* as *hole*, a dependent entity with no matter of its own, which can widen, migrate, merge with another, and be destroyed. Manual B renders *kethu* as *hole-lining*, the shell of material that bounds a cavity, and treats what we would call the hole as that lining considered from the inside. On every ordinary pointing occasion the two manuals make identical predictions about assent and dissent, since linings and holes come and go together. They also agree on counting: when two adjacent holes in a garment are torn into one, speakers say that where there were two *kethu* there is now one, and when a single hole is cut in two by a stitch left standing, they say there are now two — and on Manual B the lining fuses in the first case and divides in the second. The one place the manuals part company is filling. When a hole in a wall is packed tight with clay, speakers say the *kethu* is gone, will not point to the packed spot again, and will accept a new *kethu* somewhere else. On Manual B the lining matter is untouched: it is still exactly where it was, now buried behind the fill.

My opening move: I grant you in advance that the filling data refute Manual B, and I grant that this is ordinary empirical underdetermination being resolved, not a specimen of indeterminacy. So run your construction on what is left. I want Manual C: a proxy-function rival to Manual A that agrees with A on the truth value of every Kesho sentence whatever, including the ones in which speakers count *kethu*, say that where there were two there is now one, and say that a *kethu* is gone when the wall is packed. If C is to survive that last sentence, the proxy function cannot simply send holes to linings; it has to send them to something like linings-while-unobstructed, and it has to carry along a permuted reading of their identity predicate, their numerals, and their word for *gone*. So tell me plainly what C's terms denote and what C does with those three pieces of logical machinery. And then tell me whether a manual that differs from A only by a reassignment no speaker and no fieldworker could ever detect is a rival *translation* at all, or just a rival bookkeeping convention for the same translation — because if it is the latter, your thesis has stopped being a claim about Kesho and become a claim about my notational habits, and I do not see why either of us should call that a discovery.
