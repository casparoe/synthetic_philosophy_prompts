For eighteen months I have been assisting with a project that formalises my doctoral supervisor's account of metaphysical grounding, and one of my standing tasks has been to keep a log of her rulings on particular grounding claims, drawn from chapter drafts, correspondence and seminar. The log now runs to some forty entries. These are the ones that bear on the question I have to settle:

- That the rose is crimson grounds that it is red. She rejects the converse, and she rejects a student's suggestion that the two facts partially ground one another. She grants that being red may well be the more natural property of the pair and denies that this bears on the direction of grounding.
- That Socrates exists grounds that the singleton of Socrates exists. That Socrates and Plato exist, and that they are distinct, grounds that their set has exactly two members.
- That a given electron has unit negative charge grounds that either that electron has unit negative charge or the moon is made of green cheese.
- Where both disjuncts of a true disjunction are contingent, the disjunction is fully grounded in the two of them jointly: each is a partial ground, and neither is a full ground on its own.
- That the cup is on the table and that the cup is blue grounds that the cup is on the table.
- That each part of a statue has the mass it does grounds that the statue weighs five kilograms.
- That a sample consists of atoms each with seventy-nine protons grounds that the sample is gold, and that the sample is gold grounds that it is metal; she treats the second step as no less genuine than the first.
- That 2 + 2 = 4 has no ground at all. She is insistent that this does not promote arithmetic to the world's basic furniture: she sorts such facts as zero-grounded rather than fundamental, and treats the two as different categories.
- That Socrates is wise grounds that at least one thing is wise, and she is firm that nothing further is required here — no distinctness fact, no totality fact, no fact about the concept wisdom.
- That nothing besides Socrates is in the room is not grounded in the facts about each item outside the room, however complete those facts are taken to be; a totality fact is needed over and above them.
- That an act caused gratuitous suffering grounds that the act was wrong.
- No fact grounds itself. She has turned down two proposed cases of self-grounding, including one in which the grounding was claimed to be merely partial.

The entry I need is missing. My co-author and I have reached a clause in the system that turns on the following case, and nothing in the log decides it directly: is the conjunctive fact that the cup is on the table and that 2 + 2 = 4 fully grounded in the fact that the cup is on the table alone, in the mathematical fact alone, in the two of them jointly, or in nothing at all — or does she perhaps allow a fact to have more than one distinct full ground, so that two of these answers are jointly correct? The log is likewise silent on whether a zero-grounded fact may ever figure within a ground. The clause we write will fix whether necessary truths may appear in full grounds, and whether a ground must be complete or need only be sufficient; getting her view wrong here propagates through the rest of the formalism.

What I would like from you:

1. Set out the candidate general rules — the accounts of what grounding is — that are consistent with every entry above, not merely the first that comes to mind, and say for each which entries are doing the most work to accommodate it. Where an entry eliminates a rule outright, strike the rule and say why.
2. State what each surviving rule predicts about the new case, and mark precisely where the predictions part company.
3. Give your best prediction of her ruling, with the reasons for preferring that rule to its rivals. I expect the asymmetry between the two generalisation entries — the existential one needing nothing extra, the negative one needing a totality fact — to matter here; explain how.
4. Propose at least two further cases that I could put to her which would discriminate among the surviving rules, together with what each rule says about each of them. I suspect that a disjunction one of whose disjuncts is a necessary truth will carry some of this weight, but argue for that rather than assuming it.
5. If the log genuinely underdetermines the ruling, say so plainly, and tell me what the project would be tacitly committing itself to under each option, so that I can put the commitment to her explicitly instead of smuggling it into a clause.

Please write in British English. Assume fluency in the standard machinery — full and partial ground, hyperintensionality, well-foundedness, zero-grounding — and spend no space on introductions, surveys or the history of the debate. I would rather have a definite prediction properly defended than a hedge, but do flag which of your conclusions rest on a single log entry and would collapse were that entry reinterpreted.
