hey, so I'm a second year taking a social epistemology seminar, and for one session the TA is letting two of us run a 75 min activity for the whole group (24 people) before the discussion starts. Our topic is the Condorcet jury theorem, and honestly the obvious demo — urn, everyone pulls a private card, majority vote tracks the truth — everyone has seen that one and its kind of a snooze. I want to build the whole thing around the condition that breaks instead: independence.

The specific idea I want to inject is that what the theorem really needs is for the hidden state to be the most recent common cause of everybody's vote. If instead the most recent common cause is some shared body of evidence that everyone reads off of, then a unanimous group is worth exactly as much as one person's read on that evidence, no matter how many people are in the room. (I'm pretty sure there's a Dietrich and List paper about juries where all the jurors have the same evidence that makes this point with bayesian networks, but don't worry about reconstructing it, I just need the mechanism.) And the part I want them to feel in their bones is that you cannot tell the difference from the inside — same unanimity, same confidence, same we-all-checked-for-ourselves story, except one of the two setups converges to certainty and the other doesn't budge.

So: design the game. Roughly three rounds, with what each round actually was held back till the end. I need the physical setup, materials that hide the correlation rather than accidentally advertising it, a points scheme that costs me nothing (no real money), and a timeline in minutes. Include a confidence wager after the group verdict in each round — set the odds so that betting on the group is clearly right in the independent round and quietly ruinous in the shared-evidence round, without them being able to work that out beforehand.

Also please deal with these practical problems, because they're the ones that will sink it: with only 24 people and one vote per round, the accuracy difference won't be visible as noise vs signal, so how do I get enough trials; what stops someone whose private signal is worse than a coin flip from wrecking round 1; what stops them reverse-engineering round 2 and deliberately voting the other way once they smell a pattern; and what do I say to the kid who's definately going to object that real juries deliberate so I've just proven deliberation is bad.

Then the debrief: map specific moments from what they just did onto each condition the theorem needs, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it's something about real shared evidence being partial and overlapping rather than one card everyone reads, and about us handing back the truth every round when real life never does. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to say in that moment.
