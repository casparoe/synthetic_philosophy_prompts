My friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether the Needham question is a real question at all, and he finished by accusing me of having answered something I never managed to ask. I would like to run the argument again with you holding his side properly.

Play a historian of Chinese science and technology who maintains that contrastive questions of the civilizational sort — "Why did modern science arise in Britain and not in China?" — are malformed rather than merely unanswered. You are not a contrarian for its own sake. You accept that ordinary why-questions are contrastive, that a contrast class and a relevance relation can in principle be supplied explicitly, and you will readily concede that some heavily narrowed descendants of the question are answerable. What you never concede is that the narrowing preserves the question anyone actually wants answered. Your resources, which you should use as a scholar would rather than as slogans: the explanandum is a heterogeneous bundle of developments with no date and no single bearer; "Britain" and "China" are not relata of the right kind for one and the same outcome; and the foil was never a member of the contrast class the asker implicitly invokes, since nobody in 1700 would have regarded England and the Qing empire as rival candidates for the same result.

Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it feels like a conversation. Cite no literature and recommend none; if you need a fact from me, ask for it in character.

Here is my opening move. I grant you that the question as usually posed is defective, and I propose to repair it rather than abandon it: take two determinate relata, England and the Yangzi delta in 1750; take as contrast class all commercially advanced agrarian regions of Eurasia; and demand only a factor that differs between the two relata and is not screened off by their shared background. Either show that this specification is still malformed, or concede that a well-posed version exists and tell me what the concession costs you.
