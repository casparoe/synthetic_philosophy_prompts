Six weeks ago I transferred from hydraulic modelling into the risk-assessment unit of our national flood authority, and I am now the person whose number determines the crest height of a coastal defence. The difficulty is that for most sites my number is not a number. We have a short tide-gauge record, two overtopping models that disagree structurally rather than merely numerically, and three elicited experts whose ranges barely intersect. Current guidance resolves this by having me report the mean of an uncertainty distribution, which averages away precisely the disagreement that should be driving the decision, and then a bright-line code provision compares that mean to a threshold.

I have been reading on imprecise probability, and I want to work in that idiom: belief represented as a set of distributions rather than one; the puzzle in which conditioning on new evidence can widen an interval rather than sharpen it; the related failure in which an unconditional interval does not lie inside the hull of the conditional intervals across the cases that partition it. What I need is a procedure, not an exposition.

Please write it as ordered steps my unit can run, covering: how to construct the set from thin data, incompatible models, and conflicting elicitations — and whether combining opinions means taking an intersection, a convex hull, or a weighted mixture, with the cost of each made explicit; how to update it, including how to distinguish a genuine widening from the symptom of a set that was over-large at the outset; what to publish when a downstream rule accepts only a single figure; and how to select among the decision criteria a set licenses, since worst-case, admissibility, and maximality order the same options differently. Mark which steps are technical and which are normative hand-offs to the committee that owns the consequences.

Constraints: one analyst-day per site, one page of output, read by a chartered engineer with no measure theory and by a residents' association that hears any range as a confession of ignorance. The committee sits quarterly and resents escalation.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a wall upgrade where the interval straddles the code threshold so that the site is neither compliant nor non-compliant; a reanalysis in which a decade of additional gauge data widens our published interval, which the committee will read as a failure; and a minor culvert where I suspect the whole apparatus buys nothing. Give the answer in each, including the one I will not want to deliver, and close with what this procedure systematically cannot settle and where I should stop pretending it does.
