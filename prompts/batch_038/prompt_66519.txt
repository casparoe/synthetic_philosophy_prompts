I have to give a speech next Thursday at the leaving do for Pat, who's been manager of our care home near Wrexham for nineteen years — the do is in the function room above the pub and about eighty people are coming: most of the residents who can be transported, a lot of their adult children, the current care staff (many of whom Pat hired), our two kitchen porters, the council commissioner who sets our rates, and one member of the board who used to teach applied ethics and will notice everything. I'm the deputy. I've been reading around the ethics of care for the last few months, partly because Pat is the sort of person who'd want that and partly because I kept finding that the obvious things to say about her felt false as soon as I said them out loud, and I want this speech to be built out of that material rather than just decorated with it.

Here's the specific problem I need help with. My draft starts "thank you is the wrong word, and I'm going to say it anyway", and I think there's something real behind that instinct: gratitude is the response to a gift or to someone going beyond what they owed, so a speech made entirely of thanks quietly concedes that nineteen years of getting people up, washed, fed, medicated and listened to was supererogatory kindness rather than work that was owed and that someone is entitled to be properly paid and properly supported for. That commissioner is sitting three tables away. Same issue with the wall card we've had going round, which is full of "she was like family" — I cant read that aloud in good conscience, because the familialism is doing precisely the hiding that care ethics complains about, converting paid labour by non-relatives into natural affection that supposedly doesn't need resourcing. And I don't want the word "selfless" in there once, or "angel", or "she gave everything", because Kittay's point about the dependency worker is that the person doing the caring has needs and a life of her own, and a description that renders her transparent is a description that gets used to underpay her later. I also don't want the residents in the room cast as the passive objects of Pat's benevolence; if anything I'd rather put the weight on the fourth of Tronto and Fisher's phases, care-receiving, since a lot of what made Pat good was that she let herself be changed and bossed about by the people she was looking after, and she dropped to three days a week for two years to care for her own mother, which nobody ever mentions.

So: acting as a professional philosopher who specialises in the ethics of care and has also written real speeches for real rooms, think carefully before drafting. Spend a paragraph reasoning out loud about what these constraints genuinely rule out, where they pull against each other, and where they collide with the fact that eighty tired people in a pub want to be moved and slightly amused rather than lectured at — I want to see that thinking, and then the speech. Around 750–900 words, six minutes spoken, warm, funny in two or three places, no jargon at all in the body (no "relational autonomy" or "dependency work" said out loud), at most one philosopher's name and only if it can go in so lightly that just the board member registers it, and please no reading list or suggested further material afterwards. It has to work for the residents and the families without patronising them, land the funding point without turning into a grievance speech, and end on something Pat herself would recognise as true rather than as flattering. Write the whole thing in British English. Then, in a short note kept separate from the speech, tell me where you've taken liberties with care ethics, which contested reading of it you've leaned on (I'm aware the anti-gratitude line I handed you may be my own invention rather than anybody's actual argument), how confident you are in the claims you've made about what the theory commits a speaker to, and which single point in the speech you're least sure about.
