I teach political philosophy, but my first training was in law and I spent a decade teaching property, so I still think in the vocabulary of estates, easements and covenants running with the land, the numerus clausus principle, restraints on alienation, private necessity, and the choice between injunctive and damages remedies. Please restate the case for a state's right to exclude would-be immigrants — the freedom-of-association line, the collective self-determination line, and the relational-equality or institutional-integrity line — in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, treating the polity as though it were an owner and its admission rules as though they were servitudes or restrictive covenants. Do not smuggle property law's own normative commitments in and then report them as translations of the philosophers' claims.

Then give me the residue report. Which of these arguments become, in property terms, a claim about the right to determine use rather than a bare right to exclude? Which survive only if I distort the doctrine — by treating necessity as irrelevant, say, or by permitting a restraint on alienation that a court would void as unreasonable? And which cannot be formulated at all, because property doctrine has no way of asking the question the philosophers are asking? Where translation fails, tell me whether you take that to be a poverty in the property framework or evidence that the philosophical claim was less determinate than its partisans suppose. Finally: does the doctrinal machinery of conditional and compensated exclusion mark out a position the immigration literature treats as unavailable, and if so, is that because the analogy is strained or because the debate is framed too coarsely?
