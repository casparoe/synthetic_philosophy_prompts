I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I look after the governance side of a mid-sized open-source project — about 500 contributors, plus a few companies that pay people to work on it. We have a small steering council that decides things like licence changes, whether to split a package out, and who gets to approve contentious RFCs. Last month a seat came up, two people wanted it, and it turned into the ugliest thread we've ever had; someone half-jokingly suggested we just pick the council by lottery from the contributor list. I laughed, then got annoyed at myself for laughing, then spent three weekends down a rabbit hole on sortition and lottocracy, and now I honestly can't tell which position I hold. I'd like to work that out, and hand the same thing to the other maintainers, because right now we're arguing past each other using words like "legitimacy" and "competence" as though each of them meant only one thing.

So: build me a sorting instrument. A quiz of nine or ten items, each one a concrete situation rather than a slogan, two or three options per item, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions. Please build it out of the logical space first and only then tell me who occupies which region of it. The distinctions I care about, roughly in order: why you'd want a lottery at all — is it because it's the only impartial way of sharing power among people who are equals, even if the decisions get worse; because it kills campaigning, money and favour-trading; because it gets you a body that's a miniature of the community; because sampled people given long single terms and proper expert staff would actually decide better than elected ones; because it breaks a clique that's captured the institution? Then the scope questions: does the sampled body replace elections entirely, sit as a second chamber checking an elected one, or advise an elected one that's free to ignore it; is it bound by what it deliberated to; who's eligible, how long is a term, and what happens when a sampled body does something most contributors hate? Every item has to be answerable by someone who's never heard of any of this — nothing like "do you think selection by lot is democratic", and nothing where the wording of the options gives the game away. I'd like these to turn up as leaves, whatever their proper names are: the fair-share view that makes no claim about better outcomes; the epistemic view where long terms and expert staff are doing the real work; the mini-public view where the sampled body informs an elected one and never binds it; the two-chamber hybrid where lot and election check each other; the anti-capture view where the whole point is to stop people fighting over the seat; and at least two combinations the space permits but nobody seems to defend, with a line each on whether they're empty because they're incoherent, because they're unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered. Where a pair of leaves can't actually be separated by any question I could ask, flag that instead of papering over it.

Two more things. I've got it into my head that the Athenians chose basically everything by lot, including their generals, which I now strongly suspect is wrong — if any of my framing above rests on a mistake like that, say so rather than quietly working around it. And please seperate out what's genuinely settled among people who work on this from what's a live argument: I keep hitting confident claims about what sortition does to legitimacy, or to the quality of decisions, and I can't tell whether anyone has ever tested them. Don't give me a contested view dressed up as the consensus, and don't give me a reading list — I won't read it, and I've no access to a library. It may matter that when the seat comes up and its contested, "randomly sampling from the contributor list" for us really means sampling from about 60 people who've merged something in the past year, which is probably not what most of the theorising assumes; tell me where that changes the answer.
