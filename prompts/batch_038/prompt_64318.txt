I teach the weekly discussion section for a third-year course cross-listed between philosophy and computer science, and next Thursday I have fifty minutes on sphexishness: mechanical rigidity, flexibility, and what either of them has to do with free will. Fourteen students, nearly all of whom have written reinforcement-learning code, two of whom have built tool-using agents on top of a large language model, and none of whom have taken a philosophy course before this one; the course proper reaches the frame problem in two weeks and free will in five, so my section is supposed to leave hooks rather than close the questions. I do not want an explanation of the concept, a lecture script, or a reading list. I want a plan for how I should explain it that I could deliver from a single page of notes.

First, the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what. My guess is that before the wasp story can mean anything to them at all, they need some notion of a routine whose individual steps are triggered by local cues rather than by any representation of the situation as a whole, and that the distinction between failing to reach a goal and failing to notice that the goal-situation has changed has to be in place before the punchline lands — but tell me whether that is right, and tell me what I lose if I invert any part of it. Second, which single case should carry the concept. My default is the familiar digger-wasp routine: paralyzed prey dragged to the burrow, dropped at the entrance while the wasp goes inside to inspect, an experimenter shifting the prey a few inches, the wasp quickly finding it and dragging it back to the entrance, inspecting again, and so on round after round. The alternatives I have been turning over are the older observation that the wasp hauls its prey by the antennae and, if the antennae are removed, does not think to take hold of a leg instead; and something drawn from the students' own work, such as an agent that retries an identical failing tool call after reading each error message. Say which of these should bear the weight, what the carrying example must exhibit and must not exhibit, and what a second example later in the hour would have to contribute that the first cannot. My worry is that a machine-learning example will make the concept look like a claim about training data rather than about the structure of the control, while the antennae case seems to collapse rigidity into plain incompetence.

Then the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than argued with afterwards. I expect at least these: that sphexishness is a polite name for stupidity, so that more compute or more data dissolves it; that it is all-or-nothing rather than a matter of degree — and, if a matter of degree, no idea what the degrees are degrees of; that whatever is innate is rigid and whatever is learned is not; that putting stochasticity into a policy makes an agent non-sphexish; and, underneath all of these, that the concept is a claim about causal determination, as though the wasp lacked free will because her behaviour was fixed in advance, when the concept never mentions causal history at all. There is also a live empirical problem I want handled explicitly rather than dodged: one of the students has sent me a paper arguing that the wasp anecdote is an old and questionable report, that repetition typically breaks off after a limited number of iterations, and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may itself be an adaptive thing to do. Tell me where in the hour I should raise that objection myself, what I ought to concede to it, and what survives the concession — because if the answer is that nothing survives, I need to know that before Thursday and not in front of the class. Finally, give me one check to put to them in the last five minutes that would show whether they have the concept rather than merely the anecdote, ideally something that requires them to locate where a given routine can be interrupted and what it is structurally unable to notice; and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level, with the sentence I should use to say that I am bracketing those things deliberately.
