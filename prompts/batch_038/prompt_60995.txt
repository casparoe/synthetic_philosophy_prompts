Every second week our department co-hosts a lunch series with the knowledge-representation group down the hall, and next Thursday it is my turn. They have asked me, half in jest, to explain what philosophers mean by the unity of the proposition and why anyone has thought there is a regress threatening it. I have forty minutes, one whiteboard, no slides, and no way of getting them to read anything in advance. Their background is formidable on the formal side and empty on the philosophical side: they write first-order logic and type theory for a living, they maintain triple stores in which every claim is a (subject, predicate, object) record, and not one of them has ever had to consider what a proposition is supposed to be apart from the strings or data structures that encode it. I expect several of them to hold, without embarrassment, that a proposition simply is a well-formed record under a schema. I intend to spend at most two sentences on history — that the worry originated as a general point about relations and that Russell is the one who turned it on propositions — and I would like you to tell me whether even that much is a distraction at this level.

What I need is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. Give me the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what: in particular, what has to be in place before the question is a question for them at all, since the whole difficulty evaporates if a proposition is nothing but an entry in a database whose structure the schema guarantees. Then tell me the single case that should carry the session and what that case must have and must lack. I have been assuming something of the form a-loves-b, with a non-symmetric relation and two relata of the same kind, so that reversing them gives a different claim out of all the same constituents; but I suspect that case invites them to reply that one simply goes and checks who loves whom, and I would rather you told me whether to repair it, bracket the reply, or replace the case outright, and why. Then the misconception map. I predict they will say the regress is harmless because it is only recursion and their systems recurse all day; that they will reach for ordered pairs or n-tuples as the glue; that they will treat function application as though it discharged the problem rather than restated it; that one of them has picked up somewhere the line that objects in a fact need no link because they hang together like links of a chain; and that at least one will insist the whole business is a pseudo-problem manufactured by reifying sentences. For each, say where in the sequence it should be headed off, which one I should let them walk into and fail at productively before I intervene, and what the intervention actually consists in.

Finally, give me a check I can run in the last five minutes that would show the problem has landed rather than been politely dismissed — something they can be asked to say or predict about a case of their own choosing, not something they can nod along to — and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover, together with the sentence I should use when refusing, since I want the Fregean concept/object trouble, the contemporary literature on structured propositions, and the parallel question about the unity of facts fenced off rather than half-treated. One complication I should flag: my own view is that the regress is not vicious and that much of this is a notational fuss rather than a metaphysical crisis, and I do not want to teach my view. I want them to leave with the sense that there is a real, unsettled question about what makes a structured whole into a single claim, and that their own triple stores are instances of it — not the sense that they watched a philosopher score a point on engineers. Tell me where in the plan that neutrality is hardest to maintain, what a partisan slip would sound like at each of those points, and what I should say instead.
