Three years ago I started running a collaborative project that keeps failing in the same way, and I've finally decided the failure is my fault at the planning stage rather than everyone's fault at the writing stage. Here's the pattern. I take one big question, break it into pieces, hand pieces to grad students and postdocs, and stitch the returns back together into a chapter. About half the time the stitched thing is worse than if I'd just thought about it myself for a month, and I can never tell in advance which half. So I want a procedure I can run on a proposed decomposition *before* I commit a year of someone's life to it.

The idea I keep reaching for comes from the scalable-oversight corner of AI alignment, which I've read a bit of and want you to use but not worship. There's HCH: a human who has a question and can consult copies of themselves, each of whom can consult further copies, with answers passing back up the tree — deployed as an idealization of what someone would conclude if they could think about a question indefinitely with unlimited help from people just like them. And there's factored cognition: the claim that a hard reasoning task can be broken into many small, mostly independent subtasks whose answers recombine into an answer to the big one. What interests me is the thing the idealization quietly assumes away — what has to be true of a decomposition for the top of the tree to get back something it can actually trust, given that the top of the tree is just a tired philosophy professor.

Write me the procedure. The constraints: my postdoc and I run it together in under an hour, we're the only people who know anything about the alignment literature and she knows less than I do, and the output is one page that goes in the student's file and that I'd have to defend to a co-PI. I get maybe a dozen proposals a term, so it has to be fast, not thorough. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a pass at each, where the procedure terminates in "don't decompose this, go think about it yourself," and where it stops being a procedure and hands the call to me because the question is about values or about how much I trust a particular student. Be blunt about which of your criteria are genuine tests and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.

A few criteria I suspect matter; keep them if they survive scrutiny, kill them if they don't, and tell me which you killed. (1) Verification asymmetry: I should be able to check a returned answer more cheaply than producing it. (2) The subquestion should be answerable by someone who never sees the parent question — if you have to know where it fits to know what would count as answering it, it isn't a subquestion. (3) The recombination rule has to be writable before the answers come back; if I can only figure out how to merge them after reading them, I didn't decompose anything. (4) The load has to actually move, not get renamed — no residual subquestion that is the original question with different words on it. (5) My own job at the top, writing the subquestions and judging the returns, has to be smaller than the original job.

Also tell me what the procedure inherits from HCH's assumptions, and what happens when they break: the copies are supposed to be faithful to the consulter, the tree is supposed to terminate, and the consulter is supposed to be able to recognize a good answer. In ethics I often can't do that last part without doing the subwork myself, and I want to know whether that's a special problem for normative questions or just me being slow.

Then run it on three of my actual proposals. (a) Does the principle of alternate purposes — sorry, alternate possibilities — survive determinism? Split into: what does the principle commit you to, what does determinism entail about the past and the laws, and does the second rule out the first? (b) An inventory: what are the live objections to a view I'm defending, who pressed each one, what response was given, and did the objector accept it? Split by objection, one student each. (c) My pet scheme, which I want a straight verdict on even if it's not the one I want: is legal punishment justified, split three ways into retributivism, consequentialism, and expressivism, three students, twenty pages each, and me writing the synthesis chapter.

Finally, the honest part. Which cases will this procedure systematically misjudge in each direction? I already know about two: the decomposition that passes every test and shouldn't be run, because the point was for the student to come to understand the question and outsourcing it prevents that; and the decomposition that fails every test and should be run anyway, because the subwork is the only way anybody gets into a hard problem at all. Say what the procedure cannot do, and keep the two seperate in your writeup — the part that decides and the part that only flags.
