Consider the following case. It is constructed for this question, but every element of it is the sort of thing that arises in practice.

A city's transport model, which I will call Model A, is parameter-free. It assumes that each of the roughly 200,000 daily drivers has complete knowledge of travel times across the whole network and selects the unique fastest route from origin to destination, adjusting immediately to congestion. A household travel survey of 400 respondents establishes that this assumption is false of the people it studies: drivers keep at most three memorised routes per trip purpose, rank them lexicographically (fewest left turns, then shortest distance, then least congestion), and do not abandon a memorised route even after a new bypass measurably shortens their commute. A colleague builds Model B, which encodes the survey's route-choice sets and the lexicographic rule. B has fourteen fitted parameters, calibrated on the same survey and on six months of loop-detector counts.

Held out on the following twelve months of counts, Model A predicts link flows with 4% root-mean-square error and Model B with 9%. A's advantage is stable across months and across parts of the network, including the corridor where the bypass opened, where A had no opportunity to be tuned to the new geometry.

My colleague reads this as vindicating the instrumentalist methodological thesis associated with Milton Friedman, sometimes called the F-twist: the realism of a model's assumptions is beside the point provided the model predicts well, and here the supposedly false assumption is doing real work rather than being harmlessly false. I read it as showing only that B was badly estimated from a small and unrepresentative survey, and that A is a convenient fiction whose individual-level errors happen to cancel at the aggregate level.

Give me your credence in this proposition:

P: Model A's superior out-of-sample accuracy is good evidence that A's assumption is approximately true of individual drivers in this network, and not merely evidence that A is a useful falsehood whose errors cancel in aggregation.

I want the decomposition, not a bare number. Break P into the sub-claims you would actually need in order to assign it. You may use or discard my list: the probability that A's aggregate accuracy is produced by a mechanism that could obtain only if individual drivers were near-optimisers; the probability that B's inferiority reflects the falsity of A's assumption rather than estimation noise, survey bias, or overfitting on fourteen parameters; the probability that "approximately true" has determinate content when applied to a dispositional assumption of this kind, as opposed to a measurable one; the probability that some third factor, such as error cancellation across heterogeneous drivers or the redundancy built into the network itself, explains A's accuracy without any approximation to the truth of the assumption. Assign each term a number and one line of justification, state explicitly how you combine them, and give the resulting credence in P.

Then answer three further questions. Which term are you least confident about? What specific further result, whether a new measurement, a new comparison, or a stipulated change to the case, would move that term the most, and in which direction? And my own credence in P is about 0.15 while my colleague's is about 0.8: identify the single sub-question on which the two of us most plausibly disagree, such that settling it would collapse most of the gap.
