In my decision-theory seminar two of us have spent the term arguing about the St. Petersburg paradox, and we would like to replace the argument with a joint statement of the kind a real adversarial collaboration would produce. Position one: the paradox is dissolved by getting the utility function right — bounded utility together with a countable probability distribution guarantees absolute convergence, so no idealized coin-toss game ever forces a revision of expected-utility maximization. Position two: the paradox family shows the criterion is incomplete — gambles can be described for which expected utility fails to exist at all, and a theory that falls silent about them has not finished its job.

Draft the document the two of us should be able to sign together: first a numbered list of agreed claims, covering what the mathematics actually settles, what Bernoulli's diminishing-utility diagnosis does and does not establish, and what we each take a decision theory to owe an agent; then a table of residual disagreements. Each row should state the dispute twice, once in wording its own side would accept, label it as substantive or as merely a quarrel over what it means for a gamble to have an expected utility, and identify the crux whose settlement would move at least one of us. Use this gamble as the shared test case: a fair coin is tossed until the first head, and a head on toss n yields utility (-1)^(n+1) 2^n/(2n−1), so that the series of expected-utility contributions sums to π/4 but only conditionally. Record in advance what each side predicts the theory ought to say about it, and what would count as that prediction having failed.
