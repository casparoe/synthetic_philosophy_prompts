I am assembling the closing item for an advanced undergraduate philosophy-of-mind seminar on mental content, and I would like you to write a dialogue for it. The session's question is whether aboutness comes in two fundamentally different kinds — the underived content of a belief and the borrowed content of a physical symbol — or only in degrees of interpretive entanglement. I want that question argued out over one concrete object rather than over the familiar thought experiments, so the students have to track the object and the argument at the same time.

The scene. A university plant-physiology laboratory is being decommissioned, and two people are clearing a bench. Dr. Amara Osei is a philosopher of mind who holds that some intentionality is intrinsic: what a belief is about is not fixed by anyone's interpretive practice, and the content of a machine's states is always parasitic on minds like ours. Dr. Tomas Lindqvist holds the opposing view: all intentionality, including the human kind, consists in the reliability with which a state can be read as being about something, given what causes it and what it is used to do; there is no further, non-interpretive core, and the appearance of one comes from the fact that in us the reader and the thing read are the same system running many overlapping passes. Between them sits a data logger built in the 1980s. One of its status flags, a single bit, was originally set by a soil-moisture probe; a graduate student later re-purposed it in firmware as an indicator that the greenhouse door is open; the probe is still physically wired to the input but no longer read, and the documentation was never updated. The logger is going to the university archive, whose accession record will say only: flag bit, function unknown.

This is the sort of case that was pressed against Searle with the two-bitser: one physical state that is simultaneously a sign of the water in a tank and a sign of its own condition inside a machine, depending on which interpretive arrangement you count. The dialogue should turn on three questions, arising from the object and the paperwork rather than being announced as cases:

1. The bit as it stands now — probe still connected, firmware reading it as a door sensor. What, if anything, is it about at this moment, and who or what settles that?
2. A re-wiring carried out during the conversation, after which the flag's downstream use changes with no alteration to the bit's physical type. Does its content change instantly and completely? What would the human counterpart of that switch have to look like?
3. The abandonment case. Suppose the archive accession is completed, everyone who worked with the logger dies, and all documentation is lost. Is there still a fact about what the bit meant? Osei should say that the borrowed aboutness goes when we do; Lindqvist must then say positively what, if anything, remains — and Osei must face the parallel question about a solitary believer rather than dodge it.

Requirements:

- Both characters are expert and arguing in good faith, and neither may be reduced to a slogan. Osei may not rest her case on the claim that intentionality is a biological feature of brains without answering Lindqvist's rejoinder that this locates intrinsic aboutness instead of explaining it. Lindqvist may not dismiss her target as a useful fiction, and must give a real answer about content when no interpreter is left.
- Include Osei's diagnostic proposal, which she calls the switch test: if what a state is about can be changed purely by changing how it is read downstream, with no change to its causes or its effects, then its aboutness is not intrinsic. Let Lindqvist accept the test and try to show that human belief fails it too, through the subject's own re-reading of her states — confabulation, the way the content of a remembered event shifts when it is re-contextualized. This should be the sharpest exchange, and it should not resolve cleanly. Osei should land a real hit: in the human case the causal role of the state changes along with the reading, so the test is not met; Lindqvist must answer that specifically rather than repeat himself. He should land one too: Osei cannot say which of a belief's many candidate contents is the intrinsic one without appealing to something like predictive reliability, which is exactly the resource he is using.
- At least one explicit concession from each side.
- No survey-article exposition. Neither character may deliver a paragraph that reads like a textbook entry, and neither may name-drop literature, cite works, or gesture at authorities. Do not use the Chinese Room, do not use zombies, and do not treat the word homunculus as a refutation.

Form. Between 1,200 and 1,500 words of dialogue in plain prose with speaker labels, beginning in the middle of the clearing-out rather than with a scene-setting paragraph. End with each of them naming the single consideration that would change their mind, neither having converted. Then add a referee's note of no more than 200 words, in your own voice, stating who had the better of the exchange on each of the three questions and what the seminar should make of the fact that the disagreement survives the object sitting in front of both of them.
