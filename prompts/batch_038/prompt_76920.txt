I am in the fourth year of a cognitive science PhD, and the chapter of my dissertation that was supposed to be the easy one — a diagnostic history of the frame problem — has become the one I cannot write. My committee reproduces the dispute internally, which is why I want an outside diagnosis rather than more exposition.

My chair is a knowledge-representation researcher who came up in the logic-based tradition. Her position is that the narrow problem, as McCarthy and Hayes posed it in 1969, is a mathematical problem about formalizing the common sense law of inertia, and that it was substantially solved to a degree the wider debate has never acknowledged: circumscription, Sandewall's occlusion-based treatments, successor-state axioms, and the event calculus as Kowalski and Sergot formulated it and Shanahan and Miller extended it, all with known results and known residual limits (concurrency, continuous change, non-deterministic effects, and the Yale shooting problem standing as the permanent cautionary case). On her view, the philosophers simply stopped reading the technical literature sometime in the mid-1980s and have been arguing with a straw formalism ever since.

My second reader builds behavior-based robots. His position is that the whole difficulty was an artifact of the sense-model-plan-act architecture that generated it: if a controller is a set of coupled layers in continuous interaction with an environment, there is no central store of world-descriptions to keep in register with the world, and therefore no frame about which to write axioms. For him the technical solutions are not solutions but elaborations of the original mistake, and the fact that the field declared the problem closed by the end of the 1980s while its systems still could not cope with a mug nudged into the workspace is, on its own, proof.

My third reader is a philosopher of mind in the Fodor and Pylyshyn lineage. She says both of the others have answered a question that was never hard. Given representations whose content is already fixed, of course you can axiomatize persistence, and of course you can wire up a reactive controller; the difficulty is that relevance is a global, holistic property of what a representation means, and it is not the sort of property that can be inherited piecemeal by the parts of a system. On her account the frame problem is a problem about content wearing an engineering costume, and no benchmark will ever decide it.

Here is the datum that has convinced me something is wrong with the way all three of them talk. For my own experiments I run a modest tabletop-manipulation suite in a physics simulator and compare a planner that reasons over explicit effect axioms against a learned reactive policy on identical tasks. The result is not clean. The reactive policy is markedly more robust when I inject unmodelled distractors — an object moved into the workspace, a change in lighting — and markedly worse on tasks where the correct move is to withhold action until a condition two or three steps downstream obtains. All three committee members read me the same table as a confirmation of their own camp. The first says it shows the formal account works wherever the architecture is appropriate and that distractor robustness is a perception problem, not a reasoning problem. The second says it shows that representation is the bottleneck. The third says it shows nothing at all, because both systems are being described in our own intentional idiom and the prior question of what their internal states mean has not even been raised.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that this is not one disagreement but three, sharing a name and a citation trail, and that the stall is produced by cross-level scorekeeping: each camp's success criterion is internal to its own level (proof-theoretic adequacy, behavioral competence in an arena, a naturalistic account of content), and no camp accepts another's criterion as a criterion, so refutations never land anywhere. But I have a rival worry. Maybe there is a genuinely substantive disagreement buried underneath, namely whether relevance is a feature of the causal structure of the world that a sufficiently rich formalism can capture, or a feature that exists only relative to an agent's goals, embodiment, and history. If that is the real question, it is not merely verbal, and part of it may be empirical.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Please diagnose it by engaging what each side actually says, rather than surveying literature I have already read. I want to know whether my level-crossing account is right, right in part, or a way of dissolving a real dispute that I should not be permitted to get away with; whether the third camp's objection to the first two is substantive or a category error; whether the second camp's dissolution claim rests on an unstated and possibly false premise about what its own controllers are doing; and whether anything at all — a distinction, a formal result, a new experimental paradigm, a change of question — could count as progress here, and what it would have to look like in order to be accepted by all three of my readers at once. If your conclusion is that no such thing is possible, I would rather have the argument for that than a hopeful list.

Please write to me in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. And please write in American English.
