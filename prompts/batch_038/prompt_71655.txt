I'm a third-year physics PhD student and our department's cosmology reading group has been quietly at war for two months. Last week someone gave a talk arguing that a multiverse proposal can earn real confirmation without ever being tested directly — roughly, on three grounds: there's no serious rival that handles the same phenomena, the framework keeps producing unexpected explanatory hooks into problems it wasn't built for, and the neighbouring research programme has a track record of paying off where you could check it. (He called these the three non-empirical arguments; I think there's a philosopher behind them but I dont remember the name and it isn't important here.) Our resident senior guy, a lattice person with zero patience for any of it, cut him off with "that's not even wrong," the room laughed, and we moved on.

I'm convinced that phrase did a lot of illegitimate work, but when I tried to say why afterwards I could only manage "well, it's a slogan, you haven't said anything yet." So help me actually say it. I want the distinct precise claims someone could be making when they say a theory isn't even wrong, from the boring truism all the way to the radical thesis, laid out as a numbered menu. For each one: what exactly a theory would have to be like to satisfy or violate it, whether it's true, and whether it's the sort of thing you could in principle settle by looking carefully at a single theory rather than at the sociology around it. Then the part that matters most — which of those readings, if any, actually licenses what he did, i.e. shutting down the specific claim that no-alternatives plus explanatory interconnection plus track record can raise your confidence in a theory that we can't test?

My guess, which you should push on rather than just confirm: at least one reading is true of some multiverse proposals but does nothing to touch the confirmation argument, and at least one other reading would condemn most of theoretical physics if it were applied consistently, including his own field. Tell me which is which, and tell me if my guess is wrong.

One complication I want you to engage with rather than dodge. His real objection, as far as I could reconstruct it, is about typicality: a theory that says our universe is typical among the ones it predicts looks like it's making a prediction, but if you're free to swap the measure over universes every time the observations come out badly, there's nothing left that could be got wrong. Is that a version of the slogan, a completely different objection, or both at once? And does it survive being stated that precisely?
