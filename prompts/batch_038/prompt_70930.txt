Heard a philosophy podcast do an episode on whether things in games are real, and the host summed up the Chalmers/Matrix line like this:

"Chalmers' argument is that the Matrix isn't a mass illusion. The apple Neo eats is a real apple — it's just made of bits instead of atoms, the same way a table is made of atoms instead of quarks. Virtual water is genuine water; its molecules are computational rather than chemical, that's all. Once you see that, skepticism about the external world loses its bite. And it has consequences beyond epistemology: if virtual objects are real, then nothing that happens in a game is fake, which means scamming or griefing someone in an MMO is exactly as bad as doing it to them on the street. The only difference is what the objects are made of, and what something is made of is never morally relevant."

A caller pushed back near the end and the host offered a rescue: we should separate "real objects" from "real stakes," and virtual harms have real stakes because there are actual people on both ends of them.

Can you audit this claim by claim? I want to know what a defender of the view would sign off on, what they'd call a sloppy-but-harmless simplification, and what they'd say flatly misstates the position. That "computational molecules" line felt wrong to me but I couldnt say why, and I also don't know whether the table/quark analogy is doing legitimate work or smuggling something in. Then: does the moral conclusion survive once the metaphysics is cleaned up, or was it only ever held up by the bad part? And is the caller's real-objects/real-stakes split a genuine rescue, or just the original problem restated with nicer words?
