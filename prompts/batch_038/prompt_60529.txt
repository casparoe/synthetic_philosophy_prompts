I am drafting a section of a graduate handout on decision theory under extreme uncertainty, and I need a rigorous, self-contained argument for a claim that is usually asserted in passing rather than actually established. Please write that argument.

**The claim I want argued.** Anti-fanatics about expected value commonly reach for a probability floor: below some fixed threshold, probabilities are to be treated as zero for the purposes of choice. I want the case that this repair is worse than the fanaticism it was introduced to escape. Specifically: an agent who adopts a probability floor acquires a family of defects that are more systematic and less tolerable than the Pascalian verdicts she was trying to avoid, and an anti-fanatic who genuinely wants to limit the damage should constrain her utilities rather than her probabilities.

**The rules to argue against.** Let ε be a fixed negligibility threshold in (0,1). Prospects are finite lists of (probability, utility) pairs, and the agent has an ordinary utility function over outcomes and otherwise satisfies expected utility theory. Two variants:

- The *hard floor* HF(ε): V(X) = Σ over branches with pᵢ ≥ ε of pᵢ·uᵢ. Sub-threshold branches contribute nothing and there is no renormalization.
- The *renormalized floor* RF(ε): V(X) = [Σ over branches with pᵢ ≥ ε of pᵢ·uᵢ] ÷ [Σ over branches with pᵢ ≥ ε of pᵢ], assuming the denominator is positive.

The agent ranks prospects by V. Assume, charitably, that any comparison is carried out on the common refinement of the two descriptions, so that the trivial move of getting dominance violations out of mismatched partitions is unavailable.

**What the argument must contain.**

1. Fix an explicit standard of comparison. "Worse than fanaticism" is not self-interpreting: say whether you are comparing the sets of unacceptable verdicts each view generates, their vulnerability to exploitation, their sensitivity to how a problem is described, their cost to the agent's own aims, or some combination, and justify the choice. You may, if it helps, frame the comparison in terms of a familiar trilemma — that a theory of the value of uncertain prospects is pressured to be reckless, timid, or non-transitive — but then say plainly which horn you assign to each rule and why the floor's horn is the worse one.

2. Construct the defects, with explicit numbers a reader can check, rather than announcing that they exist. I want at least three:
   - The blindness result. Show that on any fixed partition the floor makes the agent indifferent between prospects that differ only in what happens on a sub-threshold branch, and hence willing to trade an arbitrarily large loss there for an arbitrarily small gain on a kept branch. Then show what happens when such trades are offered repeatedly, and say whether the loss is bounded.
   - Dynamic inconsistency. Give a two-stage decision problem in which the branch that is negligible at the outset becomes certain from inside, so the rule's ex ante endorsement of a plan is overturned ex post. Exhibit the sequence of choices the rule endorses at each moment, compare it against the plan the agent would have chosen had she been able to commit, and state precisely in which states she ends up worse off, by how much, and whether the loss was foreseeable to her at the start.
   - Description-sensitivity. Explain what the common-refinement requirement does and does not fix, and argue that it is not costless: address whether a finest partition is always available, and what it means that two agents facing the same situation but individuating outcomes at equally defensible grains can rank their options differently.

3. Argue that the floor does not in fact cure the disease. For any ε there are Pascalian offers whose probability sits just above the threshold, and these are untouched. So the anti-fanatic needs a bound on utilities as well. Show that once such a bound is in place the floor is doing no independent work — or, if you think it still does work, say exactly what and at what price.

4. Take seriously at least three objections and answer them: that a sophisticated agent who chooses plans rather than myopically choosing at each node escapes the exploitation; that the floor is meant as a heuristic for cognitively limited agents rather than as a criterion of right choice, so the dynamic-inconsistency charge misses its target; and that ε should not be a constant but should be scaled to stakes or context. If you think any of these objections succeeds against part of your case, concede it and say what survives.

5. End with the constructive upshot. Given the failure of the floor, what should the anti-fanatic do — cap utilities, adopt a globally risk-averse transform, accept timidity, or bite the bullet and become a fanatic — and what does each option cost? Argue that at least one of these is preferable to the floor and say why.

6. Include a short scope note stating which of your steps require utilities that are genuinely unbounded, which need only very large finite utilities, and which go through even at modest stakes. If some of your argument turns out not to be about fanaticism at all, say so; that would be a result worth reporting rather than hiding.

**Constraints.** Your reader is a competent graduate student with no access to the literature, so define every term you use and do not send her looking anything up. Give no citations, no bibliography, and no attributions to named authors or papers; the argument has to stand on its own constructions. Keep a formal register throughout, aim for roughly 1,500 words and no more than 2,000, and write it as an argument that takes a side and presses it, not as a balanced survey. Where the thesis overreaches or depends on a contestable assumption about how prospects are individuated, flag it in the text rather than glossing over it.
