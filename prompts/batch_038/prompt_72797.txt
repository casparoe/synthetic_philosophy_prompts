The question set was as follows.

> In the *Treatise on Probability* Keynes holds that a probability is a logical relation between a conclusion and a body of premises, and that such relations need be neither numerically measurable nor mutually comparable. In Chapter 12 of *The General Theory* he holds that expectations about the remoter future cannot be grounded in any calculation of probabilistic prospects at all. Explain the doctrine of the *Treatise*, including the distinction between the probability of an argument and its weight. Explain what Chapter 12 adds to it. Then argue for one of the following: that the later position develops the earlier one, that it abandons it, or that the two are not really about the same thing.

Here is the student's answer, in full.

> Keynes's *Treatise on Probability* is an attempt to save probability from two reductions: the view that a probability statement reports a frequency in the world, and the view that it reports the strength of someone's feeling. For Keynes a probability is a relation of partial entailment holding between a conclusion and a body of premises, and when we grasp the premises we can sometimes perceive directly that they confer on the conclusion a probability of some degree. That makes probability objective without making it a feature of the world independent of minds: it is a logical relation, apprehended rather than measured. Two consequences matter for the rest of the question. The first is that not every such relation is numerically measurable; many probabilities can be given no number at all. The second is that two arguments may be outright non-comparable, since the relation always holds relative to its own body of evidence and there need be no fact about which of two differently-grounded arguments is better supported.
>
> The second key notion is weight. Two arguments may have precisely the same probability while resting on very different quantities of relevant evidence: a coin observed to land heads once and a coin observed to land heads five hundred times in a thousand trials license the same probability for the next toss, but the second argument carries vastly more weight. Weight is therefore best understood as the reliability, or precision, attaching to a probability — the spread around the estimate, so to speak — and Keynes's real innovation is to insist that a rational agent responds to the probability and its precision together rather than to the probability alone. On this reading weight is a second-order probability, a probability about a probability, and the *Treatise* anticipates the modern treatment of uncertainty as an ordered pair of a mean and a variance.
>
> Keynes also attacks the principle of indifference, the licence to distribute probability equally among alternatives about which we know nothing. His objection is that the principle yields contradictions, since the same state of affairs can be carved up into different sets of alternatives each claiming an equal division. From this he draws the general conclusion that no probability can be established a priori, and that every probability must ultimately be grounded in observed frequencies.
>
> Chapter 12 turns all of this onto investment. Long-term expectation concerns matters — the price of copper twenty years hence, the obsolescence of a new invention, the prospect of a European war — which, Keynes argues, admit of no calculable probability whatever. Because the facts of the present enter our expectations disproportionately, and because there is nothing to calculate from, we fall back on convention: we assume the existing state of affairs will persist unless we have some definite reason to expect change. The newspaper competition in which readers are asked to pick the prettiest of a hundred photographs gives the structure of the resulting game, since the sophisticated participant is not trying to identify the prettiest face, nor even the face he himself finds prettiest, but the face he expects average opinion to expect average opinion to prefer. Convention is fragile exactly because it lacks an evidential foundation, which is why waves of optimism and pessimism — what Keynes calls animal spirits — drive investment, and why the demand for liquidity swells whenever confidence falls.
>
> The relation between the two books is, I think, continuity through conversion. Ramsey's critique of the *Treatise*, written in the mid-1920s and published only after his death, persuaded Keynes that probability is not an objective logical relation but a degree of belief whose rationality consists in coherence. Chapter 12 is the working out of that conversion: animal spirits is simply Keynes's name for the subjective prior an investor must supply where the evidence gives out, and the instability of investment is the instability of priors that no data can pin down. The early objectivism was a mistake Keynes came to recognize as such, and the later work therefore does not extend the *Treatise* so much as replace it.
