So, kind of a weird request but bear with me. I work in the parks department of a mid-size city (~180k people) and I somehow got put on the committee that has to make a recommendation about Ridgeline, which is this gorgeous 40-acre hillside park in the richest part of town. Long version: it was a private estate left to the city decades ago with strings attached, it eats an insane share of our maintenance budget, there's no bus line anywhere near it, and the surrounding neighbors' association has basically made it clear it's "theirs." Meanwhile the south side, which is much poorer, has effectively no park land at all, and there is nowhere near enough money to build anything comparable down there. At last month's meeting a member of the public stood up and said the city should just close Ridgeline and let it revert to woodland, because if we can't all have a nice park, nobody should. Half the room nodded. I thought it was obviously insane — then I found out my reading group is split on it too, and we ended up three hours deep in Parfit's levelling down objection and nobody could actually articulate why they think what they think.

I'd like to sort this out in my own head before the vote, and also use it as a handout for the intro ethics night class I help run at the community college. So: write a dialogue between two people, a telic egalitarian (who thinks equality between people is intrinsically valuable, including in cases where nobody gains from it) and a prioritarian (who thinks only people's absolute well-being matters, but that a benefit matters more the worse off the person receiving it is). Set it at the actual committee meeting, maybe walking the Ridgeline trailhead parking lot, and have them argue about the specific question of whether closing the park would be better in any respect at all. Both of them need to be genuinely sharp — no strawmen, I definately don't want an "and then the egalitarian conceded" ending, but I also don't want the prioritarian to win just by repeating "but nobody's life goes better" on loop.

A few things I'd like them to actually wrestle with instead of skipping: the egalitarian's "better in one respect, though not better all things considered" move, and whether that's a substantive claim or just a way of having it both ways; the prioritarian's worry that if no person's life improves, calling the outcome "impersonally better" is empty; and then a twist I want you to build in — someone points out that Ridgeline is exactly what props up the housing prices and the school boundary lines that keep the south side poor, so closing it might actually make some south-side residents better off. Have them fight about whether that resolves the case or just shows the two views always agree whenever levelling down helps somebody, in which case what is left of their disagreement. And if you can work in the objection that prioritarian weighting only means anything once you've pinned down a zero point and a scale for well-being — so the view can flip its answer depending on how welfare gets measured, while a plain sum doesn't care — that would be great, because a friend of mine keeps raising it and I never know what to say back. Somewhere around 1200–1500 words, and it's fine — better, actually — if it ends without a winner, as long as the two of them have narrowed it down to exactly where they part company.
