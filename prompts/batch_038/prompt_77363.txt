I run the annotation side of a small AI data company — six of us in the office in Manchester, plus about fifteen freelance labellers spread over three time zones, most of them not native English speakers. What I actually spend my week doing is writing the guidelines: a client sends a spec, I turn it into something a normal person can follow without asking me questions every ten minutes, we label a calibration batch, argue about the disagreements, revise, and go again. Every single project hits the same wall. The labellers agree with me on all forty example items, and then in the first real batch they split into two or three camps doing systematically different things — and adding more examples doesn't fix it, it just moves the disagreement further out. A friend who did a philosophy degree mentioned at the pub that this is roughly what Kripke's Wittgenstein is on about, that a finite set of examples can't pin down a rule because you can always cook up a rival rule that fits everything you've shown them and diverges later, and honestly it's been rattling around in my head ever since, because that is a precise description of my job.

So: could you write me a procedure I can actually run on a guideline, ideally in the couple of hours between the client sending the spec and us starting calibration? I want the steps in order — what to do to a spec before it ever reaches a labeller, what to look for when a disagreement shows up, how to tell apart the cases where the guideline is genuinely indeterminate from the cases where somebody hasn't read it properly (or has read it properly and spotted something I missed), what counts as enough evidence to declare a divergence real rather than noise, and what to do once you've declared it: rewrite, stipulate a tie-breaker, or escalate. I need it to say where the procedure stops and hands the thing to a human because there's no further fact of the matter to dig up, and where it stops and hands it to the client because it's their spec and their risk. Please flag which steps are judgement calls wearing a procedure costume — I'd rather know than discover it in month three. And don't give me an agreement-metric answer: we already compute kappa on every batch and I've come to think its measuring the wrong thing here, since agreeing on the calibration items is exactly what the sceptical problem grants you from the start.

Then run it on three cases that are basically real. (A) Escalation project, guideline says escalate any message where the customer mentions cancelling their account; two labellers agree on sixty items, then one of them starts not escalating messages where the cancellation is hypothetical or denied — "nothing would make me cancel after eight years" — and when I check, she's been doing it consistently the whole time, not randomly, and every single one of my example items is compatible with what she's doing. (B) A PII project where the guideline lists categories with examples; one labeller starts flagging a first name plus a partial postcode in what turns out to be a village of three hundred people, and job titles at small named companies. I think he's over-labelling and slowing us down, and I'd like the procedure to tell me whether it is. If the honest output is that my guideline is the broken thing and we should re-specify and re-label four thousand items, I want it to say that plainly, because there's a delivery date and I will otherwise talk myself into a patch. (C) A toxicity project where the two readings are both defensible and the team just won't converge — sarcastic praise, and people quoting a slur in order to object to it — and here I want to see what the procedure does when there isn't a more correct reading, only a choice somebody has to own. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do, and what a guideline-writer should stop pretending to want; and if any step needs a workshop or a second calibration round to detect something, say how many days that costs, because I have to justify it to our ops lead.
