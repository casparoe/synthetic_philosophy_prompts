My younger brother joined the Navy and spent the first part of this year on patrol on one of the Vanguard boats — he won't say which, obviously, and half of what he did say he's since walked back — and one evening he mentioned the sealed letters in the safe aboard her, and that the skipper only opens them if Britain's gone and there's nobody left who can give him orders. I hadn't really thought about that before, the fact that the whole decision has to be made in advance by somebody who will be dead or unreachable by the time it actually matters, and who will never find out whether they decided well. That got me into deterrence theory properly and I've now got myself into a bit of a state about it, because I can't get the theory and the historical record to line up and I'd like someone to have a proper go at it.

The episode is Stanislav Petrov, 26 September 1983. As I've got it: three weeks after the Soviets shot down the Korean airliner, with Andropov convinced a surprise American first strike was coming and the KGB running a huge intelligence operation to spot the signs of one, Petrov is the duty officer in the bunker handling the Oko satellite early-warning system. It reports one missile launched from the US and then four more behind it. He waits for ground radar to corroborate, it never does, and he decides it's a malfunction and reports it as such, against protocol. The eventual explanation was something about sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds over the American Midwest lining up with the odd elliptical orbits of the warning satellites. I'm aware people argue about how much his call really mattered — some say the leadership would have launched on his report within minutes, others say there were several more layers of assessment above him and that they'd have waited for actual detonations on Soviet territory before firing. Don't build your analysis on which of those is true; if anything, tell me whether the theory's verdict changes depending on which you assume. The lens I want is the paradox of deterrence, roughly as Kavka sets it out: it can be rational — maybe even required — to hold the conditional intention to retaliate, because holding it is what prevents the attack, but if the condition ever actually obtains, carrying it out looks irrational, since you can't undo your own destruction by adding theirs; so you're left with an intention you have every reason to form and no reason to act on, and you can't repair that by simply resolving to make yourself irrational, because that resolution is itself a perfectly rational act.

The thing I can't work out, and what I'd most like you to dig into rather than the standard telling, is that the paradox seems to assume the condition is a fact about the world — you strike, I strike. On that night in 1983 the condition wasn't available as a fact to anyone; it was available as a reading on a screen with an unknown false-positive rate. So the intention Petrov's superiors were supposed to hold couldn't have been conditional on being attacked, only on appearing to be attacked, and once you rewrite it that way the payoffs change shape entirely: the threat gets more credible and more catastrophic at the same time, and the rational thing to intend is no longer obvious. Run that through the episode carefully and tell me what falls out. I also want you to deal with the fact that Petrov's individual discretion made Soviet retaliation less believable while making the world less likely to end, which seems to be exactly backwards from what all the talk about the rationality of irrationality and about threats that leave something to chance would recommend — the chance here wasn't cultivated as a bargaining asset, it was a bug in a system that was too new to trust, and the man who spotted it had no stake whatsoever in the credibility of his own side's threat, because his job was accurate reporting, not signalling. Does the theory have any slot at all for an agent like that, or does it only ever describe the principal and treat everyone below him as plumbing? And set that against the letters my brother mentioned, which look like the opposite solution to the same problem: the conditional intention written down in advance, in sealed form, by an agent who expects to be dead, one of whose permitted contents is simply to tell the commander to decide for himself — which reads to me like the prime minister delegating away the very intention she was supposed to be holding to make deterrence work. So: who is the agent in each case, what is the intention conditional on, which features of the Petrov night does the paradox actually explain and which does it misdescribe or skip, and does the episode end up confirming the theory, standing as an anomaly it has to swallow, or being a kind of case it never anticipated in the first place? Be as honest as you can about where it goes quiet rather than making it speak. No bibliography or reading list, I'm not chasing references — I just want the analysis, and I'd rather you were precise about the mapping than comprehensive about the literature.
