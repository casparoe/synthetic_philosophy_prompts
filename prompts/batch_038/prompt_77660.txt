I teach animal ethics and have spent the last few years working through the welfare literature on farmed fish and decapod crustaceans. There is a pattern in it I have been unable to name, and I should like to know whether a name exists.

The evidence for sentience in these animals is, on everyone's account, partial: nociception, opioid modulation, motivated trade-offs, nothing that settles the matter. What I notice is that the weight this one body of evidence is permitted to carry varies with the price of acting on it. Where the implication is inexpensive — a change in handling practice, a ban on a single slaughter method, a sentence in a code of practice — the uncertainty is treated as a reason for caution: we cannot rule out suffering, so we err on the side of the animal. Where the implication is expensive — capital investment in stunning across an entire sector, a restriction that would materially raise production costs — the identical findings are described as inconclusive, contested, and premature, and the same authors warn that policy must not run ahead of the science. The distribution of caution tracks the distribution of cost almost perfectly, and this is never announced; each judgment is offered as a neutral reading of the evidence. The sharpest instance I have met is internal to a single paper, whose discussion section concludes that sentience is probable and whose policy section concludes that the evidence will not bear costly regulation. No reviewer flags the tension.

I know the neighbourhood. There is work holding that what is at stake can affect what an agent knows or is justified in believing; there is the precautionary principle in environmental decision-making; there is the standing charge of motivated reasoning. None seems to be this, because here the stakes belong to the party making the attribution rather than to the being attributed to, and the shift is presented as evidential rather than pragmatic.

So: does this configuration have an established name? If it does not, I want considerably more than a coinage. Please structure the response as follows. "Bottom line" — three sentences: whether a term exists, and which candidate comes closest. "The phenomenon restated" — about 150 words in your own terms, separating its essential features from what is merely incidental to my examples. "Nearest concepts" — about 500 words on at least four candidates, each with what it captures here and precisely where the fit fails; names only, please, and no citations, as I can find those myself. "The residue" — about 200 words on what none of them covers, in particular the asymmetry of direction and the fact that the move is dressed as evidential. "A name and a definition" — about 150 words, with conditions for applying the term. "Boundary cases" — about 300 words, taking each in turn: a regulator who concedes the uncertainty and adopts precaution anyway because precaution is cheap; an anaesthetist who treats an infant's pain responses as real though unprovable because acting costs little; a researcher who lowers her confidence in fish sentience after encountering better evidence; and someone who accepts fish sentience while resisting insect sentience on the grounds that the implications of the latter would be unmanageable rather than expensive.
