I am in the third year of a doctorate in political theory and am applying to my faculty's summer research fund: a modest award that buys out three months, during which I intend to develop one dissertation chapter into a standalone article. The application requires a proposal of about 1,200 words. My difficulty is that I can see the shape of my argument but not yet its question, and I would like the proposal to do the work of stating it.

What I have so far is closer to a diagnosis than to a thesis. Normative writing on secession is organised largely around two positions: the remedial-right-only view, on which a group acquires a claim to unilateral secession only in response to some sustained and grave injustice, and the primary or plebiscitary view, on which a clear majority in a fair vote is itself sufficient to ground the claim. The two disagree sharply about when a group may leave, but they agree tacitly about something prior: that the electorate is settled once the territory is. On both accounts the second question is downstream of the first — identify the unit that has the claim and you have identified who votes.

My suspicion is that this is wrong, and that the franchise does independent normative work rather than merely implementing a prior result. The Scottish vote of 2014 is the clearest case. Extending the franchise to sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds while confining it to those resident in Scotland was not an administrative gloss on an antecedently determinate Scottish people; it was a decision about which conception of that people was to be authoritative, and a different franchise would have made a different claim about self-determination true. Quebec and Catalonia press the same problem in other registers, though there the contested variable is not age but residence and constitutional standing: whether the relevant electorate tracks those who live under the institutions in question, those who belong to the nation, or some body larger than either.

Please draft the proposal for me, making sure that it contains the following. First, a research question stated precisely enough that a determinate answer is possible — not an announcement that a distinction matters, but something on which the article could end up being right or wrong. Second, an account of why neither of the positions above, nor anything they imply, settles that question, and why the gap it leaves is a genuine gap rather than a concession both sides would make without argument. Third, the method: what I would actually do across the three months, stage by stage, and why that procedure rather than the obvious alternatives — a purely conceptual analysis of the notion of a people, a comparative constitutional survey, or a formal treatment of franchise design. Fourth, what a result would look like: name the two or three conclusions the chapter could reach and say what considerations would decide between them. Fifth, an honest failure condition. I am acutely aware that my project may be the democratic boundary problem in new clothes, the familiar circularity whereby constituting the demos presupposes an answer to the very question the demos is convened to answer; the proposal should say what is distinct about the franchise question as it arises inside a secession referendum, and should treat collapse into that older problem as one of the ways the work could fail. Sixth, a realistic assessment of scope: what must be cut to fit three months and a single article, and what I should promise nobody.

The panel includes readers who work nowhere near this literature, so the opening paragraph must be intelligible to them without condescension. Refer freely to positions and arguments by name, but do not assemble a bibliography or a cited literature review; I will add the references myself.
