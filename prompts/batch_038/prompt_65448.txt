I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a short piece on the ethics of an infinite universe for our ideas section. I have a working draft below. I would like comments on it as an editor would give them: tell me where the argument is wrong, where it is unfair, and where it is dull, in that order.

Draft, "The Arithmetic of Everything":

Cosmologists tell us the universe may be spatially infinite, and if life is not a fluke, it may contain infinitely many happy people and infinitely many miserable ones. Suppose that is so. Then a utilitarian who adds up welfare faces an arithmetic problem rather than a moral one: any act you perform changes the total by a finite amount, and infinity plus a hundred is still infinity. On the summing view, saving a hundred lives, or a hundred thousand, makes literally no difference to the value of the world. Philosophers call this paralysis, and it is not a sceptical thought experiment; it is what the theory says once you take its numbers seriously.

The natural repair is to stop asking what each world is worth in total and to ask only how two worlds differ. That works when the difference between them is finite. It fails when it is not, and here the mathematics gets uncomfortable. A series that converges only conditionally — one with infinitely many positive and infinitely many negative terms, whose sum nonetheless converges — can be rearranged to converge to any number you like, or to diverge outright. Decision theorists know this from a coin-flip gamble whose payoffs form exactly such a series, and whose expected value is therefore not a fact about the gamble but about the order in which you list its outcomes. If the value of an infinite world is a sum of this kind, then infinite sums are simply never well defined: what a world is worth depends on how you add it up.

And the ordering is not handed over by the physics. Spacetime supplies no canonical list of its own regions. To rank worlds you must impose one — by time, by distance from here, by causal connection to this particular agent — and every such ordering privileges an indexical. The familiar advice to care only about what you can causally affect is therefore not a modest retreat from cosmic arithmetic. It is the claim that value is defined relative to a point of view. Infinite ethics, on this reading, is not really about infinity at all. It is about who is doing the counting, and the mathematics merely makes that question impossible to dodge.

The upshot for the current fashion of thinking in cosmic scales is deflationary. Scaling up does not give you bigger numbers. Past a certain point it stops giving you numbers at all.

Four things I particularly want from you. First, check every technical claim for scope, not just for truth: I am confident about the conditional-convergence result itself, but I suspect I have leaned on it further than it will bear, and I would rather you find that than my fact-checker. Second, is the central claim defensible — that any escape from paralysis requires an ordering of the parts of the universe, that no such ordering is available without appealing to here, now, or this agent, and that the causal-accessibility response is therefore a claim about indexicality rather than a technical fix? If there is a way to get a non-indexical ordering out of causal structure or from the geometry of spacetime, I want to hear it, because the piece currently assumes there is not. Third, tell me whether the closing move is fair, or whether I am attacking a position nobody holds: my deflationary conclusion treats infinity as a live commitment, and I have a nagging worry that the people I am implicitly criticising need only very large finite numbers for their arguments to go through. Where exactly does the draft overreach, and where does the worry dissolve? Fourth, a craft question: does the coin-flip gamble earn its place, or would a single concrete case — a charity that saves a fixed number of lives every year forever — land better with a general reader than my abstractions?

Please be explicit about epistemic status throughout. I want to know which parts of this are settled, which are genuinely contested among people who work on these problems, and above all where I have dressed a normative choice in the clothes of arithmetic and presented it as though it were mathematics. Do not let a contested view pass as consensus merely because I have written it confidently, and do not hesitate to say that a claim I have stated flatly is a live dispute.

Write your comments in British English, in continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just paragraphs, the way you would write to a colleague.
