My sister has this thing she does. Every year at my mums birthday we play a game where somebody describes a made-up life in a paragraph and she's the judge — she rules on whether it was "meaningful," and she takes it very seriously and gets cross if you write her a lazy life. She's been doing it for about twelve years so I've built up a big pile of her verdicts.

The ones I remember best:

- Woman who taught year three for forty years in a rough part of town, never won anything, most of her old students couldn't have told you her name. Meaningful.
- Man who spent sixty years cataloguing his bottle cap collection, was perfectly happy the whole time, hurt nobody, died content. Not meaningful. She said something like "that's a pleasant life, it's just not a meaningful one," and wouldn't budge an inch.
- Woman who looked after her brother after his accident for forty years. Bitter about it basically the entire time, told everyone she'd leave tomorrow if she had anywhere to go, never left once. Meaningful, and my sister got quite firm about this one.
- Physicist who spent thirty years on a theory that turned out flat wrong, but who trained a load of students who went on and did real work. Meaningful.
- Novelist who wrote one brilliant book, kept it in a drawer her whole life, died, and it came out thirty years later and people went wild for it. Meaningful.
- Man who spent twenty five years rebuilding a burnt out chapel by himself, stone by stone. People got married in it for about two decades and then the roof went in and it came down again. Meaningful.
- Man who left a fortune to a children's hospital purely out of spite, to stop his kids getting it, saved who knows how many lives. She sat on that one for ages and then said not meaningful.

She also flatly refuses to let anyone play the "well, in a thousand years nobody will remember any of it" card — she thinks that's a cheat and a different question altogether.

So here's the life I want to run past you. A man who loved a cycle of old poems in a language almost nobody can read any more. He spent forty years copying them out by hand, beautifully, because he thought he was keeping them alive. Never showed them to anybody, no wife, no friends to speak of, though he was quietly happy enough. When he died his nephew cleared out the shed and the copies went in the skip — the damp had got into them and they were illegible anyway. And the poems were never in any danger: eleven complete copies sit in university libraries and they were all digitised years ago. So nothing he did changed anything, and nothing of his is left.

What's she going to say? I genuinely don't know, which is why I'm asking, and I'm less interested in the answer than in the machinery underneath it. What rule — or rules — is she actually running that makes all seven of those come out the way they do? There's obviously more than one that fits, so lay them out separately, say what each one predicts about the poem man, tell me which of the seven lives is the closest analog to his since I suspect that's doing most of the work, and then give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it. And finally: what life should I invent for next year that would force the rules apart, i.e. one where they hand out opposite verdicts? Don't bother naming philosophers or pointing me at books, I just want the reasoning worked through.
