I'm finishing a seminar paper in a political philosophy course on distributive justice, and the case I've built it around keeps resisting the framework I'm supposed to be applying. The case is the Seattle dialysis selection committee of the early 1960s and the congressional decision that replaced it. The facts as I have them — correct me if I've garbled any of them, but please don't let the analysis depend on details beyond these: after Belding Scribner's group devised a reusable vascular shunt around 1960, chronic kidney failure went from untreatable to treatable, though only for as many patients as there were machines, staff, and money. The Seattle Artificial Kidney Center opened in 1962 with a handful of beds at an annual treatment cost of roughly fifteen thousand dollars per patient, which no insurer would cover. Physicians applied a first, explicitly medical screen — under forty-five, no other serious illness, emotionally able to tolerate the regimen, and a resident of Washington State, since state money had funded the research — and then, unwilling to make the final choice themselves, handed the remainder to a committee of seven anonymous, unpaid lay citizens: a lawyer, a minister, a banker, a homemaker, a labor leader, a state official, and a surgeon. A magazine journalist was allowed to sit in on the committee's deliberations for several months and reported that it worked from dossiers listing occupation, income and savings, education, marital history, family stability, church and civic involvement, apparent motivation, and the ages of the patient's children — that is, from an estimate of the patient's anticipated contribution to society rather than from anything about how the patient had come to be sick. The committee was widely denounced as a god panel. A federally convened expert panel in the late sixties recommended public funding for everyone instead; in 1972 Congress extended Medicare coverage to essentially all end-stage renal disease patients eligible for Social Security, abolishing the local sorting procedure outright. The program was projected to be modest and became one of the most expensive single items in Medicare, and the population it eventually served turned out to be older, sicker, and far less likely to return to work than the projections assumed.

My framework is luck egalitarianism: justice requires neutralizing inequalities that trace to brute luck, while leaving in place those that trace to choices for which the agent can fairly be held responsible. The contribution I'd hoped to make was a device I've been calling the screening problem. Luck egalitarianism is ordinarily stated as a claim about what a just distribution looks like, but any implementation must first run a procedure that sorts actual claimants into the lucky and the unlucky, and that procedure is performed by fallible, interested, time-pressed people working under scarcity, using proxies, with error rates and costs of its own. The Seattle committee is the screening problem made visible, because the sorting step had to be done by hand, case by case, in a hospital basement, with a named person dying if it was done slowly. What I want is a disciplined analysis running in both directions. Start with the mapping: who is the distributor here — the committee, the physicians who set the medical screen, the state that funded the research and thereby created the residency requirement, Congress, or the society whose shares of health and money are ultimately at issue — and what exactly is being distributed: dialysis slots, public funds, functioning kidneys, years of life? Then say what the brute luck / option luck line picks out in this setting, given that kidney failure might arrive through an inherited condition, through untreated hypertension, or through decades of work and diet that the patient did not choose either, and given that being a Washington resident in 1962 was itself a matter of luck. The harder part, and where I most want pressure: the committee's criteria seem to me to sort into at least three distinct species that luck egalitarianism treats very differently — criteria of causal responsibility for one's condition, criteria of predicted compliance with a gruelling regimen, and criteria of social contribution to third parties — and I suspect the view licenses only the first, while the practice ran overwhelmingly on the third, with the second supplying its respectable cover. Test that suspicion. Can a luck egalitarian admit the third species at all without collapsing into a general maximizing account of who should be saved? Is the second species a legitimate application of responsibility, or is it a prediction about future behavior masquerading as an assessment of past agency? And do the age ceiling and the residency requirement look like brute-luck neutralization or like its opposite?

Then run it the other way, and don't soften the results. Which features of the episode does luck egalitarianism genuinely explain or justify, which does it misdescribe, and about which is it simply silent — I want the silences named rather than filled in on the theory's behalf. In particular: does Dworkin's hypothetical-insurance device have anything to say about a body deciding at a desk whether a specific patient gets a specific machine, or is there a level mismatch between a theory about the shape of a distribution and a task that is essentially the governance of a procedure? Is the 1972 law best read as the luck egalitarian solution — a collective declaration that end-stage renal disease is brute luck by definition, purchased in advance, as it were, through a social insurance arrangement — or as a retreat from responsibility-tracking altogether, and does the fact that it produced unbounded cost and a population nobody predicted count against the view that generated it? And does the episode vindicate the standard complaint, pressed most famously by Anderson, that luck egalitarianism is harsh and humiliating because it abandons the imprudent? My worry is that the case actually points somewhere else: the committee abandoned the prudent and the imprudent alike and sorted on social worth, so the objection the episode really invites may not be about harshness toward the responsible but about the impossibility of auditing a responsibility judgment when it is rendered by proxies, in secret, by amateurs, with no way to tell whether a given exclusion reflected the patient's choices or the committee's prejudices. Tell me whether that reframing survives contact with the theory or whether I'm special-pleading for my own device. Finish with a verdict on the relationship between case and theory — confirmation, anomaly, or an episode the theory never anticipated — and with a short, blunt statement of what my paper's thesis should now say. I know the canonical texts and don't need citations or a reading list; what I need is the reasoning, and an honest account of what you'd have to stipulate to make luck egalitarianism speak to this case at all.
