So — my aunt died in February and left me two shoeboxes of letters my grandfather wrote her from a tanker between about '58 and '64, envelopes and all. And I did the stupid thing: read them out of order, pulled the sheets out of the envelopes to scan them flat, then binned the paper because I live in a small flat. Six weeks later I was rereading the chapter in GEB on the location of meaning — Hofstadter's frame message / outer message / inner message business, which I'm probably mangling slightly — and it landed on me that I'd been treating the ink as the record and everything around it as packaging, when most of what those letters meant was in the packaging. The stamp, the port of posting, the fact that one of them was never actually sent, the coffee ring, the way she'd kept them banded by year.

I've got a 30-minute slot at our regional archives association's spring meeting and I want to make the general argument: a record's meaning isn't a payload with a wrapper, its a stack, and the outer layers can invert the inner one — an unsent letter reads differently from a sent letter with identical text, a photograph filed under "evidence" reads differently from the same photograph filed under "family." From which: every processing decision (flattening, refoldering, weeding, re-describing) is a semantic decision dressed up as housekeeping. And digitization doesn't strip the stack so much as silently re-frame it, because the new frame says this-is-data, an outer message nobody authored or signed off on. My practical pitch is a "layer budget": for each accession you state in the finding aid which layer you're sacrificing and why, so the loss is visible and in principle reversible instead of invisible.

Audience is about 35 people, nearly all working archivists from county record offices and two university repositories, plus a few digital preservation people, one book historian, and one philosopher of history who I suspect will enjoy me too much. These are people with backlogs and no money, who wear original order and provenance as professional identity, who mostly think the more-product-less-process crowd were right, and who have no patience at all for theory that shows up as an unfunded mandate. They're polite, which is the problem — the objection will arrive as a soft question from the floor rather than a fight.

Build me the prep sheet. Six to nine objections ranked by how much damage they'd actually do, not by how clever they sound, in the words this room would use them in, each with about a 90-second reply and a flag on where that reply goes soft. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to buy any credibility whatsoever, and the single point that has to land or the layer-budget idea dies on the table. And tell me the question I'm praying doesn't come up — I suspect it's something like "are you just dressing up your own regret as a theory of meaning," but tell me if there's a worse one waiting.
