**Question (25 marks):**

> State the paradox of voting. Explain in what sense it is a free-rider problem and in what sense it is a problem about probabilities, and say why the difference between those two framings matters. Then assess two proposed resolutions: (i) adding a D term to the calculus of voting, and (ii) the claim that the expected-value calculation has been applied to the wrong quantity.

**Student answer:**

The paradox of voting is that voting looks irrational on a normal expected-utility calculation. If p is the chance your vote is decisive and B is the benefit to you of your preferred candidate winning, the expected instrumental return is pB, and p is vanishingly small in any large election — often put at something like one in ten million — while the costs C of getting informed, registering, and standing in a queue are real. Since pB − C is negative, an expected-utility maximiser should stay home. And yet turnout in national elections is routinely 50–70 percent, so the model predicts a behaviour that almost nobody actually exhibits. That is the paradox.

It is a free-rider problem in the sense that the outcome of an election is a public good: non-excludable and non-rival, so you get whatever government everyone else's votes produced whether or not you paid C. Each voter reasons that the collective good will be supplied anyway, so the rational move is to let others pay. But there is a difference between this and a textbook public goods game. In a public goods game your own contribution is small but not negligible — you can see its marginal effect. Here the problem is specifically that p is essentially zero even holding everybody else's behaviour fixed, which is a claim about probabilities rather than about incentives to defect. The distinction matters because the two framings suggest different repairs: the free-rider framing suggests you need selective incentives or sanctions of the kind Olson talked about, whereas the probability framing suggests you have to go back and re-examine p, or what is being multiplied by it.

The first of those repairs was made by Downs himself, who added the D term to get R = pB − C + D, where D stands for the benefits attaching to the act of voting itself rather than to its outcome — civic duty, expressing allegiance to the system, the sense of efficacy. This does fix the arithmetic, because D doesn't depend on p or on B, so it can stay positive however hopeless the race looks and R can stay positive with it. The obvious cost is that the theory gets hard to falsify: D can absorb any turnout figure you throw at it. But it also yields a real prediction, namely that since D is constant, turnout shouldn't track how close the contest is. And it doesn't — turnout in most established democracies is fairly stable from election to election, swinging only a few points. So the D term isn't as ad hoc as it first appears.

The second repair, from Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan, is that the calculation is aimed at the wrong quantity. A self-interested voter multiplies p by their own private benefit, but a vote affects the whole polity, so the right thing to multiply p by is the aggregate benefit to everyone. Since the number of people affected is enormous — call it N — and p is roughly of order 1/N, the two large numbers cancel, and the expected value of a vote turns out comparable to the expected value of a modest charitable donation. On this view voting is instrumentally rational even for a self-interested voter, and the paradox dissolves with no need for a duty term at all.

The same insight about small p runs through the rest of the literature. Because your vote almost certainly won't matter, it is cheap to vote on the basis of how a candidate makes you feel rather than what they would do, which is Brennan and Lomasky's expressive account; it is also cheap to hold politically biased beliefs, which is Caplan's rational irrationality; and it explains Downs's rational ignorance. These are all the same argument applied to different objects. The upshot is that the paradox of voting isn't really a puzzle about turnout so much as a warning that whatever gets people to the polls is not tracking the truth or the common good.
