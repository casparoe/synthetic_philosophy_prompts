I recently read an essay in a general-audience philosophy magazine about the limits of knowledge, and a friend of mine who works on Dummett told me it was careless. I could not tell whether she was being defensive or whether something is genuinely wrong with the piece, so I would like an accuracy audit. The essay's central paragraph runs as follows:

"In a short 1963 paper the logician Frederic Fitch devised a proof intended to demolish the slogan at the heart of anti-realist theories of truth — that every truth is knowable — by showing that it entails something manifestly false. The proof begins from a truth of a kind there are obviously plenty of: a conjunction of an ordinary matter of fact with the claim that nobody knows that fact — for instance, that a certain unvisited cave contains a speck of dust, and that nobody knows it. Given the anti-realist's principle, that conjunction must be possibly known; but knowledge is factive and distributes over conjunction, so whoever knew it would have to know its second part, and knowing that nobody knows a thing cannot go together with knowing the thing — hence that second part is a truth no one can ever know. So if there is any unknown truth at all the principle is false, and since there plainly are unknown truths, what looked like a modest middle course between truth-as-what-we-know and truth-as-what-outstrips-all-knowing turns out not to be available. The upshot is a substantive discovery about the world rather than a piece of logical housekeeping: there are ordinary facts, sitting in caves and in the past, that lie permanently beyond the reach of any possible investigation, which is exactly what the realist has always maintained and the anti-realist could never concede."

For what it is worth, I lean realist and found the paragraph persuasive, which is a further reason to have it checked. Please give me exactly five numbered points, one for each sentence of the paragraph in order. For each, say whether the sentence is accurate, a fair simplification of something accurate, or wrong; where it is simplified or wrong, give the correct claim and note what an anti-realist has available by way of reply. In the fifth point, also state explicitly whether the essay's conclusion survives once the earlier sentences are corrected. Each point should run to one to three sentences, and I want no introduction, no overall verdict, and no concluding remarks beyond the fifth point.
