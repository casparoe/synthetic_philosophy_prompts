so I've been reading Williams on internal reasons off and on for a couple of years, mostly because it keeps coming up in arguments with my brother, and I've finally tried to write down what I actually think. My reading group wants a one-pager from everyone and I want someon to poke holes in mine before I hand it in. Here's the list:

1. For something to be a reason for a person to act, there has to be a route — can be long, can go through imagination, can involve getting the facts straight, can involve noticing what other things they already care about commit them to — from what that person actually cares about to doing the thing. No route, then "you had every reason to" isn't a claim about him at all, it's just me making a noise about how awful I think it was.

2. Blame isn't only about reasons though. A lot of it is my reaction to him as a person, and I don't think it stands or falls with whether he had a reason in the sense of (1).

3. But when I say what he did was inexcusable, I do think I'm committed to there having been a reason for him not to do it, and to his having been able to act on it. Otherwise "inexcusable" is just noise with a fancy register.

4. Case that seems easy to me: a man drinks heavily, never once sits down and thinks about what it's doing to his kid, but he loves the kid and would have been shaken if he'd ever actually pictured it. He had a reason. He's blameworthy. I don't feel any strain here.

5. Case that I know is harder: someone raised somewhere that a particular group is treated as barely human. He's reflective, he's not stupid, he's decent to his friends, he has all the relevant facts about what's being done. But as far as I can tell there is genuinely no route from anything in him to seeing it as wrong. I still want to say he's blameworthy, and that "he was made by his society" isn't an excuse, it's just a description of how he got that way.

6. I also think a conversation can create a reason that wasn't there before. Not by uncovering a route that was there all along and he'd missed — sometimes the exchange just gives him a concern he plainly didn't have, and from then on reasons flow from it. I don't want to say the concern was latent in his old set, because then (6) collapses into (1) and nothing was created.

7. You can have reasons you don't know about and couldn't state if someone asked you to. Articulating them isn't part of having them.

8. And I don't want reasons flickering on and off depending on which afternoon you catch someone in. If he had it in June he had it in July, whatever his mood or his current obsessions.

Which of these actually pull against each other? I'm sure not all of them do, and I'd rather not get eight complaints of equal weight. Rank the real ones by how bad they are, tell me which pairs only look bad and why, and for each real tension say what the cheapest revision is and what that revision costs me elsewhere on the list. If one item would clean up the most once dropped, say which and don't spare my feelings. Two specific things I'd like checked: whether I'm using "could have" in (3) in a sense that (1) rules out, and whether (6) is available to me as a way of rescuing (5) or whether using it there would break (1) in a way I haven't noticed.
