I serve on my department's replication panel, which decides whether a replication attempt counts as confirming, disconfirming, or failing to bear on a published effect. The panel is chaired by a senior methodologist whose verdicts have been strikingly consistent, and I would like to anticipate his ruling before Thursday's meeting.

His record to date:

- A 1990s study reporting p = 0.01, with no record of how the sample size was arrived at: declined to treat it as confirmatory, on the grounds that there was no stopping rule to audit.
- A team that halted data collection once significance emerged, contrary to its own protocol: replication failed, and he ruled the original finding inadmissible as confirmatory evidence.
- A Bayesian sequential design that sampled until the Bayes factor exceeded thirty, stopping at 187 of a planned 400: successful replication, with no complaint whatsoever about the stopping.
- A pre-registered fixed-n study that came up non-significant, but whose interval excluded everything above the smallest effect of interest: successful replication of a null.
- A Bayesian analysis whose headline conclusion reversed between a sceptical and a favourable prior: failed, because the conclusion was carried by the prior rather than the data.
- An honestly labelled exploratory subgroup analysis appended to a pre-registered confirmatory test: acceptable, provided it stayed out of the abstract.

Thursday's submission followed its protocol exactly, reached its pre-registered sample, and reports p = 0.02 with an interval that excludes the smallest effect of interest. The authors also report, unregistered but clearly flagged, that the Bayes factor under the protocol's own default prior falls below two; they rest their claim on the significance test and treat the Bayes factor as a curiosity.

Predict his verdict. Then set out the candidate rules his record admits — I have in mind at least protocol adherence as such, the auditability of the stopping rule and the resulting error probabilities, evidential weight located in Bayes factors, precision relative to the smallest effect of interest, and robustness to the prior — and say which of these his six verdicts genuinely distinguish and which remain co-extensive on the evidence so far. For each surviving rule, state what it implies about Thursday's case, identify the one he is most likely running, and justify that preference rather than merely asserting it. Name a further case, one the panel might plausibly encounter, that would separate the two best-fitting rules. Finally, since I shall have to defend the decision to the authors in writing, tell me what principle the panel would be committing itself to if it ruled the other way, and what that principle would license in future submissions.
