Got into an hours-long argument with a friend last night about Fitch's paradox and I still don't know where I land. I'm not scared of the conclusion — I'm happy to deny that all truths are knowable — but I can't say *which* truths drop out, and my gut verdicts pull in different directions. Here they are:

- the number of hairs on my dog right now: knowable, boring.
- what my great-grandmother was thinking on a particular platform in 1919, which nobody wrote down: I want to say it WAS knowable — by her, then — but isn't now. So knowability needs a time slot?
- the exact grain count of a beach that eroded ten thousand years ago leaving no trace: never knowable by anyone at any time, and I'm completely unbothered saying that.
- "it's raining outside and nobody knows it": not knowable, fine — but the damage seems stuck inside the conjunction, it doesn't make the rain itself unknowable.
- "there are truths no one will ever know": I claim to know this one. Generality as an escape hatch feels too cheap and I cant say why.
- a true but unprovable sentence of arithmetic: knowable, I'm a realist there — and this is the one that worries me, because I don't know what makes it different from the beach.

My friend's jab: if the difference is "the numbers are still there to be investigated," the beach facts were once there too, so my distinction collapses into the 1919 case, which I wanted to come out differently.

So: give me a principle, or a couple of competing ones, that captures these verdicts. State it precisely, say which case motivates which clause, and tell me what it predicts for a case I didn't list — a truth that could only ever be known by exactly one person, for about a minute, in a way they could never convey to anyone else. Then tell me whether the paradox still bites on my restricted principle or whether the restriction blocks it at the generalization step, and whether the old trick of rigidifying the principle (knowable-that-actually-p, or whatever the right formulation is) does any work for the 1919 and beach cases. Be blunt about which of my verdicts I have to give up to keep the rest.
