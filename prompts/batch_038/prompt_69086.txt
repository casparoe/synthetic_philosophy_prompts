I'm a biostatistician, ten-ish years doing systematic reviews and meta-analyses for a medical evidence group, so my whole working life is pooling noisy estimates: fixed-effect vs random-effects models, τ², Higgins' I², funnel plots and Egger's test when we're worried about publication bias, GRADE when somebody downstream wants to know how much confidence to put in the pooled number. My partner does political theory and I let myself get dragged along to their reading group a couple times a year. Last week it was the Condorcet jury theorem, and the room treated it like it was basically the same result I work with: a bunch of estimators that are each better than a coin flip, pooled by majority vote, converge on the truth as you add voters. One of them said the theorem is just a meta-analysis where the effect size happens to be a truth value, and I laughed along, but on the drive home I couldn't actually articulate why that's too glib. So I want the translation done properly, in my vocabulary rather than in theirs.

Take the theorem's own claims one at a time — the competence-above-one-half condition, the independence assumption, the odd-number business, the "as the group grows the probability the majority is right goes to one" part, and the corollary that adding voters strictly improves things — and restate each in terms of the objects I actually manipulate: study-level effects, within-study variance, inverse-variance weights, between-study heterogeneity, the pooled point estimate and its interval, and the difference between assuming one shared effect and assuming a distribution of them. Then do the same for the places the theorem gets contested in that reading group: people arguing about wether voters are really independent, whether competence is genuinely above a half or just assumed, what deliberation does to it, whether a group can be competent about some questions and hopeless about others, and whether there is even a single well-defined correct answer that everyone is voting on. Which contest is which knob in my framework? I especially want to know if correlated votes is just τ² > 0 wearing a philosophy costume, and whether the theorem's asymptotic certainty has any real counterpart in what I do — in my world, piling on more studies under substantial heterogeneity doesn't drive the random-effects interval to a point, it plateaus at a width set by τ². If that plateau is literally the same phenomenon as the failure of the jury theorem under correlated error, say so and show the correspondence; if it only looks similar, say that instead and tell me where the resemblance runs out.

Then give me the residue report, and I want you to actually commit rather than lay out the options and let me choose. For each piece of the jury-theorem story: what carries over cleanly, what you had to bend out of shape to make it fit, and what has no expression at all in meta-analysis. My own guess is that the stubborn residue is normative — the theorem gets recruited to say the majority is entitled to decide, that the outvoted should defer, that democratic outcomes carry some epistemic authority — and my framework has no slot whatsoever for that, because a pooled estimate doesn't command anybody, it just sits there with an interval around it. Tell me whether I'm right about that, or whether there's non-normative residue too that I've missed. And then take a side on the question I actually care about: does the residue indict my vocabulary, i.e. is meta-analysis missing something real about how groups track truth that only the political-theory framing can say — or does it show that the philosophical claim was leaning on its probabilistic core for more than that core can bear? Argue for whichever verdict you land on, and flag any spot where you think I've garbled the theorem or where you're working from a nonstandard version of it.
