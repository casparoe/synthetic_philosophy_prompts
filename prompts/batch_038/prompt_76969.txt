I sit on a municipal commission that will spend the next six weeks deciding whether the city should staff a new standing oversight panel — the one that reviews misconduct complaints against police officers and recommends discipline — by sortition rather than by appointment or election. I am the member charged with presenting the evaluation criteria at the public hearing, and the drafting has made it uncomfortably clear that until now I have been arguing from instinct. Both camps have been at me. The reformers point to the Athenian boule, to jury service, and to the recent citizens' assemblies on electoral reform and climate policy, and argue that a randomly drawn body is the only one that cannot be bought or lobbied. The city attorney argues that a panel nobody elected can be neither held to account nor meaningfully answerable for harm, and that its recommendations will be quietly ignored the first time they cut against an organized constituency.

I do not want a defense of either side. I want the specification: the list of requirements that any adequate account of the legitimacy of selection-by-lot must satisfy before it can be applied to a body like this one, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Please be concrete about the hard ones. What must such an account say about the mandate problem — what, if anything, transfers authority to people no one chose? About accountability — is the absence of electoral incentive a fatal gap, or precisely the feature that purchases independence, and can a single account hold both claims at once? About composition — the summons is not the panel: who declines, who cannot get time off work, who is paid to attend, and does a body that has deliberated for six weeks still represent the population it was drawn from? About the epistemic claim — jury-theorem-style arguments assume a binary question and independent judgments that are better than chance, so I want to know whether the argument survives deliberation, which correlates votes, and survives multi-option choices, where there is no majority-vote analogue at all. About capture — random selection eliminates campaign donors but not the agenda-setter: whoever writes the charge, selects the briefing materials and the expert witnesses, and facilitates the room. And about the excluded, who must watch a panel of strangers adjudicate a complaint of their own.

Then tell me which of these are genuinely non-negotiable and which are the settled preferences of one camp wearing the clothes of requirements — I suspect several of the attorney's are, and several of the reformers' as well. Mark any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied: if mirroring the public and producing informed public judgment pull in opposite directions, say so plainly, and say which of the two has to carry the legitimacy, because that is the part I most need settled before the hearing. Close with the single requirement that excludes the greatest number of otherwise plausible accounts, and, briefly, how the main families of sortition design fare against the list.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations. I want the argument, not the scholarly apparatus; I will chase down the literature on my own afterwards.
