I sit on the board of a small community fund in a former mill town. For four years we've run a paper voucher thing — call them Kettle Notes — that about 35 local businesses accept, that we hand out as emergency assistance to households, and that any merchant can bring back to us and redeem one-for-one in cash out of our reserve account. At Tuesday's finance meeting two board members went at it over whether to renew, and I've been asked to write up my read for the rest of the board, which is basically an invitation to referee. Neither of them is a strawman, so I'd like a real adjudication rather than a tie-breaker platitude.

Owen spent thirty years as a credit union examiner. Dee wrote a dissertation on Berkeley and now teaches composition at the community college. Roughly in order:

1) Owen: kill it. The notes are redeemable at 100 cents on the dollar out of our own reserve, so every note in circulation is a dollar we already spent, and the merchants who accept them are the same merchants our grantmaking would have reached anyway. Purchasing power in this town has not increased. We relabeled a transfer and then spent another $40k a year on printing, bookkeeping, and counterfeit checks to make the relabeling feel special. It's a coupon with extra steps.

2) Dee: that's the exact mistake Berkeley built a whole pamphlet to attack — the Querist, hundreds of numbered questions about why Ireland stayed poor while sitting next to a rich country. She brought a handout. One query says money shouldn't be thought of as a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, and asks whether its true idea isn't "altogether that of a ticket or counter." Another asks whether there's any "vertue in gold or silver, other than as they set people at work, or create industry." Her point: you're asking how much purchasing power the notes contain, which is the wrong question — ask what they set in motion. Our logs show notes get spent heavily on Tuesday mornings and in January, i.e. into the bakery's dead hours and the shop's empty bays. If a note gets an idle oven to bake, that bread is wealth that didn't exist before, and no new dollar was needed to produce it.

3) Owen: then your Berkeley proves too much. On "whatever sets people working is money," a Monopoly board creates wealth whenever anyone trades on it and a punch card is a monetary system. Worse, there's no principled line between your notes and a Ponzi — both work by getting people to act on counters. Give me a constraint.

4) Dee: it's in the same handout. One query asks whether money isn't "only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour," and another says less money circulating fast is equivalent to more money circulating slow. So the counters have to be proportioned to actual labor, and they have to circulate among people who take each other's work as payment. A Ponzi's counters never command labor — they only ever command more counters, and the circulation ends at the issuer. Ours ends in bread, brake jobs, and haircuts.

5) Owen: ours also ends at the issuer. 61% of notes were redeemed last year, and redemption is where the counter has to be settled in hard money out of the reserve. Berkeley wanted the bullion gone and the denominations kept — there's a query about whether, "the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone," trade and industry could carry on anyway. Our notes fail his own test. If the reserve ran dry they'd be confetti inside a week, so the balance underneath is doing the real work after all.

6) Dee: he never said convertibility was worthless, he said it isn't the source of value — and he lost this fight in his own lifetime, since the paper national bank he pushed for Ireland was voted down in the early 1720s by people making approximately your argument. So the live question isn't who's right about money in the abstract, it's whether 35 merchants are a network whose trust in the counter sustains itself. And here's the actual move: get the city to take up to $50 of any parking ticket in notes. Notes paid to the city die there instead of coming back to us, redemption pressure drops, and the notes start looking like counters instead of gift certificates.

7) Owen: congratulations, you've converted a bounded charity program into a municipal quasi-currency with an implicit public guarantee. And your redemption rate is the only honest thermometer we have for whether merchants believe the notes. If you engineer that number down, you've smashed the thermometer and called it a cure.

Separately, and this is the part I can't resolve from the minutes: they're fighting about the 61%. Dee says it double-counts, because a note a merchant passes to her supplier and which is then redeemed shows up once as "redeemed" even though it did two transactions of work. Owen says our logs don't track note-level paths at all, so her correction is unbacked and possibly convenient.

So: go through it move by move and rate each of the seven — clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, clearly wrong — with a sentence or two each, not a paragraph. Then tell me (a) who wins on the conceptual question of what the notes are, (b) who wins on the practical question of whether to renew, and whether those are even the same question; (c) what the empirical crux really is and what we'd have to start collecting to settle it, since I suspect neither of them have it; and (d) what both of them are missing. I've read enough sequences-era stuff to smell a possible motte-and-bailey in Dee's use of Berkeley — money-as-counter when she's on offense, "well, convertibility still matters" when pressed — but I honestly can't tell whether that's fair or whether I'm just pattern-matching, and the symmetric worry is that "a coupon with extra steps" quietly assumes only dollars count as wealth. Related question I care about most: is Berkeley load-bearing for Dee at all, or is he a vivid way of saying something Owen could accept in his own ledger vocabulary? Be specific about which of her moves would survive if you deleted the 18th century.

I'm the swing vote and I'm not telling you which way I lean, partly because I dont know yet.
