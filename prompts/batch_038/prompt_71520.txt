I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for something like fifteen years, and I have a bad habit of trying to turn philosophical views into specifications — not because I expect to build them, but because the attempt to state a view precisely usually exposes exactly where the view has been doing work with vagueness. My current victim is Scanlonian contractualism's answer to the aggregation problem. I want to know what it would take to state that answer as something runnable: an input consisting of a finite set of individuals and a finite set of candidate allocations of a fixed budget, an output consisting of which allocations are permissible, and a rule for getting from one to the other that never sums welfare across persons. A friend of mine, a fairly orthodox EA type, insists this is a waste of time because contractualism is just utilitarianism with a veto, and I want to be able to answer him with a requirements list rather than with vibes. I've read the relevant chapter of What We Owe to Each Other twice and skimmed Parfit's argument that the view either collapses into consequentialism or goes empty, so please don't recap the debate; I want the spec.

So: write the desiderata that any contractualist theory of aggregation must meet to count as adequate — adequate both as moral philosophy and as something statable. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and be concrete about the cases (one ventilator for a patient who will otherwise die against ten thousand inhalers for asthmatics; a policy that certainly kills one person against a policy that gives each of a million people a one-in-a-million chance of dying; a choice where the affected population depends on the choice itself). I want the formal properties pinned down as well: whether the deliverable is a verdict about acts or about principles, whether the procedure must be complete or may legitimately return ties, whether it must be invariant under relabeling the individuals, whether totals are permitted to appear anywhere at all and if so where, what is supposed to make one person's complaint comparable to another's when there is no common cardinal scale to compare them on, whether complaints are assessed against the actual alternative chosen or against a distribution over outcomes, and how the account is meant to handle non-identity cases and populations of variable size. Then sort the requirements into the ones that are non-negotiable — drop them and you no longer have contractualism, you have consequentialism wearing a Kantian hat — and the ones that are really the contingent commitments of particular camps: the numbers skeptics, the moderate aggregators who let small harms sum up to some threshold, the weighted-lottery people, and those who relocate the whole question to complaints about risk rather than outcomes.

The part I most want is the impossibility result, if there is one. My working hypothesis is that the following three cannot all hold: the individualist restriction, understood as a flat ban on summing complaints across persons; determinacy in cases where the complaints on both sides are exactly equally strong, which most people want to deliver the result that the greater number should be saved; and some minimal adequacy condition ruling out the consequence that one person's death always outweighs any finite number of much smaller harms, no matter how many and no matter how bad the smaller harms get. Tell me whether that's the right triad or whether I've misdescribed the tension — if a different subset is the real choke point, say which, and say what it costs to give up each member. Also flag any requirement on my list or yours that turns out to be two requirements wearing one trenchcoat, and tell me which single requirement eliminates the largest share of the candidate views. No bibliography, no citations, no name-checking: I'd rather have your reconstruction than your reference list, and I'll chase down whoever I need myself. A two- or three-line scorecard of the main contenders against your list at the very end is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on the scoring.
