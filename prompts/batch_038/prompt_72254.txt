I TA a first-year grad metaontology seminar this term and my prof asked me to put together a handout for the week after we wrap the composition unit, mostly because half the class has started using "realist" and "that's just a verbal dispute" as insults and nobody can say what they'd actually have to believe to end up on one side or the other. So I want to build a decision tree that sorts people by what they'd say about concrete cases, not by what they call themselves. The entry point should be the usual one — are there tables, or only simples arranged tablewise — but every node after that has to be a question somebody can answer without already knowing any of the labels: no jargon like quantifier variance, joint-carving, or elite expressions at the branch points, those are only allowed to show up at the leaves (if at all). Something like seven to nine nodes and six to eight leaves, each leaf getting a one-sentence statement of the commitment it's most embarassed by, and if a leaf doesn't have a name anybody actually uses, say so rather than coining one.

Here's what I need covered, in whatever arrangement makes sense to you: the Carnapian who thinks the external question is a practical choice of framework once the internal questions are settled; the Hirsch-style view that the two sides mean different things by "exists" and so each is right in her own mouth; the Chalmers-style diagnosis that runs the dispute through the rephrase-it-without-the-disputed-word test and calls it verbal if no disagreement survives, bedrock if some does; the Sider-style view that there's one objective, structure-marking sense of the existential quantifier and the composition question is a hard substantive matter with a right answer; the easy-ontology view on which the question gets settled trivially by uncontroversial inference from premises both sides already grant; a first-order realist leaf for the person who thinks the answer is that only organisms and simples compose anything; and ontological pluralism, which I suspect your tree is going to struggle with — be honest about whether it gets a leaf of its own or collapses into something else. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate. My guess going in is that Hirsch and Chalmers will look identical from the inside, and that the Sider-style realist and the organisms-only realist will share every branch until the very last question, but I'd rather you show where the separation fails than paper over it.

Two extras. First, a small grid: take whichever three or four yes/no questions do the real work in your tree, use them as axes, and mark the empty cells, diagnosing each one — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just nobody's gotten around to defending it yet. Second, and this is the part I actually care about: rerun the whole tree on a different dispute, your pick between whether there are races and whether there are holes, whichever behaves better under the questions, and tell me which leaves end up with different occupants and why. My hunch is that the Sider/Hirsch part of the tree is secretly calibrated on composition, where both sides agree about every arrangement-of-simples fact and only disagree about the bookkeeping, and that on something like race the same nodes stop sorting people because there the empirical and political facts aren't held fixed on both sides. If that hunch is wrong, tell me it's wrong — the handout is only worth handing out if it survives being applied twice.
