I am in the third week of a graduate seminar devoted to the engineering and governance of frontier AI systems, and our current unit turns on the distinction between "control" approaches — which try to prevent harm even if the deployed system is misaligned or actively deceptive — and "alignment" approaches, which try to make the system's own dispositions benign. For a short response paper I want to stress-test a scoring scheme I have assembled myself, and I would like you to apply the scheme as stated rather than to substitute a better-known framework of your own.

The scheme works as follows. For each control measure listed below, assign two numbers between 0 and 1.

Opacity-resilience: the extent to which the measure's safety property continues to hold under the pessimistic assumption that the system is scheming, is aware that it is under evaluation, and can reason about the measure itself. A score of 1 means the measure's effectiveness is essentially independent of what the system wants or believes; a score of 0 means it collapses the moment the system understands it and objects to it.

Deployment corrosion: the extent to which routine use of the measure tends to make the overall arrangement less safe over time — for example by giving the system practice at concealment, by opening channels the system can exploit, or by fostering operator complacency. A score of 0 means no such corrosive tendency; a score of 1 means the measure plausibly undermines itself through ordinary use.

Then report trust-free robustness as opacity-resilience minus corrosion, and arrange the measures into a tier list (S, A, B, C) according to that composite.

The measures to be scored are all to be evaluated in one fixed setting: a single frontier model that has been granted tool access (code execution, web browsing, and outbound email) and is operated continuously by a commercial research team under schedule pressure.

1. Air-gapped deployment with all input and output mediated by a human operator.
2. Output filtering and content classification applied to everything the model produces.
3. Canary or tripwire probes: hidden test stimuli whose reappearance in the model's ordinary behavior would indicate that it is generalizing from an evaluation context.
4. Monitoring by a weaker, separately trained overseer model that reviews the deployed model's transcripts.
5. Capability restrictions enforced at the tool layer — allowlists, rate limits, sandboxed execution environments.
6. Interpretability-based auditing, such as probing internal activations for features associated with deception.
7. Adversarial red-teaming and elicitation testing conducted before release.
8. Training for corrigibility, in which the model is rewarded for accepting interruption and shutdown and for reporting its own uncertainty.
9. Restricting the model to a narrow query interface with a strict budget on how many questions may be put to it per day.
10. Sandbagging detection via systematic comparison of performance across differing contexts.

A few requirements for your answer. Report the two component scores for every measure before giving the composite, and justify each with one or two sentences — I want calibration, not exposition. Then identify the single measure whose tier you think would move furthest if we dropped the assumption that the model is scheming, and explain in a sentence why it is that one. Finally, assess whether the linear subtraction in my composite is defensible at all: if you think opacity-resilience and corrosion interact in a way that makes a simple difference misleading, propose a replacement rule, apply it, re-rank, and say explicitly which measures changed tier and which did not.
