Ok, some background first, because I have zero academic training in any of this — I work nights at a grocery distribution warehouse, mostly making sure pallets get on the right trucks — and I fell down a rabbit hole about a year ago after half-watching a documentary on the 1943 Bengal famine and then not being able to stop thinking about it. I got Amartya Sen's book on famines out of the library and slogged maybe a third of the way through it, plus a lot of odds and ends online about Ireland in the 1840s and about the Great Leap Forward. My sister actually studied philosophy and when I laid my views out to her she said "those can't all be true together" and then wouldn't say which one breaks, which is infuriating. So I'm going to write them all down and you can be less coy than her.

Here's the list. (1) Famines are never natural disasters — a blight or a cyclone can wreck a harvest, but whether people actually starve to death is a separate question about who is permitted to get at the food that's sitting there. (2) So a famine is a political crime, and I mean that pretty literally, not as a figure of speech. (3) But obviously not every awful thing that happens is a crime, so I came up with my own test, which I call the signpost test: to count as a crime you have to be able to stick a signpost at a fork in the road — a date, an identifiable person or body that really could have decided, and a choice they made where the other fork was open and the cost of taking it was one they could have carried. If the best you can do is gesture at "the market" or "the system" or "nobody in particular," it's a tragedy, not a crime. (4) Rider on (3): you work out whether a cost was carriable by looking at what that same decider was already spending money and effort on that mattered less. (5) Bengal passes the signpost test easily. (6) Ireland passes it too, because food kept leaving the country while people inside it were dying. (7) The Great Leap Forward is the worst case of the lot and passes obviously. (8) I also hold Sen's line that famines basically don't happen in countries with real elections and a press allowed to report freely, because the people in charge get thrown out, and I want to keep that as an exceptionless rule, not a tendency. (9) And I don't think Ireland counts against (8), since the starving Irish weren't really the people that government had to answer to.

So: which of these can't all stand up?
