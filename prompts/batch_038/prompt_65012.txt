I am a fourth-year statistics PhD student. My dissertation concerns hierarchical models for aggregating forecasts from sources whose reliability is unknown, heterogeneous, and only partially observable: each source carries a latent skill parameter, I place a prior over the population of skills, and I obtain a working prediction by integrating that parameter out. For the past two years I have also sat in a Sanskrit reading group working through Nyāya epistemology, and I am preparing a talk for our formal epistemology group in which I want to test a suspicion of mine: that the Nyāya insistence on testimony as an independent means of knowledge, irreducible to inference, is either a substantive claim my own framework cannot express, or a distinction that dissolves once it is stated probabilistically. I would like you to help me determine which, by translating rather than by summarizing either side.

Here is the position I want translated, in my own crude rendering. Please correct the rendering first if it is wrong or too flat, and then translate the corrected version rather than mine. The Naiyāyikas enumerate four means of valid knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, and verbal testimony. Testimony is characterized as the utterance of a reliable speaker — one who knows the matter at first hand and has no intention to mislead — and it is held to yield knowledge in its own right. Buddhist epistemologists and the materialists deny this, arguing that what looks like knowledge from a speaker's word is really inference: the hearer infers the content from the utterance together with the speaker's reliability. The Nyāya reply, as I understand it, is structural rather than empirical: in inference the knower must be aware of a reason and must grasp that the reason is pervaded by what is to be proved, whereas in understanding a speaker nothing of the sort occurs — the hearer is aware only of the content, and comes to know it directly. There is in addition a set of conditions on the comprehension of a sentence itself: mutual expectancy among the words, semantic fitness of the combination, contiguity of the words in time, and a fourth condition concerning the speaker's intended meaning, which the Mīmāṃsakas disputed. These are treated as part of the account of how testimony produces understanding.

Now the target vocabulary, in which I am entirely fluent: latent parameters and priors over them, likelihoods, marginalization and integration over nuisance parameters, exchangeability, identifiability, sufficiency, conditional independence, calibration, posterior predictive distributions, and the standing fact that for many of the questions I model I will never observe a ground-truth label. Restate the Nyāya position in those terms, item by item: the fourfold enumeration, the characterization of the reliable speaker, the irreducibility thesis, the structural reply about awareness of a reason, and the comprehension conditions.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the main deliverable rather than as an afterthought. For each item, say whether it (a) translates cleanly, (b) translates only with distortion — and what the distortion costs — or (c) has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Two places I want pressed specifically. First: whether integrating the reliability parameter out of a hierarchical model, so that the posterior is a function of the utterance alone, is a genuine counterpart to the claim that testimonial knowledge involves no awareness of a reason, or only a behavioral mimicry of it — and what turns on the difference between a model that represents reliability and one in which reliability has been marginalized away. Second: whether my framework can express at all the difference between testimony producing knowledge and testimony producing a high posterior, given that I have no factive operator and treat every belief change as inference by construction. For each residue item I want a verdict: is this a defect of my vocabulary, something a probabilistic epistemology structurally cannot say, or a sign that the Nyāya distinction was less substantive than it appeared — an artifact of not having the notion of a latent variable?

Work briefly in the reverse direction as well. Which features of my own framework have no Nyāya counterpart — unidentified reliability, correlated bias across sources, the possibility of being well calibrated and systematically wrong — and does their absence mark a limit of the Nyāya account, or is it addressed there by other means, such as the conditions placed on the speaker or the examination of claims in formal debate?

Two constraints on your answer. Do not smuggle my assumptions in and call it translation: if rendering the reliable speaker as a source with a favorable likelihood parameter already presupposes that all epistemic warrant is probabilistic, flag that at the step where it happens, rather than presenting it as a neutral mapping and then announcing that the reduction has been established. And give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no names of contemporary authors; I want the argument, not the apparatus.
