I am a second-year graduate student, and our early modern proseminar has now spent parts of three consecutive semesters on the same handful of sentences in Berkeley — the challenge, issued in the Principles and then staged again between Hylas and Philonous in the Three Dialogues, to conceive of a tree standing in a park, or books shut in a closet, existing all the while unperceived, together with the inference that the very attempt shows that whatever is conceived exists in a mind. Everyone in the room knows the standard reply by heart: conceiving of an unperceived tree is still conceiving of it, so at best the passage establishes that nothing can be conceived as unconceived, which is either trivial or a slide on the word conceived. Everyone also knows that this does not close the case, since a second camp holds that the reply misreads what Berkeley means by framing an idea in the first place. Three years on, nobody has moved, and the exchange has become a ritual we perform at the start of each term.

Concretely, there are three positions in the room. My professor's is that the argument is invalid, or valid only on a reading that assumes exactly what the materialist denies, and that its endurance in the literature reflects a failure of nerve rather than any depth in the reasoning. A classmate of mine, a former litigator who is relentless about this, holds that the argument is valid and the objection simply confused: on his account, Berkeley's talk of conceiving is a success term, so that a genuine act of conception delivers an object actually present to the mind, and the materialist who claims to conceive an unperceived tree has either framed no idea at all or has smuggled in a perceiver — herself, or, on Berkeley's own resources, God. A third view surfaces every year and never gains traction: that Berkeley never intended the passage to establish immaterialism at all, but only to expose the unintelligibility of a material substratum, in which case both of the first two camps are arguing about a conclusion he was not drawing. My own hypothesis, sketched so far only on the back of a handout, is that the standoff is really about which of two things a conception is. If conceiving is a relation to an object, then inconceivability carries no modal weight beyond the psychological, and the materialist's challenge never gets off the ground. If conceiving is an ability to entertain a content, then one can perfectly well conceive that a tree exists unperceived without thereby perceiving the tree, and the argument requires an unstated bridge from inconceivability to impossibility that Berkeley never supplies. On that picture each camp selects the reading of conceive that yields the verdict it already wants, and the texts are too brief to adjudicate between them.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on Berkeley. What kind of disagreement is this: a dispute about the logic of a piece of reasoning, a dispute about what an author meant, a dispute about the nature of conception conducted under an exegetical cover, or several of these tangled together? Do the three positions genuinely contradict one another, or are they answering different questions under the single heading of whether the master argument works — for instance, questions about validity, about interpretive charity, and about philosophical interest? I am especially curious whether the third position is a live rival or a red herring, and whether the imagistic-versus-propositional reading of framing an idea could be settled independently of the verdict each side wants, say from Berkeley's own treatment of ideas and of the notions we have of spirits. Finally, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction that dissolves the standoff, a test that fixes the reading of conceive without presupposing the conclusion, a reframing of the question the argument is meant to answer — or whether none should be expected, and why. I want the diagnosis argued for step by step; a survey of how the passage has been received or a list of who defended what is precisely what I am not after.
