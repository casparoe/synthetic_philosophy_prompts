In three weeks I present a fifteen-minute case, followed by forty-five minutes of discussion, to the review panel of a funder's pilot programme on invertebrate welfare in agriculture. The panel is small and specific, and I want to be prepared for them rather than for the audience I usually get.

The panel:

1. An insect neuroethologist who has spent her career on nociceptive withdrawal and central processing in dipteran larvae, and who is impatient with anyone who slides from "responds adaptively to noxious stimuli" to "suffers."
2. A population ecologist whose instinct is that insect numbers are so vast and so density-regulated that per-capita welfare is close to meaningless inside his models, and that most insect mortality is background mortality that would have happened anyway.
3. An animal welfare scientist working on vertebrate models, broadly sympathetic to my conclusion, but who holds that no policy recommendation is admissible without validated welfare indicators — behavioural, physiological, or preference-based.
4. A philosopher of biology, generally sceptical of behaviour-to-phenomenology inferences and allergic to the idea that a functional marker can license a claim about experience.
5. The programme officer: an agricultural economist focused on opportunity cost, perverse incentives, and whether a recommendation survives being wrong by two orders of magnitude.

The thesis I will state in my first two minutes, and which they should be assumed to have read in summary:

- Expected disvalue of a practice = P(sentience) × N(individuals affected) × severity, where severity is a three-point score tied to duration of harm and to whether the harm terminates the animal's life.
- P(sentience) is set by markers — behavioural flexibility, trade-off decisions against noxious stimuli, learning, evidence of centralised integration — with no requirement of vertebrate-like neuroanatomy. For the taxa in my case I use credences between 0.05 and 0.35.
- I deliberately do not aggregate linearly in N. In the worked version I apply a concave transform, N^0.5, precisely to defuse the "sheer numbers swamp everything" reductio.
- I reject precaution ("if in doubt, refrain") as the operative decision rule; I want explicit arithmetic that can be interrogated parameter by parameter.
- Applied to two practices — broad-spectrum insecticide use in row crops, and industrial rearing of larvae for feed — my own figures, after the concave transform, still rank at least one of them above several vertebrate-welfare interventions in the same portfolio.

What I need is a prep sheet, not a defence of my view. Please give me the eight to ten objections this particular panel will actually raise, ordered by likelihood of being voiced multiplied by damage to the proposal if I fumble it. Attribute each to the panelist most likely to make it and phrase it the way that panelist would phrase it: the ecologist will not say "utility monster," and the welfare scientist will not say "the hard problem." For each objection, supply a reply I can deliver in roughly ninety seconds, and a candid note on where that reply is soft — which assumption it is quietly leaning on, and whether leaning on it is defensible in front of this room.

Then add three further items:

- What I should concede during the opening two minutes, so that the concession cannot later be wielded as a gotcha — and what I must not concede, because the whole proposal collapses without it.
- The single reply that has to land for the funding recommendation to survive the discussion.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks: the place where my own parameter choices — the exponent on N, the three-point severity scale, the width of the credence range — are most exposed. Tell me honestly whether being forced to answer it would be fatal, merely embarrassing, or survivable if I handle it a particular way.
