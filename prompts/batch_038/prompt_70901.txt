I'm in a monthly forecasting circle, and for the next session each of us has to write down our actual method for long-horizon, historical-scale questions — the kind where you're asking whether some big institution or great power is still standing in fifty years. Mine is below. I wrote it out honestly and I know some of it doesnt sit right with the rest, but I can't tell which pieces are genuinely incompatible and which ones just feel strange sitting next to each other. Can you audit the set for me?

1. For these questions you start with the outside view: find a reference class of comparable cases, work out the base rate for the outcome in that class, and only then adjust away from it using what you know about this particular case. Big adjustments need big reasons.
2. The most common failure in forecasting is doing that in reverse — building a vivid story about why this case is special and using it to land on a number nowhere near the base rate.
3. But for great powers specifically the historical sample is tiny. However many hegemonic or imperial powers you count over the last five hundred years, it's a dozen at most, and nothing you compute from a dozen cases means anything. When a historian announces that eight of the last eleven empires broke apart within forty years of overextending themselves, I think that's numerology in a nice jacket.
4. Also, the class itself is basically up for grabs — reference class tennis. Almost any case can be sorted into a class where the base rate is 90% or one where it's 5%, and the person doing the sorting usually knows which one they're aiming for before they start.
5. That said, I do use the Copernican duration rule that Gott popularized: if something has already lasted T years, expect it to last somewhere between T/3 and 3T more, at 50% confidence. I've applied it to alliances, to constitutions, to the postwar international order, to a couple of companies.
6. I also think we're in a real structural break — nuclear weapons, the demographic transition, globally integrated finance, now AI — so pre-1945 cases just aren't in the same reference class as today's great powers. Different generative process, different base rate.
7. A forecast only counts if it's a sharp single number on a question that resolves cleanly. Wide hedged ranges and "honestly it's deeply uncertain" are a cop-out.
8. What separates good forecasters from credentialed experts in tournaments, as far as I can tell, is calibration plus incremental updating, and I pride myself on moving my numbers regularly as new information comes in.
9. "This time is different" is the most expensive phrase in the business, and most expert arguments for why it is are motivated reasoning.

For texture, here's what happened when I tried to use all of this at once. My question was whether the UN Security Council still has the same five permanent members in 2075. Step 1 handed me a base rate I didn't trust, step 6 then told me the class was basically empty, so I fell back on step 5, got a range, and step 7 forced me to collapse the range into one number. I submitted 35% and I could not defend it against 15% or 60%.
