I'm auditing a philosophy of biology seminar this term, mostly to keep my options open for a rotation, and I have to hand in a two-page response by Friday. The move I keep hearing — from two different people now, so I don't think it's just one confident guy — is that an organism is like a whirlpool in a river. The water passing through it is never the same twice, the shape holds only because of what flows through it, and if you ask exactly where the whirlpool ends and the river begins there's no fact of the matter. My lab-mate uses it to argue that the whole "is a virus alive" fight is a fake dispute: a whirlpool is just a stable pattern in a flow, so the only sensible question is how strongly and how completely something maintains itself, and that's always a matter of degree. She also says the NASA-ish definition — something about a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution — is the wrong kind of thing altogether, like trying to define a whirlpool by listing what's in the water.

My objection so far is weak, which is why I'm asking. Best I've got: a whirlpool that stops spinning is just water again, but a frozen tardigrade in a museum drawer or a seed sitting in a vault has had its flow stopped for decades and we still want to say it's the same organism waiting to resume. Also her version slides in that a hurricane "reproduces" by throwing off smaller storms, and I don't buy that the little ones are offspring of the big one. But I can tell these read as nitpicks and she'd just wave them off, and I'd rather not show up with nitpicks.

So can you do the actual audit for me? Give me the mapping explicitly — a table is fine — of what plays the role of the water, the flow, the basin, the shape, the persistence conditions, and the boundary. Then the disanalogies, but sorted into seperate piles: which ones genuinely threaten the use she's putting the thing to, and which ones are real differences that don't matter because nothing in the argument rides on them. I especially want to know what the whirlpool story quietly imports that organisms lack, or the reverse — something about parts serving the whole, about error-correction and history, about whether there's anywhere a ledger is kept of what happened to the thing. Then tell me whether "it's all a matter of degree, so the virus question dissolves" survives once those are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying that conclusion the whole time. Last bit: some people in the same seminar use a flame instead of a whirlpool and I honestly can't tell if that's cosmetic. Do flame and whirlpool license different conclusions about viruses, about a seed, about where an organism stops and its environment starts? And if there's a better source domain for what she's trying to say, what would it be and what would it cost her to switch to it?
