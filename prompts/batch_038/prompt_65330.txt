I have no academic training and have never taken a course in anthropology or philosophy. I ran a dispatch office for a small regional freight carrier for twenty-two years, and since retiring I have been reading slowly and irregularly about the transition from foraging to farming, and about the recurring argument that this transition made human life worse. I have written a draft essay of roughly a thousand words. It is complete enough to judge, and it is below. I would rather have substantive criticism than encouragement.

---

**The Wrong Word: Agriculture as a Ratchet Rather Than a Mistake**

A well-known magazine essay from 1987 argued that farming was the worst mistake in the history of the human race. The case it made rests largely on bones and teeth, and I found the case persuasive. I did not find the word persuasive. This essay is about the word.

A mistake requires a mistake-maker and a moment of error, and agriculture has neither. It arose independently in at least six or seven regions — the Fertile Crescent, the Yellow and Yangtze river valleys, Mesoamerica, the Andes and the Amazon basin, eastern North America, New Guinea, parts of West Africa — over several thousand years, and no one signed anything. Domestication also creeps up on both of its parties rather than being adopted: a weed colonizing disturbed ground around a camp, a weed that gets spared, a weed that gets sown, a seed head that does not shatter and therefore stays in the hand. So when people say that agriculture was a mistake, they cannot mean that somebody erred. They mean something else, and I want to say what.

I propose four words where one is usually used. A *trade* is a change both of whose endpoints the person making it can roughly compare: you know what you are giving up and what you are getting. A *trap* is a change whose individual steps are locally acceptable — each one better than, or no worse than, the last by the standards of the person taking it — but whose cumulative result is worse, and from which retreat costs more than staying. A *ratchet* is a trap whose lock is not the payoff but the population that the payoff supports. A *transformation* is a change that alters the scale on which better and worse are measured, so that the two sides cannot be compared at all.

Agriculture, on the evidence as I read it, was a ratchet. Two premises do the work. First: farming feeds more people from a given area of land at a higher cost in labor per person and on a narrower diet. Second: a foraging life holds its own numbers down by means that are tied to mobility — long birth intervals, a camp that moves, and in hard seasons the exposure of infants. Once a settlement is fixed and its granaries are full, births come closer together and more of them survive. It follows that the third generation of a farming village never faced the question *forage or farm*. It faced the question *farm at this population, or lose much of this population*. The comparison the pessimists want to draw, between the foragers who were there and the farmers who replaced them, was never a comparison available to anyone living inside the change.

There is a second lock, and I am not sure I need it. Competence erodes. What a foraging group knows is not general cleverness but a dense local calendar: which slope, which week, which plant, which herd moves where. That calendar is rebuilt every generation by walking it, and three generations of sowing and weeding will not preserve it. A village that wanted to go back might therefore be unable to, quite apart from its numbers. I flag this paragraph as possibly decorative, because I suspect the demographic lock alone carries the argument.

The part I most want examined is the part I believe I worked out myself. It is agreed on all sides that surplus made inequality possible. I think this is not quite right: surplus is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. What matters is surplus that can be audited. A tax is an assessment, and an assessment requires that the thing assessed be (1) synchronized, ripening all at once, so the collector knows when to come; (2) visible above ground, so it cannot be hidden; (3) storable, so the collected share does not rot before the state can use it; (4) divisible and measurable in some standard unit; and (5) transportable, so it can be moved to a storehouse. Cereal grains satisfy all five. Tubers and root crops satisfy almost none: they can be left in the ground, lifted when convenient, and eaten where they stand. Herds satisfy some, but they walk, they die, and they are awkward to count. If this is right, the deepest fact about the origin of states is not that there was more food but that food became a thing you could put a number on — and once a thing has a number, someone will come to collect a fraction of it.

I should be honest about what I concede. The skeletons favor the pessimists: early farmers in most studied regions are shorter than the foragers they replaced, with more tooth decay, more enamel defects, more evidence of specific nutritional deficiencies, and more crowded infectious disease, much of it plausibly arriving with the animals kept close at hand. I also concede that the famous claim that foragers worked fifteen hours a week rests on one study of one group in one favorable period and excluded food preparation; a fuller accounting of acquisition plus processing raises the figure a great deal, though I believe it still lands below what most farming populations managed. I concede that killing did not wait for farming: there is a cemetery in the Nile Valley, thousands of years older than agriculture, whose skeletons carry embedded projectile points, though I have read that the current interpretation is a series of raids and ambushes rather than a single battle, which weakens the word *warfare* without weakening the fact of violence. And I concede that the great carved-pillar site on a hilltop in southeastern Turkey is older than established farming in its region, though I have also read more recent work suggesting people lived there and processed cereals, which may weaken the use I would like to make of it.

The hardest problem for my own view is this. Suppose the pessimists are right about the ten thousand years and wrong about the direction. Agriculture made cities, writing, bookkeeping, medicine, and the world I am sitting in, which by any standard I actually hold is better than the foraging world. Does a bad road with a good destination count as a mistake? My answer is that everything turns on whether you count lives or count the quality of a life, and that the two diverge sharply here, since farming supported vastly more people each of whom was, for much of the period, worse off than a forager would have been. There is a further point I cannot phrase cleanly. The people who benefited from agriculture would not have existed without it, so the benefit cannot be credited to them as a gain over an alternative in which they were foragers; there would have been no them. I suspect this point already has a name and a literature and that I have arrived at it from the side. I want to know whether it survives scrutiny or whether it is the sort of thing that sounds profound and dissolves when touched.

So my claim is narrow. Not that farming was good, and not that it was a mistake: that it was a ratchet, made of locally acceptable steps, a cumulative result nobody chose, and a lock held by population rather than by preference. And the question worth asking is therefore not whether it was a mistake but whether it is still one — because the same structure seems to describe several changes I watched inside my own working life, changes where every step was sensible and the option to undo them was quietly removed by the size of the thing they built.

**What I would like from you**

1. Accuracy. Go through the empirical claims and flag every one that is wrong, outdated, contested in a way I have not registered, or stated with more confidence than the evidence supports. Point to the sentence. If a claim is simply false, say false rather than noting that the picture is complex. The number of independent domestication centers, the stature and dental evidence, the work-hours figure, the Nile Valley cemetery, the hilltop site, and the claim that early infectious disease came substantially from domesticated animals are the places I most expect trouble.

2. The taxonomy. Are *mistake*, *trap*, *ratchet*, and *transformation* four real categories or four words for two? Is a ratchet just a species of trap? Is *transformation* a genuine category, or an escape hatch that lets me declare a comparison impossible whenever the comparison is inconvenient for me? If the scheme collapses, tell me which single distinction is worth keeping.

3. The central inference. The fourth paragraph is the load-bearing wall. Does it actually establish that no one faced the forage-or-farm choice? My worry is that in some regions foraging remained viable for centuries alongside farming, and that some groups took up cultivation and later dropped it. If that is right, my irreversibility claim is too strong. How much of the rest of the essay survives that correction, and what is the weakest step in the argument as written?

4. The auditability argument. Is it original, obvious, or already well known? Is the five-condition list the right decomposition, or does one condition secretly do all the work — I suspect transportability and storability may collapse into each other? Are there counterexamples: stratified, unequal, taxing societies built on food that fails the test, or long-surplus societies that never developed the state?

5. The eighth paragraph. Does counting lives versus counting the quality of a life really decide the question, or have I described a dilemma without resolving it? And is the point about beneficiaries who would not otherwise have existed a real objection or a cheap one?

6. Interest, bluntly. Is there anything here worth a stranger's time, or have I written a fluent restatement of a familiar argument? If you had to cut the essay to half its length, which paragraphs go?

7. Rank the three most serious problems in the essay, worst first, and say for each whether it is a factual problem or a structural one.

One constraint on your reply: no reading lists, no citations, no titles, no author names, and no suggestions about where to look. I cannot look anything up, and being sent somewhere I cannot go is useless to me. Tell me what you think in your own words. Where you are genuinely unsure, mark it as unsure instead of dressing it up in confident language; where you are confident that I have something wrong, say so plainly. And if you think the central thesis is false, argue against it directly rather than presenting me with a balanced menu of positions.
