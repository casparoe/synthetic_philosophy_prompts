I spent three hours last night arguing with a friend about Scandinavian prisons, and I came away suspecting that his case is more rhetorically effective than it is sound. Here is the summary he kept returning to, and which he says is just the standard description of the Norwegian system:

"Norway's official position is that it does not punish people at all — the only punishment is the loss of liberty, so everything inside is supposed to resemble ordinary life as closely as possible. That is what Halden is: no bars on the cell windows, unarmed officers who cook, play sports, and record music with the prisoners, a campus that looks more like a college than a prison. It is restorative justice instead of punishment, and the statistics settle the matter — roughly 20 percent of released Norwegian prisoners reoffend within two years, against more than 70 percent of released American prisoners within five. So humane treatment demonstrably reduces crime, and brutality demonstrably increases it. American cruelty is not merely immoral, it is self-defeating, which means there is no genuine trade-off to weigh: if we built Halden-style prisons here, we would cut reoffending by about two-thirds."

I share his politics and I want his conclusion to be true, which is precisely why I do not trust my own judgment about the argument. Please act as a philosopher of punishment who is equally at home with the methodology of comparative criminology, and go through this claim by claim rather than by topic. For each stretch, tell me what an accurate defender of the Norwegian model would accept, what is a fair popular simplification, what subtly misstates either the doctrine or the evidence, and what simply does not follow. Four things I especially want resolved: whether the doctrine really holds that imprisonment is not punishment, or something importantly different; whether "restorative justice" is being used in anything like its technical sense, and if not, what work the substitution is doing; what would have to be true for those two recidivism figures to be comparable at all, and what a serious comparison would have to hold fixed or explicitly acknowledge as uncontrolled; and whether the closing inference — transplant the architecture, recover two-thirds of the effect — rests on premises the summary never states.

Think it through carefully before writing. If it helps, first sketch the strongest honest version of his case, and then say where that version breaks down. Where your uncertainty is factual rather than conceptual, flag it plainly instead of supplying a plausible-sounding number. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: which consideration, if it turned out to be true, would make you judge the summary substantially fair, and at which point in your own reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
