So I keep coming back to Portugal's drug law and I can't quite figure out what the machinery commits you to once you actually build it.

Roughly as I understand it: since 2001, being caught with a small amount of anything — heroin, cocaine, cannabis, up to about ten days' personal supply — is not a crime. But it isn't nothing either. The cops confiscate and refer you to a regional Commission for the Dissuasion of Drug Addiction, a three-person panel of a legal professional, a health professional and a social worker. They sit down with you, go over your use, and can do anything from a warning and dropping the case, to a fine or community service, to steering you into treatment. They're supposed to handle the kid who took MDMA twice at a festival differently from someone who's actually dependent. The name of the body is doing a lot of work: talking you out of it is the job description.

Meanwhile nobody gets a letter for being a heavy drinker. Alcohol, which does more aggregate damage on almost any measure, is handled with taxes, age limits, advertising rules — population-level price and friction, no individual panels.

What theory is the commission running on? Specifically: does a mandatory conversation aimed at dissuasion treat the user as a competent agent who's short on information, or as someone whose preferences are themselves suspect — use as a symptom of broken preference-formation rather than a preference? If it's the first, what does the panel add that a tax wouldn't? If it's the second, why isn't it a crime? And what does the three-profession composition presuppose about who gets to adjudicate a person's own good?

Also tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out — a Millian experiments-in-living view, a straight prohibitionist censure view, a licensed-retail consumer view — and be honest about underdetermination: separate what the machinery strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit, and name the theories that could run the identical panel for totally different reasons. Then say what would have to change if you swapped in an explicit consumer-sovereignty model: which pieces survive, which can't, and what the current setup was actually buying that we'd lose.

My own hunch is that the whole thing is a compromise that secretly assumes use is a symptom, and that's what lets it be gentle and compulsory at once. Could easily be wrong. Say how confident you are in the reconstruction and flag the single claim in it you'd be least surprised to see turn out false.
