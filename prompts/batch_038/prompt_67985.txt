I am a second-year philosophy PhD student working on market ethics, and I am trying to get clear about a family of exchanges I have taken to calling moral trades: transactions in which what one party supplies is not an ordinary good or service but a moral performance or forbearance — abstaining from an act the payer thinks wrong, performing an act the payer thinks good or required, or deferring to the payer's values rather than one's own. Two discussions that rarely speak to each other seem to bear on this: the commodification debate over whether market norms crowd out the attitudes appropriate to certain goods, and the more technical suggestion that agents who hold different moral views can each advance their own values by purchasing the other's compliance. What I want is to know whether a single principle governs when such a trade is permissible, when it is impermissible, and when it is permissible but degrading — I take that three-way structure seriously and would like any candidate principle to reproduce it rather than flatten it into a binary. Below are my own verdicts, arrived at by intuition and argued over with friends; I suspect that some of them are mutually inconsistent.

1. I pay a pork supplier a premium to keep sows on straw with room to turn rather than in crates. Fine — good, in fact, and I would encourage others to do it.
2. A man tells me he will smash my bicycle unless I pay him fifty dollars, and I pay. Not fine. This has the same shape as 1 — money buys forbearance from a wrong — but I would rather report him, and I think paying is presumptively wrong rather than merely imprudent.
3. I pay my brother two hundred dollars for every visit he makes to our mother in her nursing home. Not fine; it degrades him and it degrades the visits.
4. I pay the neighbor's teenage daughter two hundred dollars to visit my mother and read to her. Fine, and good.
5. I sell my vote for a hundred dollars. Not fine. Yet donating that hundred dollars to a campaign whose advertisements may change other people's votes is fine, and so is lobbying: the line I am drawing is clearly not about money touching politics.
6. A firm keeps emitting and purchases carbon offsets. Here I go back and forth. I do not think the firm acts wrongly, but I think something has gone wrong — that the purchase buys absolution and thereby licenses the emitting.
7. A firm keeps selling sugary drinks and pays the excise tax on every bottle. Fine, with no residue of unease whatsoever, even though 6 and 7 strike me as structurally the same transaction.
8. I think eating animals is a serious wrong and you think it morally neutral; you think long-haul flying is a serious wrong and I think it mildly regrettable. You go vegetarian, I stop flying, and each of us has bought the other's compliance with a value we hold more strongly than they do. Fine — and I think such trades are badly underused.
9. A pharmacist who believes dispensing emergency contraception is wrong accepts money to dispense it anyway. Not fine: she sells her conscience and I buy it, though I concede the patients are better served. The same pharmacist accepting the same money to hire a colleague who will dispense it is fine.
10. A family pays a kidnapper's ransom to recover a child. Fine, I think, even though it funds and encourages further kidnapping — and yet I think it is not fine as a standing policy for a shipping company to adopt toward its crews.

What I am after is a principle, or a small competing set of them, stated precisely enough to be applied to a new case — conditions and their scope, not slogans — with each clause tied to the case that earns it. Tell me which of the pairs above it separates cleanly and which it cannot, and in particular what it makes of 6 and 7, since if my unease there is not tracking anything real I am willing to abandon it. Then apply it to cases I have not adjudicated: a state paying a logging community not to clear its rainforest, which I suspect is fine but which looks worryingly like 2; a clinic paying a stipend to a woman with six children to adopt long-acting contraception, which I cannot place between 1 and 5; and a university paying a student newspaper to kill a story. Add one case of your own choosing — ideally one where you expect the principle to deliver a verdict I would find counterintuitive — and say what it delivers. Finally, be explicit about the cost: if the only principle that saves all ten verdicts is baroque, say so, and give me the simpler alternative together with the verdict it forces me to retract. If something has to go, I would rather lose 5 than 3, but argue with me about that rather than accepting it. Please do not give me a bibliography or a survey of positions in the literature; I know the standard moves and will handle the references myself. End by telling me how confident you are in the principle you favor, and which single claim in your answer you are least sure of.
