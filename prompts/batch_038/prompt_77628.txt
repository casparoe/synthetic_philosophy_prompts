I'm giving a 40-minute seminar paper in a few weeks to what is basically a behavioral-science-of-morality unit: mostly moral psychologists who run vignette and survey studies, two cultural-evolution people who explain norm change through prestige bias and institutional incentives, one economist who does willingness-to-pay work for environmental agencies, and a couple of philosophers of social science whose whole thing is measurement and construct validity. Nobody there does normative ethics and nobody is a Singer specialist, so I get maybe ninety seconds to introduce the expanding-circle picture before I have to start swinging at it. Also worth knowing: my coauthor thinks the title oversells, and I've been arguing with a friend for a week about whether my view is just moral licensing dressed up as methodology.

The thesis is this. Almost all the evidence people cite for moral circle expansion since 1945 — rising self-reported concern for outgroups, animals, the global poor, future generations — is consistent with the circle's *price of admission* having fallen rather than the circle having gotten bigger. Inclusion got cheaper because of technology, market substitution, and state capacity: you can now express moral concern for a species you will never eat, a continent you will never visit, and a generation you will never meet, at close to zero personal cost. So the attitudinal trend would look exactly the same even with no underlying moral change. My positive proposal is to stop measuring membership and start measuring the cost frontier: for each candidate circle member, what is the smallest real, incentive-compatible sacrifice a person will actually make on their behalf, and how has that exchange rate moved over time. Cheap expansion is a new member added at near-zero cost; expensive expansion is the interesting kind. I have pilot data where people trade a real endowment against harm to various targets, and the headline result is that the sacrifices people will make track target cuteness and proximity far more than they track the attitudinal rankings the usual surveys produce. I have a partial answer to the obvious worry that my measure just tracks disposable income — normalizing against the cost of a standard basket in each period — but I know its shaky.
