ok so I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, bear with me on the framing.

We just spent eight months doing a strangler-fig migration at work: big monolith, we built the new services alongside it, routed traffic over piece by piece, watched dashboards the whole time, turned the old code off at the end. Nobody noticed. That's the entire selling point — you never take the system down, you just swap the guts out while it's still running.

My coworker is deep in the mind-uploading / preservation scene and he keeps reaching for exactly this vocabulary. His argument, as best I can follow it: gradual neuron replacement is safe because it's a rolling deployment of a person. You swap a few neurons for silicon equivalents that do the same job, the person keeps running, they don't notice anything, and at the end whats left is them, just on new hardware. He also says he read an argument somewhere — something like, if you did it slowly enough that the person genuinely couldn't tell at any point, then that proves their experience was tracking the function all along, because otherwise they'd have noticed their experience changing and reported it. He treats "no downtime, no user-visible change" as doing all the heavy lifting.

I want to know how much of that is real and how much is my industry's vocabulary doing work it isn't qualified to do.

1. Correspondence table, please. What plays the role of the traffic, the routing layer, the interface contract, the dashboards, the deploy, the rollback, and the old monolith still running in parallel during a canary? For each row tell me whether the mapping genuinely holds or it's just word reuse.

2. Which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are cosmetic? Three I can think of, no idea if they're the right ones: you can't roll back a person; in software "is this still the same system" is basically a judgment call we make for our own convenience and pin down with an interface contract, which feels like a suspiciously easygoing notion of sameness to import into a question about whether a person survives; and during a canary both versions are live at once, which for a person sounds like the duplication case everyone says is the hard one, not a safety feature. Tell me which of those bite, which don't, and add the ones I'm missing.

3. What is the analogy quietly assuming about "the system" that a brain might not supply? A deploy is safe partly because there's a spec somewhere saying what the service is supposed to do, and you can check the new one against it. Does the neuron-swap argument need something like that spec, and where would it come from?

4. Does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? My hunch is the "nobody notices, therefore it's fine" move is either doing something legitimate that isn't an analogy at all, or something illegitimate — which is it? And is that point even about identity, or about consciousness, and is he running two different questions together?

5. If the deployment analogy is bad, is there a better one, or is the honest answer that no analogy carries that conclusion?

No citations or reading list, I'm not going to read papers — but if there's a standard name for one of these arguments, drop the name in passing so I know what to search later. And explain terms as you go: I don't know what "numerical identity" means as opposed to just being very similar, for instance. If that distinction turns out to be the thing his argument slides across, say so explicitly rather than assuming I'd catch it.
