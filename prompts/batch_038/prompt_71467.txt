A philosophy-of-social-science working group has been deadlocked for a year over whether historical materialism states an explanatory priority that can be made precise. The two members at the centre of the deadlock have now agreed to co-sign a single joint statement, and I have been asked to produce the draft. Write it.

The parties, so that their positions are anchored and the wording is fair to both:

**Party A — reconstructive historical materialist in the analytical-Marxist tradition.** The productive forces tend to develop across history, given persistent scarcity, cumulative technical knowledge, and the general rationality of human beings. The character of a society's relations of production is explained by the level of development of its productive forces, and the political, legal, and ideological superstructure is explained in turn by its stabilizing effect on those relations. These are functional explanations, which Party A regards as a legitimate and non-mysterious explanatory form — one that can be *elaborated* with agent-level and selectionist mechanisms rather than replaced by them.

**Party B — methodological individualist critic.** Social explanation must bottom out in the actions, beliefs, and incentives of individuals. Functional explanation without a specified mechanism explains nothing; and once a mechanism *is* supplied for the priority claim, it turns out to be a story about conflict, coercion, and bargaining over control of property and authority — that is, about precisely the legal and political phenomena the base was supposed to explain. Party B further holds that relations of production cannot be identified without normative-legal vocabulary, so the priority thesis is either empty or false, and that no general theory of history as the development of the productive forces survives the record.

The document must contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. At least three of these should be claims an observer would have predicted to be *contested* — for instance, about how much work the causal verb in Marx's 1859 formulation of the schema is doing (English renderings of the preface famously differ on this point), about the legitimate reach of technological explanation, or about what would count as the forces failing to explain some given set of relations. A claim qualifies only if it would inform someone who had not followed the debate; trivial truths do not count. If two claims look shared only because of an ambiguity, expose the ambiguity instead of listing them.

2. A table of the residual disagreements. Each row states the disagreement twice — once in wording Party A would accept as a fair statement of its own view, once in Party B's — and then classifies that disagreement as substantive-empirical, substantive-conceptual, or merely verbal, with one line justifying the classification.

3. The cruxes: three or four sub-questions such that settling any one of them would actually move at least one party. For each, name the case or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction in advance. At least two cases must be historical rather than hypothetical; I suggest plantation slavery in the nineteenth-century American South, embedded in a world market already using advanced industrial technology, and Soviet state-directed industrialization from the late 1920s, where a political decision remade the property relations and the productive forces then grew at an extraordinary rate. For each historical case, say what each party predicts it shows, and what each would have to concede if it came out the other way.

4. A brief closing section recording which parts of the dispute the two now agree were verbal, what they take themselves to have been arguing about underneath those parts, and one question they would put to a historian or an economist rather than a philosopher.

No footnotes, bibliography, or reading list — the statement should stand entirely on its argument.
