I am preparing a talk for my department's philosophy of mind reading group on the timing of experience, and in assembling the material I constructed the following argument out of the colour phi case. I am now fairly convinced that it is unsound, though not because I can see anything wrong with it: rather because its conclusion sweeps in a great deal more than colour phi, and I cannot identify the point at which the sweep begins. The argument runs as follows.

(1) In colour phi, a subject is shown a spot of one colour — say green — and then, a few degrees away and a fraction of a second later, a spot of a different colour; at suitable spacings and intervals the subject reports a single spot travelling between the two positions and changing colour somewhere along the way. (2) Nothing available to the visual system at the moment the middle of that path would be traversed can specify the colour the spot changes into, since that information arrives only with the second spot. (3) So whatever the subject is conscious of at the mid-point is partly determined by what happens afterwards. (4) Two models accommodate this. On the Stalinesque model, processing is held back until the second spot has arrived, and a single already-reconciled sequence is then presented, so that the subject genuinely experiences the change in mid-flight. On the Orwellian model, the subject experiences the first spot as it actually is and has the record overwritten only afterwards, so that the mid-flight change was never experienced at all. (5) As Dennett puts the point, the two theorists hold the same theory of what happens in the brain: they agree about where and when the mistaken content enters the causal pathway, and they disagree only about "whether that location is to be deemed pre-experiential or post-experiential". They also agree about how it ought to feel, since a misbegotten experience and an instantly misremembered one are, by construction, indistinguishable from the inside. (6) If two accounts of a stream of consciousness agree about every report, every disposition to report, every memory, and every neural event together with its timing, then they do not describe two streams but one, there being no further fact left for them to disagree about. (7) So the Stalinesque and the Orwellian subject have the very same stream of consciousness, and it is not determinate whether the colour change was experienced and then forgotten or was never experienced at all. (8) That is merely an instance of a general principle: for any pair of experiences differing solely in a way that a suitable memory revision could reproduce, there is no fact of the matter about which of them occurred; and since a subject's record is at every moment exposed to being overwritten by later processing, for any pair of experiences some such revision can be described. (9) So nothing is determinate about the order, or even about the occurrence, of anyone's experiences. (10) Yet the entire question my reading group exists to discuss — when experience occurs, and in what order — presupposes that the question admits of an answer, and (9) says that it does not.

If I were forced to choose, I would rather abandon (8) than (6), since (6) seems to be what makes the question tractable in the first place, and abandoning it looks like conceding the ground to the Cartesian materialist whom the whole discussion is meant to dislodge. I cannot tell, however, whether that is a principled preference or a mere prejudice inherited from having read the case in a particular author's framing. Which step would you reject? For each of the serious candidates, I should like to know why it looks perfectly acceptable on a first reading, and what a philosopher of mind who rejects it is thereby left without.
