I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on the honor/dignity/victimhood trichotomy that has been doing a lot of work in the sociology of moral cultures — the rough idea being that honor cultures treat a person's worth as something conferred and withdrawn by an audience and therefore defended personally, dignity cultures treat worth as inherent and therefore as not requiring defense against insult, and victimhood cultures combine a fine-grained sensitivity to slight with a habit of routing grievances through third parties who can certify the injury. For several weeks I've been corresponding with a sociologist who codes dispute vignettes into those three buckets, and her coding sheet is unusually explicit, which is why I want to check my reading of it against yours. She scores each vignette on four things: whether the aggrieved person treats the slight as requiring any response at all; whether the response runs through the person's own action or through an outside authority; whether the person's standing in the episode depends on the estimate of some audience; and whether the person asks to have the wrong formally recorded. Her verdicts so far, as she gave them to me: a man accused of cheating at cards who demands satisfaction on the spot and, when refused, strikes his accuser and never mentions the matter again — honor; a commuter who is shouldered past and insulted on a train platform, says nothing, and tells no one — dignity; a graduate student addressed by a demeaning nickname in a lab meeting who does not confront the supervisor but emails the chair, asks that the incident be logged, and requests training for the lab — victimhood; a weaver who learns that a rival has been privately telling buyers her dyes are inferior, does not confront the rival or appeal to any guild, but invites the buyers to inspect her cloth and considers the matter closed once they have — dignity; a man defrauded by a business partner who declines to sue and tells no mutual acquaintances, but writes the partner a private letter stating what was done and requesting acknowledgment, saying he will simply end the relationship if it is not forthcoming — dignity; a tenant whose landlord has illegally withheld heat, who organizes her neighbors, files with the housing authority, and asks that the landlord's conduct be formally found wrongful — victimhood; and one she refused to code, in which a publicly insulted man both retaliates in person and files an institutional complaint seeking a record, which she called a genuine mixture rather than a fourth type.

The vignette she has agreed to code on the record for my piece, next week, is this: an engineer at a mid-sized firm discovers that inventor credit on a patent has been quietly reassigned to a senior colleague. She does not confront him, does not go to human resources, and does not ask anyone's sympathy. Instead she posts a long, sourced account of what happened to an industry forum read mostly by strangers, states plainly that she wants no apology, no compensation, and no sanction against anyone, and asks only that the community adopt a standing disclosure rule so that the next person in her position does not have to make a fuss. She signs it in her own name. My difficulty is that at least two readings of her own coding sheet point in opposite directions here. If what matters is whether an outside authority is asked to certify the injury, and whether the person's standing depends on an audience's estimate, this looks like dignity: she disclaims any need for anyone's good opinion and asks for a general rule rather than a finding about herself. If what matters is the pairing of sensitivity to slight with escalation into a public, documented record, it looks like victimhood. There are probably candidates I have not thought of — something keyed to whether the person claims moral standing from having been wronged, or to whether the relevant audience is a bounded community that knows the person or a diffuse public that does not.

So, three things. First, reconstruct the rule or rules she could be running: give me the candidate decision procedures that account for all seven earlier verdicts, including the one she declined to code, and tell me where they genuinely differ rather than differing only in emphasis. Second, say how each candidate would rule on the engineer, and give me your best single prediction with the reasons for preferring it to the rest. Third, propose one further vignette — short enough to describe in a sentence or two, and one I could plausibly put to her — whose coding would separate the leading candidates. I will also flag a difficulty that may end up being the spine of the piece. She insists the categories are purely descriptive and that no one of the three cultures is morally better than the others, yet every example she offers under the victimhood heading has a complaining register, while her dignity examples read as admirable self-possession. Is that an artifact of the coding dimensions she has chosen — that is, does defining victimhood as third-party certification of slight already build in a verdict — or is it a contingent selection effect in her examples that a differently chosen case could dissolve? If the latter, sketch a case she could code as victimhood that carries no pejorative charge at all, and tell me what has to be true of it for that to work.
