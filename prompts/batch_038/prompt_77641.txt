I convene a nine-member patient and family advisory committee at a regional teaching trust, and three or four times a month we rule on requests in which a family's observance collides with ward routine: a body that must not be moved for hours after death, a bereaved mother who will not eat, a consent conversation the family insists on holding collectively rather than with the patient alone. Over the summer I reread Montaigne's essay on the Tupinambá of Brazil — the one in which he describes their treatment of war captives, concedes that such things may be called barbarous by the rules of reason while insisting they are not barbarous in comparison with ourselves, and argues that tormenting a living man is a worse thing than consuming a dead one — and I was struck by how directly it bears on my day job. He also prefers the plain testimony of a servant who had actually lived in that part of the New World to the confident reports of learned men who had only read about it, which is exactly why our committee insists on hearing from the family rather than from the cultural liaison officer's briefing note. What unsettles me is that the standard interpretation on my committee is that Montaigne forbids our judging at all, whereas he plainly does judge, and does so by appeal to cruelty rather than to custom. I have been trying to work out what principle my own verdicts are actually tracking, and I have drafted one — but it does not survive the cases.

My draft principle, which I have been calling the shared-standard test: an outsider's criticism of a practice is apt only if the reason offered is one the practitioners can recognise as a reason, even if they reject it — that is, they could accept it without abandoning their own conception of what makes a life go well. Here are my verdicts on the cases I have collected.

- A family objects to a sedation protocol on the ground that it is cruel to a dying man; the clinical team replies that it is not cruel, only unfamiliar. I think criticism here is wholly legitimate, because both sides accept that cruelty is disqualifying and are disagreeing about a fact.
- A colleague criticises a family for deciding collectively rather than deferring to the patient's own wishes, in a framework in which the individual is simply not the locus of such decisions. I think the criticism is legitimate in some abstract sense, but that as an intervention it is not a criticism at all — it is a demand, and you cannot argue your way to it. I realise how muddled that sounds.
- A nurse who grew up in the community criticises the practice to us in the community's own terms. Fully legitimate, and the most useful speech we ever hear.
- A man who left that community after a bitter dispute, now employed as a patient advocate, criticises the identical practice in identical terms, to the same committee, but addresses an outside audience and calls for a change in trust policy. I think this is less legitimate than the nurse's, and I cannot say why: the content and the standing look the same. I suspect the difference lies in what the speech does rather than what it says, and if that is right my principle is covertly consequentialist, which I do not want it to be.
- A well-informed member abstains from every such vote on the ground that the practice is no business of ours. I think this is not respect but a refusal to judge, and I think Montaigne is wrongly invoked to dignify it.
- The same colleague who calls a family's mourning observance harsh on the dying has signed off a protocol that keeps patients awake two nights running. I think her criticism is weakened by this — weakened, not void.
- The community is split roughly down the middle on the practice. I feel entitled to side with the internal critics, though I notice that I always side with whichever faction happens to agree with me, which makes me doubt this is a verdict rather than a preference.
- A mourning observance that binds a widow's choices for a year, endorsed enthusiastically by every widow we have spoken to, with no complainant anywhere. I think criticism is still legitimate, but I hold this far more loosely than the sedation case.
