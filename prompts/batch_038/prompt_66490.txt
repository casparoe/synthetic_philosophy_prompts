You're a philosopher of physics who also genuinely knows the thermodynamics-of-computation literature cold — Landauer, Bennett, the whole information-is-physical tradition — and you've graded something like a hundred of these student-submitted perpetual-motion designs. Think carefully and brainstorm openly before you commit to anything, because I care more about the reasoning than about being handed a conclusion.

Here's the situation. I'm TA-ing a grad seminar on the physics of information. Every week the professor posts one student-submitted "demon design" and issues a one-word ruling — pass or fail — sometimes with a single sentence of justification. There's a betting pool in the lab on next week's ruling and I want to enter, but more than that I want to figure out what rule she's actually running, because her justifications are maddeningly short. Her track record so far this term (I'm paraphrasing from the handouts, so don't sweat the exact wording, and note that "pass" can't literally mean "violates the second law," since nothing does — it must mean something like "delivers sustained net work under a well-specified accounting"):

1. Szilard's one-molecule engine: demon measures which half the molecule is in, writes the result into a one-bit memory, extracts kT ln 2 of work, then erases the bit into the same bath. FAIL.
2. The same engine, but the demon never erases — it has a finite register of N bits and just stops when the register fills up. FAIL, annotated "a battery, not an engine."
3. Feynman-style ratchet and pawl, both at the same temperature as the surrounding gas, no memory, no observer, pawl heavy enough that individual molecular kicks don't obviously dominate. FAIL.
4. The same ratchet and pawl, except the pawl is deliberately held colder than the wheel. PASS.
5. Smoluchowski's automatic trapdoor, built so light that the gas molecules themselves knock it around. FAIL.
6. Brillouin-style demon that shines a flashlight into a uniformly warm box in order to see the molecules it's sorting. FAIL.
7. A design that stipulates the measurement is reversible and thermodynamically free, the memory is finite, and the erasure is dumped into a bath colder than the gas. PASS.
8. A "demon as bacterium" proposal — a tiny organism doing chemotaxis up a sugar gradient, framed as measurement-and-feedback with no nervous system. She declined to rule and wrote only that the submission "doesn't specify a closed system."

Now the new case, the one I'd be betting on. A student proposes: the demon performs an ideally reversible, costless measurement of which side of the partition each fast molecule is on, and writes the outcome into a memory register. It uses the register to drive a trapdoor and thereby siphon work out of a single heat bath at uniform temperature. The twist is that the register is never erased — it's write-once and effectively unbounded, and after each run it's physically boxed up and shipped to a warehouse across town where nobody ever reads it again. All of the demon's moving parts, trapdoor included, are held at the same temperature as the gas. There is no cold reservoir anywhere in the description.

What I want from you:

(a) Lay out the candidate rules that could reproduce her earlier rulings — at least three. For instance "any cycle that has to erase pays for it," "any controller whose parts have thermalized with the system it's sorting fails by detailed balance," "measurement itself is never free," "entropy has to be dumped into something genuinely outside the specified boundary," "if the boundary isn't specified, don't rule." For each, say whether it actually covers all eight precedents or exactly which one or two it stumbles on. I'm especially unsure whether #2 and #6 are doing the same work in her head or completely different work, and whether #8's refusal is a hint about how she treats system boundaries.
(b) For every rule that survives (a), say what it predicts about the warehouse-register design.
(c) Give me your single best prediction of her ruling and your reasons, and identify which of the eight precedents is the closest analog and why it beats the others.
(d) Since I suspect the precedents genuinely underdetermine this one, spell out the physical or philosophical commitment she'd be taking on with each possible ruling. Does ruling FAIL commit her to counting the shipped-away register as still inside the system? Or to holding that mutual information between memory and gas is itself an entropy debt that has to be settled somewhere, even un-erased? Does ruling PASS commit her to treating a fresh blank register as equivalent to a cold bath, and if so why doesn't #3 and #5 then pass too?
(e) Design one further submission, as concrete as the eight above, that would discriminate between your top two candidate rules, and state what each of those two rules predicts about it.

If it helps, work the warehouse case twice — once with the boundary drawn around the lab bench and once with it drawn to include the warehouse — and then tell me which framing you think she's using and what tipped you off.
