And here's the script:

---

Urmson's point was that the vocabulary moral philosophers inherited — duty, obligation, right, wrong, permitted — could not register the saint and the hero. Their acts had to be shoehorned in as duties, which makes them ordinary, or else they went unmentioned altogether. A supererogatory act is meant to fill that gap: it is morally good, and good beyond what duty demands, but it is not required, and the agent is not to blame for leaving it undone. Donating a kidney to a stranger or pulling someone out of a burning car are the stock cases. These involve real cost or risk to the agent, and cost clearly matters to our judgement of them, though it does not have to be enormous.

Why is this a philosophical problem rather than a gap in a list? Because the theory has to say where duty stops, and there is no principled place to draw that line. If saving a drowning child at the cost of muddy shoes is obligatory, then saving a second child at the cost of muddy shoes and a ruined phone looks obligatory too, and so on up to giving away most of what you own. Each step is trivial, but the endpoint is not. So the supererogatory is really the residue of our inability to fix how demanding morality is — it's the demandingness objection to consequentialism wearing a different hat.

Consequentialism shows this most clearly. If the right act is the one that produces at least as much good as any alternative available, then any act falling short of the best is wrong, and "wrong" has no room for praise. A maximizing consequentialist cannot even state the distinction between the saint and the merely decent, so it cannot be true: it leaves out something our ordinary moral thinking plainly contains. It is worth contrasting Kant, who does have room. His duties of virtue, beneficence for instance, are wide: they tell you to adopt a maxim but not how much to give or to whom, so on any given occasion you are not to be blamed for not acting. Wide duties are in effect supererogatory, which is why Kantian ethics can describe the hero without having to insist that the hero was only doing his duty.
