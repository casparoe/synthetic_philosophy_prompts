Thomson's lamp is the cleanest demonstration that you simply can't complete infinitely many tasks. Start with the lamp off, then flip the switch so it's on for half a minute, off for a quarter of a minute, on for an eighth, off for a sixteenth, and so on, so that the whole infinite sequence of flips is finished inside one minute. Now ask: at the end of that minute, is the lamp on or off? It can't be on, because whenever it was on it was switched off again moments later; it can't be off, because whenever it was off it was switched on again moments later; and it obviously has to be one or the other. So the description of the machine is contradictory and no such machine can exist. What people miss is that the whole thing is really the old series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ... in disguise, since the lamp's states are just its partial sums, and Euler was perfectly happy to give that series the value 1/2 and Cesàro summation later made that respectable. So the mathematically honest answer is that the lamp ends up half on, which is exactly what a dimmer would give you, and the paradox goes away. That's the point Benacerraf was making in his famous reply to Thomson: the description is incomplete, it never tells you the state at the end of the minute, so there's no contradiction, and it follows that supertasks are logically possible after all — Thomson just hadn't proved they aren't. The only thing that actually stops you building one is physics: to flip a switch infinitely often you need parts moving ever faster, and nothing goes faster than light, so the ban on supertasks is an engineering limit, not a logical one, and that's the whole story.
