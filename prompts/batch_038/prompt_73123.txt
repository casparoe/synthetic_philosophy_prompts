I am putting together a case study for a seminar on governance by prediction markets, and what I want is a disciplined analysis of one particular episode rather than a general brief for or against the idea. The episode is the Policy Analysis Market — the DARPA project that was killed at the end of July 2003. I am supplying the facts I take to be settled, so that the analysis does not rest on your recall of them; please treat the list as given, and if you think one of these facts is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear, say so rather than quietly discarding it.

1. The solicitation went out in May 2001 under the deliberately unremarkable heading of electronic market-based decision support, and two small firms won grants. One of them assembled a team including two George Mason economists, one of whom had spent years arguing that speculative markets on events outperform expert committees at forecasting. That team's project became the Policy Analysis Market.
2. The design was an exchange on the political and economic future of eight Middle Eastern countries. For each country and each quarter: contracts on military activity, political instability, economic growth, US military activity, and US financial involvement. Above those, aggregate contracts on US GDP, world trade, US military casualties, and Western terrorist casualties, plus a residual category of miscellaneous items.
3. The distinctive ambition was combinatorial trading — packages of the form: if US troops are withdrawn from Saudi Arabia, then instability there, then instability in neighboring states, then oil prices. These conditional contracts were the intellectual center of the project and the part that received almost no public attention.
4. The exchange was to be open rather than internal to the intelligence community; the designers had considered running markets inside agencies and chose public ones instead. A pilot was to register in August 2003, roughly a hundred testers were each to be given a hundred dollars, and public trading was slated to begin in January 2004. Because the proposed market-maker mechanism required a subsidy and the subsidy was small, individual stakes were capped at a few tens of dollars.
5. The program sat inside the office associated with Total Information Awareness, whose funding the Senate had already moved to strip that summer on privacy grounds.
6. On July 28, 2003, two senators held a press conference characterizing the project as the federal government running a betting parlor on atrocities and terrorism. Their evidence was the project's own website: in the faint background of two sample screens, occupying well under two percent of the displayed area, were colorful illustrations of possible miscellaneous contracts — the assassination of Yasser Arafat, a missile attack by North Korea, the overthrow of the King of Jordan.
7. The Pentagon cancelled the program within a day, and the executive who headed the office left weeks later, although by the account of the project's own designer that executive had little to do with PAM's design. A private-sector successor was subsequently floated with the contracts on violent events removed.
8. As far as I can determine, nobody in the public exchange argued that the market's forecasts would be inaccurate. The objections concerned incentives, complicity, and what the institution would mean.

Now the lens. Futarchy, in its slogan form, is vote on values, bet on beliefs: the polity votes on a measure of national welfare, and prediction markets on the welfare-relevant consequences of candidate policies then determine which policies are adopted. The label and the idea were circulating by 2000, but the formal statement of the view was published roughly a decade after PAM died, and PAM was not a governance mechanism at all — no decision was to be made by price. I want you to be explicit about whether PAM is a case of futarchy, a partial case whose conditional contracts are the only part that resembles it, or something else entirely, and whether your subsequent analysis changes depending on that answer.

With that settled, do four things.

First, the mapping. Take futarchy's structural elements one at a time — who votes on values, what the welfare measure is, what the space of policies is, which prices are decision-relevant, what the decision rule linking prices to action is — and state for each either its counterpart in PAM or its absence. Be specific about the difference between pricing an event and pricing the consequence of a policy, since almost everything in PAM was the former, and say what the combinatorial contracts do to that distinction.

Second, the objections. Three were in the air: that traders profit if the priced event occurs and therefore acquire a financial interest in bringing it about; that a liquid public market on an assassination or a coup gives would-be plotters a credible way to find one another and to signal, and gives targets a warning, in ways that shift the probabilities the market is supposed to be tracking; and that a state's act of pricing death has a meaning independent of its accuracy, and that this meaning is itself a cost. For each, say whether futarchy's own framework can state it. Its warrant is epistemic — markets beat rival aggregators at estimating consequences — so ask whether each objection can be folded into the welfare measure that futarchy leaves to the vote, and whether folding it in is a legitimate move or a way of converting an objection about the character of an institution into a term in a calculus, thereby begging the question against the objector.

Third, evaluate a device of my own, and do not be gentle with it. I want to distinguish four ways a prediction market can stand to its subject: as a thermometer, measuring a variable it does not causally touch; as a lever, where prices change the variable measured because traders act to make their positions pay; as a signal, where prices transmit the sponsor's own beliefs to the very agents whose behavior is being predicted, changing that behavior with or without anyone wanting the event; and, fourth, as a case in which the subject matter is reflexive enough that there is no prior probability for the price to track, so accuracy talk loses its referent. PAM was designed and defended as a thermometer; the objections alleged a lever. My hunch, which I do not trust, is that what actually killed it was the signal — a public market on Middle Eastern stability is a broadcast of one government's beliefs to the regimes and groups being priced, in a domain where keeping those beliefs private has strategic value. Tell me whether the trichotomy plus the fourth clause does real analytic work or merely relabels the objections, whether the fourth clause is coherent at all, and which of the four the affair is best read as evidence against.

Fourth, what the episode shows about the theory. Was PAM's death an accuracy objection in disguise, a motivational objection futarchy can absorb, or evidence that the binding constraint on governance by market is legitimacy rather than epistemology? Take account of the successor proposal, which kept the mechanism and dropped the violent contracts: if the objection had been to the mechanism, that move would not have made sense. And say where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak — in particular, whether futarchy has any threshold claim about how thin a market can be before its prices stop being evidence, given that PAM's own budget capped stakes at a few tens of dollars with a hundred pilot traders, and whether that thinness disqualifies PAM as a test of the theory or is merely an implementation detail.

Format: work in the four numbered parts above, in order. Part one should be a compact table or list of framework element against PAM counterpart or absence. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or attributions to particular papers; argue from what you know and mark your uncertainty plainly where you have it. End with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by exactly one of ACCURACY, INCENTIVES, SIGNAL, REFLEXIVITY, or SCOPE, then a dash and one sentence giving your reason.
