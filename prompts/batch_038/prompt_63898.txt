I teach a night class on Bayesian epistemology at a community college, and we just burned two sessions on van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin puzzle. Quick recap of how I set it up for them, since I want to make sure we're talking about the same case: Judy gets dropped into a territory split into a northern and a southern half, each half split into two sectors, so four quadrants total, and her prior is uniform, 1/4 each. Then HQ radios her that if she's in the north, the probability she's in the northern half's second sector is three-quarters. Everyone in the room reached for the same move: impose P(second northern sector | north) = 3/4 as a constraint, hold P(north) fixed at 1/2 because the message supposedly says nothing about which half she's in, and get the posterior 1/8, 3/8, 1/4, 1/4. Neat, tidy, and the conditional is taken completely at face value as a report of somebody's epistemic state.

Here's the wrinkle. One of my students spent six years as a military comms officer, and after class he told me the whole discussion assumes something false about where the number comes from. He sketched a variant of the scenario that I want you to argue about, and I'll spell out his protocol as precisely as I can: HQ planned the drop, so HQ knows exactly which of the four sectors Judy is actually in. Comms discipline forbids telling her directly. Instead the duty officer sends exactly one conditional message of the form "if you're in region R, the probability you're in sector i is q." The officer picks which region to name by a deliberate randomization built into the doctrine: he names the region Judy is really in with probability 3/4 and names the other region with probability 1/4, so that anyone intercepting the traffic can't just read off her location. The number q he quotes is not a report of his own uncertainty at all — it's read off a fixed cover table printed in the doctrine manual, which always lists three-quarters for the northern half's second sector and one-half for the southern half's second sector, no matter what. And Judy has the same manual; she memorized the protocol in training, so none of this is hidden from her. The message she actually receives is: if you're in the north, the probability you're in the northern half's second sector is three-quarters.

So: argue for the position that in this variant the correct posterior is P(north) = 3/4 with the two northern sectors still evenly split conditional on being in the north — i.e. almost exactly the reverse of what my class did, with the antecedent carrying all the information and the quoted consequent carrying none. I don't want a survey of the options or a both-sides treatment; take that position and defend it. Work out the likelihoods explicitly (you only need the four quadrant hypotheses, don't need anything fancier), explain why shoving the quoted three-quarters into a Jeffrey-style constraint here is a category mistake about what kind of speech act the message is, and then tell me what you think this does to the original van Fraassen case where we're given no information at all about how HQ chose its message. On that last point commit to something: is holding P(north) at 1/2 in the original case a defensible default, or does it smuggle in an unstated assumption about the selection process? Also flag anywhere you think my student's protocol description is underdetermined or incoherent, and say how you'd fill the gap rather than just noting it. Finish with the single sentence you'd write on the whiteboard for my students.
