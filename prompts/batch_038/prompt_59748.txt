I am applying for an eight-week summer fellowship at my department's center for philosophy of science. The deliverable would be a paper of roughly 8,000 words and one seminar talk, and the committee wants a proposal rather than a literature review. Please write the proposal.

My half-formed idea is this. Bertrand's chord problem — the probability that a chord of a circle, drawn at random, is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, with the three classical constructions yielding one third, one half, and one quarter — is nearly always taught as delivering a single lesson: the principle of indifference is untrustworthy once the possibility space is continuous and unstructured, so the paradox is really a warning about ill-posed questions. I think that framing runs together three puzzles with different statuses and different remedies, and that only one of them is genuinely philosophical:

- A denotation puzzle: a chord drawn at random does not determine a unique distribution because the phrase does not specify a generating procedure. This is a failure of the question to have a determinate answer, not a finding about indifference as a principle of reasoning.
- An invariance puzzle: even once a physical setup is fixed, there remains the question of which symmetry requirements an admissible measure must satisfy — the mid-century proposal was that the problem statement fixes no position or size for the circle, so the answer must be invariant under translation and under change of scale — and, more pointedly, the question of why symmetry considerations should constrain rational credence at all rather than merely describe the apparatus that happens to be in front of us.
- An implementation puzzle: given an actual procedure (choosing two endpoints, choosing a midpoint along a radius, choosing a midpoint anywhere in the disc, tossing straws onto a circle), which distribution it produces, and whether the agreement between certain procedures and the invariance-demanded measure is a contingent empirical fact about those procedures or a constraint on which procedures count as legitimate.

My hypothesis, which I want the proposal to sharpen rather than soften: the standard lesson is the first puzzle doing the work of the second. Once they are separated, indifference comes out less damaged than the textbooks suggest, while the second puzzle turns out not to be about indifference in particular — it is about the standing of symmetry-based constraints in probabilistic reasoning, and it would survive intact even if nobody ever invoked indifference again.

There is also a fourth candidate I want the proposal to adjudicate instead of assuming: the worry that Bertrand is simply one instance of the general reference-class problem for unrestricted probability statements, and therefore has no content of its own beyond what every probability question already carries. My instinct is that taking it on strengthens the project, because it forces me to say what is distinctive about the continuous case, but I can see it swallowing all eight weeks. Tell me which way you came out and why.

One further thing the proposal should engage rather than ignore: there is a standing objection that the invariance constraints are not as selective as their defenders claim, and that applied with different auxiliary assumptions they can be made to license more than one of the three classical answers. If that objection lands, say what is left of my middle puzzle.

Please write the proposal with the following sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** (about 150 words). The question in one paragraph, and the case that it is worth eight weeks of someone's time.
2. **The question, precisely stated** (about 250 words). Stated so that it has a determinate answer, with the three-way decomposition defined sharply enough that a reader could tell which puzzle a given argument belongs to. Include a short note on what the question is not asking, so the committee does not expect a treatise on the interpretation of probability.
3. **Why the standard treatments leave it open** (about 350 words). The ill-posedness diagnosis, the invariance-based rescue, and the textbook moral about indifference: what each one does establish and what each one overlooks. Describe positions and arguments; do not name-drop a bibliography.
4. **Method and week-by-week plan** (about 400 words). What I would actually do in eight weeks — which distinctions I would argue for, which arguments I would reconstruct, what the paper's section structure would be, and what the seminar talk would cover. Explain why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives: a formal reconstruction of the candidate measures, a history of the dispute from the nineteenth century onward, or a survey of proposed well-posed reformulations. Say what I would borrow from each of those without doing it.
5. **What a result looks like, and what failure looks like** (about 250 words). State the two or three claims the paper would have to establish to count as having delivered, and give at least two concrete ways the project could fail — one of them a failure I might not notice until late in the eight weeks.
6. **Scope check** (about 200 words). An honest assessment of what is too big for this period and what I would cut first if the decomposition turns out to be less clean than I think.
7. **Confidence** (about 150 words). How confident you are in the decomposition I have proposed and in each of the substantive claims you have made on its behalf, and the single point you are least sure of.

Constraints: write in American English. No bibliography, no reference list, no invented citations — I will add references myself. Assume the reader already knows the paradox and its three answers; spend the space on framing, not on background. And do not hedge the proposal into vagueness in order to be safe: the committee should be able to see exactly what I am claiming and exactly where the risk lies.
