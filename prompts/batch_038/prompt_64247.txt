I am a third-year PhD student in social history, working on parish poor-law administration in eighteenth-century England, and I have run into something in my sources that I cannot name. I would like to know whether there is an established concept for it, and if not, what it ought to be called.

The pattern is this. A group of people appears in the documentary record very heavily — far more heavily than any comparable group I work with — and yet almost nothing that I want to know about them is retrievable from that record, and the failure is not caused by anyone having destroyed, suppressed, or withheld anything.

Concretely: a pauper brought before the justices for examination is described at length, sometimes over several pages, in her own reported words. But the examination has one job, which is to establish which parish is legally obliged to maintain her, so the deposition consists of her movements, her hirings, her marriages, and her residence, in that order and at that length, because those are the questions the examiners were obliged to put. The document is dense, verbatim in places, and personally revealing — and it contains no field whatsoever in which anything else about her life could have been recorded even if she had volunteered it. The same is true of the workhouse and estate ledgers I use: a labourer appears every week for thirty years, without a gap, as a number in a column. Nothing about him is missing from the archive. There is simply no slot in which the things I want could have gone.

Two features are what make me think this is its own phenomenon rather than a species of something already named. First, the density is a direct function of the institution's need to sort, not of any interest in the person: the more efficiently the paperwork did its job, the more there is of it and the less it is about anyone. Second — and this is the part that keeps recurring in my reading — the abundance is systematically mistaken for recoverability. A colleague counts four thousand case files and concludes that four thousand lives are available; what is available is four thousand instances of intake. And there is an inverse relation I cannot account for with the vocabulary I have: the richer and more personally detailed a file is, the more readily historians read it as evidence about the clerk, the magistrate, or the institution, so that the subject seems to recede exactly in proportion to the documentation that was produced of her.

I have considered and rejected several nearby terms. Archival silence assumes a gap where a record might have been, and here there is no gap. Symbolic annihilation assumes absence, trivialization, or condemnation, and these people are neither absent nor trivialized; they are exhaustively documented. Legibility is close in spirit, but as I understand it the emphasis falls on what the state can see in order to act, whereas what I need is a term for what the surviving form makes permanently unavailable to anyone reading it later.

So: is there a name for this, and if several near-neighbor concepts compete, which fits best and where does my description strain each of them? If nothing fits, I would like you to coin and define a term for it — with the mechanism stated precisely enough that I could apply it as a diagnostic to a new body of records, with at least one case that looks like it but is not, and one that is easily mistaken for ordinary absence or bias but on your definition counts as an instance. I am not asking for a reading list; I am asking for the carving.
