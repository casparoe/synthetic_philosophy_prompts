For the past year I have been preparing a comparative review of price controls for a municipal policy office — rent stabilization on one side, the federal wage-and-price programs of the early 1970s on the other — and I have kept running into a two-beat pattern that I cannot find a name for, despite having read the economist's case against controls and the housing advocate's case for them with equal care. The first beat is this. A control attaches to a thing under a description: a class of vehicle, a unit of housing let at a stated rent, an article with a stated price in a stated reference period. The substance being regulated does not change, but its description is made to change, and the control then no longer reaches it. A fleet-average fuel standard written for one class of vehicle ends up governing a steadily shrinking share of what is actually sold, because the same steel, sold to the same buyers, driven on the same roads, is built on a chassis that classifies it elsewhere. Under rent stabilization, the same building stops being a regulated rental without ceasing to be housing: units are combined into a larger unit that is newly outside the schedule, or let furnished by the month, or converted and sold, or in one state withdrawn from the rental market altogether under an exit statute rather than let at the controlled rent. The older analysts I have interviewed describe the same move under the 1970s freeze: an article that had a controlled price acquires a slightly altered specification and a new catalogue name, and the question of what its old price was becomes unanswerable. What I want stressed is that in every one of these cases nothing has gone underground, nothing illegal has happened, and nobody has fled to a friendlier regulator; the transaction stays exactly where it was, in the open, and only its label has moved.

The second beat is what makes me think this may not be the familiar thing I keep being told it is. When the migrated transactions are offered as evidence that the control failed, the defenders' answer is that the control succeeded at everything it was written to do, and that whatever left the category was never inside it to begin with. The regulated stock shrinks, the shrinking stock is precisely what is measured, and the measurement then looks fine: the rents of units that remain covered are indeed below market, the vehicles that remain classified are indeed efficient, the goods that still have a reference price are indeed unraised. The escapees are not counted as failures because they are not counted at all, and a policy appears to be confirmed by the very sample its own operation produced. I have heard the first beat called finding a loophole, and I have come across the term regulatory arbitrage, and I know there is a literature on bright-line rules being sorted around and on measures ceasing to be good once they are targets; but each of those seems to capture only half of what I am seeing, and none of them, so far as I can tell, captures the second beat at all — the way the rule's own scope conditions make the policy immune to the evidence of its own escapes. I should also flag a worry of my own: I may have glued together two distinct phenomena, one about reclassification and one about how a policy is evaluated, and a good answer should tell me plainly if that is what I have done rather than indulging the composite. There is an ethical half I want addressed too, and not merely an epistemic one. Is migrating the description a legitimate use of the freedom the control leaves open, or a defection from a cooperative scheme that harms those who comply? And who actually bears the cost, given that the households left inside the regulated category are the ones who face the shrunken supply, the side payments for a lease, and the slow degradation of every attribute the schedule does not mention?

I am not asking for a bibliography and I know you cannot look anything up, so please do not manufacture citations; where you are confident a term is genuinely established in a literature, say which one, and where you suspect you are reconstructing rather than recalling, mark that explicitly. What I want from you is the carving, not the label.
