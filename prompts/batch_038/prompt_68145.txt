You are a professional philosopher of science who also works on the foundations of statistical learning theory. I run a graduate seminar that reads Hume on induction alongside the no-free-lunch results, and I need one essay question for the final: answerable in 90 minutes, marked out of 25, and self-contained enough that a candidate who has never seen the proofs can still engage with it seriously.

The question must be impossible to pass by summarising either Hume or Wolpert. Build it around a dispute between two speakers, whose positions are stated in the question itself. An ML researcher argues that the no-free-lunch result settles Hume's problem, since it shows there can be no a priori distinction between learning algorithms and hence no generalisation without assumptions about the space of possible target functions. A philosopher replies that the result is philosophically inert: it says only that performance, averaged over a weighting of environments fixed in advance, comes out the same for every algorithm, and therefore presupposes rather than answers the question of which weighting deserves our attention. You may exploit the fact that the popular formulation — a uniform average over all possible target functions — is weaker than what the original theorems establish, which concern the relative weighting of the sets of environments in which one algorithm outperforms another. I want candidates forced to say what philosophical work, if any, that weighting is doing, and whether a theorem about expected error could in principle discharge a justificatory challenge at all.

Before drafting, spend a few sentences identifying where you take the crux to lie and why the two speakers are not merely talking past one another, so that I can check we agree. Then give me: (a) the question exactly as candidates will see it, including any technical stipulations they need; (b) a model answer of about 700 words; (c) a marking scheme distributing the 25 marks across five components, naming for each the specific error that should cost marks rather than describing performance generically, and closing with a short paragraph on what separates a first from a strong upper second.

Take a definite side. The model answer must argue for one of the two speakers and give its reasons, not split the difference; the marking scheme must nonetheless state what a candidate who argues coherently for the other side would need to contain to earn full marks. Write in British English, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
