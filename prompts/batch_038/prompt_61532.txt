I'm doing an MA in modern history and my seminar leader has a way of framing the whole "who started the Cold War" debate that I want to check before I repeat it in an essay. He says it's basically a civil case: the orthodox people are counsel for one side (Stalin did it), the revisionists are counsel for the other (it was Truman and the whole open-door, markets-for-America thing), and the post-revisionists are the judge, who hears both and then apportions liability — some to each, with reasons. On his account that's why the debate is worth teaching: like apportionment in a damages claim, a split isn't a fudge, it's a finding, and it's still a real answer to a real question. He also says the Soviet archives opening in the ninties was like disclosure — new documents turned up and the case got properly tried.

Can you audit this for me? I'd like the correspondence table first — what plays the claimant, the defendant, counsel, the judge, the standard of proof, disclosure, the apportionment — and then where the mapping breaks. A few places I can already half-see the trouble and want you to push on:

- who is the claimant supposed to be, exactly? and doesn't that mean he's smuggling a criminal frame (blame, guilt) into a civil one (apportionment), when criminal courts don't apportion at all — they convict or acquit? if the two frames are fighting each other, which one is his conclusion actually resting on?
- tort apportionment works because there's a shared causal-and-normative framework for separating two parties' contributions. is there anything corresponding to that here, or does an escalating back-and-forth just not have separable contributions to divide up?
- does a courtroom have any analogue for *when the thing began*, for a conflict with no declared start and no opening act everyone agrees on?
- the disclosure idea — is it fair, given that people on both sides of the argument were working from the same Western files for decades and only one side's records arrived late, partial and politically curated?
