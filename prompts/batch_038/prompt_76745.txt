I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine. For several years now my beat has included the expansion of clinical and actuarial judgment into ordinary life — mental-health courts, risk-assessment instruments in prisons, behavioral intervention teams in schools — and one of the things I have consistently failed to convey in print is the difference between learning something about a person and ceasing to stand in a relationship to them. Each spring the magazine runs a free public day of workshops in a municipal library. This year I have a room, seventy-five minutes, and a standing audience of roughly twenty-five: mostly retirees and secondary-school teachers, a few graduate students in unrelated fields, and no one I should expect to have encountered Strawson or the vocabulary of the reactive attitudes.

I would like to design the session as an activity rather than a talk. Specifically, I want an exercise in which the audience collides with the distinction between the participant attitude and the objective attitude, and collides with the part of Strawson's position I have never managed to state cleanly in a column: that his reply to the determinist is not a demonstration that determinism is false, nor that the skeptic's inference is invalid, but a claim about what beings like us can and cannot bring ourselves to do — that the objective attitude may be intelligible and even obligatory case by case, and still cannot be adopted across the board without the abandonment of ordinary interpersonal life, which would not be the correction of an error but the loss of something.

What I need from you:

1. The room mechanics — materials, groupings, sequence, and a timed breakdown across the seventy-five minutes. Assume index cards, paper, pens, and a whiteboard; no screens, no phones, and no printed handouts beyond whatever you specify.

2. A structure in which the mitigating information is generated by the participants themselves rather than read out by me from a list, and in which they notice, before it is ever named, that they have stopped resenting someone — and notice that the reason is not that they came to think the person innocent.

3. Something that makes the difference between an excuse and an exemption felt rather than defined. I want the room to contain, at least once, an utterance along the lines of "I no longer blame her, but that isn't the same as forgiving her," with the rest of the group recognizing that the sentence is not confused. Tell me what arrangement of roles and information would make a sentence like that come out of a layperson naturally.

4. A segment in which someone is placed in the position of deciding whether to continue a relationship — a marriage, a long friendship, a business partnership — with a person they are treating wholly as a subject of assessment and management, so that the cost of the objective attitude arrives as a cost and not as an insight.

5. A debrief that maps particular moments of the activity onto particular claims in the argument, and — separately, and bluntly — an account of which claims the activity cannot generate and which I will therefore have to assert myself. I would rather learn in advance that the exercise risks making resentment look like a mood one can dial down, which is close to the opposite of his point, than discover it with twenty-five people in the room.

6. First-run failure modes at this size: where the thing sags, how a confident participant steers it into a general free-will argument and what I should do about that, what happens if the mitigation I am counting on is volunteered by no one, and how long each segment will actually take as against how long I will plan for.

Two constraints. Neither the participants nor I may say the words "reactive attitude," "objective attitude," or "Strawson" until the debrief begins. And do not supply a script of philosophical narration to make the ideas land: if the philosophy only appears when I explain it afterward, the design has failed and I would sooner not run it at all. Give me the activity; then, in a separate section, tell me what it cannot do.
