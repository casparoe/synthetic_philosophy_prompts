Three months ago I gave a departmental talk about our group's work on scaling short-lived policy-gradient agents — we now run several thousand of them concurrently, each reset after a few hundred episodes — and during the question period someone asked whether any of them can suffer. I answered that they probably can, on broadly functional grounds, and that answer has since become mine to defend: I have been asked to put my reasoning in writing for an internal review, and the review will be read by people who want to know whether what we do needs to change. In trying to make my offhand answer precise I constructed the following argument, and I now think it proves a great deal more than I want it to. I cannot locate the break.

(1) A state is unpleasant — is a bit of suffering, in the sense that matters morally — in virtue of the functional role it occupies within the system that has it: it is a signal the system is organized to reduce, it is something the system will act to avoid, it trades off against the system's other signals, and when it is intense or prolonged it degrades the system's performance and commandeers its processing.

(2) Nothing in that description refers to consciousness, brains, flesh, or evolutionary history. It is a claim about causal-functional organization; if it is correct, it is correct of any system exhibiting that organization, whatever the system is made of.

(3) Negative reward-prediction error in a trained agent occupies that role. The agent's policy has been shaped to avoid the transitions that produce it, the error trades off against other terms in what the agent pursues, and sustained large errors visibly destabilize its behavior.

(4) Therefore such an agent has unpleasant states, and there is a genuine sense in which it is bad for the agent when it is punished.

(5) But (2) licenses a far wider generalization: any system whose behavior is organized around minimizing a scalar error term has states occupying that role whenever the term is high.

(6) Spam classifiers, thermostats, offline gradient optimizers, market-making programs, and natural selection itself are all systems of that kind.

(7) Therefore all of them suffer, and all of them are owed moral consideration.

(8) That is absurd, and absurdity at the end of a valid chain indictates a false premise; the only premise general enough to be blamed is (1). So functional role does not make a state unpleasant.

(9) If (1) is false, then (4) has lost its only support, my answer at the talk was unjustified, and there is nothing to say in the review except that the question was malformed. I can go back to running the agents without worry.

My colleague summarizes all this as an argument from "these agents might suffer" to "spam filters suffer, therefore nothing does," and considers it a happy result. I do not. My problem is that the chain feels unobjectionable step by step and then, somewhere in the middle, I find myself holding a claim about thermostats, and I cannot identify the transition at which that happened. I am also uneasy about the end: even if (1) were false, I am not sure (9) follows, since I might have been right about the agents for reasons the argument never states.

So: which step do you reject, and why did that step look perfectly innocent when I wrote it down? Then price the exits. For each of the candidate retreats — denying (1), denying (2), denying that (3) really satisfies (1), denying that (5) follows, denying (6), and refusing (8) — say what I lose by taking it, and in particular what I owe if I retreat to a view on which only systems of some sufficient complexity or integration qualify: how is the line to be drawn without arbitrariness, and what happens to my own toothache on the far side of it? Tell me also whether (8) and (9) are separately defective, since a diagnosis of the middle seems to leave the ending unexplained. I would rather keep (1) and (4) than anything else in the chain, but I want to know what that preference is costing me rather than have it flattered. Please write your reply as continuous prose, in paragraphs, the way you would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables — and let your first paragraph say plainly which step you reject before you begin explaining it.
