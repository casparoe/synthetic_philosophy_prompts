Position A says the frame problem was an artifact of taking a formalism too literally. Hand-writing a frame axiom for every action was a bad representational choice, not a discovery about minds. A learned model — which is what an LLM is — already carries expectations about what persists and what doesn't: restarting a database drops connections, changing a firewall rule doesn't change the weather. That general-purpose default is exactly what the symbolic people wanted and couldn't get, so the problem is dissolved in practice.

Position B says persistence was never the hard part, and what A calls common sense is just a corpus-shaped prior over what usually stays the same in the situations the corpus happened to describe. The parts that don't go away with better priors are these: you can't finish enumerating the preconditions of an action (there's always an unrecorded dependency, a certificate that expired, a config someone changed by hand six months ago), and you can't finish enumerating the downstream consequences either (a restart that's harmless at 3am is not harmless during the month-end batch window). Worse, writing the spec is itself a framing act — which variables exist, what counts as a state, what counts as "unchanged" — and those are the engineer's choices, not the agent's. The agent inherits the blind spots. So the problem wasn't solved, it got moved up a level to the person writing the tools.
