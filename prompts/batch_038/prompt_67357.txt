I'm in a philosophy-of-medicine reading group and one of us turned up with an argument against the whole-brain criterion that nobody in the room could answer, except its conclusion is way too strong for anybody there to swallow either. Here's roughly how he laid it out.

(1) The reason a brain-dead body on a ventilator counts as dead, on the official neurological account, isn't that the person is gone — it's that the organism has stopped functioning as an integrated whole. The parts are still doing things, but nothing is coordinating them into one body anymore.

(2) Defenders of the criterion insist on exactly this reading, because the alternative — the person has left, so the patient is dead — would turn death into a psychological matter and would be a change of subject away from biology. That's also why the 1981 commission line rejected the higher-brain approach.

(3) So on this account what death consists in is the loss of somatic integration.

(4) But integration is graded and it comes apart in layers. The brainstem buys you blood pressure, temperature regulation, breathing control, stress response. The cortex buys you consciousness, planning, purposeful engagement with the world, the capacity to organise your own behaviour as one agent across time.

(5) A patient in permanent vegetative state has lost the second layer and kept the first. Someone in late-stage dementia has lost most of it. Both are, by everyone's agreement, alive.

(6) And the integration that brain-dead bodies retain is not nothing — these are the cases people keep citing where a body declared dead has been maintained for months or years, healing wounds, fighting off infections, growing, regulating temperature, in some of them gestating a fetus to the point of delivery. That looks like a lot of coordinated whole-body work happening without a brain.

(7) If death is loss of integration, then by (4)–(6) the vegetative and dementia patients have lost more of it than some declared-dead bodies have. So they're dead too. Or deader, anyway.

(8) That's absurd, and it isn't just absurd morally — legally and clinically we treat them as unambiguously alive, we treat them, we don't harvest them.

(9) So integration can't be what death consists in. And then the neurological criterion has no principled basis left: either it's a personhood criterion wearing a biology costume, which (2) rules out by its own lights, or the line it draws is just convenient.

He says (9) is the entire point of the exercise. I think something goes wrong somewhere around (4) through (6) but I cant pin it, and I know if I just say "you're equivocating on integration" he'll say the criterion is the equivocal one, since it's the one that needs integration to be biological while grading it in the way (4) grades it.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Go through the chain rather than jumping at the conclusion. Specifically I want: whether (4) slides between two senses of integration and if so which sense (1) and (3) actually need; whether (6) is a separate problem from (4) or the same one; what it costs to reject (1) or (2) instead — i.e. to keep the criterion and give up the integration rationale, or to keep the rationale and go higher-brain; and what happens if you just accept (9), since for someone who already endorses a cerebral criterion (7) reads as a conclusion rather than a reductio and I want to know what they're committed to that the rest of us aren't.

And the thing I most want settled: does the evidence in (6) cut against the criterion or against (1)? Both camps in the room claimed it — one side said it shows brain-dead bodies aren't disintegrated so they aren't dead, the other said it shows integration was never the right rationale to begin with. Those can't both be right, and I don't know which way the fact actually points.
