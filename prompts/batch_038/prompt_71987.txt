I evaluate public programmes for a living — deciding whether an intervention actually produced the outcome its sponsors claim — and for the past year I have been reading Nyāya epistemology on the side, chiefly because its account of inference takes the warrant for a generalisation seriously in a way that talk of sample sizes does not. I have run into a pattern that I cannot place in anything I have read, in Nyāya or elsewhere.

Two instances.

First: an evaluation finds that a job-training scheme raises earnings at every site where it has been run. A critic concedes the data and then says — suppose there is some further feature, call it F, present at every site where earnings rose and absent from at least some sites where the scheme ran and nothing happened. If F is what actually produces the rise, then the scheme is only a marker of F, and the inference does not go through. The critic names no F, cites no site where one operated, and agrees that no such F may exist at all. Still, they maintain that the conclusion is not established until F has been excluded, and that further observation of the same kind could never exclude it.

Second: in a dispute over whether a manuscript is genuine, one side points to a feature it shares with every known example of the author's hand. The other side says that feature could equally have been produced by a competent copyist working in the same workshop and period, and that until the existence of such a copyist is ruled out, the feature establishes nothing. Again, no copyist is alleged to exist; the bare possibility does all the work.

What strikes me as distinctive is the structure. The challenge posits a condition that would be present wherever the thing to be proved is present, yet not present wherever the reason is present, so that the reason would only ever coincide with the conclusion because of that condition — and the challenge is discharged by a condition nobody claims to have observed and which no further observations of the relevant kind could eliminate.

There is a wrinkle I care about even more. Once the first F is dealt with — the evaluator measures the obvious candidates and shows that they are not carrying the effect — the critic can propose F′, then F″, indefinitely, each one individually reasonable and each one untestable in the same way. The dispute can be kept alive forever without a single actual counterexample ever being produced. So it looks as though there are two different things here: a legitimate one-shot challenge of this form, and a serial version that is nothing but stalling.

What I want from you:

1. Does the challenge itself have an established name — in the Nyāya apparatus for what can go wrong with a reason, or in any other tradition that has worked on it? I know the familiar catalogue of defective reasons: the reason that strays outside the target, the reason that proves the opposite conclusion, the reason that is not really established in the subject, the reason already ruled out by some other knowledge source, and the reason matched by an equally good reason for the contrary. As I understand them, none of these involves a condition nobody claims to have observed — tell me plainly if I have misread the catalogue.

2. Take each of the nearest things I can think of on my own — a confounder or common cause, Reichenbach-style screening off, an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis, underdetermination by the evidence, and an undercutting defeater in the defeasibility literature — and say precisely where each comes apart from what I have described, and which of them, if any, is closest.

3. Is the difference between the one-shot version and the serial version captured anywhere? If so, what is the criterion that makes a hypothetical condition of this shape a genuine defeater rather than a stall, and what does it turn on — antecedent reason to think the condition is present, its specificity, whether it is independently checkable, something else?

4. I have also read that Nyāya's Buddhist critics ground a generalisation differently, in a causal or an identity relation rather than in observed agreement and difference. Say whether that changes how much force a challenge of this kind can have, and why.

5. If nothing fits, coin a term, define it, and give me the boundary cases that separate it from both its legitimate and its illegitimate neighbours.

I am not after a label. What I want is the carving: exactly what this move is, exactly what it is not, and where its edges fall. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
