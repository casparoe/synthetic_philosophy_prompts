I direct a first-person methods program at a cognitive science institute. Our core instrument is a beep-sampling protocol in the descriptive-experience-sampling tradition: participants carry a pager, are interrupted at random moments during ordinary activity, and are trained across several sessions to report what was present in their experience at the instant of the beep, after which an interviewer conducts a short structured conversation about that single moment. Our participants are mostly long-term meditation practitioners enrolled in a contemplative-care trial, and their reports are not treated as anecdote but as data — in several studies, as the primary data. Two objections from our external review panel have exposed gaps in the coding manual. The first is the general one: introspective access is unreliable in ways that are now well documented — people confabulate not only the causes of their attitudes but sometimes their contents; they answer questions about their own states partly by reasoning outward from the evidence and from what a person like them would plausibly feel; they are moved by the wording of a prompt; and, on the view of introspection my team keeps returning to, even ideally situated subjects are not merely noisy but persistently perplexed about their own experience in the moment, which is a worse problem because confusion does not average out across repeated measurements. The second objection is local and, I think, the harder one: our training may be changing what it claims to reveal. A practitioner who reports a sustained interval with no experiential content after forty hours of training may be exercising a newly acquired capacity, speaking in categories the interviewer supplied, or reconstructing the moment on demand, and nothing in the current protocol lets a coder tell these apart. One of my colleagues says the whole enterprise is navel-gazing with a spreadsheet; I disagree, but only if we can say precisely when a report earns its place in a dataset.

What I need is a triage procedure — not a defense of the method, not a survey of the debate, and not a set of design recommendations for future studies — that a research assistant with no background in philosophy of mind can run on one completed report in under ten minutes and record as a single grade plus a one-line justification in a spreadsheet column. It should proceed as ordered questions with explicit stopping points: what the coder asks first, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, and which answers terminate the procedure with a rejection rather than a downgrade. Central to it must be a requirement to localize the failure rather than globally discount the report. I want the procedure to force the coder to decide which stage went wrong — that no such state was ever present, that it was present but inaccessible, that it was accessed but not retained to the moment of reporting, that it was retained but mis-described in the interview, or that it was described only under a category the interviewer's phrasing imposed — and to explain, in terms the coder can act on, why the grade differs by stage: a report corrupted by the wording of a prompt is a different kind of failure from a report describing a state the subject never had, and one is fixable by re-coding while the other is not. Please build in the distinction between random error, which repeated sampling can wash out, and systematic error with a known direction, which can be corrected but only if someone has established the direction independently; the procedure should say what the coder is licensed to conclude in each case and should refuse the temptation to treat confident, articulate, richly detailed reports as more trustworthy than vague ones. It should also mark explicitly the places where the question is not empirical at all and the decision must be handed to a person: above all, whether a given report is a claim to have detected a state that was already there or an act by which the subject settles what her attitude is, since in the second case inaccuracy may simply be the wrong concept and a coder who grades it as a failed measurement has made a category error.

Alongside the procedure itself, I need four worked applications, each showing the coder's path through the steps and the grade that falls out. First: a practitioner reports a twenty-minute stretch of experience with no content whatsoever, and the concurrent EEG shows three brief episodes consistent with micro-sleep inside that stretch. Second: a participant reports a vivid visual image standing in the far corner of her awareness during a demanding task, while her detection performance at that location was at chance and the eye-tracking record shows no fixations anywhere near it. Third: a subject reports sharp irritation directed at the interviewer at the beep, then five minutes later insists she felt nothing of the kind and that the interviewer had done nothing to warrant irritation — though the interviewer's own contemporaneous notes confirm a genuine provocation. Fourth, and the one I expect to hurt: a report from the program's most highly trained subject, on which a paper already under review depends, which the procedure must be allowed to grade downward on grounds the procedure itself specifies rather than on my say-so. For each case, note where the procedure's output is a judgment call wearing a procedural costume, and say who signs off. Finally, close with an honest statement of the procedure's limits: what it can never license — in particular, whether agreement between independently collected reports can ever support a positive conclusion about the character of experience, or whether the procedure can only screen bad reports out — and what the institute should say to a reviewer who claims that any grading scheme of this kind simply encodes the researchers' theory of introspection into the data. Please write the answer in American English.
