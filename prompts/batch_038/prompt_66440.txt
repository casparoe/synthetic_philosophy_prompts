I direct design-ethics review at a mid-sized nonprofit that runs two lines of work: patient-engagement programs for chronic conditions, funded largely by public health grants, and a donor communications team. About eight submissions a month reach me: an SMS reminder sequence, a chatbot script, a default setting in the patient portal, an email appeal. I have two hours per submission, my two reviewers are a behavioral scientist and a former clinic operations manager, neither of whom has studied philosophy, sign-off is mine, and general counsel and the chief medical officer review my decisions quarterly rather than case by case. Legal exposure is not the problem. Everything we send is already truthful in the narrow sense and cleared by privacy counsel and our marketing rules. The problem is that our current internal test is two questions — is the claim true, and can the recipient opt out — and it passes nearly everything, including artifacts that our own clinicians find distasteful and could not explain why.

I want that replaced with a decision procedure my reviewers can run without me in the room: an ordered set of questions about a concrete artifact, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which a submission is returned with a no rather than sent back for revision, and the point at which the procedure stops producing an answer and hands the question to a human because what remains is a contest over whose ends are being served rather than a fact that can be established. Ground it in whatever the work on manipulation and legitimate persuasion offers that is actually usable: the difference between giving someone reasons and influencing them through channels that do not engage their capacity to assess reasons; the tests people have proposed for whether a technique would still work if its mechanism were disclosed to the person it works on; and the distinction between helping someone reach an end they already endorse and steering them toward an end that is ours. But state it as questions a non-specialist can answer about a screen, a script, or a mailing, not as a survey of positions. You do not need to cite or attribute anything; I need the distinctions, not the references. Where a step is a judgment call dressed as procedure, say so explicitly and say what the judgment consists in. Build in the asymmetry of our errors: a false negative means we deploy something that quietly degrades trust in a population we serve for years, and a false positive means we forgo a several-point gain in uptake on a screening program that prevents deaths.

Then run the procedure on three live submissions and report the answer each step yields, the final verdict, and, wherever the verdict turns on a judgment call, what fact or framing change would flip it.

First: patients who take no action in the portal are enrolled into a booked flu-shot appointment and receive an SMS telling them the appointment is reserved for them and that they may cancel by replying at any time. Cancellation is one tap. The reserved framing was adopted because it produced a nine-point uplift over a neutral reminder in a pilot.

Second: an adherence coach for type 2 diabetes medication. The script is deliberately client-led. It asks patients to state their own reasons for wanting to stay well, reflects their own words back, and asks them to rate and articulate a commitment to a plan they themselves propose. It asserts nothing, presents no statistic, and lets the patient set the tone. We regard this as our most respectful intervention and expect it to pass cleanly.

Third: a mailed reminder for colorectal cancer screening stating that 12 of every 100 people with the recipient's diagnosis who went untreated were hospitalized within a year. The figure is accurate for our catchment population and comes from the registry; no corresponding figure for treated patients is offered. A calmer sentence conveying the same absolute risk was available and was rejected after testing. We expect this one to fail.

If the procedure does not rank them the way we do, identify precisely which step produced the divergence and whether the step or our expectation is at fault. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the classes of submissions it will systematically misjudge, which way it errs, how reviewers will drift once they have run it forty times, and what to do about that drift.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, including your three verdicts, then give the procedure and the worked applications. No preamble and no restating of the question.
