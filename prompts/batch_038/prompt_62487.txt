The latest episode was about Bayesian confirmation theory, and the host pitched what he called the killer objection to the whole enterpise, the so-called problem of old evidence. Here's the gist, paraphrased from memory, so forgive the garbling:

- The showcase case is Einstein. General relativity got taken seriously largely because it accounted for the weird leftover bit in Mercury's orbit — the precession of the perihelion that astronomers had known about since the 1800s and couldn't explain with Newtonian gravity. Huge triumph, textbook stuff.
- But on Bayesianism that datum couldn't have raised the probability of relativity by one iota. Once you know the evidence for certain, P(e) = 1, and then P(h|e) is just P(h). Conditionalizing on something you're already sure of is a no-op.
- Which means Bayesians are stuck saying that everything a theory was built to explain — its whole track record, exactly the data it was designed for — is confirmationally worthless, and the only thing that ever counts is a guess about something nobody had observed yet. Novel prediction: worth something. Explained anomaly: worth nothing.
- The usual repairs don't help. One is to conditionalize on the fact that you came to learn e rather than on e itself. Another is Jeffrey conditioning, where the probability of the evidence itself gets to shift. But if P(e) was already 1, Jeffrey conditioning collapses back into ordinary conditioning, so it never touches the case that generated the problem.
- Therefore the problem of old evidence is the one objection to Bayesianism that can't be explained away.
