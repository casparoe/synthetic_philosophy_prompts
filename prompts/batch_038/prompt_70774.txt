I've been living in Zurich for two years now and I still can't get over the fact that my flatmate's assault rifle stands in the hallway cupboard between the vacuum cleaner and his skis, in a cloth bag, with no ammunition — the swiss decided to stop issuing the sealed box of rounds for home storage back in 2007 but left the weapon itself where it was, which I think is the whole political idea in a single object. He does a few weeks of refresher training every year and has since he was twenty, and in 2013 he voted, along with something like 73% of the country, to keep conscription exactly as it is. My reading group is finishing a term on the state's monopoly of force and the last session is on armies, and I want to open it with something that isn't a paper.

So: a pantoum, twenty lines, five quatrains, with the proper ending where the poem's first line comes back as the very last line and the opening quatrain's third line turns up as the second line of the final stanza. I want this form rather than a sonnet because conscription in these places isn't a block of years, its a thing that keeps coming back around — same man, same field, same three weeks, different government and different age each time — and the returning lines should carry that. Two of the four lines of the first quatrain should be the two claims the whole tradition trades on: that the army is only the people under arms, so it can't turn on them, and that the people are the army's raw material, so it can. Let the rotation move each of them into the position where the other one started, so that by the end neither is sitting where it began. And at least one of the repeated lines has to mean something different the last time it comes round than it did the first time, without a single word of it changing — that's the part I most want to see, because that's the part that's hard.

Please don't let it become a war poem. Nothing happens here, and the nothing-happening is the achievement: armed neutrality means what's being maintained is the absence of events, so a form that builds to a climax would be telling a lie. The speaker shouldn't be proud or traumatised or archly ironic, just somebody inside the rotation. Boring concrete details very welcome — the envelope from the recruitment centre, the state compensation for lost earnings, the extra 3% income tax you pay if you're declared unfit, which drops by four percent for every day of service you do manage, the colleague covering your shift, Sweden switching conscription off in 2010 and back on in 2017 and then calling up four thousand out of a hundred thousand nineteen-year-olds, so that most of them get measured, sorted and released. Rhyme if you can do it without straining; slant rhyme is fine and I'd rather have the images than a forced end-rhyme. No bugles, no poppies, no dulce anything. If it works, someone who knows the debate should be able to hear that the poem has taken seriously both the objection that this is a year of your life taken from you by force and the answer that a professional standing army is precisely what a republic ought to be afraid of, without either one ever being stated as an argument.
