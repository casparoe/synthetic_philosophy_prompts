I'm the discussion-section leader for a seminar week on the ethics of deception, and I want a one-page sorting instrument my section can fill in before we meet: a sequence of seven questions, each presenting a concrete speech act and two or three reactions to it, that lands the reader on their actual position in the lying-versus-misleading debate. Build it out of three separable issues, roughly in this order: whether a speaker can lie by saying something literally true (Frankfurt-style cases, plus the casuistry tradition's devices of equivocation and mental reservation); whether the relevant dividing line sits in the content asserted, in what the speaker intends the hearer to take away, or in what the hearer is entitled to expect from the exchange; and, if misleading turns out to be something other than lying, whether that difference carries any moral weight at all once the hearer has been steered into the same false belief either way.

The reader must be able to answer every question from the case alone, so none of them may ask for a verdict on a label, a slogan, or a definition, and none may name a theorist. Give each question a scoring key mapping answer combinations onto leaves, with at least: a view on which lying and misleading differ in kind and the difference is morally load-bearing; a view on which they differ in kind but the difference is morally idle; a view that collapses misleading into lying by widening what counts as asserted content; a view that collapses it by reference to the speaker's intention; a sceptical leaf holding that the line is too context-sensitive to do any of this work; and a leaf for the hearer's-entitlement account. Where a combination of answers has no defender, say so and diagnose whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has developed it. Finish by flagging any two leaves the questions cannot genuinely separate, rather than implying a cleaner triage than the cases support.
