I commission the alternate-history strand for a monthly history magazine — four-ish companion essays online a month, tied to whatever the print issue is doing — and our advisory board of academic historians has started pushing back hard. Their complaint is basically the old Carr charge that counterfactual history is a pastime for people who'd rather not do the work of explaining what actually happened. Fair enough, but I'm not shutting the strand down, and "it's unfalsifiable" isn't a usable rejection to me, because a huge amount of good economic history is counterfactual reasoning with the label scraped off — Fogel's railroads work is a giant what-if and it's about as rigorous as the field gets. So what I need is something my two fact-checkers can run on a pitch in twenty minutes flat. Both are bright, neither has any training in historiography, and their output either gets acted on by me or escalated to one of the board members.

Write me the procedure. Questions in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where it dead-ends in a reject, and where it hands the decision to a person because the sticking point is contested scholarship or taste rather than method. A few distinctions I already suspect belong in there, though tell me if I've got them wrong: whether the pitch contains a real fork at all (could the people at the time plausibly have chosen otherwise, or did the structural situation make the outcome near-inevitable no matter what anyone decided); how much of the actual past is allowed to travel along with the changed thing — the minimal-rewrite discipline the military historians use, and the Lewis-style question of which nearby world you're even describing once you start letting facts come along for the ride; and the difference between a one-shot counterfactual (one decision, one day) and a structural one (a rate, an institution, an absence), since the second kind seems to have no divergence point and I can't work out whether that makes it harder to do or just a different genre that needs its own rules. Also cover what the procedure does when two equally plausible rewrites point in opposite directions, and whether it's defensible for us to publish the range rather than picking a side. A friend of mine who teaches early modern history swears the dominant failure mode isn't a bogus antecedent at all — you can almost always find *some* nearby world — it's an untraceable consequent, where nobody can say anything beyond about two years out. Build that in if you think she's right, and say so if you don't.

Then run it on three live pitches. (1) The Archduke's driver doesn't take the wrong turn in Sarajevo in 1914, so no July crisis and no world war. (2) The Black Death never reaches Europe, and the fourteenth century plays out on pre-plague population and labour relations. (3) The one I've already half-commissioned and would like to save: the Armada gets Parma's troops across in 1588 and England goes back to Rome. For each, give me the verdict your procedure produces, the single sentence in the pitch that would have to be rewritten if it's a conditional pass, and which step did the killing. Be explicit about where the procedure is smuggling in an answer on determinism — my suspicion is that at least one of these rejections evaporates if I refuse to assume history is settled in advance, and I'd rather know that now than get caught out at the quarterly review. Finally, flag which of your steps are judgment calls in a checklist costume, because if my fact-checkers can't tell those apart from the mechanical ones they'll start killing pitches I like for reasons they can't articulate. No reading list, please — I've got a pile of books already and its the procedure I'm short of.
