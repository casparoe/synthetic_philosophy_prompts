I'm running a two-hour evening workshop for a mixed crowd — a few philosophy grad students, two astrophysics people, and a bunch of effective-altruism types who've only read the popular versions of this debate — and the topic is what's left of ethics if the universe turns out to contain infinitely much value. I want to hand everyone a decision tree on the way in and have them walk it silently before we talk, so the room sorts itself by what people actually believe rather than by which slogan they'd be embarrassed not to repeat.

The tree should start from a single commitment the whole room can share without arguing: that the universe is spatially infinite, that it therefore already contains infinitely many people with lives worth living, and that anything I do personally can only ever change things for finitely many of them. From there it should walk down to a position on what, if anything, that leaves me obliged to do. Aim for something like seven to nine nodes and ten-ish leaves, and a leaf can be reached by more than one route. Every node has to be a concrete question about a case, answerable by someone who has no idea where it leads — so no node may contain the words "aggregate", "anonymous", "utilitarian", "extended", "non-Archimedean" or "paralysis". The cases should be roughly these: a dimmer switch that sets the wellbeing level of every person in an infinite future population, where setting it a hair higher (100.0001 instead of 100) requires wiping out a village of ten thousand people right now; the same dimmer left alone, plus one extra happy person created somewhere whose existence nobody else ever learns about; a machine offering a one-in-ten-to-the-hundred chance of creating infinitely many happy lives versus the certainty of saving a billion people today; and a straight choice between making the infinite stretch of lives slightly better and making a finite stretch of them dramatically better. Nodes I'd expect to find somewhere along the way: whether one infinite total can be compared with another at all; whether it matters who gets the value or only how much value there is; whether you're allowed values that are infinitely large but still finely ordered, instead of no infinite values whatsoever; whether a tiny probability of something infinite beats a certainty of any finite good; whether "ought implies can" is meant to survive any of this; and whether someone is entitled to just deny the setup and go home.

For each leaf, use its name in the literature if it has one and write "unnamed" if you don't think anybody actually defends it, then add one line saying what the person standing there has to swallow. After the tree, tell me which combinations of answers my nodes permit that nobody occupies — and for each say whether that's because the view is incoherent, because it's repellant once stated out loud, or just because nobody's got round to it yet. Then flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really seperate, i.e. where the nodes hand out identical verdicts on all four of my cases and only diverge on cases I didn't list. Half the room is going to want to say "stop doing arithmetic and go help someone" — I want that route to end up somewhere honest and specific, not dumped at a leaf called confused. Start straight with the tree itself: no preamble, no summary of what you're about to produce, no restating of my setup.
