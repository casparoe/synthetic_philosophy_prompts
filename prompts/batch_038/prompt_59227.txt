I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, so I'd rather ask for a disciplined case analysis than a survey of positions.

The episode is the American spoils system and its collapse between 1881 and 1883. Charles Guiteau shot President Garfield in July 1881 after being refused the Paris consulship he believed his campaign work had earned; Garfield died eleven weeks later. In 1883 President Chester Arthur signed the Pendleton Act, which substituted competitive examinations for party patronage in a covered class of federal posts — and Arthur was himself a former New York machine man who had been collector of the port until President Hayes removed him for defying an order that barred officeholders from campaign work and forced contributions. Three features matter for my question. First, Guiteau neither offered nor expected a bribe in the ordinary sense: office was the publicly understood reward for political service, and appointees were in turn expected to remit part of their earnings to the party that had placed them. Second, Garfield's quarrel with Senator Conkling was over an appointment Conkling claimed a veto on by the informal custom of senatorial deference to a home-state senator — a norm the Senate treated as binding, and whose breach cost Conkling his seat when he resigned it in protest and lost the special election. Third, Pendleton initially covered only about a tenth of federal jobs, and later presidents kept widening the covered class, which I have read had the side effect of insulating their own appointees from whoever came next.

Take the working definition of corruption as deviation from the formal duties of a public role for private-regarding gain, alongside the rival line of thought on which gift-giving and patron-client ties are binding reciprocity rather than corruption, so that the impersonal-office standard is a local institutional achievement rather than a universal norm. Then: map the episode onto that definition — who here occupied a public role, what its formal duties were, where the private gain sits in each case. Say what the definition catches and what it misdescribes, for Guiteau, for the salary remittances, for Conkling's custom, and for Arthur's reversal, and in particular whether condemning Conkling requires importing a purpose for the office that the formal duties alone do not supply. Say whether the episode tells for or against the universality claim, and what would decide it. Finally, my own frame: an open-source maintainer who accepts money from a company and then prioritizes that company's features is usually judged acceptable if the arrangement is disclosed. Does disclosure offer a genuine alternative to prohibition, or does it presuppose the separation of office from person that the spoils system lacked? And mark where the analysis should stop because the definition has nothing to say.
