I write for a general-audience outlet that covers the border zone where technical science shades into metaphysics and policy, and I am currently reporting a long feature on the species problem: the fact that biologists working in good faith cannot agree on what a species is, that there are at least two dozen defensible definitions in circulation, and that the choice among them has consequences that are not merely academic, since conservation listings, trade restrictions, and hybrid-management decisions all inherit whatever ontology the taxonomist happens to favor. My editor's instinct is that this is too bloodless a controversy for a feature, and my counter-proposal is to route it through literature. Specifically, through H. G. Wells's The Island of Doctor Moreau, published 1896, which I think is the best narrative we have of what it feels like when a natural kind stops behaving like one.

What I want from you is a piece of criticism, not a plot summary and not a survey of the biology. Take the novel seriously as an argument about the metaphysics of kinds, and test that argument against the actual philosophical literature on the species problem.

Some specific pressure points I would like you to work through:

The Beast Folk are not a lineage. They are ontogenetic products, assembled by surgery out of animals that already belonged to existing species, several of them chimeric composites, none of them capable of reproducing as a population, all of them held together not by gene flow but by a chant and a punisher. That makes me suspect the island is not a failed speciation event but not a candidate for one. I want you to press this against the species-as-individuals thesis associated with Ghiselin and Hull: if a species is a spatiotemporally bounded historical particular rather than a repeatable class with an essence, then Moreau's project is not only monstrous but conceptually incoherent, because there is no kind-membership for a scalpel to confer. Does that reading make the novel deeper or does it simply anachronize it? And what becomes of the individual creatures — M'ling above all, who is presented as a patchwork and yet is unmistakably a someone — under a view on which individuality is precisely what matters and kind-membership is derivative?

Then run the standard menu of species concepts against the island and tell me whether any of them yields a determinate answer about what these creatures are. Mayr's biological species concept and its well-known failures; the diagnostic and phylogenetic alternatives, and the multiplication of taxa they produce; de Queiroz's attempt to reconcile the disagreement by demoting the various concepts to operational criteria under a single general lineage idea; Ereshefsky's more radical suggestion that we should stop looking for the correct concept and keep an honest pluralism instead. I am especially interested in whether there is a scene in which the answer visibly depends on which concept the reader brings — the puma who becomes a woman, the Leopard-Man's transgression and punishment, the Sayer of the Law's authority, the Ape-Man's claim on Prendick.

Reversion is the novel's central biological claim and I do not know what to do with it. Moreau insists that the animal nature comes back, that the flesh reasserts itself over time. Read as Lamarckian atavism this is simply bad 1890s biology. But read as a claim about developmental canalization, or about homeostatic property clusters and the difficulty of relocating an individual across a kind boundary by intervention on its parts alone, it says something quite different and arguably still interesting. Which reading does the text actually support, and does the difference matter for what the novel is arguing?

The Law is where I most want your skepticism. If kind-membership on the island is a status conferred and policed by a litany rather than a biological fact, Wells may be dramatizing a thoroughly social-constructionist picture of taxa. Or he may only be saying that civilization is a thin veneer over appetite, which is a much duller claim and the one every reviewer since 1896 has made. Take the first possibility as far as it will bear weight, then tell me where it snaps.

Finally, the frame narrative and the ending: Prendick's return to England, his solitude, his inability to stop seeing the beast in his fellow citizens. Does the epilogue convert a taxonomic anxiety into an ethical one, and what does that conversion cost?

Two conditions on the piece. First, I want it faithful in both directions. Include an explicit section arguing against my framing — where I am importing a debate Wells could not have had, where the novel actively resists being read through the species problem, and where the more natural readings (vivisection ethics, imperial allegory, degeneration, the problem of pain, a theodicy in which Moreau plays a cruel and indifferent god) do better explanatory work with less strain. Second, tell me what the novel contributes that a journal article on species concepts structurally cannot — something about the phenomenology of classification failure, the felt experience of a boundary dissolving under one's hands, that no formal definition carries.

On practicalities: no bibliography, no reading list, and do not reproduce lines of Wells from memory; I would rather have your paraphrase and my fact-checker against the text than a quotation you are not certain of. State your thesis in a single sentence at the top. Flag any plot detail or attribution you are not confident about — including one of mine, since I believe Prendick early on traces his own scientific formation to Huxley, and I may be conflating this novel with another. Length: roughly two thousand words, with a clear argumentative spine rather than a tour.
