I'm the duty officer for a county technical rescue outfit — swift water, rope, some collapsed structure — and about thirty or forty times a year I have to decide, on the radio, where the one helicopter or the one heavy crew goes. Right now our draft SOP has a single line in it that says commit resources to the option with the highest expected number of survivors, and it's causing me problems. Two of them are recent. In one, a storm put a party of six tourists on a roof in the north fork and pinned two teenagers in a car down a seperate drainage, and the bird had fuel for one, maybe two lifts before dark; I sent it north and the reasoning I wrote in the after-action report was basically six beats two, which our union rep then read back to me as if it were arithmetic. In the other, we held a crew on a kayaker who was alive but had maybe an hour of cold water tolerance left, rather than pulling them to four swimmers who were savable for the next six hours, and I think that call was right but our SOP can't tell me why, because it has no way to keep the probability question apart from the numbers question and it collapses them into one multiply.

I've been reading Taurek on whether the numbers count, and something in it has stuck with me: the idea that there isn't an entity out there that loses six, that each of those deaths is the whole of the loss for the person it happens to, so my SOP is summing things that don't live in the same place. I also take seriously his thought that when you genuinely can't save everyone you owe each of them a fair chance rather than just a tally — but I can't hand a crew chief a doctrine that says flip a coin, on an open channel, with a caller on the other end listening, and have anybody still trust us. Our county attorney also wants whatever we adopt to be auditable three months later, and neither he nor the fire chief has read any philosophy, nor should they have to.

So: write the decision procedure I could actually drop into the SOP and run in under ten minutes while tired and on the radio. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where numbers are allowed to enter the decision and where they're barred from entering it, how the procedure treats the two things my SOP currently conflates — how many could be saved versus how likely it is that we save them at all — and what to do when two groups really are evenly matched on everything that's left over, including how a random choice would be executed and recorded in a way that a reviewer could audit and a family could be told about. Mark clearly every point where the procedure stops and hands the call to a person because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. Then be honest with me about the translation itself: which parts of Taurek's argument survive into a field document and which don't, and where my procedure is at risk of quietly reintroducing aggregation through the back door — say by letting a probability weighting do the summing that the numbers were supposed to be barred from doing. Run it on the three cases I described (the six versus two, the kayaker versus the four swimmers, and construct a third where the groups are evenly matched) and tell me which result I'm going to hate. Finally say what the procedure cannot do — I suspect it can't be used to justify a decision that was already made on instinct, and I want that limitation written down rather than discovered at the review board.
