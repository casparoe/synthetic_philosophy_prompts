I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so please write for someone who is fluent in mathematics but new to this literature, and define any technical term the first time you use it.

Here is my situation. I work on a system that ranks long-horizon interventions by expected value. The payoff distributions are heavy-tailed and sometimes have no finite upper bound, and the state space is effectively infinite because we model outcomes over an unbounded time horizon. In practice we truncate the tail and estimate by simulation, and I have become uneasy that the truncation is not an implementation detail but a substantive assumption doing normative work that nobody on my team can articulate. A colleague pointed me at the Pasadena game as the cleanest philosophical version of my problem, and it looks to me as though it is exactly that: flip a fair coin until it first lands heads on toss n; if n is odd you win 2^n/n dollars, if n is even you lose 2^n/n dollars. Since the probability that the first heads arrives on toss n is 2^-n, the expectation is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 in the natural order but only conditionally — so by Riemann's rearrangement theorem the terms can be reordered to yield any real number you like, or positive or negative infinity.

I do not want an answer to whether one should play, and I do not want a survey of what people have said. I want the specification: the list of requirements an account of rational choice for gambles with undefined expectations must meet to count as adequate, which I can then use to evaluate proposals, including proposals that come out of my own field. Note that my purpose is narrower than a philosopher's in one respect and broader in another: I need a theory that also yields verdicts an implementer could act on, not merely a correct diagnosis of a paradox. Please say where those two purposes pull the requirements list in different directions.

For each requirement, give me three things: the consideration or concrete case behind it; a short acceptance test, meaning a specific gamble or pair of gambles that a candidate theory gets wrong if it fails the requirement; and your judgment of whether it is non-negotiable or whether it is really the preference of one camp dressed up as a requirement.

I have drafted some candidate requirements myself. Please assess each — keep it, sharpen it, or tell me it is an engineer's prejudice that smuggles in the conclusion:

1. Order invariance. The verdict must not depend on the order in which I happen to enumerate the states, since the enumeration is an artifact of my data structure and carries no normative information.
2. Finite approximability. Whatever answer the theory gives must be approachable to within any epsilon by examining only finitely many states. I suspect this may rule out the cases I care about by fiat, but I cannot see how to give it up and still compute anything.
3. Stability under perturbation. A small change to the payoff schedule should not flip the ranking, in the sense that a numerically unstable routine returns wildly different results for nearly identical inputs.
4. Conservative extension. On gambles whose expectation series converges absolutely, the theory must agree with ordinary expected utility maximization.
5. Closure under mixtures, or no sure loss. If the theory's separate verdicts can be combined into a portfolio that loses money however the coin lands, that is a defect rather than a curiosity.
6. Statewise dominance. If one act pays at least as well as another in every state and strictly better in some, it must not be ranked below it.
7. Non-arbitrariness of any cutoff. If the theory tells me to truncate, it must say where and why there, rather than leaving the cutoff to be tuned until the answer looks acceptable.
8. One pathology or two. Should the specification treat an expectation that diverges to positive infinity, as in the St. Petersburg game, and an expectation that fails to exist at all, as in Pasadena, as the same problem with the same demands, or as different problems?

Then, separately:

- Identify any subset of these requirements, or of the ones you add, that cannot be jointly satisfied. This is the part I care about most. If the list is over-constrained, the inconsistency is itself the finding, and I would rather learn which of my own intuitions has to go than be handed a list that looks coherent.
- Tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories.
- Tell me what a good specification would have to require that I have missed. In particular, I want to know whether there should be a requirement governing a gamble whose description happens to present the outcomes in a fixed order, and whether such a presentation can carry normative weight at all, or whether that amounts to letting the notation choose the answer.
- Finally, and briefly, no more than a few lines each: state which requirements are failed by bounding the utility function; by representing uncertainty with a set of probability functions rather than a single one; by adopting a rule that is not linear in the probabilities, such as minimax regret; and by restricting the theory to finite state spaces.

Aim for ten to fourteen requirements in total. Spend most of your length on the requirements and their acceptance tests rather than on the comparison at the end, and please do not include references or a reading list.
