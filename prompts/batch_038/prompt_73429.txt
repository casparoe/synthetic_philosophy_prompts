I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student and next week I'm running the "measurement" session of a 200-level interdisciplinary seminar on numbers and public policy — 22 students, roughly half philosophy and poli sci, half CS/data-science minors, 75 minutes, they sit at five tables of four or five. Here's my problem: they have all had Goodhart's law before, in at least two other classes. They can recite the Soviet nail factory, the cobra bounty, the emergency-department wait-time scandals, and the line about a measure ceasing to be a good measure once it becomes a target, all without any prompting from me, and it has gone completely inert. They treat it as a cute gotcha about *other people's* institutions. So I don't want another case-study discussion or a slide on indicator regimes. I want them to *be* the institution, to be explicitly told up front that their score is only a proxy, to nod sagely at that, and then to optimize it into the ground anyway. The takeaway I'm after is that knowing the law is not protection from it.

So: design me a runnable game. Pick a low-stakes, slightly funny but concrete public program for them to operate (something like a city tree-planting office, a shelter intake desk, a department's tutoring scheme — your call), and run it over at least four rounds. Each round, every table spends a fixed budget of effort tokens across, say, five available actions; you as facilitator score them on one public proxy metric announced on a card; the true target value is tracked secretly by you and only revealed at the very end, alongside each table's proxy score. I want the four flavors of the thing staged at least once each and never labeled: a proxy that's correlated with the target but noisy, so pushing it up buys less than expected; a correlation that holds inside the observed range and falls apart past it; a proxy that's a symptom rather than a cause, so purchasing it does literally nothing; and one action that inflates the score while actively damaging the target. Please give me the actual arithmetic — a table of actions, token cost, proxy points, true-target effect, and note where those numbers change from round to round — because I have to run this live and I can't improvise a payoff matrix. Index cards, one printed sheet per table, and maybe dice is the materials ceiling; no real money, no apps.

The bit I care most about is a twist of my own, and you should tell me honestly if it won't survive contact with 22 undergrads. I want one table — or one late round for everyone — to be a control condition where the proxy is genuinely good: tightly correlated, causally upstream, awkward to game. And I want that table to *still* come out worse than its own score predicts, because the target is partly constituted by the measuring of it — something like "residents feel the street is safe," which is partly a function of being surveyed about it and of what the survey gets recorded as, so running an immaculately designed scorecard shifts the thing the scorecard is tracking. I want the debrief to arrive at the question of whether that is still Goodhart's law or a different animal: divergence between proxy and target versus no independently specifiable target to diverge from. If you think that collapses into confusion in the time I have, say so and hand me a defanged version instead. Also give me: what you say out loud versus what you just do as facilitator; a ~15-minute debrief with beats mapped onto each mechanism and onto the constitutive question; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my specific fears are the CS kids reverse-engineering your hidden target function by round two, and the philosophy kids refusing to game because they suspect they're being tested for virtue — I want fixes for both, plus whatever else you expect); a frank note on which feature of the phenomenon the game structurally cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking; and one compressed variant for 40 people in 50 minutes.
