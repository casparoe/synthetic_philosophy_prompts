I am applying for a one-year fellowship at my university's humanities institute, and the application requires a two-page proposal. My half-formed idea concerns Mill's unfinished Chapters on Socialism, published after his death. The standard readings treat that work as Mill's reckoning with the continental socialist schools, and then take it as evidence either that he drifted leftward late in life or that his utilitarian liberalism was latently socialist all along. I think both readings miss the hinge of the argument. My hypothesis is that Mill's critique runs on a premise about the motivational plasticity of human beings: he concedes — indeed welcomes — that voluntary, small-scale cooperative association can reshape character and produce forms of public spirit that wage labor does not, but he denies that the reorganization of an entire economy can generate the level of disinterestedness its incentive structure would require. If that is right, his disagreement with the socialists is not primarily about justice or desert, but about the institutional scale at which predictions about human behavior under changed incentives remain reliable, and his verdicts ought to be scale-sensitive in a way his text never makes explicit.

Turn this into a proposal. State the question precisely enough that a reader could tell what would settle it. Explain why the two standard readings leave the question open rather than answering it, and what a successful result would look like — a claim that would change how a reader approaches Mill's later economic and political writings, not a tidier summary of them. Lay out the method stage by stage across the twelve months: which parts of the corpus I would put under pressure (Chapters on Socialism, the closing book of the Principles of Political Economy on the probable future of the labouring classes and on the stationary state, the Autobiography, his remarks on cooperation and on land tenure), in what order, and why a textual reconstruction has to be paired with a constructive step asking whether the plasticity premise is itself defensible rather than merely attributable to Mill. The fellowship also expects a stated deliverable; I have in mind two articles and a workshop, but tell me if that is overreach.

Then say what would count as the project failing. I want two failure conditions addressed explicitly: the possibility that the plasticity premise turns out to do no real work in Mill's argument, so the reconstruction yields no payoff; and the possibility that the constructive half slides into a general debate about ideal and non-ideal theory with nothing distinctively Millian left in it. Give me an honest assessment of what would make this too large for a year, and propose the specific cut you would make.

Please do not produce a bibliography or a literature review by citation — I will insert the passages and references myself; refer to positions and arguments only. And I want commitment rather than a menu. Where the proposal has to choose — between reading Mill as consistent and reading him as inconsistent, between a historical and a reconstructive center of gravity, between the two failure conditions I have named — choose, and give the reason. If you think my hypothesis is mistaken in some particular respect, say so plainly and propose the better project in its place rather than hedging quietly around mine.
