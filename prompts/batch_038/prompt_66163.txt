So my debate partner and I put together this argument about borders and it ended up proving something way weirder than what we started with, and I can't find where it breaks. It goes:

(1) If you own a place, you get to decide who comes into it, for a reason or for no reason at all.
(2) A country's authority over its territory is just the authority of all the individual owners inside it pooled together — a state can't hold a right that its members didn't already have.
(3) So a country gets to decide who comes in, for a reason or no reason.
(4) Pretty much every stretch of land a person could actually live on belongs to some country. So all the countries, each one just exercising the right from (3), could between them decide that one specific person isn't allowed into any of them.
(5) Somebody who isn't allowed into any country isn't allowed to be anywhere, and if your not allowed to be anywhere you aren't allowed to exist.
(6) But a property right that includes the right to make someone's existence impossible is basically a right to kill them, and (1) obviously doesn't give you that.
(7) So (1) is false, and countries can't just turn people away because they feel like it.

Every single step looks like it's only adding stuff up, but the conclusion is doing way more work then I gave it credit for. I'd really like to keep (1), because saying a homeowner has to justify turning someone away at their own front door feels wrong to me, and I also don't want to accept (5). Which step do you cut, and what does cutting it cost you in other cases? Also my partner says you can patch it by sticking "unless that person has literally nowhere else to go" onto (1), but that sounds to me like it's smuggling the whole conclusion into the premise. Does the patch actually work?
