I'm in a philosophy-of-history reading group and we've been going around in circles for three weeks about where archival silences actually come from, and I want to settle it with a contructed case rather than another round of everyone reaching for the example they already know. There are basically two camps. Camp one — call it the suppression account — says the record is skewed because power acts on records and on record-makers: things get destroyed, weeded at appraisal, redacted, classified, priced out, or the people concerned were never let near the apparatus that makes documents in the first place. On that view silence is something done to a corpus, and it should shrink as preservation, access, and inclusiveness improve. Camp two — my friend M, who's read some Foucault — says that's looking in the wrong place: the skew is fixed upstream, at the moment of creation, because institutions only ever produce documents in genres, and a genre is a list of fields, and the fields decide in advance what can be stated at all. A payroll ledger can carry a wage and cannot carry a grievance about the way wages are counted. On that view a perfectly preserved, perfectly open, perfectly inclusive archive would be just as skewed as a burned one, which seems like a strong claim to me but I can't pin down why.

Build me a case that seperates them. One paragraph of scenario, realistic — a real kind of institution, no sci-fi, no stipulated mind-reading. Constraints: total capture (nothing destroyed, nothing weeded, nothing redacted, everything digitised and indexed, open to anyone who walks in off the street); no villain (nobody is hiding anything, the record-keepers are diligent and even unusually well intentioned, and the people whose lives are at issue are not merely present in the archive but heavily documented); and no cheap exits — the question the archive fails to answer can't be one that's unanswerable by any archive in principle, and the failure can't turn on illiteracy, a language barrier, restricted access, or the group being barred from making records of their own. It also has to survive reading against the grain: I want a question where inference from what IS there genuinely can't close the gap, no matter how clever or how patient the historian is. Give me the question concretely, the sort of thing a social historian would actually want to know and could reasonably hope to find out.

Then do the check. Say what each camp predicts about the case and what actually happens, and name the single feature that's doing the work — plus what happens to the case if you take that feature out. And give me the best attack camp one can make: I expect something like "whoever settled on the reason codes and the intake form was exercising power over the record too, camp two is just camp one relocated a bit earlier", and I want to know whether that attack lands, i.e. whether camp two is an independent thesis or a redescription of camp one wearing a different hat. That's the whole point of the case, so please don't just stipulate the verdicts — they have to fall out of the setup or the case is worthless to me. One formatting thing: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose the way you'd write an email, and open with a sentence or two on whether you think a case meeting all my constraints is even possible, since M and I disagree about that as well.
