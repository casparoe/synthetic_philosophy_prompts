I run the drug information service at a 400-bed teaching hospital — me plus two rotating PGY2 residents — and between the written consults and the 3am phone calls we field maybe thirty real questions a month. Every answer gets documented in the chart with a name attached to it.

The pharmacology is mostly not the hard part. The hard part is deciding how confident we're allowed to sound.

Here's the pattern that bothers me. The evidence is often thin — a case series, a compatibility table from the nineties, one adult crossover study getting stretched over a 28-weeker — and it stays equally thin across questions whose stakes are nothing alike. Whether we can crush a tablet for a hospice patient who's lost her swallow, and whether we can sign off an unlicensed dose for a neonate on ECMO, can rest on exactly the same grade of evidence, and yet my gut says the first is something I know and the second is something I don't. I've read enough philosophy to know there's a standing fight about what to say about this: one side says the practical stakes genuinely change whether the person has the knowledge, the other side says the stakes only change what the word knows means in the mouth of whoever's asking, and what the pharmacist herself is entitled to is a separate matter. I don't need you to referee that fight and I don't want a literature essay. But I do want the procedure to be runnable by someone who hasn't taken a side, and I want it to flag at each step which of those two moves it's making — because they come apart in practice, and one place they come apart matters a lot to me: our chart entries get read two years later by people standing in a very different conversational context than the resident who phoned at 3am.

So: write me a decision procedure my residents can run, on one page. Specifically I want:

- The questions asked in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each — not "assess the evidence" but what you actually look for and when you're allowed to stop looking.
- The stakes variable made explicit. What features of a case raise or lower the bar for asserting something flatly, and roughly how much. I don't want fake numbers, just an ordered scale with the features that move you along it.
- The exits. Where the procedure stops at "I can't answer that in the time you have", where it produces a hedged answer of a specified form, where it licenses a flat yes or no, and where it hands the call to me instead of to the literature.
- Something on two-sided stakes: cases where the cost of being wrong and the cost of hedging both land on the same patient. Right now we have no way to talk about those.
- A rule about error possibilities that somebody in the conversation has already said out loud. My experience is that once it's been named — by the prescriber, by the family, by me — it stops being safely ignorable, and I want that built in or a reason why not.
- Timing. Fifteen minutes max on a written consult, ninety seconds on a stat call. Tell me which steps get dropped in the ninety-second version and what that costs.
- Honesty about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes.

Then run it end to end on three cases, and for each give both what the responder says out loud and what goes in the chart, since those have different audiences:

1. A 3.1 kg ex-28-weeker on ECMO. The question is a dosing regimen for an antifungal that's only ever been modelled in adults plus two neonatal case reports. The attending wants a number within the hour and there's no paediatric ID cover tonight.

2. A hospice patient at home, no longer swallowing, PEG in situ. Family on the phone asking whether we can crush her modified-release oxycodone. Prescriber wants a yes or no now. Dose dumping would be grim, but she has days to live and uncontrolled pain is also a harm.

3. A mother six weeks postpartum, bipolar, on a mood stabiliser, asking whether she can carry on breastfeeding. Evidence thin and pulling both ways. My honest worry is that if we hedge she stops the drug cold and relapses, and if we say continue and something turns up in the infant we've hurt a baby. I expect your procedure to tell me to hedge and escalate here and I expect to hate that. If that's what it says, say it plainly rather than softening it, and tell me whether that's a defect in the procedure or a defect in me.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do — the class of questions it will systematically get wrong or push onto me, and what it's doing to my residents' judgment if they run it a hundred times a year without ever having to think about the thing it's standing in for.
