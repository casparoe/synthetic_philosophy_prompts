I have spent fifteen years in medical-device risk management: hazard analysis and failure-mode work at the front end, clinical evaluation and benefit–risk determination in the middle, post-market surveillance and field safety corrective actions at the back. I read philosophy for pleasure but I do not think in its vocabulary. When a philosopher writes about markets I find myself silently translating into hazard logs and risk controls, and I suspect the translation is lossy in an interesting way.

Here is the material I want translated. A certain strand of the debate over repugnant markets — Alvin Roth's observation that repugnance operates as a constraint on the transactions societies will permit, and the more ambitious claim pressed by philosophers such as Elizabeth Anderson, Michael Sandel and Debra Satz — holds that some exchanges are objectionable not, or not only, because of how they distribute harm, but because putting a good up for sale degrades the good and corrupts the attitudes properly taken towards it. Richard Titmuss's comparison of voluntary and commercial blood supply is routinely recruited as evidence for the stronger claim: that paying donors made the supply worse and somehow made giving less valuable. My question is whether that claim survives translation into my own working vocabulary.

Please attempt the translation as faithfully as you can, using three running test cases: financial compensation for live kidney donors, commercial surrogacy arrangements, and paid plasma donation. Be warned about the two obvious ways to cheat. First, do not quietly substitute harm for degradation and then congratulate yourself on a clean mapping; if the thesis collapses into a claim about harm to identifiable parties, say so explicitly and log it as a distortion rather than a translation. Second, do not import my framework's presuppositions — that risk is a product of severity and probability of occurrence, that the relevant parties are those who can be injured, that residual risk is whatever remains after controls — and present them as neutral renderings of what the philosophers meant.

Structure your answer under the following headings, in this order, and keep to the indicated lengths.

**1. The thesis to be translated** (about 200 words). State the corruption claim precisely enough that it could in principle fail. Distinguish it from the neighbouring objections with which it is routinely conflated: exploitation of the badly off, the background injustice that makes the offer attractive in the first place, asymmetries of information and bargaining power, and preference-based disgust treated as data. If the claim cannot be prised apart from those, say so; that is itself a finding.

**2. The translation** (about 500 words). Work through the components one at a time — the hazard, the hazardous situation and the sequence leading to harm, the affected party, the severity and occurrence estimates, the detectability question, the available risk control options and what each leaves by way of residual risk, the benefit–risk determination, and the post-market surveillance obligations that would follow from permitting the transaction at all. For each component, state plainly whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has to be stipulated from outside my framework. Use the three test cases to show where the mapping holds and where it buckles.

**3. The residue** (about 300 words). List what cannot be expressed at all: claims that the value of the thing itself changes rather than the welfare of its users; harms that fall on people who never entered the transaction; effects on the standing of a class of persons rather than on any individual; the thought that a market in something alters how that thing is properly regarded even by those who decline to buy it. Keep the list honest — if any of these turns out to be expressible after all, move it back into Section 2 and explain what did the work.

**4. Diagnosis** (about 300 words). For each genuine residue, give your judgement: is this a defect of risk-management thinking, which has nowhere to put a harm that is not a harm to someone, or a sign that the corruption thesis was less determinate than its proponents supposed? Argue for the assignment rather than asserting it. Where you judge the defect to lie in my framework, propose the smallest amendment that would let it express the claim — a new axis on the severity scale, a widened definition of the affected party, a distinct category of non-safety risk carried in the technical file — and say what that amendment would cost in auditability.

**5. Bottom line** (about 150 words). Given what survived and what did not, would you advise me to go on translating this debate into risk vocabulary when I sit on ethics committees, or is the mismatch large enough that the translation does more harm than good?

Please write in British English. I do not want a bibliography and I do not want invented citations; refer to positions and arguments as such, and I will supply the references myself.
