I'm working my way through a widely recommended intellectual-history paperback, and this is the treatment it gives the master–slave dialectic:

"Two self-consciousnesses meet, and neither can be a self on its own: each needs to be recognized by the other. They therefore fight, and each must risk its life, because only a consciousness that holds its own life cheap has shown that it is more than a merely living thing. The one that cannot face death submits and becomes the bondsman. The master now enjoys recognition and consumes the products of the bondsman's labour without labouring himself. But the recognition he receives is worthless, since it comes from someone he does not recognize in turn; so the master turns out to be the dependent one, a hollow figure whose standing rests on a subordinate he despises. The bondsman, meanwhile, works on the world, finds his own form stamped into things, and is thereby on the way to genuine self-consciousness and freedom. Hegel's moral is that freedom is won by transforming the world through labour — which is why Marx could simply invert the passage and take it over."

The book then applies all of this to contemporary life, arguing that recognition from anyone with no standing to withhold it — an assistant's praise, an audience's applause, a machine's compliments — is structurally worthless for exactly the reason the master's was.

I've spent far more time in status-and-signalling threads than in German idealism, so I can't tell where this bends. Could you audit it sentence by sentence: what a Hegel scholar would sign off on, what is an acceptable popular compression, and what is actually wrong? Two points I'd especially like settled. First, is the labour-is-freedom moral Hegel's own, or a later reading filtered through twentieth-century interpreters — and what does the argument do immediately after the bondsman's formation, given that the book implies his story ends in freedom? Second, does that closing inference about recognition survive correction of the passage, or does it rest on a claim about the power to withhold recognition that Hegel never makes? I'd also like to know whether the framing as masters and slaves is itself a translation artefact worth flagging to the book's readers.
