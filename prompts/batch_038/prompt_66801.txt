Write a dialogue between a Nyāya epistemologist who defends the four pramāṇas and a Buddhist in the Dignāga/Dharmakīrti line who thinks there are only two — perception and inference, with everything else a package deal of those plus memory.

Here's the case they're arguing over. I made it up, so please actually engage with it instead of retreating to the usual wild-ox/gavaya one: a birder is told by a local guide that a certain bird's call "sounds like a rusty gate hinge." She has never seen or heard the bird. Weeks later, walking in the forest, she hears a call that strikes her as exactly that, and she comes to know — correctly, let's say — that it's the bird the guide described. No generalization available to her links hinge-like sounds to that species, and the guide's words didnt tell her what she now believes about the sound in front of her.

I want both of them at full strength. The Buddhist needs a real reduction, not hand-waving about "it's just memory plus expectation," and the Naiyāyika needs something more than the observation that the content feels new to her. Have them also fight about the method behind the counting: what would actually settle how many pramāṇas there are — irreducibility of the resulting cognition, the kinds of objects known, the reliability of the process, something else? No cheap win at the end. Don't bother with citations or sourcing, I'm not after a bibliography.

Format, in American English:

- **Setup** (~100 words): what the two already agree on, so the disagreement comes out clean.
- **The exchange** (~1000 words).
- **Scorecard** (~150 words): which moves landed for each side and which ones didn't.
- **Least sure** (~60 words): the claim in the whole thing you'd be most nervous defending.

And tell me how confident you are in the scorecard verdicts, including whether you think you've stated each school's position fairly — I know the scholarship here is contested and I don't expect you to hedge around that, just flag it.
