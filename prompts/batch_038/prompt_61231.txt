**DRAFT — Reach-Sensitive Induction: What the Centipede Argument Actually Proves**

**1. The game**

Consider a six-move centipede of the familiar Rosenthal shape. Players A and B alternate, A moving first. At each of six decision nodes the mover chooses Take or Pass. Take ends the game at the payoff pair shown in the table; Pass hands the decision to the other player at the next node. If B passes at the sixth node, the game ends at (8, 6). Payoffs are written (A, B).

| Node | Mover | If Take | If Pass |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | (2, 0) | to node 2 |
| 2 | B | (1, 3) | to node 3 |
| 3 | A | (4, 2) | to node 4 |
| 4 | B | (3, 5) | to node 5 |
| 5 | A | (6, 4) | to node 6 |
| 6 | B | (5, 7) | (8, 6) |

Three properties of this table do the work in what follows.

(i) At node 6, B's choice is unambiguous: Take pays her 7 and Pass pays her 6, and there is no further continuation to form a view about. This is the anchor of the whole argument.

(ii) At each of nodes 1 through 5, Take pays the mover strictly more than she would receive if the opponent took at the immediately following node, and strictly less than she would receive if everybody passed from that point to the end. Take at an early node is therefore neither dominant nor dominated: it is optimal under some conjectures about the continuation and not under others.

(iii) The payoff both players would receive by passing all the way through, (8, 6), Pareto-dominates every payoff that any take anywhere in the game can produce. The centipede is accordingly not a conflict game in the way the prisoner's dilemma is. There is no tension between the two players' interests at the far end of the tree — only between each player's interest and the structure of the argument itself.

Backward induction from the anchor: at node 6, B takes, since 7 > 6. So at node 5, A takes, since 6 beats the 5 she would get by passing. So at node 4, B takes, since 5 beats 4. So at node 3, A takes, since 4 beats 3. So at node 2, B takes, since 3 beats 2. So at node 1, A takes, since 2 beats 1. The unique subgame-perfect equilibrium is immediate termination at (2, 0).

**2. Two repairs, and why neither touches the inference**

There are two standard ways of responding to this, and I want to set them aside, because both accept the inference and quarrel only with its inputs.

The first is descriptive. Subjects in the laboratory do not take at node 1; they pass, often several times; therefore backward induction predicts badly. But backward induction is not a statistical hypothesis about human beings. It is a claim about what follows from premises concerning rationality and common knowledge of rationality. Observing that the premises are rarely satisfied by an undergraduate subject pool does not rebut the inference; it changes the subject.

The second perturbs the premises. Give each player a small prior probability of facing a type who passes for non-strategic reasons, and rational players will pass for most of the game in order to signal that they are such types. This is more interesting, but it is also, so to speak, a concession: it agrees that if common knowledge of ordinary rationality obtained, the induction would go through, and denies the antecedent. Neither repair is about the inference.

**3. The shape of the inference**

What I think is strange about the inference has nothing to do with whether anybody is in fact rational.

The conclusion of the argument is that node 2 is never reached. Yet every step of the argument after the anchor is a claim about what a player would do at a node the conclusion declares unreachable. The argument is a chain of counterfactuals whose antecedents are, on the argument's own showing, impossible: the final step tells us what A does at node 1 given what B would do at node 2, and what B would do at node 2 is something the argument has already established cannot happen.

Counterfactual reasoning about impossible antecedents is not automatically illegitimate — we do it constantly, and the whole apparatus of subgame perfection is built out of it. But it does mean that the conclusion draws its entire content from premises about a region of the tree that the conclusion then deletes. And if those premises are not reports of what a rational player would do — since at a node that cannot be reached there is nothing for them to report on — we should ask what they are instead.

**4. Reach-sensitive induction**

My proposal is to restrict the optimality requirement to nodes that are live relative to the players' own conjectures, and to require conjectures and actions to be settled together rather than in sequence.

Call a node *live*, relative to a profile and a system of conjectures, if it is reached with positive probability when the players follow that profile; call it *dead* otherwise. A profile together with a conjecture-system is **reach-sensitive admissible** (RS-admissible) when:

(a) at every live node, the mover's action maximizes her expected payoff given her conjecture at that node;
(b) at every live node, her conjecture about the continuation is derived from the profile;
(c) at dead nodes, no requirement whatsoever is imposed on action or conjecture.

Backward induction is not RS-valid in the centipede. Its first step is a claim about B's behavior at node 6, and its conclusion makes nodes 2 through 6 dead. Clause (c) is precisely the clause that would have to license an inference backwards from a dead node to a live one, and RS-admissibility contains no such clause.

**5. Circularity**

The obvious objection is that (a) through (c) are circular. Whether a node is live depends on the profile; the profile depends on what is admissible at live nodes; and admissibility at a live node depends on a conjecture about the continuation, which runs through dead nodes, where (c) says nothing at all. If conjectures at dead nodes are wholly unconstrained, then the player at node 5 is free to conjecture that B will pass at node 6 and hand her 8, in which case taking at node 5 is not optimal, and the unravelling runs in the opposite direction until nothing anywhere is admissible.

My reply is that the definition is not meant to be applied once but solved for. An RS-solution is a fixed point: a profile-and-conjecture pair that is admissible relative to itself. I take the fixed-point reading to be what separates a self-referential definition from a viciously circular one. I do not have an existence or uniqueness result. I conjecture that the game of §1 has more than one fixed point and that immediate take is one of them; I cannot presently say whether termination at node 5 is another, and I suspect the answer turns on a rule for conjectures at dead nodes that I have not yet formulated.

**6. What I am not claiming, and what I am**

I am not claiming that backward induction is invalid. I am claiming that it yields a statement about a game in which its premises are common knowledge, and that we have been reading it as a statement about games in which they are not.

Nor am I leaning on the laboratory. Whether anyone takes at node 1 is beside the point; §3 would stand unchanged if every subject in every experiment ever run had taken at node 1 on every trial.

What §4 shows, if it shows anything, is that the centipede paradox is not a paradox about rationality but an artifact of a solution concept that was never entitled to make a prediction in the first place. Once that is seen, the entire industry of experiments designed to test backward induction loses its rationale, and with it the reputation-based repairs of §2, which were only ever needed to make the experiments come out right.

**§7 (unwritten)** is meant to state the missing rule for conjectures at dead nodes.
