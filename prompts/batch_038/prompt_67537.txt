Write a short story of about 1,200 words set in a river port divided between two kinds of rule. Most of the town answers to the Charter Steward, an administrative apparatus installed after a famine: its ordinances are published a year in advance, bind its own officers exactly as they bind residents, are appealable to standing tribunals, and may be revised only on a fixed calendar. No resident, family, or faction can dismiss the Steward or bend it to a private end. One ward, by contrast, is held by a merchant house whose factor has the same powers on paper, has issued no order in living memory, remits half the dues, and built the ward a school the Steward's wards lack.

Follow two young people — one from the Steward's wards, one from the factor's — who keep a joint tally over a single year of every order, prohibition, levy, inspection, and reversal directed at either of them, and find the two tallies identical. Their quarrel over what the tally proves should carry the plot.

The story should let a reader see that the two differ in something other than the number of interferences they suffer, and should let them feel what it costs that no one can dismiss the Steward — without anyone stating either point. Name no philosopher and use no vocabulary drawn from political theory; make the case entirely through incident, dialogue, and what the year does to them. End without a verdict.
