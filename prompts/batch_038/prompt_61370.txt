Ok so I've been rereading the introspection-skeptic stuff (Nisbett & Wilson's old Psych Review piece, Wegner on apparent mental causation, bits of Schwitzgebel) and I keep thinking the canonical case study everyone should be using isn't a lab experiment at all — it's the facilitated communication blowup of the early-to-mid 90s.

Facts as I have them, correct me if I'm off: Crossley develops supported typing in Australia, Biklen brings it to Syracuse around 1990, and within a couple of years you have nonverbal autistic people, held at the wrist or forearm, producing eloquent first-person prose about their inner lives. Parents and teachers are overwhelmed. Then some of the output starts naming abusers, there's a wave of allegations, and when people run message-passing tests — show the kid a picture the facilitator can't see, or vice versa — the typed answers track what the facilitator knows, not what the person being supported knows. Professional bodies conclude there's no real evidence the supported person is the author. The facilitators werent lying, exactly: they respond, sincerely, that the testing is invalid because it stresses the kid and destroys the trust the method runs on, and most of them keep going. The ones who recanted, like Boynton, seem to have done it only after personally running the blind tests and watching their own hands do it.

So run that episode through the introspection theories properly, not just "people are bad at introspection, QED." I want the mapping first — who exactly is the introspector here (facilitator? parent? the person being supported?), what is the report a report *of*, and what would even count as ground truth. Then tell me what the Nisbett & Wilson thesis actually predicts about the facilitators, and whether it's the right lens at all, since their claim is about having no access to the higher-order processes behind your own responses, whereas what the facilitators got wrong was the authorship of a movement. Same failure, or a different one that just gets lumped in? Same question for Wegner: he says the sense of willing comes from priority, consistency and exclusivity, and in FC all three seem wierdly satisfied for the facilitator — she has the thought, the typing matches it, no conspicuous alternative cause on her radar — and yet she experiences *not* willing it. Does his framework eat that, need an extra clause, or is FC just outside its scope?

And here's my actual hobbyhorse: the Boynton pattern. The only reliable route to changing a facilitator's mind seems to have been handing her own body a discrepancy signal in real time. Which makes me suspect the honest thesis isn't "introspection is unreliable" but something weaker and more specific — call it the no-discrepancy-no-update thesis: introspective confidence stays pinned at whatever the vividness of the experience supports until something forces a mismatch you can't explain away. Tell me whether that's a real position or a dodge, and whether FC is evidence for it or merely consistent with it.

Finally, where is this literature just silent? I'm thinking about the cost asymmetry (an accusation is not the mirror image of a missed voice) and about the fact that the exact same skeptical machinery gets pointed at the testimony of disabled people generally. Does the theory ever say when a first-person report deserves the benefit of the doubt, or does it only cut one way?

Bottom line up front in two sentences, then the analysis. Hard cap of 600 words — if you hit the cap mid-thought, cut the thought.
