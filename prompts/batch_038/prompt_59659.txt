For about eleven years I have kept a private log of the verdicts of a woman I will call R., an argument partner from my old rationalist meetup group and, more recently, a fellow member of a small grantmaking committee. The log exists because she is unusually disciplined about not contradicting herself, and because I have never managed to reverse-engineer the rule she is actually running. Her verdicts, in the order I recorded them:

1. The familiar dust-specks trade: one person tortured for fifty years, or a barely noticeable speck of dust in the eye of each of a number of people too large to be written down using the resources of the observable universe. She takes the specks without hesitation and describes the alternative as scope insensitivity dressed up as compassion.

2. A mugging case: a stranger on the street claims he can inflict suffering of exactly that scale on exactly that number of people unless he is handed the contents of your wallet. She refuses, and insists the refusal requires no special machinery — the prior probability that a stranger on the street can do what he says he can do is doing all the work.

3. A choice between a world of a billion people whose lives are excellent and a world of a hundred quadrillion people whose lives are barely worth living. She takes the second and calls the first an average-view error.

4. She would spend a fixed sum on insecticide-treated nets rather than on rescuing one identified trapped miner, even at some expected-lives cost, and says that "identified" is doing no normative work in the choice.

5. Two structurally similar proposals from teams she trusts equally. Proposal A: a monitoring program whose own model assigns a one-in-a-million chance per year of catching something catastrophic that nothing else would catch; she funded it. Proposal B: a program whose own model assigns a one-in-ten-billion chance of catching something a thousand times worse, the figure extrapolated from theory rather than from any observed base rate; she refused it, on the grounds that the number in B is not a probability but a shrug rendered in scientific notation. Asked whether she would fund B if the same figure were grounded in a frequency, she said she would have to think about it. That is the most she has ever conceded.

The new case is ours, and she holds the deciding vote on a five-person committee. We are being asked to redirect roughly seven million dollars — money currently committed to a portfolio of ordinary, well-evidenced grants — into a research program whose entire justification is a tail risk. The technical case comes from a group with no stake in the outcome: they lose nothing if we decline, they have published their model, and two reviewers outside their institution have checked the argument and signed off on it. They do not offer a point estimate. They offer a distribution over the annual probability of an outcome everyone on the committee agrees is effectively unbounded in badness — the permanent disempowerment of every person who will ever live — spread roughly log-uniformly between one in a million and one in a trillion, with a median near one in a billion. Their calibration data genuinely supports the upper end of that range; the lower end is extrapolation. The proposed program would, on their own account, shift the whole distribution down by a factor of fifty.

What I want from you:

- Lay out the candidate decision rules that are jointly consistent with verdicts 1 through 5, including rules R. would indignantly disown. Then say which verdicts already rule out which candidates — bounded utility, maximin, the average view, a ceiling on how many beings may be aggregated across, any weighting of identified over statistical victims — and show each elimination rather than asserting it.
- For every surviving rule, state what it implies about the new case, and give me the sentence she is most likely to say aloud in the meeting when defending that vote, since I will be in the room.
- Commit to a prediction, and tell me how much evidential weight her own account of her rule in verdict 2 deserves, given that self-reports about one's decision procedure are precisely what I started the log to check.
- Identify where the track record genuinely underdetermines her. In particular: does a rule that thresholds credence even have a well-defined output when it is handed a distribution over credences instead of a single number? If it does not, say what principle she would have to adopt on the spot in order to vote at all, and what she would thereby be committing the committee to.
- Finally, name one or two cases I could put to her next month that would separate the surviving rules, saying for each which rule it would falsify. They need to be cheap enough to settle over dinner.

I am not after a survey of the literature or anyone's name attached to a position. I want the rule-space, the vote, and the discriminating case.
