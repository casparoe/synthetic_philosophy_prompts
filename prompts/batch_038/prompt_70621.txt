I am drafting the coding protocol for a comparative project on democratic backsliding, and I need help with an objection I was unable to answer at the time.

The protocol's centerpiece is a trigger rule. A country-year is coded as backsliding only when the incumbent has, within a single electoral cycle, carried out at least three items from a closed list: lowering the compulsory retirement age of judges and filling the resulting vacancies in one appointment round; converting leadership of the public broadcaster into a political appointment; amending the electoral law so that it disadvantages the opposition retroactively; deploying tax or audit authorities against opposition-aligned media owners; and disqualifying candidates by administrative rather than judicial means. Each item was chosen because it is individually reversible by a successor government, and that is deliberate: we want a threshold that can be declared from the outside, on a date, in terms that do not depend on whether the domestic press has yet noticed what is happening.

A colleague's objection at the workshop was that this is the wrong shape of question altogether. Her words were roughly that I am looking for the moment the water boils, but this is the frog; by the time three of my five have happened the frog is cooked, and the people in the pot are the ones who will insist the water is fine. She added that the trigger rule would have coded nothing during the years that actually mattered, and that demanding a threshold is itself a concession to the incumbents' framing of the situation.

I would like to take the image seriously rather than wave it off, and to do that I need it unpacked. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims the boiling-frog metaphor could be making — claims about perception and adaptation, about the structure of reasons available to an agent at each increment, about thresholds and irreversibility, about whether the heating is deliberate or merely cumulative drift, about the epistemic standing of insiders relative to outside coders — and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical claim, a claim that is false or badly unsupported, or a claim that does no real work in the argument. Address, as a separate item, the empirical status of the fable itself: as I understand it the underlying biology is not accurate, and I want to know whether that undermines any of the political readings, all of them, or none of them. Then tell me which of the readings, if true, would actually license my colleague's two conclusions — that a threshold rule is the wrong instrument, and that insisting on one is a form of complicity — and which readings would leave the trigger rule standing, perhaps amended. Finally, if some reading implies that the question whether the line has been crossed is malformed rather than merely difficult to answer, flag it distinctly and say what a coding protocol should do in its place.
