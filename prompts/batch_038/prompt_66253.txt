Two months ago the council of our federation elected me chair of its rules committee, a post I accepted largely because no one else wanted the minutes. The committee rules on questions of order and on the validity of amendments, subject to appeal to the assembly. I now have to file a written opinion by the end of the month on a pair of amendments that has split the committee three ways, and in preparing it I have discovered that positions I have held for years about how our rules work, and which I had never needed to state, do not obviously fit together. I would like an audit of them before I write anything.

The setting. We are a federation of sixty-one community orchestras; each member orchestra has one vote in the assembly regardless of how many players it fields. The six largest orchestras, which between them account for most of the federation's musicians and a disproportionate share of its budget, have pressed for weighted voting for a decade. The constitution's amendment provision requires notice in advance of the assembly and a two-thirds majority of member orchestras voting. A separate sentence, added when the constitution was consolidated in 1948 at the insistence of the smaller orchestras, provides that the equal-vote guarantee may not be altered, repealed, or suspended, and that this sentence itself may not be altered, whether by express repeal or by any other means.

At last month's assembly two amendments were carried in sequence, each with the required notice and each by more than two-thirds. The first deletes from the 1948 sentence the words that make it apply to itself, leaving the equal-vote guarantee entrenched but the entrenchment provision amendable. The second repeals the guarantee outright and takes effect at the next assembly. Everyone agrees the second is void if the first is.

Here is what I believe, stated as flatly as I can and, I hope, without weighting any item over another.

1. All authority to make or change the federation's rules comes from the constitution. Neither the assembly, nor the council, nor the membership at large has any residual power to do what the constitution does not authorize in the manner it authorizes. A change made otherwise is not a defective rule; it is no rule at all.

2. No body can bind its successors as to the rules themselves. A provision that purports to be unamendable is not, in the relevant sense, a rule at all: it records the wishes of the people who wrote it and nothing more. Each assembly must remain free to change any rule, including any rule about how rules are changed.

3. The 1948 sentence nevertheless binds this assembly. I intend to advise the council that the first amendment was beyond the assembly's power and that the second falls with it.

4. Where provisions of the constitution conflict with one another, the later in time prevails. I have always treated this as an ordinary interpretive default rather than as a substantive claim about anything.

5. Legitimacy in an institution like ours is entirely a matter of procedure. If a change is made in the prescribed way, I do not think there is a further question about its standing; if it is not, no amount of wisdom, popularity, or urgency in the change supplies the defect.

6. Long acquiescence cannot make a rule, but it does shift the burden of proof. In 1974 the method of assessing dues was changed; no one on the committee has been able to locate a record of the vote, and every member orchestra has paid under the new method for fifty years. I hold that we cannot now establish that the change was validly adopted and must nonetheless treat it as valid, on the footing that anyone denying it must prove the negative.

I have not attempted to diagnose these myself, beyond noticing that the committee's disagreement tracks something deeper than the text of the 1948 sentence, and that when I rehearse my opinion aloud I find myself reaching for different items on this list at different points in the same argument.

What I want is specific. Which of these six are in genuine conflict, and which merely look as though they are? Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a list of comparable problems, and for each say which item has to give and what I lose by giving it up. Then, for each coherent package that survives, tell me what my opinion on the two amendments should be. I am not asking which theory of constitutional change is correct; I know there are several and I am not going to settle that by the end of the month. I am asking what this particular set of commitments obliges me to say, and what each available repair would cost me in the opinion I have to file.
