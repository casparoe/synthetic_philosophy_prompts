I work in operations at a mid-sized UK logistics firm, and something happened to me that I have not been able to stop turning over. In 2019, when money was tight, I opted out of my workplace pension; it took a single form, and because I submitted it within the first month my contributions were refunded to me in full. In 2022 I noticed deductions on my payslip again. My employer had re-enrolled me without asking. HR was perfectly pleasant and perfectly unhelpful: they explained that the regulator requires them to re-enrol eligible staff roughly every three years, that the duty carries penalties if they do not comply, and that the date is theirs to choose. I opted out a second time, but outside the refund window, so that money simply stayed in the scheme. I am now doing a part-time master's in public policy, and I want to build my dissertation around this episode, because I suspect it is genuinely awkward for the theory I have been taught to reach for whenever defaults come up.

So that the analysis does not depend on recalling the details, here is the scheme as I understand it. Automatic enrolment arrived in the UK in stages from 2012: employers must enrol jobholders who are at least 22, below state pension age, and earning above a threshold that now sits at £10,000 a year; the statutory minimum contributions were legislated to step up over time and now total 8 per cent of qualifying earnings, with at least 3 per cent from the employer. Roughly ten million more people are saving in a workplace pension than were in 2012. The designers were explicit that this was choice architecture rather than compulsion: no one is forced to save, the option to leave is preserved, and the enrolment default does the work that information campaigns had failed to do. My own case adds two features I think matter. First, the default is not a starting condition but a recurring one: leaving is not a settled decision, it is a status that expires and must be renewed, on a date set by someone else. Second, exit is cheap only for a month; after that, the contributions already deducted are not returned, which makes the cost of leaving depend on when you happen to notice.

What I want is a disciplined case analysis, running in both directions, and I would rather you be explicit about where the theory runs out than force it to cover everything. Begin with the mapping: identify the players (me, my employer, the regulator, the scheme provider, and whichever of my temporal selves counts as the chooser), lay out the actual option set available to each, and specify precisely what in this arrangement is a nudge, what is a shove, and what is neither. Then test the episode against the standard criteria — that an intervention preserve the option set, that opting out be easy and cheap, that it work through predictable biases rather than through material incentives, and that it be defensible to the people it affects as judged by their own standards. Three questions interest me most, and I have no idea whether the literature has resources for them, so please treat my phrasing as provisional labels rather than as established terms. One: does an account built on a one-shot default have anything to say about a *recurring* default, where the relevant question is not whether exit is easy at a given moment but whether exit is *durable*? Two: when the state enlists a penalised intermediary to deliver the nudge — the employer is mandated so that the employee can be nudged — whose choice architecture is it, and does the coercion at one layer contaminate or excuse the libertarianism at the other? Three: what does the theory say to the people the eligibility threshold excludes entirely, for whom there is no architecture and therefore nothing to opt out of? Finally, give me your verdict on the direction of fit. Is my re-enrolment a confirmation of the framework, a manageable anomaly, or a case it never anticipated; is a ratcheting, three-yearly default better or worse than a one-time default *by the framework's own lights*; and if you think some of these questions fall outside it altogether, say so plainly and tell me which other account of paternalism, if any, would handle them better. I am not after references or a reading list — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
