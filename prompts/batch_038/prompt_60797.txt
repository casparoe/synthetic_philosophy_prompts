been chewing on this one. the numbers: a city runs two job programs. A places 90% of its 100 male participants and 70% of its 500 female participants. B places 80% of its 500 male participants and 60% of its 100 female participants. So A beats B by ten points in every subgroup and loses in the aggregate, 73.3% to 76.7%.

the standard move is: sex is a pre-treatment covariate, condition on it, the marginal is a selection artifact, trust the disaggregation. Fine — when the thing you're choosing is a treatment and the participant mix just comes along for the ride.

argue that this is a special case and not the resolution of simpson's paradox. specifically, argue that what's doing the work is whether the covariate-treatment association sits inside or outside the act you're choosing. if the city's real options are "fund A, whoever self-selects self-selects" vs "fund B, whoever self-selects self-selects," then the composition is part of the intervention, the reversal just is the causal effect of the policy, and the aggregate is the number you want. the conclusion I want defended: its not a paradox about probability, and not really one about causation either — the graph tells you which estimand is which but can't tell you which act is on the menu. it's a paradox about how coarsely you individuate the act.

make the case as hard as you can, including against the pearl-line reply that once you've written down the graph and stated the question the puzzle evaporates. and tell me where the argument overreaches, if it does.
