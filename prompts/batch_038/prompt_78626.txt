I am in my final year of high school and writing an essay on the end of the ancient world, and I have discovered that I cannot state the rule I am applying when I say that one civilization fell and another merely changed. My teacher's word for the second option is "transformation," but it has not helped me, because I cannot say what would have to be transformed before I would stop calling something a fall. These are the verdicts I have actually reached.

- Rome, 476: I want to say the western empire fell when Odoacer deposed the boy emperor and sent the imperial insignia east. But Odoacer kept the Senate, governed Italy in a Roman idiom, and struck coin in the eastern emperor's name, so I also want to say that all that happened was that Italy stopped having an emperor of its own. I do not know which of these I believe, and I would like the essay to settle it.
- Constantinople, 1453: fell, without hesitation — new dynasty, new religious law, new ruling class. Though I also know the conqueror claimed to be Caesar of Rome and drew Greeks into his administration, which embarrasses me a little.
- The Roman Republic under Augustus: fell. The Senate, the consulship and the tribunate kept their names and some of their business, the city grew richer, and nothing was sacked, yet I insist that the thing I care about was gone.
- The Fourth Crusade: not a fall, even though the capital was held by outsiders for most of six decades, because a Roman government kept operating at Nicaea and took the city back in 1261.
- The loss of Syria and Egypt in the 630s: also not a fall, although for an ordinary inhabitant the break was far sharper than anything in 476 — the language of government, the religion of the elite and the tax system all changed within a generation — because the state itself never stopped.
- Ming to Qing: China did not fall; the conquerors took over the examinations, the bureaucracy and the vocabulary of legitimate rule.
- The Soviet Union, 1991: fell — even though its largest successor kept the centre of the territory, the permanent Security Council seat and much of the administration, which looks like the Qing case, and I cannot explain why I feel differently about the two.
- I do not count the medieval church as Rome surviving, even though it kept Latin, the map of the dioceses, and one of the emperors' priestly titles.

The principle I have been testing these against is: a polity has fallen when nobody who holds power any longer claims their right to rule from it, and when making such a claim has ceased to be available. It handles the Qing, the Soviet case, 1204 and the 630s well enough, and it is why 476 makes me hesitate, since Odoacer did claim to hold Italy under the eastern emperor. But it fails on my two most confident verdicts: Augustus claimed to have restored the Republic and ruled through its offices, and Mehmed claimed the Roman imperial title, so on this principle neither the Republic nor the eastern empire fell. I would rather repair the principle than abandon the verdicts, though I accept that I may not be allowed to keep both.

Is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures my pattern, states precisely what each of its clauses is doing, and tells me what to say about the cases I have not considered? Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. The principle, as a single sentence, with each clause followed in parentheses by the case of mine that forces it; if you think no single principle can be stated, use this item for the closest family of principles and what separates them.
2. How it deals with Augustus and with 1453, the two verdicts my own version could not save, and what it says about 476 now that I have to choose.
3. Its verdict on two cases I have not listed: Babylon in 539 BCE, whose conqueror presented himself as the god's chosen restorer of the city rather than its destroyer, and Venice in 1797, whose Great Council abolished itself under French pressure while the city, its population and its buildings passed intact to another power.
4. Which one of my eight verdicts it cannot keep, and what I should replace it with.
5. What would change your mind about the principle you have offered — the strongest available argument against it, or the step in your own reasoning where you are most likely to have gone wrong.
