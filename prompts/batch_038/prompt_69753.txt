I am the production lead at a small type foundry, and I have to rule on individual glyph designs. In the run-up to a release that means several dozen rulings a week; each one has to survive scrutiny from two directions at once. Our designer argues that a decision follows from the character of the family and cannot be justified in measurements. Our engineering and QA people will only accept a criterion they can state as a number or as a test with a pass and a fail. Two years ago we tried to settle the dispute by writing a specification of the necessary and sufficient structural features of each lowercase letter — for 'a', say, a closed lower counter, a stem on the right, some kind of terminal at the upper left. The exercise collapsed. It rejected several of the best letters we have ever drawn for failing to instantiate features nobody had noticed until we wrote the list down, and it passed a set of perfectly compliant, utterly anonymous forms that no one in the building would defend. Since then I have come round to something closer to the view held by the people who have studied how letterforms are recognised: that what makes every 'a' an 'a' is not a shared checklist but something like a shared way of being drawn; that letter identity and style are not two independent properties that happen to coincide in a well-drawn glyph but outputs of one and the same thing; and that membership in the category is graded and context-sensitive, so that one glyph can be more of an 'a' than another, and which features matter can shift with point size, with the setting, and with the forms sitting beside it.

I want that view turned into a procedure my team can actually run. Specifically:

1. The procedure itself, as an ordered set of steps, each with a stated question, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, and which verdict it can produce. Use a fixed verdict vocabulary — something like pass, pass-with-note, fail-on-letter, fail-on-style, escalate — and say who signs off on each verdict. Budget: under ten minutes per glyph for the ordinary case, with an explicit longer path for the escalated ones.

2. The cheap tests to run, in the order they should be run: flash presentation at target sizes, substitution into invented words and into real text, forced choice against confusable neighbours, side-by-side against the family's existing forms, native-reader checks for expansions into other scripts. For each, say what a result has to look like to be decisive, what it can and cannot settle, and what to do when testing is impossible — a deadline, no readers available for the script in question, a design that exists only as a sketch.

3. How the procedure handles the two properties coming apart. Which has veto over which; whether "recognisably an 'a', but not our 'a'" is one decision or two; what happens when the recognition data and the designer's judgment point in opposite directions; and how a recorded dissent gets carried forward rather than buried.

4. Honest labelling of the judgment steps. Tell me which of these steps are taste wearing the clothes of procedure, and give me two sentences I can say to a client who asks why a glyph was rejected when the numbers all looked fine.

5. A short account of which of the commitments in my first paragraph must survive the simplification into a checklist and which can be dropped, with reasons.

Then run the procedure on three cases. Case A: a lowercase 'a' for the Light weight in which, to keep the counter open at small sizes, the designer has flattened the shoulder and removed the hook from the upper terminal. It matches every metric in our old specification exactly, and at 8 pixels naive readers identify it without hesitation; at 60 points, four of ten proofreaders call it an 'o' with a stem attached. Case B: in our new Greek companion, the alpha has been constructed on the same logic as the Latin 'a'. It is legible and unmistakably of the family to Latin readers; Greek-reading testers hesitate, and one describes it as a Latin letter in costume. Case C: our founder's signature design, a 'Q' whose tail is a detached dot floating inside the counter. It is charming in display, it is the mark the foundry is known for, it is the single glyph I would least like to lose — and at text sizes a substantial minority of readers take it for an 'O'. The founder wants it available at every size. I expect the procedure to cost me this one, and if it does not, I want to know why the procedure is not worth having.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the classes of design it gives no guidance on, the situations in which it will produce a confident wrong answer, and where it necessarily hands the decision back to a person. I am not asking for references or a reading list — I want the procedure, the worked cases, and your reasons.
