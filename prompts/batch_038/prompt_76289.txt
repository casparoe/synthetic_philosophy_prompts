OK so I'm the part-time archivist for a small river town (about 3,800 people, one paper mill, one weekly paper) and I've been talked into giving the main address at the 150th Lantern Walk this October. The Lantern Walk is our entire civic identity — every household puts a lantern in the window, the high school band leads a procession down to the old ferry landing, we throw bread on the water for the founders, and the mayor reads out the names of everyone who died that year. Everyone in town says it's been done without interruption since the settlement in 1674. There's a bronze plaque at the landing that says exactly that.

Here's the problem, and I should say I'm the one who found it: it hasn't. In the Sentinel's bound ledgers last spring I traced the whole thing to 1876, when the paper's editor cooked it up for the national centennial because he wanted a "custom of Old World dignity" for a place that had still been a lumber camp thirty years earlier. The bread-throwing is invented. The founders' blessing is invented — it's paraphrased out of a hymnal. And the walk lapsed twice, completely, in 1918 and then from 1942 to 1947; a women's club revived it in '47 and quietly backdated the run. The plaque is wrong in three seperate ways. Also, the family being honored this year, the Vanderwalls, don't descend from anybody who was here in 1674 — they came in 1889 with the mill.

The council knows that I know. That's actually why they asked me: they'd rather hear it from the podium than have the historical society hand it to the paper mid-procession. But I am not up there to debunk anything either, and I don't want the speech to read as a scholar explaining the town to itself. Half the room marched this walk as kids and buried a parent at that landing. There's a documentary crew from the state humanities council. There will be sixty people who've never heard of Benedict Anderson and couldn't care less. And there are two folklorists from the university driving down, one of whom is a hardliner who thinks the whole invented-traditions conversation is elitist condescension dressed as scholarship — so no winking, no "of course we all know it's made up," no irony at the town's expense, nothing that only lands for people with a seminar behind them.

Can you write the speech? Six to seven minutes out loud. What I actually need:

- Nothing false. No "since time immemorial," no "as it has always been," no tasteful fog around the dates. If I couldn't say it in the same room as the ledgers, it doesn't go in.
- The 1876 fact gets said out loud, by me, and it has to arrive as a gift rather than a correction — people should walk away prouder of the lanterns than they came, not less. That's the hard part and the whole point.
- It has to still work as a ceremony. The procession starts ten minutes after I sit down, and people need to still want to throw the bread in the water. This is where the theory should do the structural work instead of getting name-checked: Anderson's point is that a community of people who will never meet each other has to be imagined into being, and Hobsbawm's point is that a repeated ritual invented recently is what makes that imagining feel ancient and hold. I want the speech to be visibly performing that job while being honest that it is a job being performed.
- The Vanderwalls get handled without humiliating anyone, including the people who put their names on the program.
- Staging constraint: a fourth-grader from the school hands me a lit lantern partway through, because the council thinks it'll be adorable. Write that in and make it earn its place — I'd rather cut it than have it be filler.

When you're done, give me a last line in the form \answer{...} containing the single sentence of the speech that's carrying the most weight — the one that, if it doesn't land, collapses everything else into either a lie or a lecture.
