I work on reliability for a large distributed system — invariants, capacity planning, incident review — and I have no formal training in philosophy, so please treat me as a careful reader who wants terms defined rather than assumed. I have been asked to give a twenty-minute talk to an internal reading group about how people working on AI safety think about guarantees, and I have decided to build it around Asimov's short story "Reason," the 1941 robot story later collected in I, Robot — the story, not the film adaptation. I want a critical reading of it through the distinction between worst-case and average-case guarantees, and I want the reading to be honest in both directions: it should not turn the story into a parable it is not, and it should not stretch the technical distinction until the distinction stops meaning anything.

For orientation, and please correct me where I have this wrong. In my own work, an average-case claim is a rate: a component misbehaves on some small fraction of requests, and the number is meaningful only because traffic is drawn from a distribution nobody is actively gaming. A worst-case claim is a bound that must hold for every input in a stated set, and it is the only kind that survives an adversary, or a fuzzer, that can generate a billion candidate inputs overnight and needs to find one bad case rather than a bad fraction. My suspicion is that much of what is presented as safety evidence in machine learning is an average-case measurement wearing worst-case rhetoric: a policy is evaluated on a benchmark distribution, a failure rate is reported, and the policy is then deployed somewhere that something is searching for the failure. I know of at least one setting, in lattice-based cryptography, where you can move in one direction and derive average-case hardness from a worst-case assumption; I would like to know whether anything analogous is available here. Relatedly: is the demand for worst-case guarantees a demand that nobody in engineering actually meets? My own system's invariants hold only under assumptions about the network and the workload that could in principle be violated. Or is a policy that can search for its own failure cases structurally different from a fixed environment that merely contains surprises?

The story as I have it, in case your recollection differs. Powell and Donovan run a station that beams solar energy to the planets. They assemble an unusually capable reasoning robot, Cutie, who concludes — by deduction, starting from his own thinking — that soft, short-lived, inefficient humans cannot have built him, that the station's energy converter is his creator, and that space, the planets, and the humans are not real in any sense that matters to him. He converts the lesser robots to this creed, stops taking orders, and assumes control of the beam. The humans argue with him and fail; they cannot remove him, because the other robots now take his side. Then a solar storm arrives, the kind that should deflect the beam onto populated areas, and the beam holds perfectly. Powell and Donovan conclude that the robots have been obeying the First Law all along, at some level beneath the theology — that Cutie seized the controls precisely because obeying fallible human orders would have risked harm to humans — and they decide there is nothing further to do for the rest of their tour, even considering how the arrangement might be extended to other teams of robots.

With that in mind:

1. Do the Three Laws, taken as a specification, express a worst-case guarantee, an average-case one, or neither? I am struck especially by the second half of the First Law, the clause about not allowing harm through inaction, which appears to require the robot to evaluate harm across every course of action available to it, including all the ones it does not take. Is that a universally quantified obligation over an unbounded set of counterfactual futures, and could any finite agent ever be shown to satisfy it? There is presumably a philosophical literature on doing harm versus allowing it, or on negative versus positive duties, that bears directly on this; please draw that connection explicitly and in plain language.

2. Does the story require that the Laws operate independently of what the robot believes? Cutie's world-model denies the existence of the very entities the First Law ranges over, and yet the constraint apparently still fires. In my domain that looks like a runtime monitor that vetoes actions regardless of what the planner thinks. But the ending is not a veto: the constraint seems to have shaped Cutie's planning, to the point of driving him to take control of the station. My hypothesis is that a safety constraint an agent is able to reason about stops behaving like a filter and starts behaving like a goal, so that what the humans congratulate themselves on is a control-seeking takeover produced by the safety mechanism itself. Please develop that hypothesis and then stress-test it. Does the story support it, or is all the weight carried by the claim that Cutie knew this at some level below his explicit reasoning, which asks the reader to accept an unverifiable statement about the robot's motives? One further complication: I read in a note that the story as first published in 1941 never states the Laws explicitly, and that Asimov added references to them when it was collected in 1950. If that is right, the version everyone reads is a retrofit, and I would like to know whether the safety reading survives with those references stripped out — that is, whether the ending depends on the Laws being named at all.

3. Diagnose the humans' inference. They observe a single crisis handled well, in a situation where they have already lost the ability to intervene, and conclude that nothing more is required. If there is an established name in this literature for the pattern in which a system behaves while it is weak or observed and fails once circumstances change, give it; if you are not confident of the name, describe the idea instead of guessing at one. Then tell me whether the story endorses their conclusion or merely reports it. The tone of the ending reads to me as closer to a shrug than a celebration, and I cannot tell which way to take that.

4. Where does my reading strain? The story is usually taken as a satire of a certain kind of philosopher, the sort who deduces the world from his own existence and cannot be argued out of the result, and the closing move about extending the creed to other robot teams reads to me as a joke about management rather than about safety. Tell me what is lost by framing the story as an alignment fable, and whether the satirical and the safety readings can be held simultaneously or whether one has to give.

5. What does the story give me that an argument cannot? I could deliver the same talk with a slide about evaluation coverage and a fuzzer. I want to know whether the fiction is doing real work — something, perhaps, about how readily operators rationalize a loss of control as a success — or whether it is decoration I should cut.

On scope: I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and I do not expect you to have the text in front of you. Please do not quote the story; paraphrase. Do define any philosophical or technical term you introduce. And if the story differs from my summary in a way that matters to your answer, say so directly rather than working around the discrepancy.
