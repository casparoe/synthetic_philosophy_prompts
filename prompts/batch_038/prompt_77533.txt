You are a metaphysician who works on dispositions and who also writes good parody. Please work in American English.

First spend three or four lines planning which clause of your document will carry which problem, then write the piece.

The piece: a parody of a commercial insurer's underwriting manual, specifically the section governing a "Fragility Endorsement" that pays out for breakage of items certified in advance as fragile. Format it the way such a manual really is formatted — purpose and scope, a definitions section, numbered underwriting clauses, an exclusions list, and a short annex of sample denied claims. About 750 words.

The manual's central move is to define "fragile" in purely counterfactual terms: an item qualifies if and only if it would break were it struck sharply. It then discovers, clause by clause, that the definition misclassifies precisely the cases the claims department cares about. Items get certified fragile even though they would in fact survive the strike, because something built into the setup would intervene at the moment of impact. Items that are unambiguously fragile get denied because they happen to be cradled in shock-absorbing packing. And every amendment drafted to repair one class of case manufactures another. Let the drafter's retreat be legible on the page: the language slides from what would happen, to what the item is made of, to what a competent appraiser would expect, until the definitions section has quietly stopped doing the work it was hired to do. The manual must also fix how sharply the certification strike is to be delivered, and that specification should keep slipping too.

Coin your own plausible bureaucratic jargon for each of these phenomena. Do not use philosophers' terms of art, and do not name any philosopher, paper, or school of thought anywhere in the manual. The humor has to come from the metaphysics being handled correctly: a specialist should see that you know exactly which cases wreck the reductive analysis and why the proviso business will never terminate, while a general reader should still find it funny as corporate nonsense.

After the manual, add a note of roughly 150 words in which you map your clauses onto the philosophical issues they parody, flag where the piece takes liberties or leans on a contested reading of them, and distinguish what is settled on these questions from what remains genuinely disputed among people who work on them. Do not present a contested position as the consensus. No citations or reading lists.
