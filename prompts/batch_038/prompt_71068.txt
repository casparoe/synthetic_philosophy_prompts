I'm TA-ing a seminar on non-human causes in history — climate, disease, eruptions — and instead of a list of -isms I want to hand the students a sorting tree. Start everyone from one shared commitment (say: I accept the 1815 Tambora eruption is relevant to explaining 1816-18) and end at a named position. The leaves should include something like hard environmental determinism, possibilism, a Braudelian long-durée structural view, Diamond-style geographic-luck accounts, disease-history readings of the McNeill/Crosby kind, an actor-network "actant" view, a meaning-mediation view where the eruption only acts through the omens and panics it produces, and the deflationary "climate is a variable, not an actor" view.

Build it out of the logical space before you reach for the names. I want the branch questions to be about things like: does the non-human factor cause or merely permit; do its effects run through human interpretation or bypass it; is the explanatory unit a dated event or a centuries-long structure; is "actor" doing any work beyond "cause"; and does landing at this leaf entitle you to moral or political conclusions. Every node has to be answerable by a student who has read nothing yet and has no idea which label they're steering toward, so no node can be a name check. Keep it to seven-ish questions and about a page.

Use these as the concrete cases the nodes get asked about: the 536 dust veil and the famine after it, smallpox in the conquest of Mexico, labor scarcity in England after 1348, the Little Ice Age and the seventeenth-century crisis, and one deliberately weak case — the weather on D-Day. Then tell me which combinations of answers are empty and why (incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's got there yet), and flag any two leaves your tree cant actually seperate.

Last thing: where do you think this tree is most likely wrong — which branch collapses under pressure or is really two branches pretending to be one — and what argument or piece of historical work would make you redraw it?
