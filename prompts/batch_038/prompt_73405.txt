And here's the student's script, typos and all:

> **(a)** The MCE objective picks the reward function under which the observed trajectories are most likely, but "likely" is doing something specific here. Instead of assuming the demonstrator takes the single best trajectory, it assumes they sample trajectories from a distribution proportional to the exponential of total trajectory reward, so better trajectories are more likely but bad ones aren't impossible. The entropy term is what makes that distribution the shape it is: maximizing the causal entropy of the trajectory distribution subject to matching the feature counts we actually observed gives you exactly this softmax form. Its called causal rather than plain entropy because the state occupancy depends on the policy, so you can't take the entropy of some fixed distribution over states — you condition the future on the past actions instead. In practice the gradient of the log-likelihood works out as observed feature counts minus expected feature counts under the current reward, and getting those expectations means solving a soft-RL problem in the inner loop at every gradient step, which is why this is expensive and why people use approximations.
>
> **(b)** The reward isn't unique because many different reward functions produce the same behavior. The standard way to state this is that R is recovered only up to a positive affine transformation: aR + b with a > 0 gives the same optimal policy, since multiplying by a positive constant and adding an offset can't change an argmax. So there's a whole family of rewards consistent with any amount of data, and more demonstrations don't resolve it — with a lot of data you converge to the reward, just up to that scale-and-shift freedom, which is harmless because anything downstream that only picks the best action can't tell the difference. The feature basis also matters: if you hand the model a coarse set of features, two genuinely different preferences can look identical because the difference between them isn't representable, but that's a limitation of the features rather than of IRL itself.
>
> **(c)** I mostly agree with the colleague. Because MCE treats the demonstrator as boundedly rational rather than assuming they're optimal, it doesn't force our own idea of a good trajectory onto the data, and it lets the human be noisy without concluding they must want something else. That's basically the position Stuart Russell argues for, that a machine should stay uncertain about the human objective and defer to the human instead of optimizing a fixed hand-written utility; the entropy term is what expresses that uncertainty about what the reward is. Where I'd push back is on the phrase "what people actually want." The reward you recover is whatever rationalizes the behavior given the dynamics you assumed and the discount you chose, so if your model of the environment is wrong you'll attribute the wrong preferences, and people also do things for reasons that have nothing to do with their own preferences, like social pressure or habit. So the inference is better than writing the reward by hand, but it isn't assumption-free, and you should be careful about labeling the output "what people want."
