General anaesthesia is not a single intervention but four separable ones aimed at four separable endpoints: hypnosis, analgesia, immobility, and amnesia. On a typical table the patient is paralysed by a neuromuscular blocker, so that movement is abolished independently of whether anything is felt; nociception is tracked only by proxy, through heart rate, blood pressure, and EEG-derived depth indices; and a benzodiazepine is very often given alongside, not in order to deepen unconsciousness but in order to prevent whatever does occur from being laid down in memory. The case is then graded by its aftermath. If the patient reports nothing, the anaesthetic is recorded as uneventful.

The specialty knows perfectly well that these two things come apart. The isolated forearm technique applies a tourniquet to one arm before the paralytic is injected, leaving that arm under voluntary control; patients managed this way have followed commands, answered questions, and signalled that they were awake and in pain — and characteristically reported nothing at all afterwards. In procedural sedation, patients who converse, cooperate, and complain throughout a colonoscopy frequently emerge with no memory of having done so, and that is counted as a success rather than a failure.

Yet the profession's normative attitudes track recall rather than experience. Waking during surgery and remembering it is treated as a catastrophe: disclosed to the patient, litigated, associated with lasting psychological injury. Waking during surgery and not remembering it is very often not counted as an event at all, and in some protocols is deliberately engineered. The consent conversation reinforces the asymmetry from the other direction — patients are told they will be unconscious, not that they will not remember.

I would like a reconstruction of the theory of consciousness that this practice presupposes.

Specifically: what view of the relation between experience, awareness, memory, and report makes the slide from "the patient recalls nothing" to "nothing was experienced" anything more than an administrative convenience? Is the practice committed to a higher-order account, on which a first-order state is conscious only in virtue of a suitable further representation of it, or to something narrower and more mnemonic, or simply to the claim that reportability is what we have evidence of and therefore what we regulate? Which rival accounts — first-order theories on which the intraoperative pain was fully conscious and fully bad at the time, whatever happened to the trace — does it rule out, and does it rule them out explicitly or only by silence?

Then separate strict presupposition from comfortable fit. Could a first-order theorist endorse the practice as it stands by relocating the harm: holding that an experience wrongs a person only insofar as it is taken up into a continuing psychological life? Is that a stable position, or does it quietly assume a view of personal identity that the practice never argues for and that the amnesia drug itself is designed to manipulate? And which half of the practice is doing the real philosophical work — the continuous titration to autonomic and nociceptive signs, which suggests that something other than memory matters, or the grading of outcomes by memory alone?

Finally, tell me where the practice is underdetermined, so that several theories would license it equally, and then tell me what would have to change if we adopted the rival view and treated unrecallable intraoperative experience as genuine, conscious, and harmful: the drug regimen, the definition of an adverse event, the consent script, the ethics of routine procedural sedation, the design of depth monitors. What would we gain, and what would the change cost in practice?
