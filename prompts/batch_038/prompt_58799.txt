Help me settle an argument about the colour phi phenomonenon — the one where a red dot flashes on the left, then about half a second later a green dot flashes a bit to the right, and people report a single dot gliding across and changing colour somewhere en route. Dennett and Kinsbourne's two stories about this are the Orwellian one (you really did just see two static flashes, but the memory gets rewritten afterwards so that you now seem to remember motion) and the Stalinesque one (the brain hangs on, builds the motion, and that's what you actually experience). The standard line is that the two can't be told apart, because any report you could give comes after the second dot anyway.

But here's the bit I keep coming back to. Flash-lag and flash-drag type effects show that motion context shifts what people do with their hands in real time, not just what they say later. So: run colour phi with no verbal report at all. The subject tracks the dot continuously with a finger on a touchscreen, and we look at whether the tracking path bows out along the illusory trajectory during the blank gap between the two flashes. That's an online motor measure — there's nothing for memory to rewrite, because the hand is still moving while the trial runs. To kill the obvious prediction confound, jitter the gap, randomise which side the second dot lands on, and include catch trials where it never appears at all; then look at whether any bowing starts before the second dot's onset or only after it.

Write me an essay on whether a design like that really breaks the tie. Steelman both sides properly: what would a defender of the unfalsifiability claim say about the tracking data, and what would have to be true of that data for it to genuinely favour one story over the other? Then commit — is the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction empirically decidable, or is it a distinction without a difference, and not just because of the crudeness of our current methods? Don't wind up on a fence.
