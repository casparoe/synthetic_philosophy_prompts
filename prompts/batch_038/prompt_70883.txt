You are a philosopher of mathematics equally at home in the analytic debate over the meaning of probability and in proof-theoretic semantics. Think the problem through carefully, considering several possible mappings before you commit to one, and only then write the answer up.

I work in dependent type theory. My vocabulary is that of meaning explanations in the Martin-Löf style: judgements rather than propositions as primitive, a type fixed by what counts as a canonical element of it, hypothetical judgements, proof objects, and Martin-Löf randomness as an effective test a sequence must pass. I know the philosophy of probability only in summary form.

Translate three positions into that vocabulary, one at a time and as faithfully as you can: the Ramsey–de Finetti view, on which probability statements express degrees of belief answerable to coherence, with exchangeability reducible to a mixture of independent and identically distributed distributions; the von Mises view, on which probability is a limiting relative frequency within a collective, invariant under admissible place selections; and the propensity view, on which a single case carries a chance in virtue of the repeatable arrangement that produced it.

Then give me the residue report: what translates without loss, what translates only if I quietly weaken it, and what has no expression at all — in particular, whether there could be a type whose canonical elements are chances, and what becomes of an actual infinite limit.

Finally, assess my working hypothesis, namely that the residue marks not a poverty of my framework but a failure of determinacy on the other side: that once probability is treated as a judgement rather than as a term denoting a worldly magnitude, the frequentist–propensity dispute loses its subject matter. Do not smuggle type-theoretic commitments into the translations and then present them as vindications; if my framework genuinely cannot say something the other views can say, say so plainly.
