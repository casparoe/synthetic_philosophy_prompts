My partner and I are expecting our first child in the spring, and we have spent the last several weeks trying to work out how to divide the parental leave that our national scheme provides. The rules are as follows. There are twelve months of paid leave per child, paid at eighty percent of the taking parent's own prior earnings, subject to a ceiling. Four of those months are reserved for each parent individually and are non-transferable in both directions: if the parent to whom they are attached does not take them, they lapse, and the family receives no cash equivalent in their place. The remaining four months may be divided between us however we choose. Subsidized childcare is available only from the child's first birthday, and nothing at all is payable for care supplied by a grandparent, by a parent who stays home beyond the leave period, or by a household that simply manages without.

The scheme presents itself as a benefit, but a number of its features seem to me to be doing considerably more work than a benefit should. First, the reserved months attach to persons rather than to households: the state declines to treat my partner and me as a single unit competent to allocate our own time, and would rather see four months of paid care destroyed than permit us to move them, even though a household that lets them expire is plainly worse off in both income and care. Second, the entitlement is priced by the market wage it displaces, so my partner, who earns substantially more than I do, would draw a larger weekly sum for performing exactly the same domestic labor — a scheme apparently aimed at loosening the grip of the labor market on care nonetheless measures care by the earnings it forgoes. Third, the reserved months are formally gender-neutral and are nevertheless described, in nearly every public discussion of them I have read, as though their target were fathers in particular. I should add that I find the scheme attractive in its effects and irritating in its logic, so what I want is an account of what it assumes, not a defense of it.

Could you reconstruct the theory of the family — and, behind that, the theory of distributive justice — that this design presupposes? I would like to know which of the following it strictly requires, and which it merely fits comfortably with: that the household is not the appropriate unit of justice and that intra-household bargaining systematically disadvantages one party; that competence at caring is built by doing and cannot be supplied by someone else's doing on one's behalf; that a child has a claim on a relationship with each parent severally, independent of what either parent prefers; or the narrower instrumental claim that without a lock of some kind the higher earner will always be pushed back to work and labor-market equality will never arrive. Please also say which rival views the design forecloses — a household-unit conception of benefits, a conception on which the state must remain neutral about the division of care, a conception on which what matters is the total quantity and quality of care a child receives but not its source — and where the practice is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license it equally well. Then assess whether the design is internally coherent: can earnings-proportional payment and individual non-transferability rest on a single theory of entitlement, or does the scheme slide between treating leave as deferred wages and treating it as a non-negotiable duty of care? Finally, a colleague of mine proposed a variant in which the reserved months stay attached to individuals but become marketable, so that a parent unwilling to take them may sell them to the other parent for a state-fixed sum. Tell me whether that variant preserves the presuppositions you identify or quietly substitutes a different theory in their place, and tell me what would have to change in each of the three features I listed if the scheme were built explicitly on the child's interests rather than on the parents' equality.
