I am drafting a term paper for my philosophy of biology seminar on the species problem, and rather than starting from the secondary literature I have been collecting my own verdicts on cases to see whether a principle is hiding behind them. I would be grateful for help with that. Please write in American English.

My verdicts:

1. Two frog populations lumped for a century, indistinguishable in preserved specimens, but never interbreeding where their ranges touch and differing consistently in male call: two species.
2. Two oak taxa that hybridize freely and produce fertile offspring across a broad contact zone, yet hold their distinct leaf form and soil preferences throughout the range: also two species, and no less securely than the frogs.
3. The *Ensatina* salamanders around California's Central Valley: the two end forms are species where they meet in the south, but the chain of intergrading populations connecting them is not a chain of species. I do not want to say the concept simply fails to apply here; I think there is a fact about the ring that my verdicts are groping toward.
4. Bdelloid rotifers, anciently asexual, which nevertheless cluster into discrete, ecologically differentiated groups that retain their identities across time and geography: those clusters are species.
5. Bacteria, exchanging genes promiscuously across what we call species boundaries: here I am a deflationist. Bacterial "species" are bookkeeping conveniences, not joints in the world.
6. A population that diverged from its sister lineage 10,000 years ago, is fixed for one diagnostic allele at one locus, and remains fully interfertile with it: not yet a species. More sampling and better sequencing would not move me, so my hesitation is not epistemic.
7. Species are individuals rather than classes, real chunks of genealogical history, and disputes over how many species a genus contains are substantive rather than merely verbal; yet a systematist and a field ecologist are entitled to go on using different species concepts for different purposes without either being wrong.

Is there a single principle that delivers all seven, or a small set of competing candidates? Please state the principle or candidates precisely enough that I could apply them to a case I have never seen, identify which of my verdicts motivates each clause, and then give the resulting verdict on one I have not listed: a fish population maintained in captivity for two hundred generations, now unable to produce fertile offspring with its wild source population, diagnosable by two alleles, which a conservation agency wants listed as a distinct species. I would also like to know which pair of my verdicts you regard as hardest to reconcile, and why.

Finally, tell me honestly which verdicts a defensible principle cannot accommodate, and rank the ones I would have to surrender by how much it would cost me to give them up, rather than treating them all as equally dispensable.
