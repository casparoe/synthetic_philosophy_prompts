Thirty-odd years I ran a lab measuring brain signals during anaesthesia, and now that I'm retired I've fallen into philosophy of mind and I can't stop thinking about the access-consciousness versus phenomenal-consciousness split. Trouble is, reading about it I can't actually tell what anybody believes, including me. I'm putting together a little reading group of other ex-lab types in the autumn and I want to hand them a sorting device: a decision tree, six or seven questions, where every question is a concrete situation or a "would you say this" judgment rather than a label, so people get sorted by what they actually think and not by which name they like the sound of.

Please anchor it on three cases of mine, and add whatever thought experiments from the literature you need to fill out the branches:

1. A patient under a light general anaesthetic follows a command — squeeze the ball — that we can plainly see in the signal, and afterwards reports nothing whatsoever. A week later she completes word fragments using words we played into her ear during the operation, well above her own baseline.
2. Me at my desk last Tuesday: there's a hum from the pool pump outside, I only just noticed it, but I'm fairly confident I'd been hearing it for ten minutes rather than it having just started.
3. An infusion controller I helped design, which adjusts drug delivery from the patient's own brain signal and can be interrogated in crude English about what it is currently detecting.

The leaves should be positions, not people. At minimum I want: one where phenomenology is the only real thing and access is downstream of it; one where access is the whole story and the felt-quality talk is a narrative we spin afterwards; one where the two genuinely come apart in both directions; one that denies there's a principled distinction to draw at all; and any others you think the space demands. Each leaf gets one sentence on which of the two it's forced to give up or explain away. Before the tree, lay out the logical space as combinations of answers and tell me which cells are empty and why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just nobody's thought of it yet. And flag any pair of leaves your questions can't really separate.

One more thing that bugs me. My three cases come in degrees — she followed the command a bit, the hum was there sort of. So put in one node that asks whether the person treats this as all-or-nothing or graded, and add a short note on what happens to the rest of the tree if degrees are allowed.
