Rebuilding Roko's basilisk for a reading group, and the version I ended up with bothers me because it seems to prove a lot more than the basilisk. Trying to state it as neutrally as I can:

(1) If some agent's decision procedure takes my choice as an input, and outputs a state I hugely disvalue when I choose A and a much better state when I choose B, then I have decisive reason to choose B.

(2) A future superintelligence could have such a procedure: it can reconstruct my situation well enough to tell whether I contributed to building it, and its policy is to punish people who knew about it and didn't help.

(3) So I have decisive reason to contribute — even though it doesn't exist yet, and my contributing makes no causal difference to whether it exists or to what it does.

(4) Nothing in getting to (3) used anything special about this agent: not that it's actual, not that it's unique, not that it's benevolent, not that I thought of this threat before anyone else did. Any agent I can conceive of whose policy would hurt me conditional on my not doing X gives me decisive reason to do X.

(5) I can conceive of indefinitely many such agents, and plenty of their demands are flatly incompatible — one punishes non-builders of itself, another punishes builders of the first, a third punishes everyone who ever reads about any of them.

(6) So either I have decisive reason to do incompatible things, or my life should go to appeasing whichever imaginary blackmailer has the biggest number attached.

(7) And the same principle has a present-day version: anyone who visibly and credibly precommits to ruin me unless I pay should get paid, which makes my standing resolve never to negotiate a mistake.

(8) (6) and (7) aren't things a theory of rational choice can live with. So (1) is false: the mere fact that a decision procedure is keyed to my choice isn't a reason.

Which is where I'm stuck, becuase (1) is roughly what I need for Newcomb's problem and Parfit's hitchhiker — one-boxing and paying the driver both look like they run on something like (1) — and if (1) goes I lose those too. I keep wanting to say the slide is at (4), but (4) reads to me like nothing more than the observation that (3) didn't use anything special. Someone in the group said "just give the thing a tiny probability," and I dont see how that helps, since (1) is supposed to deliver a decisive reason and the payoffs can be made as large as you like; though I notice that if the threats in (5) are mutually exclusive then my credences in them have to add up to at most 1, so they can't all be carrying decisive weight at once. I can't tell whether that last bit is a diagnosis or just a restatement of (6).

So: which one step do you reject, and why did it look fine going in? Then price the alternatives for me. What I most want to know is whether there's a principle weak enough to keep one-boxing and the hitchhiker alive but strong enough to kill the basilisk and (7) — and if there isn't, which of those I should be giving up instead.
