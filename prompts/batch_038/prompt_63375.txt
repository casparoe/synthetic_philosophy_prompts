I'm a junior at a small independent high school, and our history department lets one student each year design and administer a unit assessment instead of sitting it. Mine is on environmental history — specifically, on how historians ought to treat non-human causes. The class is twenty-two sophomores and juniors who have read only excerpts: a chapter of Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, selections from McNeill's Plagues and Peoples, part of Huntington's Civilization and Climate, which we read as an example of the kind of climatic determinism that got tangled up with early-twentieth-century racial theory, and two short pieces criticizing all three. They have fifty minutes, no notes, and by our school's rules the question must be answerable entirely from what is printed on the page: no outside knowledge and no works to identify.

Please write the essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The question should contain a short invented source packet: a fictional river-valley trading city that ceased to function over roughly fifteen years in the 1630s, with four or five pieces of evidence of deliberately uneven quality — a sediment or tree-ring record of drought, an epidemic described in a merchant's letters, a change in tax and grain-price policy partway through the period, a neighboring city in the same climate zone that did not collapse, and perhaps one item that cuts both ways. Make the case realistic but unmistakably invented, so that nobody can answer it from memory and so that the question does not depend on your recalling a real episode accurately. After the packet, add a two- or three-sentence note telling me roughly what real historiographical controversy the fiction stands in for; the question itself should not rest on any particular facts about that controversy.

What I want the question to turn on is not whether it was the drought or the people. It should turn on the distinction between four claims my classmates kept collapsing into one: that a non-human factor was among the causes; that it was sufficient, so that human decisions made no difference to the outcome; that it deserves explanatory priority in an account; and that it dissolves anyone's responsibility for what happened. A good answer should be able to hold the first without the others, and should say what a determinist would have to establish in order to earn the second from the first. It should also engage the harder conceptual point our discussion ended on: what it can mean to call a pathogen or an eruption an actor at all, and whether that word is doing thin causal work — a source of change that no human intended and that no human narrative absorbs — or smuggling in thick, intention-shaped language, the sort of thing one could anticipate, negotiate with, or blame, that the historian is not entitled to. The political dimension matters too, but I do not want it handled as though determinism were simply the bad side: the model answer should notice that a modern, explicitly anti-racist, geography-based version of the argument can still be criticized for letting human choices drop out, and that the older racial-climate version was objectionable for reasons that are not only evidential.

Model answer: 650 to 750 words, written so that a strong sixteen-year-old could plausibly have produced it in forty minutes. It should be an argument, not a guided tour of the packet.

Marking scheme: 25 marks, allocated to specific moves rather than to topics covered. Tell me explicitly what separates an A from a B — what a B answer will say that is perfectly competent but stops short, and what the A adds. Then list the mistakes that should cost marks, each with its deduction and one line saying why it counts as a genuine misunderstanding rather than a stylistic fault. I expect at least these to appear if they are warranted: the both-sides compromise that never states what determinism would have to claim in order to be false; the assumption that showing human agency was constrained shows it was irrelevant; treating any appeal to climate or disease as automatically a way of letting someone off the hook; and simply restating the packet's contents in a different order. No marks for naming a historian or a book.

Finally, add about 120 words of a confident, fluent, and mediocre student answer, and annotate it: which marks it earns, which it loses, and the single sentence that most clearly shows where it went wrong.
