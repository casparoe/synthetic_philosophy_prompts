So — weird favor. I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine (long features, no equations, the usual), and last spring I did a piece on hypercomputation: whether anything in the physical world could ever do something a Turing machine provably can't. It did better than expected and the philosophy-of-computation group at a research university I won't name has invited me to give a 15 minute talk on it at their workshop and then sit on a panel afterwards. I'd be the only non-academic up there and I would like to not get destroyed.

Here's who's on the panel, as far as I can tell from the program:

- Two people who work on relativistic hypercomputation. They take Malament–Hogarth type spacetimes seriously — the geometry where an observer on a finite-length path of their own can have an infinite proper time of someone else's worldline in their past, so an ordinary machine chugging away forever could hand you a halting-problem answer at a point. To them these aren't sci-fi toys, they're claims about solutions of a theory we already believe.
- One historian of computing who I'm fairly sure thinks the whole debate is a category error born of not reading the 1930s properly and of forgetting that a "computer" used to mean a person with a pencil.
- One ML researcher who thinks the strong/efficiency version of the thesis is the only interesting one, that quantum advantage results have already settled it empirically, and that the rest of the debate is philosophy cosplay.
- Several grad students, who will mostly be trying to land something memorable.

My argument in the piece, which is what I'll be defending:

1. "The Church-Turing thesis" is at least three different claims wearing one coat — a claim about effective calculability (mathematical, not the kind of thing an experiment can settle), a physical claim that any physically realizable process is simulable to arbitrary accuracy by a Turing machine, and a claim about resources/efficiency. Most popular arguments attack one while the defender retreats into another, and that mutual insulation is why the thesis feels unshakeable even though nobody has ever verified it.
2. On the physical version, my line was that a hypercomputer you cant trust isn't a counterexample to anything anyone cares about. Noise, finite precision, and the fact that the infinite-run machine's answer has to reach you as a signal you can receive reliably. Error correction is what buys trust in ordinary computing, and it's exactly what a machine that has already spent its infinite proper time doesn't have the resources to do.
3. The Penrose-flavored "the mind outcomputes the machine" stuff trades on sliding between claim 1 and the physical claim, and on treating incompleteness as if it were a theorem about what mechanisms can do.
4. I ended the piece conceding that maybe claim 1 isn't purely definitional after all — that it might be smuggling in a substantive bet about how human rule-followers are actually built. I'm not sure I want to defend that concession in public, but I did write it.

What I need from you: you're an expert philosopher of computation, technical literature included, who has also chaired a lot of these panels and knows exactly how they go sideways. Think carefully.
