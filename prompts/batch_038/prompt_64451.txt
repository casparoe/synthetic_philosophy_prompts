So I've been going round in circles about whether I should trust people's reports of their own minds — mine included — and I've decided to just write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of concrete cases and see if there's actually a principle behind them. Be warned its a bit of a mess and I half-suspect two of these verdicts can't both stand. Please answer in British English.

1. The old stockings study: shoppers pick between four identical pairs laid out left to right, and the right-hand one gets chosen about four times as often. Asked why, they talk about the knit, the sheerness, the finish. Almost nobody mentions position. Verdict: they don't know why they chose what they chose, and they're wrong about the reason as well.

2. Same shape, the jam version — someone chooses between two jams, the jars get quietly swapped, and they then defend at length, with proper tasting notes, a preference for the one they actually turned down, without noticing the swap. Verdict: same as 1, plainly confabulating.

3. A chess player of roughly my standard plays something good, and asked straight afterwards says it was to stop the knight landing on e5. An engine says the move is fine but not for that reason at all: the knight could land there anyway and do no harm, and what the move really accomplishes is something else entirely. Verdict: here I want to say he DOES know why he played it. The intention was there, conscious, the whole time; the engine only shows the intention rested on a miscalculation. But this is the pair I can't reconcile — in 1 and 2 the person also had a completely genuine conscious experience of choosing, and it tracked nothing. So what makes the chess player's report about a mental event that really happened rather than one that didn't? I honestly can't say.

4. Split-brain: something is cued to one hemisphere, the patient gets up and crosses the room, and when asked gives a fluent, confident account of why. Verdict: confabulation, no argument from me.

5. Me last Tuesday. I report that I was absolutely furious; my partner says I was mildly irritated at most, and the voicemail I left that evening backs them up. Verdict: I can be wrong about the intensity of a past feeling of my own — not misremembering a feeling I'm still clear about, but the feeling itself being mis-scaled.

6. Someone with a phantom limb reports searing pain in a hand that isn't there. Verdict: their introspection is flawless. There is a pain, it is excruciating, they are the authority on it; nothing in the hand explaining it changes nothing.

7. Tip of the tongue: I know the word I can't retrieve, I know it starts with a 'd', I know it isn't 'deluge', and I'd pick it out of a list of four before I could ever produce it. Verdict: genuine knowledge about the contents of my own mind, no confabulation — I'm not inventing the sense of near-retrieval. Though I'll grant I might be wrong that it starts with a 'd'; that bit feels like a guess dressed up as a memory.

Candidate principles I keep reaching for, all of which break somewhere:

- Introspection is reliable for present conscious experience, unreliable for causes and dispositions. Kills 5 and 6.
- Reliable for what it's like, unreliable for why. Handles 1, 2 and 4, but 3 is exactly a why-report that I want to vindicate.
- Unreliable when the report is generated by a general theory about what someone in that situation would think, reliable when it's generated by the state itself. I like this one and I also can't tell what would count as evidence for it, which makes me suspect it's empty.

So: is there a principle, or a small set, that gets most or all of these? State it precisely — I want to be able to tell whether some new case falls under it, not get a mood. For each clause say which case above forced you to add it, and give the principle's verdict on at least three cases I haven't listed (make them concrete, and include something from ordinary life rather than another lab study). Then say straight out which of my seven verdicts it cannot keep, and which one you'd tell me to abandon if holding onto all of them costs too many clauses.

And the case I actually care about: same chess player, but he offers the reason ten minutes later, once the position has moved on and he's had time to think — and what he says is true of the position but wasn't what he saw at the time. What does your principle deliver there? I keep flip-flopping and I'd like to be told I'm wrong in one direction rather than both.

If you think the whole set-up is confused — that 'introspection' isn't one thing and there's no single target running through these seven cases, or that I've got one of the studies badly wrong, or that I'm sliding between 'knows' and 'is right about' and should stop — say so plainly instead of playing along. I'd much rather be told the question is a mess than handed a tidy principle that only looks tidy because it's vague.
