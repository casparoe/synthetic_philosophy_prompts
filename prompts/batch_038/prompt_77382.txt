I am in the final weeks of a graduate seminar on intergenerational justice, taught jointly by my philosophy department and the school of public policy, and the paper I wrote for it has been selected for presentation at our department's graduate research colloquium later this month. I would like help preparing for the discussion period.

The thesis. The familiar case for setting the pure time preference rate at zero — that impatience is ethically defective because when a moment falls in time is not a morally relevant difference — is normally presented as a moral claim about how much weight we owe the welfare of future people. My paper argues that it is not a moral claim and does no moral work. What is doing the work is a structural constraint I call standpoint indifference: the requirement that an evaluation not depend on which point in time the evaluator occupies. That is a consistency condition on the evaluation function, not a thesis about the moral significance of the future. Three consequences I argue for. First, once standpoint indifference is imposed, the pure time preference parameter is not merely set to zero but ceases to be well defined, since there is no temporal index left for it to range over — which would mean that the long-running dispute between the near-zero camp and the camp that defends a materially positive rate is a dispute about a term that should be deleted from the Ramsey equation rather than estimated. Second, whatever normative work people believe that parameter was doing is in fact being done by the elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption, which is a claim about how utility varies with wealth and not about time at all. Third, the standard empirical objections to zero pure time preference — revealed impatience in market behaviour, the opportunity cost of capital displaced from private investment, and the argument that a stationary felicity function with no impatience admits of no optimum at all — target a descriptive parameter that on my account is not in the model, and therefore miss.

The audience. Roughly twenty people. Both seminar instructors will be there: a moral philosopher who works on population ethics and is broadly sympathetic to impartialism but allergic to what he calls philosophers' free reinterpretations of economists' parameters, and a welfare economist who runs integrated assessment models and regards the descriptive/prescriptive distinction as something philosophers habitually collapse. Also present: a handful of philosophy graduate students, three or four people from the policy school who care about cost-benefit practice and regulatory review, and one economic historian. Nobody needs the Ramsey equation explained, and several will be impatient with anything that reads as a refusal to engage the applied side.

What I want. A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. Give me the eight or ten objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by the product of likelihood and damage — an objection that is merely embarrassing should rank below one that guts the argument. Phrase each in the register of the person most likely to raise it: the modeller's version of an objection and the population ethicist's version are not the same objection even when they sound similar, and where two really are one objection in different clothes, tell me which formulation is the dangerous one. For each, give a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds, followed by a frank note on where that reply is soft and which of my three conclusions it puts at risk. Tell me what I should concede in the opening minutes to take the wind out of the two or three most predictable attacks, and tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the hour. Finally, identify the question I should be hoping nobody asks — the one my framework has no good answer to — and say in a sentence why I cannot answer it.

Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or pointers to further literature; the room has read the standard material and I am asking for the dialectic, not for sources. And if, in working through this, you come to think the economist is simply right on some point and that I should narrow my claim accordingly, say so plainly and tell me what the narrowed version would be.
