I am in the third year of a PhD in jurisprudence, and last week a visitor from the economics department presented work in our reading group whose whole engine was a single analogy: a criminal sanction, he said, is simply a price that the state attaches to an act. He developed it in three steps. First, a fine and a price do the same kind of work — each attaches a cost to a course of action and thereby changes what a rational agent will choose — so the difference between a tax on cigarettes and a penalty for theft is one of degree and of who collects. Second, once sanctions are prices, the question of how much to punish is just the question of optimal price-setting: set the expected cost equal to the marginal harm and you get the efficient quantity of the activity, exactly as with a Pigouvian correction. Third, and this is where the analogy does its real work, he concluded that desert has no content left over from this: to say that an offender deserves a certain punishment is only to endorse the price that deterrence happens to recommend, with an expressive surcharge attached. Retributivism, on his telling, is a wheel that turns nothing in the machinery; proportionality drops out as a special case of optimal pricing; and the only genuine remaining questions are empirical, concerning deterrent elasticity and enforcement cost.

I pushed back with three cases, and he had an answer to each, which is why I want them audited rather than recited. The first was the well-known field experiment in which Israeli day-care centres introduced a fine for late collection and lateness promptly increased: I suggested this shows that converting a moral expectation into a price changes what people take themselves to be doing, not merely what it costs them. His reply was that the fine had simply been set far too low, and that the study is about badly chosen prices rather than about the price form as such. The second was the practice of day fines, under which the same offence costs a wealthy defendant vastly more than a poor one: I argued that pricing logic should demand a single amount, since what deters is the expected cost, and that scaling to income makes sense only if the sanction is meant to be felt. He replied that income scaling is a cheap way of equalising the marginal disutility of a cost across people who value money differently, and that this is textbook. The third was the firm that writes its expected environmental penalties into its cost of doing business and offends at the efficient rate: I said this is precisely what his view licenses and precisely what everyone finds objectionable. He answered that if the harm is priced correctly the offence ceases to be objectionable, and that my revulsion only shows the price was too low.

What I need is an audit of the analogy itself, not a defence or a refutation of retributivism. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the buyer, the seller, the good, the demand curve, the moment of transaction, and the alternative uses of the money spent — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his argument from the ones that are merely verbal or that his side could absorb without damage. I want to know which features of a price the analogy quietly imports into punishment that a sanction may lack: voluntary participation, a willing counterparty, the fact that a price carries no statement about whether the purchase was wrong, and the possibility of paying up front rather than being exposed to a probability of enforcement at some later date. Say whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — in particular whether desert is left any independent work to do, or whether he is right that it is an expressive surcharge. If the analogy fails for some purposes and succeeds for others, locate the line: my suspicion is that the honest result is a good account of regulatory and strict-liability offences and a poor one of core wrongs, and I would like to know whether that division can be principled or is merely ad hoc. Finally, if the price model is the wrong master analogy, tell me what a better one would be and what it would have to preserve that this one preserves well.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the analogy holds, and how far — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question back to me.
