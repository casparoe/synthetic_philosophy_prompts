I have ninety minutes with a first-year graduate seminar in political philosophy, and the aim is to get them to see what actually separates prioritarianism from egalitarianism. I want to build the session around Parfit's leveling-down objection rather than around texts, and I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

The students arrive from three weeks on Rawls's difference principle, and I expect several to assume the priority view is just that principle restated in welfare terms. Tell me what has to be secured before the outcome comparison can do any work: I suspect the in-one-respect-better versus all-things-considered-better distinction must precede the numbers, but I don't know where welfarism and interpersonal comparison belong, or whether the person-affecting idea should surface at all. Vet the toy case I intend to use — two hundred people, half at welfare 100 and half at 200; then all at 150; then all at 100 — and say what it must contain and what it must leave out for the objection to bite rather than look like a cheap trick.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect: that the objection asserts leveling down is good, that it disqualifies any concern for the worst off, that prioritarianism is the difference principle in disguise. Fix the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, give me one closing question whose answers separate students who hold the distinction from students who have memorized a slogan, and say which neighbouring disputes — mere addition, deontic egalitarianism, whether the priority view is even consequentialist — I should defer and openly announce as deferred. Throughout, mark which moves are settled among people who work on this and which remain genuinely contested; I don't want to hand the seminar a live dispute dressed up as consensus.
