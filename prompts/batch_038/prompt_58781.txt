I am preparing a five-page handout for an interdisciplinary graduate reading group (philosophy, computer science, education) on the surprise examination, and I need the centrepiece of the second page: a sorting instrument, not an exposition.

Please write a decision tree whose leaves are positions on the paradox. The tree must be built from the logical space first — the questions, and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then populated with actual views; any combination of answers that no one occupies should be kept as a leaf and diagnosed in a clause (incoherent, merely unpopular, or simply unexplored).

Set-up for the group, which the questions must be answerable from and must not re-explain: a teacher announces on Monday that there will be an exam on exactly one of the next five weekdays, and that on the morning of the exam the students will not be able to deduce in advance that it falls on that day. The students reason backwards — if no exam by Thursday it must be Friday, so Friday is deducible, so not Friday; then Thursday, and so on — and conclude no exam is possible. On Thursday the exam is given, and they are surprised. Add one wrinkle of my own that the tree has to cope with: the teacher also said that any student who, on Monday morning, produces a sound deduction that the exam will occur on some particular day receives an automatic A and the exam is cancelled for everyone.

Constraints on the instrument:

1. Exactly five branching questions — I will not run to a second page — each phrased so that a reader could answer it from the set-up alone, without knowing which position that answer leads to and without the question containing any position's name or slogan.
2. The five questions must between them turn on: whether the backward induction is sound when the announcement it reasons about would be falsified by the induction's own success; whether, after the induction, the students count as knowing the conclusion or only as believing it; whether the announcement is best read as self-undermining, as simply unfulfillable on the later days while perfectly fulfillable on the earlier ones, or as a claim with no determinate content; and whether the teacher is assumed infallible about what the students will deduce, since my Monday-A wrinkle makes that assumption do real work.
3. The leaves must include at least: the view that the students' reasoning is sound and the teacher was simply wrong; the view that the announcement is inconsistent and therefore vacuous; the view that the reasoning fails because the students' epistemic state is not closed under the argument they perform; the view that the prediction is unfulfillable rather than false, which lets Thursday be a genuine surprise; and one leaf that nobody occupies.
4. Where two leaves cannot in fact be separated by these five questions, say so at the point in the tree where the confusion arises rather than pretending otherwise.
5. Do not include references, a reading list, or any attribution to a specific author or paper.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per branching question, each giving the question and then the positions (or the unoccupied combination, or the flag from constraint 4) that the two or three available answers lead to. Each item should run to no more than four sentences. No introduction, no conclusion, no summary of the paradox, no closing remarks.
