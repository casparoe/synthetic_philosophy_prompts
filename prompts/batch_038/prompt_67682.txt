I am in the second year of a philosophy and public policy degree, and the module I am taking this term is on evidence and inference in the social sciences. Our last two seminars were devoted to the long-running argument over whether the world has become substantially less violent over the past few centuries, and I have come away more puzzled by the shape of the disagreement than by anything the data shows. What I want is a diagnosis of that disagreement, not a verdict on it. I should say plainly what I am not asking for: not a summary of either side's case, and not a reheated list of the standard objections to the widely read popular statement of the decline thesis. Both are easy to come by and neither would help me.

Here is the puzzle as I see it. The participants appear to be working from much the same historical record: homicide rates in Western Europe since the late medieval period, battle deaths both in absolute terms and per head of population since the early nineteenth century, the absence of war between the great powers since 1945, and the changing legal and social standing of practices such as judicial torture, slavery, and the beating of wives and children. Almost nobody disputes these figures as such, and where they do, the disputes get resolved and the argument continues unchanged on exactly the same lines. Yet the disagreement has now run for well over a decade without any visible convergence, and each side tends to describe the other as failing a basic test of statistical or of moral competence. That combination — shared data, no movement, mutual contempt — is what I want explained.

Four exchanges in particular seem to me to be doing something other than trading evidence.

First, one side says that the twentieth century was the bloodiest in recorded history in absolute terms, so that any talk of decline simply ignores the two world wars; the other replies that raw counts are meaningless without a denominator, since there are many more people alive than there were. Each then accuses the other of selecting the normalisation that happens to suit it, and neither normalisation looks arbitrary when considered on its own.

Second, one side argues that the quantity being measured is not well behaved: the distribution of deaths in armed conflict has an extremely heavy tail, a single war can dominate decades of data, and a few centuries of observations therefore license very little about the upper tail, so that the calm since 1945 may be within the ordinary variability of the process rather than evidence of structural change. The other side replies that this cuts the opposite way, since a heavy tail is precisely why one should want to know what mechanism has apparently suppressed the largest events, and that eight decades without great-power war is a fact about the world demanding explanation. Both are invoking the same statistical property in support of opposite conclusions.

Third, one side maintains that the decline thesis quietly narrows what is being counted — blows and bullets, but not mass incarceration, economic coercion, ecological damage, or the suffering of non-human animals — so that part of the trend is an artefact of the definition. The other replies that this is moving the goalposts, that 'violence' has a tolerably clear core, and that expanding it to cover anything that reduces welfare makes the claim unfalsifiable and hence worthless.

Fourth, and less discussed in our reading, there is a strand in which the quarrel seems to be about whether the question could have a settled answer at all: whether a claim that the world is becoming less violent is a claim about a rate, about a propensity or disposition of the international system, about a moral climate, or about a loose family of trends that admit of no single summary, so that the two sides are not even purporting to describe the same kind of thing.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I fully expect it to be partly wrong — is that most of the heat comes from the third and fourth strands, that is, from a largely verbal or conceptual disagreement about the extension of 'violence' and about what sort of property a decline would be, with the first and second strands functioning as the technical theatre in which that disagreement gets fought out. If that is right I want to be told so, and told with reasons; if it is wrong I want to know where.

Please therefore give me a diagnosis, addressing each of the following.

1. Sort the four strands. For each, say what category of disagreement it belongs to — purely verbal; substantive but about measurement and normalisation; substantive and empirical; methodological, concerning what historical data can license about propensities; normative, concerning what counts as harm and how harms are to be weighted; or some mixture — and justify the assignment by reference to what each side actually says in that strand, not to what you suspect they secretly mean.

2. Say explicitly, strand by strand, where the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading and where they are genuinely contradicting one another. A contradiction requires that there be a proposition both are asserting and denying; tell me which of the four strands contains one.

3. Address whether this is a case in which there is no fact of the matter. I do not want a blanket answer either way. If some sub-claims are truth-apt and others are not, or are truth-apt only relative to a stipulation, identify which are which, and say what work the stipulation is doing.

4. For each strand, state what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a better measurement strategy, a formal result, a new kind of evidence, a change of question, or an admission that no progress should be expected — and then say honestly whether that progress is achievable in principle by people who already share the data, or whether it requires something the debate does not currently have.

5. Test my hypothesis directly. Where it is wrong, or too crude, or true of some strands and false of others, say so and explain the error. In particular, tell me whether the second strand really is downstream of the conceptual ones, or whether it is an independent technical dispute that has been mistaken for a proxy war.

6. Close by proposing the minimum reformulation of the original question that both sides could answer from the same dataset without talking past one another — and say what each side would have to give up in order to accept that reformulation. If no such reformulation exists, argue that it does not.

Please write the whole response in British English.
