I sit on the credentialing panel for our state's court interpreter program — seven of us, mostly working interpreters plus two people seconded from the administrative office of the courts. Here's the mess I need help with.

A vendor applied for equivalency certification for a speech-to-speech translation system they want used at arraignments and initial appearances, which is exactly where our human shortage bites hardest. Under our rules an applicant sits the same practical exam as a human candidate: recorded courtroom vignettes, a live-role segment, a terminology and ethics section. They passed comfortably — the best scores the panel has seen in four years. Then in file review it came out that the build they submitted runs a cached-response layer, basically an enormous table mapping the exam's audio patterns and scenario structures to precomputed translations, and by the vendor's own numbers that layer handled something like 94% of the exam turns. The remainder fell through to their general model, which they say is what would actually get deployed. Worse, it turns out the "live" segment has been drawn from a bank of about forty scripts that's been recycled since 2016 and is sold by the accredited test-prep vendors, so plenty of our human certificants have effectively memorized it too.

The panel has split badly. One member wants denial on the grounds that a lookup table can't understand anything, and she keeps invoking Searle's Chinese Room plus that older thought experiment about a machine that passes an interview purely by table lookup. Another says our statute has only ever certified reliability of output, we have never examined a human candidate's inner life either, and adopting "does it actually understand" as a criterion would be a new standard we cannot operationalize and that would flunk a chunk of our own membership. Meanwhile the counties with the worst backlogs are pushing hard for a yes, one of them has pilot data showing fewer error flags than with the part-time human interpreters they can barely staff, and there's a bill in committee that would exempt machine interpretation from certification entirely in misdemeanor matters — so if we deny, the legislature may just wave the vendor through with no conditions at all.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before we vote on the 14th: a recommendation (approve, deny, or approve with conditions), conditions specific enough that staff could actually enforce them and a timeframe attached to them, and reasoning rather than a summary of the fight. Two things I especially want handled. First: does the Chinese Room / lookup-table objection do any real work at the level of the decision, or does it collapse into a question about the validity of our exam — I lean toward the second but I want to be argued out of it if I'm wrong, and I want to know what the objection still buys us even if it doesn't decide the case. Second: give the strongest honest version of approving as-is, given the backlog, given the bill, and given that we certify humans on output alone. Also flag anything in here that's technically outside our jurisdiction but that the vote will expose whether we like it or not. No citations or reading list, nobody on this panel will read one, and please don't invent case law. Nine hundred to twelve hundred words is fine, casual register is fine, it's going to seven tired people.
