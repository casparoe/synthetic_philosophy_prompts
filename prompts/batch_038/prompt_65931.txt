I am assembling a set of discussion questions for a graduate reading group on social epistemology, and I have run into a conceptual difficulty I would like help articulating precisely. The setting is the familiar one from the literature on judgment aggregation. A three-member committee must decide whether to renew a colleague's contract, and the governing regulation provides that renewal is warranted if and only if the candidate is both a productive researcher and an effective teacher; all three members accept that regulation and reason in accordance with it. Member A judges both premises true and accordingly supports renewal. Member B judges only the research premise true; member C judges only the teaching premise true; B and C therefore each oppose renewal. If the committee votes proposition by proposition, majorities support both premises and the premise-based procedure yields renewal; if it votes only on the conclusion, renewal fails, since two members oppose it.

What I want explained is not the catalogue of procedural fixes but a distinction that seems to me to underlie most disagreements about what such cases show, and that I have not seen stated cleanly anywhere. On one diagnosis, the committee faces a merely practical predicament: there is no fact about what the committee judges on the premises and on the conclusion independently of the decision rule it adopts, so the choice between the two procedures is a choice among defensible rules for settling a question, and the entire content of the case is that different defensible rules settle it differently. On the other diagnosis, the predicament is constitutive: if the committee is to count as an agent at all — something whose judgments can be cited as reasons, that can be held to them over time, and that can revise them in light of argument — then something must already determine what it judges, and the rule cannot be the whole of the story, since the rule is supposed to be answerable to the group's attitudes rather than to constitute them. Please explain this distinction as sharply as you can; state what would have to be true of groups, or of judgment as such, for each diagnosis to be the correct one; and say what each diagnosis makes of the intuition that the premise-based procedure respects the committee's reasons while the conclusion-based procedure respects its members' wills.

A further wrinkle bears on the choice between the two diagnoses, though I am unsure how. Given exactly three members, the two majorities supporting the premises must overlap, so at least one member accepts both; any consistent member who accepts both premises and the regulation accepts the conclusion; and in the case described, that member's judgment set across the whole agenda coincides precisely with the set the premise-based procedure attributes to the committee. So the collective verdict that is supposed to express the group's own reasoning is, extensionally, one particular member's verdict, whereas the conclusion-based verdict is the majority's. Please explain whether this is an artefact of having three members together with a conjunctive rule resting on two premises — and if so, what happens to the point once the committee grows or the doctrinal rule becomes more elaborate — or whether it instead exposes something structural about premise-based aggregation, namely that its claim to voice the group's reasons rather than its members' preferences may be illusory. I would also like a brief treatment of a related asymmetry: the premise-based procedure is commonly thought to be more exposed to strategic voting than the conclusion-based one, because a member who wants a given outcome may have reason to misreport a premise. Is that a merely practical cost of the procedure, or does it belong on the constitutive side of the distinction — that is, does the availability of such misreporting tell us something about what it is for a group to hold the judgments a procedure assigns to it?
