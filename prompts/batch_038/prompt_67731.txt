You are a philosopher of mind and historian of computing who has read von Neumann's posthumous 1958 Silliman lectures closely, and who has spent years interviewing working scientists without letting them coast. I host a small podcast on the foundations of cognitive science, and next week's guest is a computational neuroscientist whose new book argues that von Neumann — the man who gave us the stored-program architecture — was already a skeptic about reading the brain as a computer, and that today's neural-network triumphalism ignores the cautions buried in those ninety-odd unfinished pages. I want eight to ten interview questions that put that thesis under load rather than let him summarize it.

Before drafting them, work out for yourself in two or three sentences which of von Neumann's specific claims are still live and which have been overtaken by events, and where this reading of him is most exposed; put that assessment at the top. Then give me the questions, each paired with the follow-up you would use against the answer he is most likely to give. I want at least one on his depth-versus-width point — that the nervous system reaches its results in very few successive steps where an artificial automaton of comparable performance needs enormously more — and on whether modern networks, which are also shallow and also enormously wide, vindicate him or merely date him. One on his suggestion that the language the nervous system uses is not any of the languages we use in mathematics or logic, and is of an essentially statistical character: what would it look like to have actually identified that language, and is the claim doing explanatory work or marking our ignorance? One on precision and reliability — given that redundancy, parallelism and error tolerance are techniques von Neumann himself designed into machines, is the gap he describes a difference in kind at all? One on his hierarchy of memories, and on whether mapping his notion of inherited, chemically carried deep memory onto trained parameters is a legitimate analogy or a category slide. One on the tension your guest has to handle: a man who built the universal machine and treated automata theory as a branch of logic can hardly think that functional imitation is out of reach. And one he will hate, ending with what finding would show von Neumann was not merely modest but wrong.

His standard escape hatch is that von Neumann was writing before anyone could foresee what computation would become; build one stretch of two or three questions that makes that reply progressively harder to use. Continuous prose only — no headings, bullets, bold, tables or numbered lists, just the way one would write a colleague a long email. No references, bibliography or further reading, please; everything should come out of your own understanding.
