I have just finished my first strategy cycle as the sole program officer at a small family foundation, and before I defend our ranking procedure to the trustees again I want to know what it actually commits us to. The procedure is our own, built loosely on the three-factor framing familiar from effective altruism: for each candidate cause area, the trustees assign a whole number from 1 to 10 for importance, for tractability, and for neglectedness, and we multiply the three to get a single score, with anything below a fixed threshold denied a strategy memo. Scores attach to cause areas as wholes, never to interventions or to grant sizes — and yet in the very same meetings the trustees insist that only the marginal dollar matters and that they are not in the business of rewarding fields that would have progressed anyway.

What theory of value, and what account of decision under uncertainty, does multiplying three such ratings presuppose? I want the strict presuppositions distinguished from what merely sits comfortably with the practice: does it demand that the factors be cardinal and mutually independent, that a cause's worth scale with the product rather than the sum of its features, and what follows from the fact that a single zero annihilates the score, so that an area rated 9 for importance and 0 for neglectedness lands exactly where we would put something we consider trivial? Is the marginal-dollar rhetoric consistent with cause-level scoring at all, or does the rubric in fact presuppose a claim about average value? Tell me which rival views the procedure quietly forecloses, where it is philosophically underdetermined — several theories licensing the same numbers — and what would have to change in the practice itself, not merely in our vocabulary, if we rebuilt it around diminishing returns. Please say how confident you are in each of your claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
