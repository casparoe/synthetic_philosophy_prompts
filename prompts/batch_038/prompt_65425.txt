I am assembling material for a graduate seminar on the ethics of reproductive selection, and what I want from you is a disciplined case analysis rather than a survey of the literature, which I know well.

The episode is the British "saviour sibling" controversy of the early 2000s, and in particular the Hashmi family. Their son Zain has beta-thalassemia major, for which the only curative treatment is a stem-cell transplant from a closely HLA-matched donor; no suitable donor was available. The parents sought IVF with preimplantation genetic diagnosis in order to transfer an embryo that was both free of the condition and a tissue match for Zain, with a view to using the newborn's cord blood. The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority initially took the position that tissue typing could be licensed only where the embryo tested was itself at risk of inheriting the disorder in question; after a public consultation it reversed that position and licensed treatment in cases of this kind. A campaign group then litigated the question, arguing that the regulator had no statutory power to authorise the procedure at all — succeeding at first instance and losing on appeal. A few years earlier, an essentially identical procedure had been carried out in the United States with no regulatory gatekeeping whatever, and around the same time a deaf American couple drew attention by seeking a donor in order to have a deaf child.

Please analyse this episode through two theories. The first is Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence: the claim that, of the possible children a couple might have, there is a moral reason to select the one expected to have the best life, where the reason is grounded in the expected wellbeing of the child to be born and is a reason for selection rather than for intervention. The second is Habermas's critique of liberal eugenics, which distinguishes the comparatively permissive case of prenatal diagnosis and embryo selection from positive genetic programming, and objects to the latter because a programmed person can no longer understand herself as the undivided author of her own life, which in turn disrupts the symmetry of relations among free and equal persons.

For each theory, begin by fixing the mapping explicitly: who the agent is, what the option set consists of, what the decision criterion is, and what verdict, if any, follows. Then address the following. Does procreative beneficence generate a recommendation at all when the property being selected for — histocompatibility with a particular already-existing child — is indexical to a third party rather than a welfare property of the child to be born? Is the familiar reply, that a matched child will have a better life because she will have a living brother, available within the principle, or does it substitute family-level aggregation for the comparison of possible lives that the principle actually orders? What does the non-identity problem do to any claim that the resulting child is wronged, and can a Kantian "mere means" objection be made non-comparative in a way that survives it? On the Habermas side: is his mechanism engaged by selection rather than programming, and if it is not, does his framework have the resources to capture whatever does seem objectionable here, or must the objection be imported from elsewhere — a right to an open future, the disability critique, a doctrine of permissible parental motives?

Attend carefully to where each theory is silent. Both are theories about the moment of selection, whereas the morally load-bearing events in this case happen years afterwards: cord blood at birth costs the newborn almost nothing, bone marrow harvested under general anaesthesia in toddlerhood does not; assent, refusal, and the possibility that the transplant fails and the child grows up as a rescue that did not rescue are all outside the scope of a one-shot selection principle. Say whether the public and philosophical debate was framed around the wrong event, and what a theory would have to add to handle the later ones.

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction. Is this episode a confirmation of either theory, an anomaly, or a case neither anticipated? Note that the forum that actually decided the matter was not an ethical one but a dispute over regulatory competence and over a statutory requirement that account be taken of the welfare of any child who may be born as a result of treatment — a welfarist test into which a thoroughly Kantian consideration, whether the child would be valued for her own sake, entered by the back door. Consider whether such a test is administrable at all, given what a licensing committee can realistically establish about parental motives, and what the answer implies about the practical reach of both theories.

Do not give me a reading list or citations. Where you think my summary of the facts or of either position is inaccurate, say so plainly; and where you find yourself stretching a theory to make it speak, mark the stretch rather than concealing it.
