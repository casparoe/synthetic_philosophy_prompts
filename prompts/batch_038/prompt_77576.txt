my brother is a property claims adjuster, commercial lines, fifteen years, and over thanksgiving he somehow got me into an argument about act-of-God clauses — I'm the one in the family who reads medieval Islamic philosophy for fun, which is not a sentence I expected to say out loud at a dinner table. He insists the clauses are basically vestigial now, that nobody ever litigates them because the policies define the term around natural hazards and it never gets anywhere near the metaphysics. And it hit me on the drive home that the one metaphysics that would make those clauses do enormous, ruinous work is occasionalism — specifically al-Ghazali's version in the Incoherence of the Philosophers, where the fire doesn't burn the cotton by any power of its own, God creates the burning at the moment of contact, and the only reason anyone can predict anything is that God's habit is stable and he generally does the same thing again.

So I want to turn this into a joke that is actually philosophically correct, which is the hard part. Write me a parody internal training document for a large property insurer whose legal and actuarial departments have sincerely and enthusiastically converted to occasionalism. Pick whatever corporate genre carries it best — a claims handling manual, a new-adjuster field guide, a "causal determination standard" with a FAQ box and an escalation matrix stapled to the end — and keep it completely deadpan, no winking, around 800 words, numbered sections, in that flat confident register of something written by a committee that has never once been doubted by anyone.

The humor has to come from the content, so please get the details right. The manual has to cope with: what a covered peril even is when the only genuine cause of every loss is God, and whether the act-of-God exclusion therefore excludes everything or nothing; subrogation, i.e. who exactly you sue when the faulty wiring did not in fact cause the fire; how the special investigations unit is supposed to build an arson case out of a sequence of events with no causal links in it; reserving and capital modelling when the divine habit could in principle change without notice, and what you say to the state examiners who require rates to rest on historical loss experience; and whether "God's habit has been exceptionally stable in this class of business for six thousand years" is a defensible rating factor or an unfair-discrimination complaint waiting to happen. I'd like a section on miracles — named peril, excluded peril, or uninsured maintenance issue — where the drafting committee visibly disagrees with itself in the margins of its own document. Also work in at least one piece of quoted material, a memo fragment from outside counsel or a letter from a rival carrier's marketing department, pressing the old Andalusian rejoinder that if you deny causes you destroy knowledge, since to know a thing just is to know it through its cause; invent whoever signs it.

Two constraints that matter to me. First, al-Ghazali is not the punchline and this should not be a religion joke: he isnt a fatalist and he does not deny that we observe regular sequences, he denies that the connection between them is necessary, and there's a real scholarly argument about whether that even amounts to denying causation at all — the people who wrote this document should know that perfectly well and be smug about it. The target is the industry's causal vocabulary, which turns out to be doing almost no work the moment you look at it hard. Second, commit to one outcome and don't wobble halfway through: either the conversion changes nothing operationally and the manual is functionally identical to the one they used to issue, or it changes everything and the company is insolvent by the second quarter. The reader should be able to tell which one you picked, and should come away wondering whether a metaphysical thesis that makes no difference to how you price or investigate a loss is a thesis at all — and whether the same is quietly true of the causal language already in my brother's handbook. No footnotes or references, I'll handle that part myself.
