My brother had his corpus callosum cut last spring — full section, done in two stages — after something like twenty years of seizures nothing else would touch. He's doing okay. But he fails some of the tests now: flash a picture to his left visual field and he'll swear nothing was there while his left hand reaches out and picks the right object off a table, and once in a while his hands will fight each other over a zipper. Since then I've been working through philosophy-of-mind stuff on my own, and last week I tried to write down an argument that he's still one person. It backfired and I cant see where.

(1) For a batch of experiences to be experiences of one subject, they have to be capable of being had together — there has to be a single perspective they could all show up in.

(2) Being capable of being had together is a matter of there being a route by which what's in one of them can get to the others.

(3) My brother's hemispheres lost that route when they cut the callosum, which is exactly why the left hand knows what the mouth can't say.

(4) So his left-field experiences and his speaking-side experiences can't be had together, so they aren't one subject's.

(5) And you can't say they're nobody's — each side plainly sees, wants, and remembers, so there are two subjects in that bed.

(6) Except the route in (2) isn't an on/off switch. They staged the surgery for a reason, subcortical and brainstem paths still carry things across, and there's at least one supposedly-complete case where the two sides kept sharing information under testing. In normal brains it's the same story by degree: most of what my visual system does never reaches anything I could report, and when I'm driving and arguing with my wife at the same time the integration is thinner than when I actually pay attention.

(7) If the thing that makes experiences one subject's comes in degrees, then there's no sharp answer to how many subjects there are — it's indeterminate.

(8) But how many subjects are in this body is a counting question, and counting questions have definite answers.

(9) So something above goes, and the obvious candidate is (1)/(2): experiences don't have to be capable of being had together to belong to one person.

(10) Which means the disconnection findings never showed my brother is two people. They also never showed he's one. And if integration comes in degrees while personhood doesn't, there's no determinate fact about how many people are in any body at any moment — me driving, my wife beside me, my brother in his, nobody. The argument I built to show he's still my brother makes the question meaningless.

Is this valid and I just have to swallow a premise, or did I cheat somewhere without noticing? If I have to lose one I'd rather keep (1) than (8) — I really don't want to give up on being able to count people. But tell me what keeping (1) costs: what account of unity makes the split-brain data come out right without making him two people, and without making everyone two people? And if you'd rather dump (8), say what happens to whether identity is what matters in survival, since I think that whole debate leans hard on being able to count.
