Three months ago my mother moved into a memory-care unit on the second floor of a facility twenty minutes from my house, and I have gone there every Sunday since. The unit keeps a laminated sheet by the door: eleven residents, four aides, who eats what, who must not be left alone, who signs the logbook on the way out. Standing in the corridor, that sheet is the whole of the place. Sitting in the chair by her bed, it is nothing of the kind — she is still hosting, still expecting someone, and every visit adds a person the sheet has never heard of. I have come to think of the two vantage points as irreconcilable, and not merely as a matter of sentiment.

I read mathematical logic as an undergraduate and have not thought about it in twenty years, but I have been unable to stop thinking about the point Skolem pressed in the address he gave to the Scandinavian mathematicians in 1922. If set theory is consistent at all, then it has a model whose domain contains only countably many objects — and yet that same model satisfies, as a theorem proved inside it, the claim that there are more reals than naturals. There is no contradiction here, because the pairing that would match the model's reals off against its naturals one for one is not itself among the model's objects: from outside we can see such a pairing, but from inside it does not exist, and so from inside there really are too many. What the model takes for its first uncountable ordinal is, looked at from out here, a countable ordinal — a stretch whose end is visible to anyone standing far enough away. That is precisely the shape of a Sunday afternoon in that unit.

The facility is holding a short service for residents and families in June, and the activities coordinator has asked whether anyone would read something. I should like to read a poem that carries this without announcing it.

Please write a sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, thirty-nine lines, with the first stanza closing its lines, in order, on NAME, LIST, ROOM, COUNT, REAL, WHOLE, and with the remaining stanzas following the standard rotation — 6-1-5-2-4-3, that is, each stanza opens on the previous stanza's final end-word and then alternates inward from the outside. Lines of roughly ten to twelve syllables, unrhymed, enjambed wherever you wish; I would rather the end-words landed quietly than that they announced themselves. The envoi must use all six end-words, three of them at line-ends and three buried mid-line.

The form should do the philosophical work. The six stanzas never leave the room: they are spoken from inside, and everything said in them is true as said in them. If a list appears in a stanza, it fails to be complete; the one who is left over should be produced by a procedure available to my mother herself, something she could run again on any list anyone handed her, rather than by an appeal to anything outside — a set theorist should recognise what that procedure is without my naming it, and I do not want it named. The envoi alone is spoken from the corridor, in the voice of someone holding the clipboard: flat, administrative, not cruel, and correct. It is the only place in the poem where the pairing exists, and it should not triumph on that account. Leave open whether the one always left over is my mother, or I, or the aide who signs the logbook.

Keep the jargon out entirely: no axiom, model, set, bijection, cardinal, ordinal, diagonal, paradox, no names of mathematicians, and no title that signals the subject. Ban countable and uncountable as words — the distinction has to be carried by COUNT alone, used in one sense through the stanzas and in its other sense in the envoi. You may use infinite once, and only once.

Two further requirements. First, the audience in that room will be families and staff, none of whom have heard of any of this; the poem has to work for them as a poem about a room and a mother, with the logic as its shape and not its topic. Second, it must not misstate the theorem: nothing in the poem may suggest that the inside and the outside contradict each other, or that the corridor sees through an illusion that the chair is in the grip of. Both are true where they are said. No consolation, no uplift, no turn in the final line that tells us how to feel; I would prefer the last line to be as plain as a signature.

Give me the poem under its title, and then, separately, three or four sentences telling me which lines you intend as the internal claims and which as the external ones, so that I can satisfy myself I have not asked you for something incoherent.
