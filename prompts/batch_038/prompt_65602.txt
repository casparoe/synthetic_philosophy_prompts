so i've been writing up my view on the wrong kind of reasons problem for a metaethics seminar and i want a consistency check before i stand up in front of people and get picked apart. here's the package:

1. for pro-attitudes, reasons come in two kinds. Some bear on the object — the wit of the guy you've been paid to admire, the mud's being… mud. Others bear only on whether it'd be good for you to be in the state. only the first sort are reasons for the attitude, strictly speaking. the second sort are at most reasons to want to want it, or to bring it about that you do.

2. i'm a buck-passer about value: for something to be good is just for there to be reasons to respond to it favourably. no extra property behind that.

3. so the evil demon doesn't scare me. the demon's threat is a real consideration, not a psuedo-reason, but it isn't a reason to desire the saucer of mud — it's a reason to get myself to desire it. so the buck-passing account doesn't end up saying the mud is good, and i don't feel like i'm dodging anything here.

4. what explains the split, on my view, is that each attitude settles a question. belief settles whether it's true, desire whether the thing is good, admiration whether the person is admirable. right-kind reasons are the ones that bear on the question that attitude is in the business of answering.

5. for belief specifically i think there just aren't wrong-kind reasons, full stop. not because of a definitional trick, but because you can't actually believe on practical grounds — belief-forming doesn't respond to that kind of thing.

6. and more generally: a consideration only counts as a reason for an agent if it's capable of being what they're moved by, something that could show up in their deliberation and steer them. reasons a person couldn't deliberate from aren't reasons for them.

two things people have already thrown at me that i think are nothing: someone said 3 contradicts itself, since i call the demon's threat a real reason and then deny it's a reason to desire the mud. and someone said 5 is refuted by the fact that i also think there can be practical reasons to avoid an inquiry or to stay out of a conversation, which they claim is just a wrong-kind reason for belief wearing a hat.

so: go through the pairs, tell me which are in genuine tension and which only look like it, and rank the real ones by severity instead of treating them as equally bad. for each real one, say what has to go and what it costs me to lose it. flag anything that's not in tension but just false. then tell me the single cheapest revision to the whole set — i'm not attached to any particular item and i don't want to be told which one i'd least like to drop, i want to know which ones i actually can't keep together. no survey of the literature, i've read the survey.
