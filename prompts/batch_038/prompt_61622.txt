I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long piece about how people decide which problems are worth devoting a career to. The spine of the article is the three-part test that has become standard in effective-altruism circles: a cause is worth prioritizing in proportion to its importance, its tractability, and its neglectedness. My editor is happy for me to explain the framework; I would rather pressure-test it.

Please take on the role of a decision theorist who has spent years evaluating cause-prioritization methodology and who is willing to be blunt about which parts of the framework do real work and which are decorative.

Start with a short brainstorming pass in the open, before you commit to any scores. Sketch the kinds of test cases that would reveal whether a criterion is pulling its weight: pairs of causes where two criteria point one way and the third points the other; cases where a criterion appears to be quietly redescribing something the other two already captured; cases where a criterion is invoked only after the conclusion is known. Generate several of your own examples rather than reaching for the stock ones.

Then do two things.

First, rank importance, tractability, and neglectedness by what I will call marginal decision-relevance — how often, in your judgment, that criterion actually changes the verdict the other two would have reached on their own. Score each from 0 to 10 on that dimension, where 10 means the criterion frequently flips decisions the others leave wrong or tied, and 0 means it essentially never does. State the resulting order explicitly, not merely the numbers. I want a definite answer on one sub-question in particular: if importance is properly measured at the margin — as the value of the next unit of effort — and returns to effort diminish, is neglectedness already implicit in it, with nothing independent left to say? Or does it retain a distinct job?

Second, rank four criteria often floated as additions: personal fit (how well suited this particular agent is to the problem); learning or option value (how much work on the cause teaches you or keeps doors open); robustness across moral views (how the cause fares under the range of ethical theories one finds plausible); and crowdedness construed as something other than neglectedness (how one more competent entrant changes the field, including by attracting or repelling further talent). Score each 0 to 10 on whether it deserves a permanent seat at the table rather than a footnote, and tell me which one you would cut first if the framework had to remain a triad.

A note on how I want this handled. Take a position and defend it: I am not looking for a survey of considerations on both sides, and I would rather you be wrong in an interesting direction than balanced into uselessness. Where you assign a number, say what evidence or argument would move it, so I can see the shape of your uncertainty rather than a hedge. You do not need to cite sources, name studies, or assemble a reading list — the reasoning is what I am after, and I will do the reporting myself. Please keep the whole thing to roughly 900 words.
