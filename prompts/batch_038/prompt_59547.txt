i teach a survey seminar and we've just finished Braudel's three speeds of time — the near-motionless, the conjunctures, the event — and i want a short poem to send the students out with. Triptych, three movements, no rhyme required, something like 30–40 lines total, and the line counts should run backwards relative to how loud each layer is. Movement one, the sea and the mountains and the mud, wheat, transhumance, the coast that never changes: most lines, fewest finite verbs, and it has to read as possibility and constraint rather than fate — the geography isn't deciding anything, it's just what keeps coming back. Movement two, the conjonctures: prices, credit, harvest cycles, tax farms, the Atlantic opening behind everyone's back — middling length, should feel like weather on a ten-year cycle. Movement three gets one afternoon in the gulf of Patras in October 1571, and it can have all the vivid verbs and the blood, but it gets the fewest lines by far. Then a two-line coda: the fleet rebuilt within the year, bigger than it was. One refrain line should close each of the three movements, barely changed, and it's the thing that outlives the battle — that refrain is the argument. dont name braudel or use the phrase longue duree anywhere in the poem, and don't let it slide into a lecture about great men being sea foam. if you can manage it, let the third movement be the easiest one to write, the one the chronicles are already full of, so the poem shows why events crowd out structures in the record as well as in the head. someone who knows the argument should be able to say which movement is which speed and why the last one is the shortest; someone who doesnt should just think its a good poem about the Mediterranean.
