I am assembling a discussion handout for a graduate seminar on reference and existence, and before I commit anything to paper I want the analytical scaffolding checked. Your task is not a history of the slogan and not an adjudication of the underlying disputes; it is a disambiguation exercise, and I intend to reproduce the result, in edited form, for my students.

The occasion was this. In last week's session a student disposed of the ontological argument in roughly thirty seconds with the observation that "existence is not a predicate." The same formula was then pressed into service twice more before the hour was out: once to explain how it can be true that Sherlock Holmes does not exist without our thereby being committed to some item called Sherlock Holmes, and once to wave off a classmate's unease about arithmetic on the grounds that existence claims are in any case claims about concepts being satisfied, so the worry about numbers quantifying over a dubious realm simply does not arise. Three arguments, one shared premise, and no one stopped to ask whether it was even the same premise each time.

What I want from you is an ordered inventory of the distinct theses that sentence could be used to assert, each stated in regimented terms precise enough to be evaluated. I expect at least the following to be distinguished: a claim about the logical form of singular existential sentences; a claim about what grammatical category the English verb belongs to; the Fregean and Russellian claim that existence is a property of concepts or propositional functions rather than of objects, so that existential sentences are really second-order predications; a claim about the metaphysics of properties, which will look very different depending on whether one works with an abundant or a sparse conception; the Kantian reading on which being is not a real predicate, in the sense that it adds no further determination to the concept of a thing; and the purely deflationary point that the existential quantifier already does all the work the verb might have been thought to do. If there are further readings worth separating out, include them.

For each reading, give me a verdict on its status: whether it is a platitude that no competent philosopher denies, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is substantive and genuinely contested, and by whom in broad terms — Meinongians, free logicians who happily admit a first-level existence predicate, defenders of the predicate view generally — or whether it is simply false, and against what. I also want the entailment relations made explicit: which readings, if true, force the others, and which can be true while a stronger reading that an argument actually needs is false.

Then take the three seminar uses in turn and say which reading, if any, licenses each. Specifically: does any version of the slogan refute the ontological argument, and does the answer change when one moves to the modal version, which trades on necessary existence rather than existence simpliciter; does the slogan, on any reading, resolve the problem of true negative existentials about fictional names, or does it merely relocate that problem; and is the third use — the dismissal of the worry about arithmetic — actually supported, or does it presuppose the very claim about existence being a second-order matter that was supposed to be doing the work?

Two further deliverables. First, identify sentence patterns that are diagnostic between the readings, since I want a short exercise in which students classify examples: quantificational existentials about kinds, singular existentials about named individuals, negative existentials, and existential claims inside mathematics. Say which readings are embarrassed by which pattern. Second, tell me the single confusion I should put at the top of the handout as the thing students must not do, and state it in a sentence they can hold onto.

Please resist any temptation to append a survey of the literature or a list of sources; I know the terrain and my students have the syllabus. What I need is the menu of readings, the verdicts, and the mapping from readings to the arguments they were invoked to support.
