Thucydides reports that Athens voted to put to death every adult male in Mytilene and to enslave the women and children, sent a trireme to Lesbos to carry out the decree, then reconvened the next day, rescinded it by a further show of hands, and dispatched a second ship that overtook the first with almost nothing to spare. The rescission was effected by nothing but another vote of the same body: no court annulled the decree, no magistrate or second chamber could block it, and nothing recorded the change of mind except the new decree itself. Yet Athens also exposed to prosecution any citizen who proposed a decree contrary to law, and Socrates, on trial years later, made a point of having been the one member of the presiding board after Arginusae who refused to put an illegal motion to the vote.

What theory of political authority does the Mytilenean sequence presuppose, and can it be reconciled with that scrupulousness about procedure? Reconstruct the implicit view rather than naming it: is the Assembly's will self-validating and revisable at pleasure, so that a decree's validity and its wisdom are entirely separate matters with no institution standing between them — and if so, what does the second vote establish, that it was wiser than the first, or only that it came later? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits well with it (a thesis about popular sovereignty? about the absence of any higher law? about the epistemic value of a second deliberation by the same people?), and say where the practice is underdetermined, leaving room for rival theories to license it. Finally: what would have had to change, institutionally and in the demos' understanding of itself, for Athens to treat some decisions as beyond its own reach — as it eventually did when decrees were made subordinate to laws after the restoration of 403?
