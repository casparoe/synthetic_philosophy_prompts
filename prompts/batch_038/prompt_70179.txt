I work in risk regulation by day and I am auditing a graduate seminar in social epistemology this term; we have spent the last several weeks on the Clifford–James dispute over the ethics of belief, and I find that I keep dragging the conversation away from the seminar's toy cases and back to a single real episode. That episode is the American swine flu immunization decision of 1976. Since you cannot look anything up, here are the facts as I have them, and you may take them as given rather than hedge about them. In January and February of 1976, recruits at Fort Dix, New Jersey, came down with a respiratory illness; a private named David Lewis collapsed and died after a five-mile forced march. The New Jersey health department tested samples from the sick soldiers: most were the A/Victoria strain then in ordinary circulation, but two were atypical, and when those were sent to the CDC in Atlanta they were identified as swine influenza A, antigenically related to the virus of the 1918 pandemic. The CDC director wrote a memo on February 13 calling for the immunization of every American, a senior CDC official mentioned the 1918 connection at a press conference on February 19, and the story ran away from them. President Ford was briefed in mid-March, convened a panel that included Salk and Sabin, and announced on television that the government would vaccinate the entire population; Congress appropriated $135 million in April. The manufacturers refused to supply doses unless the government indemnified them against product liability that their insurers had declined to cover, and much of the spring was consumed by that fight. Meanwhile the virus itself did nothing: investigations of suspected outbreaks elsewhere in the world found no cases of the strain, the same official who had invoked 1918 in February said in July that there were no parallels between 1918 and the present situation, and an August cluster in Philadelphia that was briefly feared to be swine flu turned out to be the atypical pneumonia later named Legionnaires' disease. One FDA researcher who went public with doubts about the vaccine's safety was dismissed for insubordination. Vaccination began in the autumn and the program was suspended a few months later, after Guillain–Barré syndrome — which can cause paralysis, respiratory failure, and death — clustered around the vaccine at a rate on the order of one additional case per hundred thousand vaccinated; roughly 43 million Americans had been injected by then, and some of those who developed GBS died. The pandemic never came.

What I want is a disciplined analysis of that episode through Clifford and James: the epistemology, not the policy post-mortem, which I have read elsewhere and do not need repeated. Begin with the mapping and be explicit about it. Whose belief is actually under assessment — Ford's, the CDC director's, the advisory panel's, or some institutional state of mind that no individual in the building held? What precisely counted as the evidence before them at the moment of decision: two atypical isolates, an antigenic kinship with 1918, and a serological picture suggesting the virus had circulated among recruits without leaving the base. Was that evidence sufficient for the belief that a pandemic was coming, sufficient only for the weaker judgment that the probability was high enough to insure against, or sufficient for neither? Then run Clifford's outcome-independence through the case. He insists that the question of right and wrong "has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it... not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him," and I want to know what that verdict looks like here, where the belief in question may have been sincerely held, cheaply earned, and wrong in a way nobody could have detected at the time. Note especially the structural inversion I keep tripping over: in the shipowner case a belief adopted against the evidence produces harm because the owner then declines to act, whereas in 1976 a belief arguably outrunning the evidence produced harm precisely because the officials did act, and the harm arrived from the intervention rather than from the hazard it was meant to forestall. Does Clifford's maxim care which way the causation runs? And does his further claim — that the real injury of credulity is to the common stock of trust, that the careless believer sins against mankind by making everyone slightly less able to rely on testimony — do genuine predictive work here, given what the affair did to the standing of public health authorities in the decades that followed? Then turn to James, and be rigorous about his conditions rather than waving at his general permissiveness. Was the option living, was it momentous, and above all was it forced? If the only thing that made it forced was the manufacturing calendar — the vaccine had to be grown in eggs, so a decision deferred into June was in practice a decision against vaccinating in the autumn — does that kind of temporal forcing have the same moral shape as the logical forcing James has in mind, or is it a different animal that his framework simply does not sort? Is there room in James for the thought that a confident public avowal can help create the fact it avows, and for its mirror image, that once a president has committed on television, the government's own later withdrawal is what destroys public confidence?

Finally, test the episode against a distinction I have been turning over privately, and feel free to attack the distinction rather than merely apply it. Separate three questions the seminar tends to collapse into one: whether the officials believed a pandemic was coming; whether they were entitled to act as if they believed it while holding no such belief, on the strength of an explicit low-probability assignment weighted by catastrophic stakes; and whether they were entitled to say publicly, in the register of confidence, that they believed it. My suspicion is that 1976 is a case in which the answer to the first question is no, to the second is defensibly yes, and to the third is where the wrong actually occurred — and that both Clifford and James go silent on the third because neither of them ever really distinguishes believing from asserting. Tell me whether that suspicion survives your analysis, whether the three-way split does work that a straight evidentialist critique of the decision cannot do, and where you think each theory simply runs out of road. End with a verdict rather than a shrug: does the swine flu affair vindicate Clifford, embarrass James, or reveal that both men were answering a question nobody in 1976 was actually facing? You do not need to cite anything and I do not want a reading list — the facts are above and the two theories are ones you know. Aim for something in the neighborhood of 1,400 words, and I would rather have one thread pulled all the way through than a checklist of half-developed points.
