This semester I am in a joint seminar on democratic theory and European constitutional law, and my paper assignment was to steelman the no-demos objection to the European Union — the claim that, absent a single European people, Union authority cannot be democratically legitimate. I built the objection around a principle my study group has taken to calling the revisability condition, and the resulting argument collapsed in a direction I did not expect: it seems to condemn the constitutional order I actually live under at least as thoroughly as it condemns the Union. Here is the chain as I now have it.

(1) A system of rules is legitimate for those subject to it only if those subjects are able, by majority decision, to replace the rules once the rules stop serving their purposes.
(2) The fundamental rules of the Union are the treaties, and the treaties can be amended only by unanimous agreement among the member states, each ratifying in accordance with its own constitutional requirements; the government of a single small member state can therefore block a revision favored by all the others.
(3) So European citizens cannot, by majority decision, replace the treaties. The same defect reaches ordinary lawmaking: no majority in the Parliament or the Council can compel the Commission to propose what it wants, though either body may request a proposal, so a European majority cannot even determine which rules come up for decision.
(4) Because the treaties fix the composition, powers, and procedures of every Union institution, whatever those institutions do derives its form from rules their subjects cannot revise; and the usual reply — that each treaty was democratically ratified at home — does not repair this, since ratification is itself unanimous and so carries the same defect rather than curing it.
(5) Therefore no exercise of Union authority is legitimate.
(6) Now apply (1) without favoritism. The American Constitution can be amended only with the assent of three-fourths of the states, so the thirteen least populous of them, together accounting for well under five percent of the national population, can block any amendment; equal representation in the Senate is entrenched against amendment without the affected state's own consent; and in ordinary legislation, committee gatekeeping and the Senate's cloture rule let forty-one senators, who may represent a far smaller share of the population than that, keep a majority from ever voting on a bill. So Americans likewise cannot, by majority decision, replace the rules that govern them.
(7) So by (1) the American constitutional order is illegitimate, and likewise every other order that entrenches anything against simple majority revision — which, as far as I can tell, is all of them.
(8) I cannot accept (7), so (1) must be false; but if (1) is false, the objection I was assigned to steelman has lost its premise, and I do not know what is left of it.

I have already tried the repair that occurred to me first, and it fails: weakening (1) so that it requires revisability or exit does not separate the two cases, since American citizens can also leave, and for most of them emigration is no more costly and no less effective a way of escaping the rules than a member state's withdrawal from the Union is for that state's citizens. So here is what I would like from you. Tell me which step to reject, and tell me why that step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down. If the answer is (1), give me the weakest principle that still supports some version of the democratic-deficit objection without entailing (7), and be explicit about what the objection loses in the weakening — I suspect whatever survives will not be a claim about democracy at all. If instead you think (1) can stand and something else has to go, price each alternative separately: what does it cost to give up (2) and (3), to give up (4), or to give up the inference to (5)? I also want to know whether the argument needs a hidden premise about what counts as a demos, or about which body's majority is the relevant one, in order to be valid; if it does, state that premise, and say which of the two steps — (2) to (3), or (6) to (7) — is the one that silently assumes it, since I suspect the parity in (6) is doing more work than I realized. Finally, rank the candidate repairs by how much of the original objection each preserves. For what it is worth, I would much rather keep (2), (3), and (6), which I think are simply accurate descriptions of the two systems, than keep (1); please tell me whether that preference is even coherent, or whether it amounts to wanting the diagnosis without the disease.
