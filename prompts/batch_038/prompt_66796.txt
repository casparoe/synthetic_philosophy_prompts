I am a second-year postgraduate on a philosophy of science programme, and this term our reading group has been working through the question of whether history can be quantified and forecast at all — cliodynamics, structural-demographic theory, secular cycles, and the various objections from historians who regard the whole enterprise as a category mistake. Last week I set out a chain of reasoning intended to show that the standard objection to Turchin's forecasting claims backfires, and two of the six of us now think the argument is sound but unwelcome, while three think it must be invalid somewhere and cannot say where. I would like a careful diagnosis rather than a verdict on cliodynamics itself.

Here is the argument as I presented it. (1) A quantitative theory of some domain is genuinely predictive only if a competent practitioner, at the moment the prediction is issued, could have stated in advance which observations would count as its failure. (2) In 2010 Turchin published a short piece in Nature forecasting that the coming decade would be a period of growing instability in the United States and western Europe; the forecast rested on structural-demographic theory and on a composite index of political stress which the same research programme had already used to fit the historical record of secular cycles. (3) The forecast fixed no window narrower than a decade, no threshold value for the index, and no list of disconfirming outcomes, and it was presented by analogy with a forest in which the theory tells you how much dry fuel has accumulated but not which event will supply the spark. (4) When the United States underwent a sharp escalation of mass mobilisation and political violence in 2020 and 2021, proponents and critics alike described the forecast as vindicated, and nobody on either side had specified in advance what would have shown it wrong. (5) So the forecast was unfalsifiable, and by (1) structural-demographic theory is not genuinely predictive but a curve-fitting exercise wearing a forecasting gloss. (6) Yet the same test condemns the ranges of warming the IPCC reports for a given emissions pathway, seismologists' probabilistic hazard maps, and central banks' inflation projections: decade-scale or similarly coarse windows, indices and ensembles built by the very modellers who deploy them, and no advance statement of failure conditions. (7) Those are paradigms of legitimate quantitative prediction. (8) Therefore (1) is false. (9) And if (1) is false, the objection in (5) has lost its only premise, so the case against cliodynamics cannot be run in this way and nothing said so far impugns its forecasting claims.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a defence of either side. Tell me which step or steps you would reject and why each of them looked acceptable in the first place — in particular, whether the move from (3) and (4) to (5) is doing something more than it appears to be, whether (6) really describes a single test that all these enterprises fail, and whether (9) is licensed even if (8) is. Then price the alternatives: for each rejection available to me, say what I lose and what I would have to rebuild in its place, including whether a weaker successor to (1) — something about calibration or sharpness rather than about practitioners announcing disconfirming conditions beforehand — could carry the objection in (5) without sweeping in (7). If pushed, I would rather keep (7) than (1), since abandoning (7) looks like conceding that nothing about the behaviour of complex social systems can be forecast at all, and I am not prepared to concede that; but I do not know what keeping (7) commits me to saying about (6), and I suspect that is where the argument is actually weak.
