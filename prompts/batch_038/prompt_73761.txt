So in my rational choice module we ran a class version of Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma — the superrationality puzzle from the same stuff as his Luring Lottery. Ours was simpler than his. Ten of us, each privately writes YES or NO on a card and hands it in. If exactly one card says YES, that person gets a $10,000 prize (real money, the department funded it). If zero say YES, or two or more say YES, nobody gets anything. Everyone knows the rules, and everyone knows everyone else has read the same chapter.

A friend in the module — I'll call her N — wrote up an argument that superrationality tells you exactly what to do here, and that it also shows superrationality isn't the magical-thinking thing everyone accuses it of being. I couldnt find a hole in it during seminar, but the conclusion has to be wrong somewhere, so I'd like a proper diagnosis rather than a vibe. Her steps:

(1) Superrationality means all ten of us, facing the same problem, arrive at the same strategy.
(2) If we all arrive at YES, ten YESes go in and nobody wins. If we all arrive at NO, nobody wins.
(3) So the superrational answer can't be a pure strategy — both pure strategies give everybody zero.
(4) Therefore it's mixed: we each play YES with some probability p.
(5) If all ten play YES with probability p, the probability that exactly one YES goes in is 10p(1−p)^9, which is maximised at p = 1/10. That's roughly a 39% chance somebody in the group wins, which is the best on offer.
(6) So the superrational move for each of us is to play YES with probability 1/10.
(7) And there's no spooky action at a distance in any of that: our ten coinflips are independent, and (1) only says we'll all settle on the same p, not that the flips land the same way.
(8) Nothing in (5) uses anything beyond probabilities and my own payoffs — i.e. exactly what an ordinary expected-utility maximiser uses.
(9) So in this game superrationality just derives something ordinary rationality was entitled to all along: randomise at 1/10.
(10) Now change one thing. Suppose nine of us are ordinary self-interested players and I somehow know each of them is playing YES with probability 1/10. Then playing YES for certain gets me 10,000 × (9/10)^9 ≈ $3,874, while playing YES with probability 1/10 gets me ≈ $387. But by (9) I should still randomise at 1/10 — apparently I should throw away about $3,500 of expected value for no reason at all.
(11) So either superrationality isn't a species of rationality after all, or something above is wrong.

N says (10) just proves you can't have both superrationality and self-interest in this game and she's happy to bite that bullet. I'm not, because the entire selling point of superrationality is that it's still careful expected-utility reasoning, only done with the symmetry taken into account.

Can you tell me which step to reject, why that step managed to look innocent, and what rejecting it costs? If I have to give something up I'd rather it be one of the early steps than the arithmetic in (5) and (10), but I want the price of each option spelled out — including whether a weaker version of (1) rescues the argument, and what N should actually have done in the (10) scenario.
