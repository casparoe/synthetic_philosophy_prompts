I have no training in history. I spent thirty-one years appraising commercial property for a regional bank, and I have been reading about the Holocaust seriously, on my own, for about six years. What I want to ask about is not the history but a pattern I keep running into while reading about it, and I have not been able to find a word for it.

The case that started this for me is the argument between the two books about the same Hamburg police battalion — Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Goldhagen's rebuttal of it. One side holds that the men doing the shooting in occupied Poland in 1942 were unremarkable middle-aged reservists who killed because of orders, peer pressure, and the sheer difficulty of standing apart from your own unit; the other holds that they killed because a murderous way of thinking about Jews had been built into German culture long before Hitler, and that the killing was, on the whole, voluntary. What struck me is not that they disagree — historians disagree about everything — but that they appear to work largely from the same documentary base and to draw the opposite lesson from each item in it, in a way that leaves neither book with anything it has to explain away.

Three instances, all of which I take from what the two authors themselves report:

- The men's own statements, taken decades afterward in the course of West German prosecutions, are the backbone of both accounts. When one of them says he was appalled and asked to be excused, the first reading takes that as an honest report of how the situation felt to him; the second takes it as the predictable self-defense of a man who, in the 1960s, had every reason to insist that he had been reluctant. The identical sentence does work for both books.
- Before one massacre the commander told his men they could step out if they did not feel up to it, and barely a dozen of the five hundred did; in another case Browning records, a man demanded release, received it, and was later promoted. For one author this is decisive in one direction — the way out was real and almost no one took it, so something other than the freedom to refuse was holding them in place. For the other it is decisive in the opposite direction — refusal was available and went unpunished, and in one instance was rewarded, so those who stayed were willing. I cannot see what further document would settle which of these readings is correct, because any newly discovered case of a man who refused successfully would strengthen both at once.
- Where the record is silent, the silence is common property too. One side says we should not expect written evidence of a pervasive cultural attitude, since nobody writes down what everyone already assumes; the other says we should not expect written evidence of coercion, since the men had every interest in keeping that off paper.

My own diagnosis, for what it is worth: the quarrel is not about what happened but about the standing of the testimony concerning what happened, and the standard for deciding whether that testimony can be trusted is being set by the conclusion each author is arguing for. The stronger a thesis is about how thoroughly a society concealed or reinterpreted its own motives, the less weight the surviving self-reports can carry against it, so that a thesis about concealment is vindicated by precisely what it predicts — statements that fail to vindicate it. The troubling part is that I cannot cleanly separate this from good historical practice. Discounting a witness because you have reason to think he was lying is what any careful historian does all day.

So:

1. Is there an established name for this configuration — for the situation in which a hypothesis fixes, in advance, the credibility of the evidence that could bear against it, so that both supporting and undermining reports come out the way the hypothesis expects?
2. If there is, tell me which of its near neighbors it actually is, and how it is to be distinguished from each: from the ordinary bias of a partisan reader; from the general point that what you notice in a document depends on what you expect to find there; from a historian discounting a source for reasons of her own; and from a thesis patched up after the fact to dodge a counterexample, which seems to me a different thing, since here the immunity is present from the start and does its work before any counterexample has arrived.
3. If there is no established name, coin one, define it, and give me its edges. I want the boundary with legitimate source criticism drawn as sharply as you can manage, and a test I could actually apply to a new dispute and get a defensible answer from — not merely a rhetorical accusation.
4. And here is the part I have not been able to get past, which I suspect is why I have not found the term: an immunity of this kind can be true. If a culture really did make it routine for people to misdescribe their own motives, then testimony that misdescribes those motives is genuinely poor evidence, and the thesis is being confirmed by its own predictions in a way that is not a trick at all. Tell me whether a dispute can have this structure and still be a legitimate one, and if it can, how one would distinguish a thesis that has earned its immunity from one that has merely claimed it. Then apply whatever test you settle on to the Browning–Goldhagen argument itself.

I am not looking for a survey of the possibilities. Take a position: say whether the thing has a name and what it is, say whether these two books are genuinely an instance of it or only partly so, and say where you come down on the fourth question, with your reasons. And if you think my framing is wrong somewhere — if one of my three examples is not really an example, or if the two accounts are not in fact equally immune — say so plainly and argue it rather than accommodating me.

Please write in American English.
