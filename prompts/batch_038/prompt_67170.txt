**Interpretable without warrant** (draft)

§1. Dennett's picture: three ways of predicting a system, the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, and you're licensed to take up the intentional one wherever it buys predictive leverage you wouldn't get otherwise. The stance is vindicated by its track record rather than by any look inside the mechanism. So the obvious pro-LLM argument goes: predicting what one of these systems will output is way easier under belief and desire attribution than under the design stance, which we don't fully have anyway. Predictive success licenses interpretation. End of story.

But there's a hole. The reason intentional attribution works so well on an LLM is that the model was trained on an enormous corpus of human text, and that text is already interpretable in intentional terms. So the warrant for interpreting the model is downstream of the warrant for interpreting its training data. Call it borrowed warrant: the model inherits interpretability from the agents whose output it reproduces, the way a good impressionist inherits the interpretability of whoever he's imitating. Prediction succeeds because the target of prediction is a competent simulator of belief-expressers, not because there are beliefs there to be simulated.

§2. Why this isn't just the old instrumentalism worry. The usual complaint about Dennett is that cheapness is the problem — anything predictable as a rational agent thereby has beliefs, so the stance proves too much. My claim is different: the issue isn't cheapness, it's provenance. Two systems can be equally interpretable while only one of them is interpretable non-derivatively, and that asymmetry is invisible from inside the intentional stance, which only ever asks whether predictions work.

Radical interpretation makes the point sharper. Too many candidate interpretations fit the behavioural evidence, so you have to add a constraint. Davidson's constraint is on the interpreted system: charity, the interpreter is entitled to assume the speaker is largely rational and largely right about the world around her. Lewis's constraint is instead on the speaker's place in a practice — roughly, that a population's language is the one its members are conventionally disposed to speak truthfully in, so interpreting someone means locating them in an ongoing practice of asserting what they take to be true. Note that the Lewis-style constraint is exactly the one an LLM can't satisfy while still looking perfectly interpretable. The model isn't a party to a practice of truthfulness in the relevant sense: it has no standing commitment to the truth of what it produces, nothing it loses by asserting falsely, no downstream interactions in which reliability gets cashed out. So you can have the observable pattern the principle predicts without the practice that generates it. Pattern without practice is, I want to say, the diagnostic of derivatively interpreted systems generally — a really good actor, a very convincing parrot, a model trained on both.

§3. A possible fix. Attribute beliefs only where intentional attribution earns predictions in cases where a belief state and a mimicry state would come apart: novel set-ups where nothing in the training distribution supplies the answer and the system still responds in the way its attributed beliefs would predict. Call it the novelty test. Obvious problem: we can't bound the training distribution, so we can never establish novelty, only conjecture it. If that's right, the test isn't a criterion that fails on LLMs, it's a criterion nobody can apply to anything, which is a worse place to end up.

§4. The bit I'm unsure about. Empirically these systems show something like local coherence with global incoherence — they hold together within a context and come apart across contexts, they contradict themselves over a long conversation, their professed commitments shift with framing. That's less like an agent with a single doxastic state and more like a fragmented one, in the sense that talk of fragmentation in the literature uses: several more-or-less internally consistent compartments that don't get integrated, with access governed by cues rather than by inference. If that's the right description, then the interesting question isn't whether the model has beliefs but how many compartments there are and what determines which one is live at a moment — and belief attribution is fine as long as it's indexed to compartments. My worry is that this is just a fancy restatement of "the model is inconsistent," and that indexing beliefs to compartments is compatible with borrowed warrant rather than an escape from it, since each compartment would still be inherited from some slice of the training data.
