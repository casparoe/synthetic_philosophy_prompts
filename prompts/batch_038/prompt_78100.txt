Every week or so I get pitched a story that turns entirely on somebody's first-person report, and I never know how much weight to put on it. An AI lab emails saying their model "describes its own experience" in fluent, quotable sentences. A sleep researcher's key result is what people said when they were woken up. A neurology team has a patient who hasn't tracked with his eyes or followed a command in four years, but who got five out of six personal questions right inside a scanner by imagining playing tennis for yes and walking through his childhood home for no. A retreat teacher describes a state with no contents whatsoever — no objects, no self, not even a sense of time — reported in detail years later by people who treat the retelling as part of the practice itself.

I'm the person who covers science and ideas at a mid-sized general-interest magazine. Pieces run about 1,200 words, my editor signs off and legal reads anything that names a company. I've got maybe 40 minutes between a pitch landing and deciding whether to take it, I can't get lab access or run anything myself, and the person who fact-checks my copy has a biology degree and no philosophy at all — so anything I hand her has to work without jargon, or with a one-line gloss attached to each term. We also have a house rule that we never run "sentient" in a headline unless somebody in the piece will define it on the record, and a standing habit of leading with a subject's own words, which our readers love and which I increasingly suspect is exactly the wrong instinct here.

A philosopher who read one of my pieces told me that what I should be doing is heterophenomenology. I liked the sound of that and I've never read the source, so part of what I want from you is to tell me what it would actually commit me to, in plain terms, and what it would cost me journalistically.

So: write me a triage procedure I can run on a consciousness pitch. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, the point where the procedure tells me to drop or substantially reframe the story, and the points where it hands the call back to a human being because the question is about values rather than facts. It needs to handle the case where the researcher never answers my follow-up email and all I have is a press release, and it needs to say what changes if the report comes from a single articulate person versus a hundred people in a pre-registered protocol. I don't want to re-derive this everytime, so it should be reusable as written.

Then run it on three cases: the AI startup whose entire pitch is the model's self-descriptions and who won't show me the eval methodology; the scanner patient with the tennis-and-house answers; and the retreat teacher's contentless awareness, as reported after the fact by devoted students. That third one is the case I'd hate to have handled in a deflating way — it's the kind of story my readers come to us for — so if the procedure spits out something that makes me look like a buzzkill, show me that result anyway, and then tell me what I can still legitimately say in the piece.

Finally, a short paragraph I could paste into our style notes: what does this procedure explicitly not license me to conclude, and separately, which of the two complaints in our corrections inbox does it actually answer? We get letters from people saying we're credulous about inner experience and from people saying we're scientistic dismissers of it, and I need to know whether adopting this procedure quiets one side, both, or neither.
