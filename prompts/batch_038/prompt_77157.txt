Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about metaethics, and neither of us budged an inch. He is an expressivist who holds that the embedding problem was solved in substance by the compositional-semantics programmes of the last two decades; I think those programmes buy logical discipline at the price of everything that made expressivism interesting. The point at which we began simply repeating ourselves is this. To answer Frege and Geach, the expressivist must show that a normative sentence contributes content to an unasserted position — the antecedent of a conditional, the scope of a negation, a disjunct — in precisely the way an ordinary descriptive sentence does, which pushes her towards conceding that such sentences are truth-apt, belief-expressing, and apt to be made true by facts in whatever thin sense the descriptivist means. To answer the charge of creeping minimalism, however, she must then identify something that still separates her position from realism once those minimalist notions have been granted across the board. The harder she pushes on the first requirement, the less she has left with which to satisfy the second, and the debate stalls in exactly that pincer.

I want you to write a joke about that bind, which I intend to deploy the next time the conversation reaches the same point. Please observe the following.

The humour must be generated by the tension itself: the punchline should be recognisably about the two demands pulling in opposite directions, so that a reader who knows the literature laughs for a different reason from one who does not. A joke that merely garnishes an otherwise generic premise with the words expressivism, non-cognitivism or a stylised Boo! will not serve, and neither will a gag whose only content is that philosophers disagree with one another.

Form: a single short scene or dialogue, no more than about two hundred words in total. The surface should be followable by someone who knows only that expressivists treat moral judgements as attitudes rather than beliefs; the force of the ending should increase with expertise.

Avoid the standard furniture entirely: no one may walk into a bar or a pub, no pearly gates or afterlife tribunal, no seminar room, no pun on a philosopher's name carrying the punchline, and no quotation from or paraphrase of any actual text — invent everything.

Write throughout in British English, in spelling, idiom and register.

Give me the joke and nothing else, with no framing, no alternatives, and no commentary — except for one closing sentence identifying which distinction does the comic work, so that I can tell whether you have grasped the bind or merely matched its shape.
