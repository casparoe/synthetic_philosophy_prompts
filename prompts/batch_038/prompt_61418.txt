I keep a spreadsheet of probability estimates, mostly about my own life plus a few friends, and I've realized basically every number I write down is secretly a choice of which group to compare my case to. My gut is pretty consistent about it, but the two rules people give me -- go as narrow as you can until the numbers get thin, and use a class that nothing statistically relevant splits further -- both break on my own cases.

Here's what I actually judge:

Whether I finish the marathon I signed up for: first-timers at this specific race over the last five years, not adults in my town. Narrower wins.

Same race, but the finish rate for first-timers whose bib ended in 7 and whose first name starts with M -- 31 of 34 finished. I refuse that one flat out, even though 34 is a real sample. It feels made up.

My mom's knee replacement: I want this surgeon at this hospital over the last three years, not the national registry number.

But that surgeon has done nine. Nine is too few and I go straight back to the national figure. So its not just narrowness.

My kid carries a variant found in maybe 1 in 40,000 people. The registry lists seven carriers and in five of them things went badly. I want to use those seven, which flatly contradicts the surgeon case and I cant tell you why.

Drowning at the lake on the hottest weekend of the year: I will not use days-with-high-ice-cream-sales no matter how much data sits behind it.

Whether a guy released from prison around here reoffends: I'll happily use people released into this zip code, and zip code doesn't cause anything. So it isn't causation either.

My lottery ticket: no pull to narrow or widen at all. The set of tickets in this drawing just is the class and I never feel the problem.

After my business folded, a friend said well, look at the rate for two-person startups where the founders met in college. I think that's cheating -- you don't get to build the class after you know how it came out.

When I can't tell which narrow class my case is even in (is the tumor stage IIA or IIB, is my job manager or senior manager), I back up to the wider class that definitely contains both.

Marriages where the wife is four years older and both of them have graduate degrees: the census has millions of those and the divorce rate is measurably different, and I still refuse to use it for my own marriage.

So -- is there a principle, or two or three competing ones, that I could apply mechanically to a new case without checking my gut first? Give me each clause and say which of the cases above forces it. Then tell me which of my verdicts a cleaner principle throws out; I suspect the seven-carriers case and the ice-cream/zip-code pair are where something has to give, and I want to know which side you'd sacrifice and what it costs. And run whatever you land on against a case I didn't list: whether I'll still have my job in three years. The obvious class is people who took jobs like mine, except I can also deliberately make myself atypical -- take a weird contract, negotiate a fixed two-year term -- so I don't know if I'm allowed to pick my own reference class, or if doing that makes the number meaningless. Last thing: when two classes genuinely tie, is averaging them ever right, or is that just cowardice with extra steps?
