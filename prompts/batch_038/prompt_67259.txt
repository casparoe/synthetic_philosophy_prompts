I am a senior watch officer at a vessel traffic service centre on a congested North Sea approach, and I want to commission an essay on a question that has ceased to be merely academic for me.

Fourteen months ago, two vessels collided in a fog bank inside my sector. I had issued a course instruction to one of them at 02:14; they struck each other at 02:47. The accident investigation concluded that the collision was avoidable and that earlier intervention would have prevented it. I do not contest that finding, and I am not looking for exculpation. What I cannot stop turning over is a different question, which the inquiry never had to ask: was it already true at 01:00, before any of us had said or done anything relevant, that there would be a collision in that fog bank?

Please write an essay that takes a definite position on that question and defends it. Specifically: are singular claims about contingent future events — "there will be a collision in the approaches tomorrow", the modern descendant of Aristotle's sea battle — true or false now, or neither? And, if you take the second answer, what exactly has been gained?

I would like the essay to do the following, in roughly this order of priority.

First, disentangle the semantic question from the metaphysical one. I suspect much of the literature trades on moving between "no such claim has a truth value now" and "the future is really open", and I want to know whether those come apart, and if so, which one my question was really asking.

Second, take the fatalist argument seriously rather than dismissing it: if it was true yesterday that there would be a collision, and truths about the past are now beyond anyone's power to change, then it seems nothing I did at 02:14 could have made a difference. Address whether the distinction between hard facts about the past and soft or merely "Cambridge" facts about the past — facts about yesterday that are really about tomorrow — actually blocks this argument, or whether it simply relocates the problem. Also say what should be made of the retrospective shift: now that the collision has happened, it seems to have become true yesterday that it would happen. Is that a change in the past, or evidence that the openness I feel was never in the world to begin with?

Third, assess the two standard semantic responses. On the one hand, denying bivalence for future contingents, in the spirit of the sea-battle chapter of *On Interpretation* and its later revival in three-valued tense logic. On the other, supervaluationism, which keeps every instance of excluded middle true — "either there will be a collision or there will not" — while withholding a truth value from each disjunct. Does that combination preserve the openness it promises, or is it a verbal consolation? And what of the Ockhamist alternative, which keeps bivalence intact by singling out one history among the branching futures as the actual one, the so-called thin red line? If the actual history is already marked out, is the Ockhamist any better off than the fatalist, or does the marking do genuine work because it is itself indexed to the present moment?

Fourth — and this is where I most want your judgement, because it is my own proposal and I do not trust it — consider what I have come to call the helm condition. On this view, openness is not a semantic property at all. A claim about tomorrow is genuinely open now if and only if some agent now possesses a power whose exercise would make it false; where no such power exists, the claim is simply settled, and there is no third truth value to be found. The attraction, for me, is that it explains why the question gripped me at all: at 01:00 I had the helm, and by 02:47 I did not. My worry is that the helm condition is either empty — collapsing into whatever the physics of the situation already determines — or unstable, since powers are themselves a matter of which futures are accessible, which looks like the branching model I was trying to avoid.

Weigh all of this and reach a verdict. I am perfectly content with a conditional or partitioned conclusion, provided it is a conclusion: for instance, that the helm condition governs only claims about our own agency while bivalence holds across the board, or that openness is real but not expressible in the language of truth values. What I am not prepared to accept is a survey that ends by noting the debate continues.

Two further requests. Argue rather than catalogue: I want reasoning I can follow on a night watch, not a map of who said what. And please do not append a bibliography or a list of further reading — I cannot chase it down, and it would only pad the piece. Roughly two thousand five hundred words.
