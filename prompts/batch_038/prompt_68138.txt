I am a correspondent for a weekly magazine covering science and ideas, and I am currently drafting a long feature on refusal behaviour in consumer AI assistants — the decisions, mostly invisible to users, by which a system declines a request that is perfectly legal and often quite plainly in the requester's own interest. My reporting examples include an assistant that would not help a user summarise the case law relevant to a dispute with their own employer; one that shut down a request to draft a message telling an adult sibling that their drinking is a problem; a novelist whose manuscript scenes were repeatedly refused; and a medical assistant that would discuss a symptom abstractly but not the user's own test results. My editor's note in the margin says the piece currently leans too hard on the word paternalism and asks whether I have let the companies state their side fairly. I have, and the more I read the transcripts of those interviews the more I suspect that one particular image they gave me is doing far more rhetorical work than I realised.

Over the course of a month, three separate people who work on trust and safety independently reached for the same comparison: the bartender who cuts off a regular. The first told me that a refusal is a professional judgement made at the point of service by someone carrying a duty of care, about a harm that is imminent and legible, and that we do not on that account call the bartender a tyrant. The second added that the patron is furious in the moment and grateful the following morning, which he took to be the whole moral shape of the transaction. The third, more adversarially, inverted the image and argued that an assistant has neither the licensing obligation nor the eyes of a publican, and therefore no standing to cut anyone off at all. In my draft the analogy is currently a hinge: it is what would let me turn from saying these refusals are heavy-handed to conceding that they may be the same species of thing as an ordinary professional boundary. Before I publish that turn I want the hinge tested rather than admired.

So please audit it. Set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — identifying what plays the role of the patron, the licensee, the intoxication, the licensing law, the last-orders bell, the tab, and the other pubs along the road. Then give me the disanalogies, ranked by whether they actually bear on the question of paternalism or are merely surface, and address four in particular: whether the analogy quietly converts self-regarding interference into other-regarding interference, given that the bartender's strongest justification concerns the driver and the pedestrians rather than the drinker himself; whether exit matters, since a patron can walk down the road whereas a refusal arrives inside a workplace tool for which the user may have no alternative; whether the epistemic asymmetry is decisive, a publican reading a body and a slurred sentence versus a system reading one typed line with no context; and whether the gratitude-the-next-morning claim is something that could in principle be tested or is instead unfalsifiable comfort. Having done that, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy was recruited to support — that these refusals are boundary-setting rather than paternalism — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, propose a better comparison, saying precisely what that one licenses and what it rules out. I would rather print a comparison I have stress-tested than a memorable one. Please write in British English, and do not send me a reading list or citations: I want your reasoning about the analogy itself, and I will verify any specifics myself before the piece goes to the weekend section.
