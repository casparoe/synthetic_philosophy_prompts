I'm about to spend a term on theories of meaning and interpretation, and I want the specification before the candidates. Take the case that motivates the whole debate: a field linguist with no bilingual informants, working only from the natives' observable behavior and the circumstances that prompt their utterances, who nonetheless produces a workable translation manual — and could in principle produce a rival one that fits every scrap of the same evidence. Write the desiderata an account of what makes such a manual correct has to meet, where the purpose is to explain ordinary linguistic understanding generally and not merely to describe the field linguist's predicament.

I want the facts any such account must accommodate (the linguist's manifest success at getting by, the apparent availability of empirically equivalent but incompatible manuals, the way speakers can agree on what an utterance means while disagreeing about what its parts refer to); the distinctions it must not collapse (indeterminacy versus ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence, the level of individual terms versus the level of whole sentences, inscrutability of reference versus skepticism about the external world); and the background constraints it must respect (that whatever there is to get right be fixed by the physical facts and by evidence available in principle, that the account be compatible with how anyone actually learns a first language, and that it not smuggle in meanings as objects waiting to be inspected). For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it, then mark it non-negotiable or a demand characteristic of one particular camp, and say why. End by identifying the subset you suspect cannot be satisfied jointly, if there is one, and by naming the single desideratum that excludes the most candidate accounts. Leave out the scorecard; I'll run that myself.
