I've been keeping a spreadsheet for about three years now — call it a calibration hobby — of the verdicts handed down by the senior guy in our department's modeling seminar. Call him R. Every week somebody presents a paper and he either waves it through or doesn't, and I log the verdict plus the one-sentence reason he gives.

His record so far:

1. Paper assumes every farmer is a price-taker, uses that to predict how much acreage shifts when a subsidy changes. Waved through.
2. Paper with no money and one representative agent, used to explain how a credit crunch propagated. Not waved through.
3. Paper that assumes bidders are perfect expected-utility maximisers, says up front it's an as-if device, predicts auction revenue, great fit in the lab. Waved through.
4. Paper with rational expectations that fit the 60s and 70s beautifully, then used to forecast inflation after the policy regime changed. Not waved through.
5. Paper where the agents literally solve fixed points in their heads, flagged by the authors as a toy, used only to generate a qualitative pattern which they then go and test. Waved through.
6. Same fixed-point psychology, same fit, but the discussion section argues the brain must be doing something roughly like this and cites some neuro. Not waved through.
7. Paper that assumes away all heterogeneity between people to predict the average effect of a job training program, with a robustness check showing that relaxing it doesn't move the average, validated out of sample. Waved through.

For a long time I had him down as a plain Friedman F-twister — assumptions don't need to be realistic, only the predictions do — but 4 and 6 obviously aren't that, and neither is 2, and 7 should have been a not-wave under at least one rule I can name. So the data underdetermines him and I've never been able to pin down what he's actually running.

This week's paper is the fun one. It's an epidemic model that assumes everyone mixes uniformly. The authors ran their own robustness check and relaxing that moves the predicted peak by about three weeks, so the idealization is clearly load-bearing for the timing claim. But they also ran it prospectively — parameters locked in before the wave — on one city, and the peak-timing call came in within a couple of days of what actually happened, closer than the relaxed version would have landed. They don't claim mixing is really uniform, they call it a convenient fiction. And they want to use the same model to time a vaccination push in that same city next season.

Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up at the end:

1. which rules are still alive after cases 1–7 — the actual candidates, not "it's complicated";
2. what each of those rules says about this week's paper;
3. your best guess at R's verdict, with a number on it, and why you weight that rule over the others;
4. whether the next-season part changes anything, i.e. whether the domain of use shifts enough to flip a rule that was otherwise happy;
5. one further paper I could hand him that would seperate the surviving rules better than this one does.
