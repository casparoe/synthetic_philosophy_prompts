I sit on the benefits committee at a mid-sized engineering firm, and this year I helped rewrite how our retirement plan takes people on. The machinery is now as follows. Every new hire is enrolled on their first day of payroll at three percent of salary, without being asked anything. Each January the rate rises by one percentage point until it reaches ten, and the increases are deliberately scheduled to land in the same pay period as the annual merit raise, so that take-home pay never actually falls in any year. The contributions are invested in a single fund that the provider assigns from the participant's birth year, and that fund's equity share slides downward automatically as the cohort ages, on the theory that a person born in a given year wants a given amount of risk at a given age. Nobody has to do anything at any point in this process; leaving it requires logging into a portal, selecting an election, and submitting a form. The provider's literature explains the design by saying the defaults are set to what participants would have chosen for themselves had they the time, attention, and information to choose, and it backs this up in two ways: a survey showing that roughly nine in ten people who were auto-enrolled report, three years later, that they are glad they were, and a note that if participation were left to active election, a fifth of the workforce would never get around to it.

What puzzles me is that the same apparatus seems to run on a second, opposite logic in places. The provider's annual report to us lists "engagement" — the share of participants who logged in and made an active election of their own — as a headline success metric, and congratulates us when it rises, even though a good deal of that movement consists of people leaving the assigned fund for something more concentrated and volatile. Contributions already in place cannot be re-defaulted without sixty days' notice and an opportunity to object, on the stated ground that participants who have been contributing for years have settled into their allocation and that allocation now expresses something about them. Hardship withdrawals and loans against the balance can be taken in a few clicks with no cooling-off period and no counseling, on the stated ground that this respects the participant's own judgment about their current needs. When a colleague proposed auto-enrolling staff into a payroll-giving scheme for local charities at one percent, with the same opt-out portal, the committee refused, on the ground that giving is a matter of personal conviction and the firm has no business presuming it — while continuing to insist that in the savings case the firm is presuming nothing at all, only supplying what people already want. And through all of it runs a line I keep hearing: the firm would never mandate a contribution rate, because the freedom to choose is what makes the plan legitimate rather than paternalistic.

I'd like a careful reconstruction of the account of preference that this whole arrangement presupposes — not a defense or a critique of the policy, but its implicit theory. Treat yourself as a philosopher who works on preference formation, welfare, and paternalism and who has spent enough time around institutional design to know how these schemes are actually administered. Before you commit, sketch the candidate accounts the design could be resting on — preference as whatever choice reveals; preference as what a person would want if fully informed and attentive; preference as what they would want if their temporal parts were weighted equally rather than the nearest one dominating; preference as something that has to be elicited rather than located — and say which the practice could survive without. Then tell me which rival views the design quietly rules out, and whether it assumes that each participant has a single determinate lifetime profile of saving-versus-spending that the provider can find better than the participant can, and on what evidence it could think so. Please be explicit about which of the inconsistencies I listed are genuine, which are only apparent, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would equally license it. Finally, say what would have to change in the scheme — concretely, in its rules and its reporting — if we replaced the presupposed view with a strict revealed-preference one, and close by telling me what would change your mind: which feature of the practice, if I had described it differently, would push you toward a different reconstruction, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
