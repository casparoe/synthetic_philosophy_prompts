I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on free will, eighteen students, all of whom have met Frankfurt-style cases only on the page. Design an in-class game in which some participants make a small-stakes choice — one option is presented to the chooser as the one they should not take, and pays slightly better — while a partner sits beside them holding a written trigger condition and the physical means to override the recorded outcome; other participants make the same choice with no partner present. A third group then judges the choosers without knowing which condition each was in. Give me the exact instruction wording for chooser and partner, trigger-and-override mechanics such that overriding is something the partner could actually perform rather than a stipulation on paper, a blind condition-assignment procedure, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Keep the stakes real but trivial, and no participant actually harmed or resented.

The debrief should treat the judges' verdicts as the data and map the play onto the principle of alternative possibilities, onto the claim that responsibility may turn only on what happens in the actual sequence, onto the reply that some residual alternative always survives such a setup, and onto the question whether the trigger was causally sufficient for the choice or merely a sign of it. Students should hit the tension in play before any of this is named.

Two further demands. Decide, and justify, whether the chooser should be told about the partner before choosing, and whether the answer differs between a causally sufficient trigger and a merely indicative one. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including what to do when a chooser deliberately takes the disallowed option in order to test whether the partner will act — plus a frank account of which feature of the thought experiment a live game with a fallible human partner cannot reproduce, and what I have to supply in discussion instead.
