Im teaching a political ethics seminar next term and I want to build a unit on the problem of dirty hands around Spielberg's Lincoln (2012, the Kushner script), but I've started to worry the film is actually a bad vehicle for Walzer's argument and I can't quite say why. Help me work it out.

The reading I want goes like this. Walzer's 1973 paper isn't just the familiar claim that leaders sometimes have to do lesser evils — everyone says that. The distinctive bit is that the one who does them should still be guilty: the guilt is what keeps the act wrong rather than merely costly, and a politician who feels fine about it is a monster, not a hero. So my working diagnostic, which you're welcome to attack, is what I'll call the residue test — does the story leave the agent morally worse off after the plan succeeds, in a way the success doesnt wash out?

Run the film against that test on each of its dirty pairs of hands, and tell me whether the film even notices that they're different cases. There's Lincoln's carefully worded answer to the House about whether there are peace envoys waiting outside Washington. There's Stevens moderating himself on the floor, getting pressed about the woman he lives with, and whatever he does once he's home. There's the lobbyists Seward puts on the payroll to buy lame-duck votes with the promise of federal jobs. My hunch, which may be flat wrong, is that the film gives Stevens a genuine residue scene and gives Lincoln almost none — so we end up with a Walzerian congressman and a straightforward consequentialist president, which is a weird inversion of who the audience is supposed to admire. Also: there's the scene where Lincoln works through whether the Emancipation Proclamation was itself a stretch of his war powers. Does that count as residue, or is it just him explaining his calculus and kind of enjoying it?

Second thing, and this is the one I actually care about. Walzer's paradigm seems to be a single ruler deciding alone in a room. This film is about a coalition — Blair, Seward, the lobbyists, the Democrats Stevens has to soften himself for, the Radicals he has to keep quiet. If the wrong is spread across all of them, whose guilt is it? Can dirty-hands theory handle a collective agent at all, or does it quietly need one pair of hands to stay intelligible? I keep feeling the film solves this by dumping the entire moral burden onto a single man in the final act, and that's a cheat, but I can't tell if it's a cheat the theory licenses.

Third, the counterfactual. If the lie about the envoys had come out and the amendment had lost, would that be a better dirty-hands film or just a tragedy? What does your answer say about whether dirty hands is a real category distinct from ordinary lesser-evil reasoning, or whether its just consequentialism with bad lighting?

Optional thread if you have room: Williams wrote about this too, in Politics and Moral Character, and my sense is he thinks the Walzer version lets a politician enjoy his own guilt as a kind of luxury good. If that's a fair reading of him, which of the two does the film side with, and does it matter alot for how I'd teach it?

Finally — and be blunt here — does the film do anything for the dirty-hands debate that argument alone can't do? I mean something specific: whether seeing guilt distributed, absorbed, or refused across a bunch of people teaches you something the papers can't. If the answer is no and it's just illustration, say so, because then I'll swap it out for something else.

Don't quote the film or Walzer verbatim; I don't need line-level accuracy and I know you can't check a script. But do tell me which scenes you'd screen and which you'd skip, and be honest about where the film resists the reading. I'd much rather find that out now than in week six.
