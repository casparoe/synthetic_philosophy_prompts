I am in the fifth year of a philosophy PhD, writing a dissertation on personal identity, and I am applying for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship at an interdisciplinary institute whose selection panel deliberately mixes metaphysicians with experimental psychologists and a cognitive neuroscientist. My half-formed idea concerns the methodology of the teletransporter debate rather than its substance. Since Parfit, arguments about what matters in survival have proceeded by engineering cases — the teletransporter, the fission and division variants, the combined spectrum — and then reporting the intuitions those cases elicit. My suspicion is that this case space has been sampled rather than explored: the canonical constructions vary one or two parameters wildly while holding several others fixed at values that are never noticed, let alone justified. Two of them interest me most. The first is the overlap between original and successor. Almost everything sits at one endpoint or the other — instantaneous destruction-and-replacement, or indefinite coexistence, as when division yields two people who both carry on — and the short-overlap variants that do exist are built precisely to make the overlap irrelevant, since their role in the argument is to show that the original's extra days add nothing. But there is a regime nobody seems to have occupied: an overlap long enough, and structured so, that the original and the successor interact repeatedly, hand over projects, correct one another's recollections, and thereby generate connectedness between the original's later stages and the successor's later stages that was not present at the moment of copying. In that regime the successor's psychology is partly inherited and partly acquired from the original by ordinary causal commerce, and I do not see how any relation stated purely between an earlier stage and a later stage can distinguish it from a case where the successor merely receives a transmission and the original is silent. The second fixed parameter is fidelity: the connectedness spectrum is always treated as one-dimensional, though there are at least two independent ways to dial down a copy — losing episodic detail while keeping commitments and character, or keeping the memories while losing the projects. If intuitions about whether what matters has been preserved flip along the overlap axis, or come apart across the two fidelity axes, then the framework itself and not merely the choice among candidate relations would need revision; if instead the whole space behaves smoothly and every flip is explicable by variables with nothing to do with survival (consent, knowledge of the outcome, fear of being harmed), that is a substantial result too, since it would be a principled reply to the charge that these intuitions are artifacts of vignette construction.

Please turn this into a proposal of roughly 1,300 to 1,500 words, written for that mixed panel. I need a precise statement of the question, framed so that it has determinate possible answers rather than dissolving into general worry about intuitions; an account of why the positions now on offer — the psychological-continuity theorist, the biological or animalist critic, and those who hold that what matters is no further question beyond identity or its absence — all leave it open, given that they share the case-design method and differ mainly in how they read its outputs; and the method laid out stage by stage across the twenty-four months. On method I especially want clarity about three things: what it takes to individuate a parameter such that varying it really is varying the case rather than smuggling in a new one; how I would argue that the combinations I care about are coherent and do not collapse into cases already discussed (this is where I expect the sharpest objections, since zero overlap plus ongoing interaction looks contradictory, and a lossy copy that keeps commitments while losing all episodic memory may not be possible at all); and where a modest vignette study would fit, if it fits, alongside an explicit answer to the panelist who asks why this is not simply a large survey waiting to be run — that is, what a philosophical project contributes here that funded data collection does not. Then give three candidate outcomes, say what would discriminate among them, and state what would count as the project's failure, including the unglamorous version in which the intermediate cases turn out to be incoherent on close inspection and the whole exercise reduces to a taxonomy nobody wanted. Finally, an honest assessment of scope: what I would have to cut to keep this a two-year project rather than a second dissertation, and which part of it is most likely to swallow the rest. No bibliography and no literature review by citation — describe positions, arguments, and cases by content and I will supply the references myself.
