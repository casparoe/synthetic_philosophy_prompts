I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in the history of the social sciences, and I am drafting the third chapter of my dissertation, which concerns the wartime American program of studying enemy populations at a distance. I need a disciplined case analysis, not a literature survey, and I want it built around a framework I have assembled myself — so please use the framework as I define it below rather than substituting one you find more familiar.

**The episode.** In 1944 the U.S. Office of War Information commissioned the anthropologist Ruth Benedict, then at Columbia and working in the Boasian configurationalist tradition of her 1934 book *Patterns of Culture*, to produce an analysis of Japanese culture that would let American policymakers understand and predict Japanese behavior in the final phase of the war and in any subsequent occupation. She had never been to Japan and could not go; her materials were the ones the "culture at a distance" method relied on — published literature, press clippings, films and recordings, and interviews, including with Japanese-Americans and Nisei and with material drawn from prisoner-of-war interrogations. The published result, *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946), opens by assembling a list of paired contrarieties — a people at once aggressive and unaggressive, militaristic and aesthetic, polite and insolent, rigid and adaptable, loyal and treacherous — and treats the puzzle of how such contrarieties cohere as the thing an analysis of national character must solve. Its most famous contribution was to popularize a contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures, with Japan assigned to the latter. The book's later career matters as much as its contents for my purposes: it sold modestly in the United States for decades, was translated into Japanese in 1948 during a period of intense national self-examination, went on to sell in the millions there, gave the shame/guilt vocabulary ordinary currency in Japanese, and is widely held to have seeded the subsequent four decades of treatises on Japaneseness. Japanese scholars — among them Watsuji Tetsurō, Yanagita Kunio, and Tsurumi Kazuko — attacked it early on as inaccurate and methodologically unsound, and the most persistent criticisms have been that it took the ideology of one class for the culture as a whole, an acute social dislocation for a normal condition, and an extraordinary historical moment for an unvarying norm of behavior. There is also a widely repeated claim that Benedict's analysis produced a recommendation to retain the emperor; treat that claim as unverified and do not let any part of your argument rest on it.

**The framework.** My working hypothesis is that "national character" is not one kind of claim but at least four, and that most of the disputes about it are disputes in which the parties are not making the same kind of claim. The four readings I want used:

1. *Distributional.* A statistical claim about the location and spread of trait scores across a population — means, variances, base rates, with the usual consequence that individual overlap between populations is large even where means differ. This is roughly what the mid-1950s reformulation of national character research into the study of modal personality and sociocultural systems — Inkeles and Levinson's chapter in the first *Handbook of Social Psychology* — was trying to discipline the notion into becoming.
2. *Configurational.* A claim about the pattern or arrangement that organizes a set of practices, institutions, and motives into a whole, such that the same trait has a different meaning depending on where it sits in the arrangement. This is not a summary of individuals and does not entail any claim about their distribution; it is a claim about structure, and it is, I think, the reading Benedict's own theoretical commitments point toward.
3. *Developmental.* A claim about etiology — that broadly shared child-rearing and socialization practices reliably produce broadly shared adult motivational structure. This reading supplies a mechanism the configurational one lacks and a causal story the distributional one lacks.
4. *Normative-institutional.* A claim not about what people are like but about what a given society's rules, sanctions, and role obligations require and reward — a description of the situation individuals face, whether or not they endorse it and whether or not it shapes their psyches.

**What I want.** Roughly 1,800 to 2,200 words, in five numbered sections.

First: identify the principal, the expert, and the object in the commission, and state precisely what kind of deliverable the Office of War Information was actually buying. Prediction of behavior at the level the agency wanted — whether a surrender could be induced, how prisoners would behave, how an occupation would be received — is an operational demand, and I want you to say which of the four readings could in principle license it and which could not. Be explicit if your answer is that none could, and if so, say what the request silently assumed.

Second: locate Benedict's text within the four readings. Which does she officially offer, which does she slip into when the argument needs support, and where — pick at least one specific juncture, such as the contrarieties passage or the shame/guilt contrast — does the analysis require two readings at once that come apart under pressure? I am especially interested in whether the configurational reading was doing protective work: a claim about pattern is harder to falsify by pointing to counterexamples among individuals, and I want an honest assessment of whether that is a virtue of the reading or an evasion.

Third: assess the criticisms. Do the standard objections — class ideology for culture, dislocation for normal condition, moment for unvarying norm — land on the reading Benedict was actually making, or on a distributional or developmental reading she had not committed to? This is the part of the chapter I most need help with, because I suspect a great deal of the mid-century critique of national character studies shot at a target the texts had already vacated, and I want to know whether that suspicion survives contact with this case. Say plainly where the critics were right anyway.

Fourth: the reception. The book's migration from an instrument of an enemy state's wartime planning into a resource for the described people's own self-understanding is the feature that my framework handles worst, and I want you to say so rather than paper over it. Does the reflexive uptake show that a fifth reading is required — one on which national character claims are partly constitutive of what they claim to describe — or does it show that the framework is being asked to do work that belongs to a different kind of explanation entirely? Diagnose which, and note what the framework goes silent about.

Fifth: the normative question. Distinguish at least three loci at which generalizing about a people can go wrong — the content (it is false, or true but so coarse as to mislead), the position (who says it, from where, with what power over the described), and the use (what it is deployed for) — and say which locus this episode actually indicts. I have my own intuitions here and I would rather not contaminate the analysis with them, so argue from the case. Address specifically whether the same content changes moral status when it circulates as self-characterization rather than external characterization, and whether the Japanese reception vindicates the position-and-use objection or exposes something it cannot explain: that the objection seems to assume the described are only ever objects, which this episode shows they were not.

No bibliography, no citations, no list of further reading — I have the sources. Where the framework is silent or where the case resists it, mark that explicitly in a sentence or two instead of forcing an answer; a stated silence is more useful to me than a strained resolution.
