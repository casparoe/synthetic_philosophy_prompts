I am drafting a paper whose central exhibit is the nineteenth-century reception of non-Euclidean geometry, treated as a stress test for conceivability-based modal epistemology. Before I commit to that structure, I want a rigorous case analysis from you that runs in both directions: what the leading accounts of conceivability say about the episode, and what the episode says about those accounts. I am not asking for a history of geometry — the facts below should be treated as given — nor for a survey of the literature, a bibliography, or suggested readings. I want disciplined mapping followed by an honest verdict.

The facts I will treat as given:

- Saccheri's 1733 work set out to vindicate Euclid by reductio. Under the hypothesis that the summit angles of his quadrilateral are acute he derived no contradiction, only a body of strange results (a finite maximum area for triangles, an absolute unit of length), and he closed by declaring that hypothesis absolutely false because, in the standard English translation, it is "repugnant to the nature of straight lines." Those results are now theorems of hyperbolic geometry.
- Lambert pursued the same strategy a generation later and reached a comparable impasse. Lobachevsky (1829–30) and János Bolyai (1832) then developed hyperbolic geometry as a system in its own right. Gauss withheld his own results, reportedly from concern about how they would be received, and reportedly checked the angle sum of a large terrestrial triangle by survey.
- Riemann's 1854 lecture generalised the setting to manifolds of variable curvature.
- Beltrami's two memoirs of 1868 constructed models of hyperbolic geometry within Euclidean space, establishing equiconsistency; Klein published a projective model in 1871 and Poincaré a conformal disc model soon after. Hilbert's axiomatisation at the end of the century completed the shift, making consistency a matter of exhibiting a model in some further theory.

Three frameworks should be applied separately rather than blended: Chalmers's distinctions between negative and positive conceivability and between prima facie and ideal conceivability, together with his thesis that ideal positive conceivability entails possibility; Yablo's account, on which the conceivable is what one can imagine without detecting a defect in the imagining, so that inconceivability is a matter of seeming to find incoherence; and Williamson's scepticism about conceivability arguments in favour of a counterfactual-based, knowledge-constrained use of imagination.

I would also like you to evaluate a distinction I am proposing and that you should feel free to reject: hosted, or transferred, conceivability. The thought is that after Beltrami and Klein a mathematician's positive conception of hyperbolic space is not achieved directly but by riding on a conception of Euclidean space already in hand — one imagines the interior of a disc with reinterpreted lines and distances. If so, the epistemic force of the conceiving is inherited from the host theory, which raises the question of what happens to hosted conceivings when the host is itself demoted, as Euclidean geometry was, first by the existence of rival consistent geometries and then by Hilbert's treatment of its primitives as implicitly defined by the axioms. Does demotion of the host revoke the hosted conceiving, and is the resulting structure a regress or a harmless circularity?

Please address the following, in this order.

1. Periodisation. For each phase — Saccheri and Lambert; Lobachevsky and Bolyai; the model constructions; Hilbert — state what was ideally positively conceivable, what was merely negatively conceivable, and what was describable without being conceivable at all. Say explicitly whether you think conceivability itself changed across the episode or whether something else changed while conceivability remained fixed, and what turns on that answer.

2. Saccheri's repugnance. On Yablo's account inconceivability consists in seeming to detect a defect in an act of imagination; Saccheri seemed to detect one and was wrong. Is his case a false positive that a more careful account can filter out, or does it indicate that the deliverances of imagination are not the kind of thing that can carry modal weight?

3. The idealisation problem. Because Chalmers's thesis is stated for ideal reflection, two millennia of failure to conceive hyperbolic space is compatible with its having been ideally positively conceivable throughout. Assess whether that is a genuine cost: what independent content does ideal conceivability retain if its extension is fixed by what is in fact possible, and is there anything in this episode that allows the idealisation to be checked from the inside?

4. Hosted conceivability. Evaluate the proposed distinction against the models and Hilbert's axiomatics. Does it capture something real about how the models functioned epistemically, or is it an artefact of the particular representational route the nineteenth century happened to take?

5. Which modality. The dispute slid among at least three questions: whether a system is formally consistent, whether a certain kind of space is metaphysically possible, and whether physical space is Euclidean — the question Gauss's survey and, later, general relativity addressed. Explain how the three accounts handle that sliding, and whether the episode suggests conceivability arguments are generally vulnerable to it.

6. Verdict and transfer. State whether the episode confirms, strains, or simply bypasses the link between conceivability and possibility, and in a paragraph or two what the result implies for the two canonical applications, the zombie argument and Kripkean a posteriori necessity, without turning those debates into a review.

Where the frameworks are silent about the episode, say so plainly rather than pressing them into service; an acknowledged silence is more useful to me than a forced fit. Around two thousand words, organised under the headings above.
