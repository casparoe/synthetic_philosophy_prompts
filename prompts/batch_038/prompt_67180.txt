(a) Parfit's claim is that when we think about surviving we are not really thinking about the bare fact of being the very same substance continuing to exist, we are thinking about the holding of certain psychological relations. He calls the relation that matters Relation R, which is psychological connectedness and/or continuity with the right kind of cause. In the teletransporter case your brain and body are scanned, the scan destroys you, the information is sent to Mars, and a replica is built there which has your memories, your intentions and your character and which carries on your projects. That person is R-related to you even though, on the further-fact view of persons, it may not literally be you. Since R is everything you would actually care about — the replica remembers your life, loves the people you love, finishes the book you were writing — the case shows that identity is a further question and not the one that mattered. Now because R comes in degrees, connectedness being stronger or weaker depending on how many direct psychological connections there are, identity itself must come in degrees too: the Mars person is me to a very high degree, someone in the last stage of a dementing illness is me to a low degree, and the person in the fission case is me to two different degrees at once. That is the real upshot of the chapter, that survival is not an on/off switch, and it is why Parfit thinks we should stop treating the question "will I still exist?" as the important one.

(b) On Cold Storage the Parfitian should say you survive. What R cares about is not the length of the gap but whether an appropriate causal chain runs from you to the person who wakes up, and here it plainly does: the stored data is what produces the replica's psychology, and there is nothing else in the world competing with it. The replica will have quasi-memories of your life, which is what Parfit needs anyway, because quasi-memory does not presuppose identity and so it escapes the circularity that Butler pressed against Locke's memory criterion. The twenty-five years of nothing is on all fours with being frozen and later revived, and a Parfitian should not be spooked by it.

The second variant is where the theory starts to look strained, in my view. The moment the second replica exists, R holds one-to-many, which is just the fission situation Parfit describes, and he is clear that what matters can hold one-to-many. But the second replica is destroyed before it wakes, so the one-to-many relation never actually obtains: there is only ever one person in the world who is R-related to you. The closest-continuer machinery Parfit relies on therefore delivers a determinate answer here — you are the Mars person, full stop, because there is no rival. And this settles the question about what matters as well, since the destroyed replica never had a psychology and was never a subject, so nothing was lost when it was destroyed and the survival of the Mars person is exactly as good as survival is in the ordinary case. Which suggests that what makes Division troubling for Parfit is not the loss of identity at all but simply the number of people R-related to you, and once the number is back down to one the worry disappears.

(c) Williams's two experiments both involve psychological information being copied from one person's body into another's. In the first, you are told that tomorrow the psychology of person A will be copied into B's body and B's into A's, and you are asked which of the two resulting people will be tortured; people answer by following the psychology, so they say the torture falls on the one who has their memories and character. In the second, Williams gets you to consider the same arrangement from the point of view of the body: you are told that your body will be tortured tomorrow, but that before then every memory will have been erased and your character entirely replaced by somebody else's. His argument is that this second description shows you cannot have self-interested fear of a torture that is going to befall someone with none of your psychology, so anticipation must track psychological continuity — what I should care about is where my memories and character end up rather than where my body ends up. That is why his cases are standardly read as supporting the sort of view Parfit develops, and the best Parfitian response is simply to accept the conclusion while adding that identity, as part (a) showed, is beside the point.
