I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a few months back I did a long piece about a flood — a pump station that was supposed to run automatically, a maintenance duty that had been contracted out, the contract lapsed, and for eleven months there was literally nobody on any org chart whose job those pumps were. Then they didn't run. After the piece ran I recieved an invite to give a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on causation in accident investigation: law, epidemiology, engineering, plus one philosophy strand.

I've told them I'll defend a claim they mostly won't like, which is that "causation by omission" isn't a metaphysical category at all. On my view an omission only causes something when the omitter was occupying an assigned slot in a system that hands out causal jobs — a duty roster, a maintenance regime, a control loop with a named owner. No slot, no omission-cause, dont care how counterfactually relevant the not-doing was. Call it the duty-slot view. My argument is roughly: (1) everyone agrees the set of true omission claims has to be restricted somehow, or else my not watering your plants caused them to die; (2) every restriction on offer — abnormality, salience, deviation from a default — turns out to be a disguised institutional fact about who was assigned what; (3) so say the institutional fact out loud and drop the metaphysics.

The room: maybe ten causal-inference people who live in DAGs and potential outcomes and will think I've reinvented variable selection with extra steps; two tort lawyers who'll think I'm collapsing cause in fact into proximate cause; three safety engineers who do systems-based accident analysis and hate any account that ends with one person failing to act; and one metaphysician of causation who thinks the whole topic is a trap.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. The eight or so questions I'll actually get, phrased the way each subgroup would phrase it, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first two minutes so it isn't dragged out of me later. And deal with the redundancy case — a second operator would have caught the failure anyway, so my slot-holder's omission changes nothing counterfactually — if you think it's as bad as I suspect, say so. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks.
