I'm a second-year epidemiology student, and last week my sister hosted a dinner at which two of her friends got into a sustained argument that she asked me to settle and I couldn't. The occasion was a local school district's decision to add forty minutes of outdoor recess to the elementary school day specifically to slow the onset of myopia — a policy borrowed from trials run in East Asia, where the proportion of school leavers who are myopic has climbed from something like a quarter to something like four-fifths within a couple of generations. Ravi is a data analyst who has read enough of the quantitative genetics literature to be dangerous; Helen teaches middle-school science and has a daughter in thick lenses. Neither was posturing; both made points I could not answer on the spot. I've tried to report the exchange fairly and in order, without signaling which of them I found more persuasive, because I genuinely don't know.

The argument was mostly fought over a table in the newspaper article, which reproduced variance components from a two-cohort study of refractive error in the same region. I'm giving you the table rather than asking you to recall any particular study: please treat these figures as stipulated and reason from them, and don't spend any effort on whether they match a real dataset (they're constructed for the argument). At age twelve, Cohort A (rural, 1975) had a mean spherical equivalent of −0.50 diopters, myopia prevalence of 25%, total phenotypic variance of 4.00 D², decomposing into additive genetic variance 1.60, shared-environment variance 1.20, and non-shared-environment variance 1.20, for a heritability of 0.40. Cohort B (urban, 2020) had a mean of −2.50 diopters, prevalence of 85%, total variance 2.50 D², decomposing into additive genetic 1.60, shared environment 0.30, non-shared environment 0.60, for a heritability of 0.64.

Here is the exchange:

(1) Ravi opened with the standard figures: twin studies put the heritability of refractive error somewhere between 0.7 and 0.9, genome-wide association studies have turned up hundreds of associated loci, and parental myopia is one of the most reliable risk factors clinicians have. On that basis, he said, myopia is substantially a genetic condition, and a district that buys forty extra minutes of recess is buying a rounding error — you would not add recess time in the hope of changing children's height, and refraction is not obviously different.

(2) Helen replied that heritability is a statistic describing a population in an environment, not a property of the trait, so "mostly genetic" cannot mean "not changeable." And she said the epidemiology refutes Ravi outright: the prevalence numbers above moved from a quarter to four-fifths in two or three generations, which is far too fast for the gene pool to have shifted, so the environment is plainly doing the heavy lifting and the district is aiming at the right thing.

(3) Ravi said she had just committed the classic level-versus-variance confusion, and that it proves his point rather than hers. A secular rise in prevalence says nothing about what accounts for the differences between children within a cohort; the same kids could be ranked identically by genetic propensity in 1975 and 2020 and the whole distribution could still slide two diopters. Since the policy shifts the distribution rather than the ordering within it, he said, it can dress up the average while leaving the genetically determined spread of outcomes exactly as it was — which is why heritability is not silent about the policy at all.

(4) Helen conceded the level-versus-variance point and said it made her case stronger: if heritability is silent about the mean, it is silent about the one quantity the board cares about, so the board should ignore it entirely and go with the direct causal evidence from the outdoor-time trials, which show a real reduction in onset. She added that the 0.7–0.9 figure is not to be trusted in any event, because twin studies inflate heritability by assuming that identical and fraternal twins share equally similar environments, and the table in the article gives 0.40 for Cohort A anyway — so Ravi cannot have it both ways.

(5) Ravi said the equal-environments worry was beside the point, because the more interesting mechanism is gene–environment correlation: children with a genetic propensity for near work select themselves into near work, so a good deal of what the decomposition books as environmental variance is genetic variance wearing a costume. It follows, he said, that a policy which herds every child outside for forty minutes removes only the environmental variation the children were generating for themselves; the genetic differences in who ends up myopic survive the intervention intact. Heritability, on his account, therefore predicts both a modest effect and substantial residual variation — precisely what "ignore it entirely" cannot predict.

(6) Helen then turned the table on him. Cohort B's heritability is higher than Cohort A's, 0.64 against 0.40, and the reason is visible in the decomposition: shared-environment variance collapsed from 1.20 to 0.30. The environment got more uniform, so the genetic share of what remains got bigger — high heritability in the modern cohort is a symptom of environmental homogeneity, not evidence of genetic dominance. Meanwhile the mean moved two full diopters between cohorts while additive genetic variance stayed at exactly 1.60. Her conclusion was that a heritability of 0.64 and a two-diopter environmental shift are the same fact described twice, and that the constancy of the genetic variance across such different environments further proves there is no gene–environment interaction, so the trial results can be generalized to any setting the district might find itself in.

(7) Ravi's last move was a policy proposal: if the district wants to spend well, it should genotype children and use a polygenic score for refractive error to target the intensive interventions — low-dose atropine, orthokeratology lenses, extended outdoor time — at the high-risk group, because that is the group for whom the trait is, on his own account, genetic. Helen objected on three grounds: current polygenic scores for refraction explain only a modest share of variance; their predictive accuracy drops sharply when applied to children whose ancestry differs from the discovery sample's; and the score would largely reproduce information the school nurse already collects on the intake form about parents' spectacles. She added that targeting the highest-scoring children means concentrating resources on the children whose outcomes are least modifiable, which is backwards.
