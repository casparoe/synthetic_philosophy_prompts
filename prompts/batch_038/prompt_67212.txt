I am four years into a novel whose second half tracks an elderly lexicographer through the gradual disintegration of her autobiographical memory: she keeps her vocabulary and her irony almost intact, but loses the story of her own life in reverse order, until the woman who has been narrating her own past becomes a person to whom that past is narrated by others. Wanting the book to be honest rather than merely affecting, I sent the draft to a philosopher of mind who reads fiction and tolerates my questions, and she has told me that my difficulty is not a difficulty about my character but about what I have assumed a self to be — that the answer lies in a familiar image, the self as a center of narrative gravity, an abstraction posited to unify the story of a life in something like the way a center of gravity is posited to unify a body's distribution of mass: not an ingredient added to the organism, but a manner of organizing what is already there. I have read enough to suspect that this image admits of at least two readings, a deflationary one and a merely descriptive one, and I cannot tell which my consultant means or which I ought to be writing toward.

So I want a number from you, with the derivation visible. The claim I would like assessed — call it NG — is this: for an ordinary human person, the self is nothing over and above a posit that organizes a life-story; there is no persisting subject, substance, or further entity that the story is about, over and above the organism, its dispositions and behavior, and the descriptive unity imposed upon them. Please decompose rather than pronounce. At a minimum I would like a probability for each of the following, with a line of justification, plus any term you think I have omitted: whether the deflationary reading is coherent at all — whether an abstractum can be the referent of the first-person pronoun, as against NG collapsing either into eliminativism about selves or into the far weaker claim that "self" simply denotes the person while narratives merely describe her; whether narrative is constitutive rather than descriptive, such that which facts obtain about the person depends on the story; whether that story must be at least partly the subject's own telling, or may be supplied in its entirety by an audience; whether the view can absorb the phenomenology, that is, whether any residue of what-it-is-like-to-be-the-subject-of-the-story survives the positing uncaptured; whether NG can accommodate a systematically false self-narrative — confabulation is the obvious case — without making the self hostage to the accuracy of the tale; and whether NG is genuinely in competition with psychological-continuity or animalist accounts of identity over time, or is instead a thesis about an adjacent question (personhood, self-understanding, selfhood as an achievement) that could be true alongside them. Say how you combine the terms — conjunction, weighted disjunction, something less tidy — and give me the resulting figure.

Two things after that. First, I sit at about 0.3 on NG and my consultant at about 0.8, and I suspect the gap between us is not where either of us thinks it is: given your decomposition, tell me which term most plausibly accounts for that difference. Second, tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument, or what kind of empirical finding, would move it furthest — because the last eighty pages of the book turn on it. If NG is true and self-authorship is not required, my character's self survives in the mouths of her daughters and the ending is a kind of consolation; if NG is true but the narrative must be her own, the ending is a catastrophe I have been flinching away from writing. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a tour of the literature; I have no way of checking any of it and it is not what I need. I want your reasoning, with its weak points marked.
