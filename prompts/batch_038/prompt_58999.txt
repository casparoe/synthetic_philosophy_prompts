I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, but I keep running into this question at work and I want to figure out what I actually think about it. Basically: when is an outcome locked in by history, and when would we have gotten there anyway? Here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and I want to know whether one principle gets all of them.

QWERTY: contingent. A different set of early typists' habits and we'd all be typing on something else. Though I've also read the Dvorak-is-faster claim doesnt really hold up in decent tests, which makes me less sure the "worse standard won" framing is even right.

Shipping containers: the fact that shipping got containerized feels inevitable, any big trading economy converges on steel boxes. But the specific sizes feel arbitrary.

Calculus: Newton and Leibniz both got there, and Darwin and Wallace both got natural selection, so the ideas were coming. But we use Leibniz's notation and not Newton's, and I think that part was a coin flip. So one thing inevitable and the other contingent, at the same moment.

Driving on the left vs the right: completely arbitrary which side, but every country is stuck with it. Except Sweden flipped in the sixties and it went fine, so stuck doesnt mean forever.

x86: I used to treat this as the textbook lock-in case, and then ARM ate the phone market. So "you can never get out" looks wrong.

VHS beating Betamax: the story I remember is that it was contingent, but I half suspect Betamax loses anyway on recording length and licensing, so maybe not.

Our own codebase: we have a config format picked in like 2009 based on one guy's preference. Everyone agrees it's bad, the migration is maybe two weeks of work, and it has not moved in fifteen years. Same shape as QWERTY except the switching cost is tiny, so my principle doesn't reach it.

My tentative version: it's like hysteresis. More than one stable state, some small early event picks which basin you land in, and moving between states costs more than it gains. Inevitable just means every nearby trajectory ends in the same state.

Also someone told me there's a distinction between "we ended up somewhere worse and nobody noticed" and "we ended up somewhere worse, everyone knows it, and we still can't move," and only the second one is supposed to be a real problem. My config format thing is apparently the second kind but it doesn't behave like it, which bugs me.

So: is there a principle that captures all of this, or a small set? State it precisely enough that I can say which clause each case is hitting. Then pick a case I didn't list, predict my verdict on it before I tell you, and be straight about which of the above I have to give up to keep the principle. My guess is calculus or x86 goes, but tell me if you think the hysteresis framing is the wrong shape entirely.
