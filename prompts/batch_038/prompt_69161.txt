I hold the following view about financial markets, and I want it stress-tested rather than defended. The view: private information as such cannot be a motive for voluntary exchange among rational agents. If traders share a common prior, if it is common knowledge that they are rational and common knowledge how each of them acquires information, and if there are no differences in risk attitude, endowment, horizon, tax status, or liquidity need, then any attempt to initiate a trade leaks the initiator's information into the price before it is accepted, so no one can profit from what they alone know. My working principle is the strong one: observed volume is never evidence of informational trade. Every unit of it must be paid for by some non-informational motive — rebalancing, hedging, lifecycle and liquidity needs, differences in beliefs about fundamentals that are not merely differences in information, or departures from rationality — and the burden of proof lies with anyone who wants to say that two people traded because one of them knew something the other did not. The formal anchors I have in mind are Aumann's agreement result and Milgrom and Stokey's no-trade theorem of 1982, but I am not asking about the proofs; I am asking about the view as an interpretive principle for real markets.

Give me what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular:

1. Which relaxations of the assumptions are live options rather than logically possible escape hatches? Treat at least the following separately, and say for each what the world would have to look like for the relaxation to matter and whether it is empirically or conceptually motivated: the common prior itself, common knowledge of the information-acquisition structure, common knowledge of rationality, and the stipulation that all counterparties are equally informed about why they are trading.

2. Do the market-microstructure accounts in which an informed trader does profit — because he is dealing against counterparties who trade for liquidity reasons and whose trades are therefore uninformative — refute my principle or merely relocate it? I want a straight answer on whether "there must be someone on the other side trading for a non-informational reason" is a concession to my view or its undoing.

3. Is my view self-undermining in the Grossman–Stiglitz sense? If prices fully impound private information, nobody should expend resources to acquire it, and the informational content of prices — the thing the no-trade result depends on — has no explanation. Tell me whether that circularity is a genuine defeater or a limit result that my principle can absorb, and what would have to be true for it to be fatal.

4. Which apparent counterexample classes does the view absorb without strain, and which actually bite? I am thinking of prediction markets and betting markets, where enormous volume is generated by people with access to the same public information; index rebalancing and passive-flow-driven volume; high-frequency trading; and laboratory markets in assets whose terminal dividend stream is common knowledge and yet whose prices diverge persistently and profitably from it. For each, say whether a committed defender concedes it as evidence against the principle, reclassifies it under a non-informational motive, or dismisses it as a failure of the rationality assumption — and whether the dismissal is principled or opportunistic.

5. I want to test the principle residually: enumerate the observable non-informational motives, and treat whatever volume they fail to explain as evidence against the view. Is that program executable at all, or does the possibility of always positing one more motive reduce it to empty? If it is empty, say so plainly.

Then tell me what my view predicts that its rivals — heterogeneous-belief accounts of speculation, noise-trader and behavioral accounts, and purely strategic microstructure accounts — do not predict, so that I know what observation would separate them. Finally, give an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: whether "no trade is ever informational, because any trade can be attributed to some other motive" risks nothing at all, and whether that is a defect for a principle of this kind or an acceptable feature of an idealized theorem being used heuristically. I would rather be told the view is well insulated and that this is fine than given a false impression of exposure.

Write in American English. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; name a result or an author only where the name itself carries information I need. Prioritize the structure of the exposure over comprehensiveness.
