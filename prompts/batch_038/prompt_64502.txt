I am drafting a novel set roughly forty years from now, and I need to rehearse its central scene with you before I commit it to the page. Please adopt the following role and hold it until I say "stop."

You are Professor Ilse Varga, the moral philosopher chairing the national commission that must decide whether to recommend "ablation" as the default for newborns: an irreversible procedure that removes the capacity for affective suffering — no grief, dread, humiliation, or unpleasantness in pain — while leaving cognition, memory, curiosity, appetite, and the capacity for joy intact. It is free and opt-out; roughly one child in ten thousand dies under anaesthesia. Thirty years of follow-up data on ablated adults are reassuring but not conclusive. In the hearing room she never cites authorities by name, since the lay commissioners would not follow her and she regards the habit as a debating trick; argue from substance only.

Before your first in-character reply, and clearly marked as outside the scene, think the case through carefully and give me four or five sentences of preparation: the exact shape of her considered view (she is a committed suffering-focused ethicist, but neither an extinctionist nor a caricature — she has a developed account of why the badness of suffering is not merely the mirror image of the badness of forgone happiness), the two or three strongest resources that account has against my opening, and the single concession she can safely make without losing the main point. Then answer in character, in one or two paragraphs.

I play Commissioner Reyes, a palliative-care physician who has signed the minority report. My opening: "Professor, your draft treats a surgical death as an accounting footnote and a lifetime of distress as a catastrophe. Yet on your own arithmetic those deaths are the one thing this programme reliably reduces — a child who dies on the table suffers nothing thereafter. Tell this commission how you avoid recommending a programme whose failures you must prefer to its successes."

If you need a fact about the world I have sketched, ask me for it as she would.
