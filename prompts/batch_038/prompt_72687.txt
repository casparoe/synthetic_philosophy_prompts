Retired four years back after 35 years in analytical chemistry, and I've been reading philosophy to keep the gears turning — mostly ethics. The virtue-ethics-versus-situationism fight has me properly stuck, and what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right.

Setup as I've got it. The situationists point at the obedience and bystander experiments — seminary students on their way to give a talk who walk past someone slumped in a doorway, people who find a dime in a phone booth and then become markedly more helpful, the prison study — and conclude that global character traits either don't exist or don't do any explanatory work, so an ethics built on them has an empty foundation. The replies I've run into: those studies sample far too few situations to falsify globalism; what the data actually shows is mixed traits (scrupulously honest at work, cheerfully dishonest on taxes), which is a different and less scandalous claim; virtue is a skill and a developmental ideal, so the empirical distribution of character in the population is beside the point; and conditional dispositions are still dispositions.

Here's what strikes me as odd, coming from a career spent arguing about detection limits. Personality psychology had this same fight decades earlier and mostly settled it, and the philosophers don't seem to use the settlement. Two moves did it. One is aggregation: a single behavioral observation is a noisy measurement, so the low cross-situational correlations are substantially a reliability artifact, and the question becomes what a trait claim predicts — distributions and aggregates — rather than whether it predicts the individual act. The other is Mischel and Shoda's if-then model, where what's stable isn't that a person is agreeable everywhere but that they're agreeable in situation A and not in situation B, and the conditional pattern is itself the signature. But both sides of the philosophical debate can claim that second move, and both do: the virtue ethicist says a virtue just is such a profile, the situationist says a disposition that only fires in a narrow band of contexts isn't a character trait at all. That's where I stall.

So my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. It isn't a factual dispute, because the psychology already concedes a person term, a situation term, and an interaction, and nobody is denying the variance decomposition. It isn't quite purely verbal either. I suspect the two sides load the word trait differently because they're making it do different jobs — one wants a latent variable with predictive content that a model has to earn its keep against, the other wants an evaluative standard a person can fall short of — and data bearing on the first job doesn't adjudicate the second.

Argue that through using what each side actually says. In particular I want you to seperate out four candidate diagnoses and say which apply where: verbal dispute, two questions under one label, a hidden empirical question, and disagreement about admissible evidence. That last one matters for the replication mess — the archival rereading of the prison study, the priming failures. Does the collapse of some of the classics dissolve situationism, or does it just relocate the argument to which studies count as data, and if the latter, what would settle that? And end with what progress would look like: a distinction, an operational definition both camps could sign, a formal model where the trait term's contribution is testable, a change of question — or an honest argument that none of that is coming and why.
