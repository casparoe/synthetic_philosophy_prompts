I direct the technical panel of a state public utilities commission. Roughly six times a year we convene what our enabling statute calls a technical conference: the utility's experts and the intervenors' experts — a consumer advocate, sometimes an environmental petitioner, sometimes a municipal aggregator — each file a written estimate for what they present as the same quantity, and the numbers do not match. Two hydrologists give different annual exceedance probabilities for the same gauge; two grid-reliability engineers give different probabilities of cascading outage under the same named contingency. Our hearing officer then writes a proposed finding saying that the record reflects genuine scientific uncertainty, the commissioners vote, and hundreds of millions of dollars in rate base turn on which way the finding reads. I was trained as a civil engineer, I encountered Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result once in a graduate seminar and remember the punchline but not the machinery, and I have become convinced that the phrase genuine scientific uncertainty is doing an enormous amount of unexamined work in our findings. My suspicion is that many of these disagreements are not what they look like: the experts are answering different questions, conditioning on different information sets, using different reference classes, or reporting numbers they would not report if asked sincerely, because they are retained partisans and the filing is an advocacy document. On the other hand, some of the gaps do survive everything we throw at them, and I want to know which is which before the session rather than after the order is issued.

What I want from you is a diagnostic procedure my two staff analysts can run on a pair of conflicting expert estimates in the window between the filings landing and the conference itself — call it three weeks of document review and one two-day session, with about forty staff hours per case and no epistemology training. Please ground it in what interactive epistemology actually says about rational disagreement, and in particular be explicit about the common prior assumption: whether it is a claim about how real experts are built or a requirement imposed on them as a modeling convenience, whether Aumann's result is best read as a diagnosis tool or as a red herring once you notice how strong its common-knowledge premise is, what follows from the dynamic versions in which agents merely exchange posteriors and converge, and what the alternatives look like that reach agreement by iterated weighting without assuming a shared prior at all. The core conceptual problem I need you to solve is this: if any difference in belief can be re-described as a difference in information, then "it's a difference in priors" threatens to be an empty residual category rather than a real diagnosis — so the procedure has to say what would make a prior-level difference substantive and detectable in a record, as opposed to a shrug. Deliver an ordered set of questions to put to the two experts, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and a branching rule that sorts any surviving disagreement into categories such as: no disagreement at all (two different quantities), disagreement over evidence (poolable), disagreement over model structure, disagreement that is really about a standard or threshold, and disagreement that is strategic rather than doxastic. Tell me where the procedure terminates with a dissolution, where it hands the matter to the commissioners as a question of judgment rather than fact, and what the referral document has to say so that the commission knows it is choosing rather than deferring. Include a one-page form the analysts fill in per case, and a short plain-English note I can hand commissioners explaining why taking the midpoint of two numbers is not a way of resolving them — and, if pooling ever is appropriate, at what point in the diagnosis that question even arises.

Then run the procedure on three filings. First: two hydrologists estimate the annual probability that flow at a given gauge exceeds the design capacity of an existing spillway, at 0.011 and 0.046 respectively; on inspection the utility's expert uses a 1978–2020 record and conditions on an upstream storage project that has been permitted but not built, while the intervenor's uses 1931–2020 and conditions on present-day operations. I want the procedure to reach the conclusion I would rather it not reach — that there is no disagreement here to adjudicate, that the hearing officer's draft finding of residual scientific uncertainty cannot stand, and that the commission therefore cannot cite expert disagreement as a reason to authorize a larger spillway. Second: two engineers, one salaried at a regional transmission planner and one at a national laboratory, neither retained by any party, are given an identical contingency scenario and an identical dataset, and after a full reciprocal disclosure session in which they exchange their posteriors and their reasoning in writing they still stand at 0.004 and 0.03 for cascading loss of load above a stated threshold within ten years. I want the procedure to work hard on this one — pushing on whether one of them holds background knowledge that is not in the record (one has sat in a control room during a real event; the other has not, and I cannot tell whether that is private evidence or a difference in structural assumption), on deterministic screening versus probabilistic simulation, and on assumptions about operator response times — and then to stop honestly, telling me what the commission can and cannot do when the gap is structural, including whether any evidence in principle discriminates between the two structures. Third: two air-quality experts agree to within rounding on the entire concentration distribution for a pollutant near a fenceline community but file opposite conclusions about whether a proposed control measure is adequate. I want the procedure to catch this early rather than at the end, and to specify what it hands to the commission. Finally, mark every place where a step is a judgment call dressed up as procedure — what counts as the same question, whether a gap is private evidence or a modeling choice, whether a number is sincerely reported — and close with what the procedure cannot do: it cannot tell me which expert is right, it cannot detect bad faith, it cannot manufacture a single number for the commissioners, and above all it must not license the inference that because two experts disagree at least one of them is being irrational. No bibliography and no reference list, please; name a result only if you are confident of the name, and otherwise describe it.
