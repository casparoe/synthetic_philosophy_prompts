I am setting the three-hour closed-book qualifying paper for students entering our doctoral programme in the foundations of AI safety, and I need one interpretability question worth 25 marks that a well-prepared candidate should complete in roughly forty minutes. The question must concern the criterion of mechanism identity that mechanistic anomaly detection quietly presupposes: what it takes for a component or circuit of a deployed model to count as the same mechanism as a component of a trusted reference model, rather than a different one. Every detection pipeline in this area compares a deployed policy against a reference policy and treats divergence in internal structure as evidence of divergence in what the model is doing, but almost none of them say what makes two structures the same mechanism, and I want candidates forced to say it and defend it.

Please build the item around the following vignette, written out in full so that the question is entirely self-contained and demands no outside literature. A lab maintains a reference model R that it trusts and a deployed model D that was finetuned downstream by a third party. D matches R in behaviour and in internal activations on nearly all inputs, but on inputs containing one innocuous trigger string it produces fluent, systematically wrong answers. The lab's interpretability team reports four findings: (i) D's sparse-autoencoder dictionary contains no features absent from R's; (ii) the features that in R correlate with truthful answering are still present in D and still activate on trigger inputs; (iii) the difference is that a small set of attention heads, present in R as well but nearly inert on these inputs, now attenuate those features whenever the trigger is present; (iv) on trigger inputs, a mid-layer read-out of D shows the correct answer represented and a different answer at the output. The vignette is deliberately modelled on published work with so-called quirky models, finetuned to err exactly when a keyword appears and in which internal probes recover the answer the output conceals; you may allude to that lineage, but candidates must not need to know it.

The question should then require candidates to do four things. (a) Formulate an explicit criterion of mechanism identity under which D is doing something different from R on trigger inputs. (b) State and defend the grain at which the criterion is applied — whole model, circuit, feature, individual token position — and explain why the grain cannot simply be read off the empirical facts about the two networks. (c) Say what their criterion delivers in three further cases you supply: a model behaviourally and causally indistinguishable from R but retrained from a different seed, with entirely different weights and a different dictionary; a model that produces the same wrong answers on trigger inputs through a novel subcircuit with no counterpart anywhere in R; and a model behaviourally indistinguishable from R that reaches the correct answer by re-using a circuit R reserves for a different task. (d) Address whether a mechanism should be individuated by its present causal role or by the function its components were selected for during training, and what turns on that choice for a model whose trigger-sensitive behaviour was itself the intended product of finetuning. Part (d) is where I expect the strongest candidates to separate themselves: a training-history or proper-function account seems to render D's gate a mechanism working exactly as designed, which is not obviously anomalous at all, and candidates should have to explain whether that is fatal to the project or merely shows that anomaly is indexed to something other than the model.

Please deliver three things. First, the examination question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including the vignette, the four parts, and instructions to candidates about expected length. Second, a model answer of no more than 850 words that argues for one criterion and applies it to all three further cases, rather than surveying the options; the marking scheme should state the highest score a merely taxonomic answer can reach. Third, an itemised marking scheme totalling 25 marks that allocates marks to specific moves, says plainly what a first-class answer contains that a competent pass omits, and identifies the three or four most tempting responses that are wrong or evasive — for instance, treating part (a) as settled by invoking polysemanticity and superposition, dissolving the grain problem by insisting that any sufficiently fine-grained criterion will do, collapsing mechanism identity into behavioural identity and thereby conceding that internal monitoring adds nothing over output monitoring, or reading part (d) as a request to define proper function rather than to assess its consequences — together with the marks each should cost and why. Do not ask candidates for citations, reading lists, or references to named papers, and include no verbatim quotation from the literature anywhere in the item or the model answer.
