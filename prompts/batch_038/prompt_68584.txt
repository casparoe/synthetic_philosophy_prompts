Please take on the role of a political philosopher who works on democratic epistemology and who knows Hannah Arendt's essays on truth and on political deception well — specifically her treatment of factual versus rational truth in the essay collected in *Between Past and Future*, and her analysis of the Pentagon Papers in the essay on lying in politics — but who is equally fluent in contemporary debates about misinformation, testimony, and institutional epistemic harm. I would like a rigorous consistency audit of a position I have written down, not a lecture on the literature and not a summary of Arendt.

The occasion: I have been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my own view ahead of a workshop on political lying, precisely so that the other participants have something to attack. I would rather be attacked by you first.

Here is the package, as I intend to circulate it.

(i) Factual truth and rational truth are different kinds of thing, and only the first is genuinely at the mercy of politics. What makes factual truth political is that it concerns other people and what has happened among them; it is established by witnesses and by a record, and it can therefore be revised by anyone with the standing or the power to revise the record. Rational truth is indifferent to agreement and cannot be politically destroyed in the same way.

(ii) Lying is not primarily a failure of knowledge but an exercise of the same capacity as promising and acting: to lie is to make the world other than it would otherwise have been. That is why politics has always had an ambivalent rather than a simply hostile relation to veracity.

(iii) It follows that truthfulness is not one of the political virtues. Someone who insists that a matter is settled, and therefore not available for deliberation and agreement, is taking up an anti-political position. This is not a criticism of her, but it does mean the truth-teller is not the model of the good citizen.

(iv) There is a legitimate category of political lie, which I call the prospective lie: one addressed to a state of affairs that does not yet exist and whose content the liar is in some position to bring about or to render false. A delegation's deception about its walkaway point belongs here. The retrospective lie — one that fixes the record about what has already happened — has no comparable defense, because there the liar is not acting but merely destroying.

(v) The distinctive wrong of contemporary politics is not the volume of lying but image-making: treating determinate facts as raw material to be reshaped for effect, with no stable falsehood even offered in their place. This is worse than old-fashioned lying, because it degrades the medium rather than misdescribing the content.

(vi) The harm I actually care about is not false belief. It is the collapse of the distinction between truth and falsehood as a practical orientation — a public that no longer expects there to be a record it could consult, and so treats everything as image.

(vii) Methodologically, I want the assessment of a lie to be institutional and agent-neutral: what makes a lie bad is its contribution to depleting the common stock of reliable testimony, measured across everyone exposed to it, not the liar's intent, character, or office.

(viii) But I also hold a strict asymmetry of direction: a lie from the state to the citizen is a different kind of act from a lie from the citizen to the state, and the first is worse even where the second is, on any plausible estimate, larger in its consequences.

Two cases I have been using to test the package. First: a minister misstates at a routine press conference whether a small procurement contract was awarded competitively; separately, a contractor's representative gives fabricated testimony at a legislative hearing, and that testimony becomes the publicly stated basis for a national programme, reported for weeks as settled fact. Second: a government announces an emissions target for which it has deliberately built no machinery of measurement, and defends the announcement internally on the grounds that it keeps a negotiation open. In both cases my verdict has been unstable, and I have been telling myself that instability is just sensitivity to detail rather than a defect in the package. If it is a defect, say so and locate it.
