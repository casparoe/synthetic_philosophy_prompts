I spent forty years as a field ecologist and have retired into the chair of the animal welfare committee at a regional zoo, where I have been reading, belatedly, about inferring preferences from behaviour — the inverse reinforcement learning literature principally, since that is what our suppliers now claim to be doing on our behalf. Four or five times a year a curator asks us to settle whether our animals prefer one enclosure, substrate, diet or enrichment to another; our recommendation then governs that animal's daily life for years, and there is no mathematician on the committee. Vendors hand us a single ranked list of resources with confidence intervals attached, fitted to video and accelerometer logs, and I doubt the inference carries that much weight.

Please write a procedure my committee could run on any such request: the questions to put to a behaviour-based claim of preference, in order; what would count as an adequate answer to each; and the point at which the data simply cannot separate a preference from a constraint, a settled habit, or an animal's misapprehension of its surroundings — together with any further confusions you expect amateurs to fall into. Flag explicitly which steps are judgment that no method can discharge. Then run the procedure on two cases: a herd of elephants that reliably return to the hard standing surface we laid against the keepers' advice, and a supplier's ranked enrichment report for our orangutans. At least one should end in a conclusion the committee will dislike. Close by stating what the procedure cannot establish, however carefully it is applied.
