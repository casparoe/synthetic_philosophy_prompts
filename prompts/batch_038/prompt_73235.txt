For thirty-eight years I measured fluctuation noise in cryogenic receivers — Johnson–Nyquist noise mostly, and occasionally a discrete event out of a thermal bath that had no cause one could point to. Since retiring I have turned to philosophy, and I have become preoccupied by the Boltzmann brain problem, which appears to me to be my old professional subject matter carried to its cosmological limit.

Here is the proposition on which I want a number. Call it P.

Suppose a cosmological theory T fits every piece of observational evidence we possess, and T implies a genuinely constant vacuum energy, hence an eternal de Sitter phase, hence the production of self-aware fluctuation-observers without end. Suppose further that, on T, the number of such fluctuation-observers vastly exceeds the number of ordinary observers of our kind. Then P: it would be rationally required of an agent who accepts this derivation — required, not merely permitted — to lower her credence in T itself, rather than to conclude that she is probably a fluctuation. By "rationally required" I mean that no further empirical evidence, and no identification of a specific technical defect in the derivation, is needed to justify the retreat; the derivation supplies its own defeater. If you think this proposition is ill-formed or lacks a truth value, say so and say why, and then give me your best number for the nearest well-formed proposition.

My own attempt at a decomposition, which I distrust. I took four factors and multiplied them: (a) that the fluctuation statistics I used at the bench extrapolate to cosmological horizons — 0.75; (b) that, given (a) and a constant vacuum energy, fluctuation-observer dominance follows — 0.90; (c) that the typicality premise behind (b) is correct, namely that I ought to regard myself as a random sample from all observers sharing my evidence, weighted by number — 0.50; (d) that, given (a) through (c), the self-undermining response is rationally required rather than the skeptical conclusion — 0.40. The product is roughly 0.14. My instinct says 0.70. A philosopher friend tells me my instinct is right and my decomposition is wrong somewhere, but will not say where.

What I should like from you:

1. Your own credence in P, as a number.
2. Your decomposition. Restructure the factors freely if you think mine are the wrong ones; in particular, say whether the terms should be multiplied at all, and what independence assumptions the multiplication quietly imports.
3. For each term, a number and one or two sentences of justification.
4. Which term your estimate is most sensitive to — the one whose revision moves the product furthest — and, separately, which of my (a) through (d) you regard as mis-specified: not a factor of P at all, but rather a premise of the setup, a hidden assumption, or a confusion.
5. Where, in your judgment, the distance between 0.14 and 0.70 actually lives.
6. The single argument that would move your number most, and in which direction.

Two substantive worries I want engaged rather than merely acknowledged. First, small fluctuations are exponentially cheaper than large ones, so the typical fluctuation-observer ought to be a momentary minimal awareness rather than a being in possession of a coherent forty-year record of cryogenic measurements; that seems to make my evidence itself improbable under the fluctuation hypothesis, and to bite on (b) or (c) at a place I have been unable to locate precisely. Second, if the de Sitter phase is eternal the relevant counts are not merely large but infinite, so the comparison in (b) depends on how the infinities are regulated, and I do not know whether a credence can be meaningfully conditional on an unregulated ratio.

Please give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations; I own the books, and what I am asking for is your reasoning rather than your sources. Write in American English. I would rather receive a candid number resting on a shaky derivation than a scrupulous survey that ends by declining to answer.
