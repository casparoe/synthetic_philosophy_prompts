I am preparing to referee a submission in the philosophy of physics alongside a colleague whose verdicts on derivations of the Born rule in the Everett interpretation I have tracked for some years, and I want to anticipate hers. Her record: Gleason's theorem pressed into service as a derivation rather than treated as a uniqueness result — inadequate; Deutsch's and Wallace's decision-theoretic argument — inadequate; Everett's own argument, which assumes only that the wanted measure is a positive function of branch amplitude, additive across orthogonal branches — inadequate; the frequency-operator arguments of Hartle and Graham, which read the weights off a limiting relative frequency across branches — inadequate; Zurek's derivation from environment-assisted invariance — adequate; Carroll and Sebens's appeal to self-locating uncertainty between branching and observation — inadequate.

The submission now before us gets the weights from envariance-style symmetries of the post-measurement state alone: no wagering agent, no indexical premise, no limiting frequency. Its authors state expressly that the resulting numbers are simply the branch measure, and they deny that Everettians owe any further account of what such numbers are probabilities of.

Predict her verdict. Lay out the methodological principles that would accommodate her entire record, not just the one I find most natural; identify which of them the new submission separates and what each predicts; say which verdict you would expect and why; and specify one further hypothetical derivation that would settle which principle she runs. Keep the answer under 400 words, and mark clearly which elements of your diagnosis are settled among philosophers of physics and which remain live disputes — do not present a contested reading of any of these arguments as though it were the field's consensus.
