Working on a novel, and there's a scene I've been circling for a month that I can't get past. Let me give you the setup first and then what I actually want.

Dana Okonjo is superintendent of a small Rust Belt school district. In October an internal investigation confirms that the district's best high school teacher — the guy who's sent more kids to college than anyone in the building's history — has been sleeping with a seventeen-year-old student. Dana is a mandated reporter, the report goes to the state, and she watched a district two counties over get put into emergency receivership over a comparable finding, which in practice meant the state broke the district apart and closed the two schools the poorest kids can actually reach by bus. So she sits on the filing. She moves the teacher into a non-classroom assignment that same week, meets the student's family privately, pays for counseling out of a discretionary fund, and gets his resignation effective in June. Then she files — in a month when the finding is a closed book and the state's own review board has just signed off on her consolidation plan. Nothing happens in the four months in between. Nobody else gets hurt. She knows exactly what she did and she isn't going to pretend otherwise. To herself, anyway.

The scene I need is a year later: Dana and her brother, who teaches political philosophy at a state school and is the only person she's told. Write me the dialogue between them. He holds something like the Walzer-flavored line — that what she did was wrong and stayed wrong even though it was the thing to do, and that she now owes something a clean-handed person doesn't owe. She holds the opposite: that the whole vocabulary is a dodge, that once you've sorted out what her role obligated her to do, which reasons got overridden and what they leave behind, and what guilt is actually for, there's no special category left. Just an ugly ordinary moral conflict that people dress up in noir language because tragic is more flattering than complicated.

Both of them need to be right about some things. No strawmen, nobody scoring off the other with a gotcha, and I don't want them talking past each other — they should pick up the specific thing the other just said and either break it or concede it. Real back and forth, they interrupt, they reload.

Here's the thing I most want them to fight over, because its my idea and I can't tell whether it holds up. Call it the confession asymmetry. In politics the dirty act almost never stops at the act, because the agent usually can't come clean afterwards either: telling the truth later would blow up the very goods the first wrong bought. Dana can't say what she did — not to her board, not to the teacher, not to that family, maybe not ever. So the dirt doesn't sit still, it compounds, and it forces a second wrong that no account of the first one predicts. I want her to press this as hard as she can on him, or rather I want him to press it on her — actually I want both: she should be the one who has to admit she can never confess, and he should be the one who argues that's the real evidence there's something left over. And she should hit back with the obvious objection: is the confession asymmetry a structural feature of political action, or just a very well-built alibi, since the one thing that would test it — going public — is exactly what youve ruled out in advance? He needs an actual answer to that, not a mood or a shrug about tragedy.

Around 1200 words. No speeches longer than a few paragraphs, they're siblings at a kitchen table, not giving papers. Let one of them land something the other can't answer. Then after the dialogue, give me three or four sentences out of character saying who you think was winning and whether the confession asymmetry survived his best shot at it.
