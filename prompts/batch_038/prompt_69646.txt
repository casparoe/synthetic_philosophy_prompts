My co-author and I are drafting a chapter for a handbook volume on the ethics of armed conflict, and we've ground to a halt at the part where we're supposed to say what a theory of combatants' moral standing has to accomplish before it's even eligable to be argued for or against. The trouble is we have different pictures of success and we keep talking past each other. He spent some years as a military lawyer and thinks the non-starter for any account is that it has to be usable by a nineteen-year-old with three weeks of training and no reliable access to information about whether his government's stated casus belli actually holds up — if the theory can't be run by that person in the field, he says it's not a theory of war, it's a theory of a seminar. I think that's smuggling action-guidance in as a criterion and letting it veto the whole enterprise before we've started, but I don't have a principled way of saying why. So I want the spec written out separately from our fight.

Here's the ask: write the desiderata for any adequate account of the moral equality — or inequality — of combatants. Which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it can't collapse, which stubborn facts about war as an institution it can't contradict, and for each one, the case or consideration that motivates it. Then flag every item as either non-negotiable or as something only a particular camp insists on, and name the camp. Among the non-negotiables I specifically want the theory made to face: the conscript fighting for the aggressor state who is personally doing nothing wrong; the pilot or drone operator killing from eight thousand kilometers away with no reciprocal risk; the combatant on the just side who is committing atrocities while pursuing a just cause; the war in which neither side has a just cause at all, so there's no "just combatant" to anchor the analysis; and whatever worse case you think I've forgotten. Finally — and this is the part I actually want — mark any subset of your list that you think is jointly unsatisfiable. If, say, symmetry between the two sides, guidance under severe epistemic poverty, and keeping jus in bello from getting swallowed by jus ad bellum can't all be had together, then our chapter's real thesis is that one of them has to go, and I'd rather say that outright than pretend the list is harmonious.

One thing to build in rather than tack on: my co-author keeps proposing a principle we've been calling the mirror condition — roughly, that for any two combatants facing each other, the theory's verdict on whether A may target B and its verdict on whether B may target A have to come out the same, both permitted or both forbidden, with no mixed cases allowed. He treats it as an axiom; I suspect it's either trivial, or false, or trivial because it's false. Give it a numbered slot on your list, say whether it survives contact with the other desiderata, and tell me what it would have to be weakened to if you want to keep it as a constraint rather than a wish. If you want to close with two or three sentences on how the live options do against the list, go ahead, but don't pad it — the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard. And be upfront about your own uncertainty: which single item on your list you'd drop first under pressure, which one you're quietly motivated to include because you already lean toward the revisionist side or against it, and what argument or case would change your mind about the shape of the whole list — i.e. is there something that would make you say "fine, that wasn't a requirement, that was a prejudice of mine." Where do you think this spec is most likely to be wrong?
