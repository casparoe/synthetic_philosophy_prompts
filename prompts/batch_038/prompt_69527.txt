I'm teaching a constitutional theory seminar, 18 second-years, 80 minutes, and I want them to hit the paradox of self-amendment in their bones instead of hearing me describe it. Plan is a stripped-down Nomic. They start with only three rules, which I'll write on the board: (1) new rules pass by simple majority of those present; (2) this rule may be amended or repealed only by a unanimous vote of everyone present; (3) this rule may not be amended or repealed by any means whatsoever. Each student also gets a seperate secret objective card worth points at the end — things like "get a rule passed fining anyone who speaks out of turn" or "make it permanently impossible to repeal any rule" — and everyone gets only two motions for the whole session, so proposals are scarce. I stay out of it unless someone asks me to rule on validity, and that referee role is itself a rule they can amend.

Build me the whole thing: exact card texts, a timeline, what I say at the start (minimal — nobody should hear the word "paradox" until the debrief), and a debrief that maps specific moments onto (a) hard-to-change versus impossible-to-change, (b) whether a lawmaking power can bind its own future selves, and (c) what it means when they just declare an amendment valid and everyone goes along with it. Then tell me what predictably breaks — I'm bracing for someone defeating the unanimity rule by stepping out of the room — and be straight about which part of the paradox a game like this can't actually reproduce.
