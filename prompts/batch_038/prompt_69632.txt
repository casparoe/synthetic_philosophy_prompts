I sit on a working group at a small liberal arts college that is redesigning the core sequence our students take from first year through to their capstone, and our external consultant has proposed a way of bringing Whitehead into the conversation without anyone having to read him. His claim is that Whitehead's rhythm of education — the three stages of romance, precision, and generalization — is nothing but the Dreyfus model of skill acquisition applied to a curriculum instead of to an individual practitioner. The stages line up one for one with novice, competent, and expert; in both pictures the middle stage is where context-free rules and technique take over from untrained enthusiasm; and in both the endpoint is a fluid, unforced performance that no longer consults the rules. If that is right, he argues, three things follow for us. We can sequence the program linearly, with a romance-heavy first year, precision-focused middle years, and a generalization phase at the end. We can assess it with the five-level competency rubrics our accreditors already require, since those rubrics are simply the Dreyfus stages laid out in table form. And Whitehead can be dropped from the reading list and cited only as a historical anticipator, because the Dreyfus literature is where the empirical and conceptual content actually lives. A colleague of mine in the philosophy department insists this is a category mistake dressed up as a simplification, but she has not been able to say precisely where.

Could you audit the analogy instead of taking either side? Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what in the Dreyfus picture is supposed to play the role of Whiteheadian romance, of precision, of generalization; what corresponds to the context-free rules; what corresponds to the situation the performer responds to; what corresponds to the transition between stages; and what corresponds to expertise as a terminus. Include in that table the question of cardinality — the skill model posits five stages, or six if mastery is counted, and the consultant's gloss compresses them into three, so say whether that compression loses anything load-bearing. Then sort the disanalogies into those that genuinely threaten his three conclusions and those that are only differences of vocabulary or emphasis; I would rather have five problems that bear weight than fifteen that do not. At a minimum, deal with these. Whether the opening stages really correspond, given that the Dreyfusian novice already operates by rules while Whitehead's romance is supposed to precede systematization altogether and to be a matter of interest and appreciation rather than of performance. Whether a rhythm that Whitehead describes as recurring at several scales at once — within a single lesson, within the study of one subject, and across the whole course of an education — can be identified with a model whose stages a given learner passes through once, in order, on the way to expertise, and what that implies for the consultant's linear sequencing. What happens to the alternation of freedom and discipline that Whitehead ties to the three stages, and whether anything in the skill model corresponds to it. Whether the Dreyfus picture has any analogue of the pathology Whitehead is chiefly worried about — ideas that are received and retained but never put to use — or of his insistence that education concerns the utilization of knowledge rather than a level of attained competence. And whether generalization is a stage beyond expertise or a return to romance with technique already in hand, which would matter directly for how we design the capstone. My colleague also suggested, in a way I was not equipped to evaluate, that Whitehead took the same threefold pattern to run through his metaphysics of experience as well, and that this is why the rhythm cannot be reduced to a psychological model of learning; tell me whether that is a real disanalogy with consequences for curriculum design or a piece of mystification we can set aside.

Then be decisive about the payoff. Which of the consultant's three conclusions survives the disanalogies, which fails outright, and which survives only in a weakened form that would have to be rewritten in the proposal? Is there content in Whitehead's account that a competency rubric cannot express, and if so what would that content look like in practice — a scheduling constraint, a form of assessment, a way of managing the transition between stages, a rule about what may not be taught before its time? And if the skill-acquisition model is the wrong source domain, offer a better analogy for the rhythm of education, drawn from something a curriculum committee could actually reason with, and say explicitly where that replacement analogy itself begins to mislead.
