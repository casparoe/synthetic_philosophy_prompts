I am drafting a short paper on the Judy Benjamin problem — the case, introduced by van Fraassen in 1981, in which an agent receives a report about a conditional probability rather than about an outright event — and I would welcome critical comments on the draft below. The draft is self-contained: all figures are my own calculations, not transcriptions from any source. Please treat it as a work in progress and be blunt about where it fails.

**Draft: Sector neutrality and the Judy Benjamin problem**

**1. The case.** Judy is dropped into an area divided into a Red sector and a Blue sector, each of which is subdivided into a second-company area and a headquarters area. Her prior is uniform over the four resulting quadrants; write them RS, RH, BS, BH, each with probability 1/4. A radio message from headquarters reports that the conditional probability of her being in the second-company area, given that she is in the Red sector, is 3/4; her prior conditional probability for that was 1/2. The report therefore constrains only the ratio between RS and RH. It says nothing, on its face, about the Red/Blue margin. Yet nearly every proposed rule for the case moves that margin. That is the puzzle.

**2. Two candidate updates.** (a) *Sector-neutral*: hold P(Red) at 1/2 and hold the internal ratio of the two Blue cells; the constraint then fixes the split within Red. This yields the unique posterior (3/8, 1/8, 1/4, 1/4) for (RS, RH, BS, BH). (b) *Entropy-minimizing*: minimize relative entropy to the prior subject to the constraint. Write t for the posterior probability of Red. The objective is symmetric in BS and BH, so the minimizer keeps them equal at (1−t)/2 each, and the constraint gives t/4·3 and t/4 for RS and RH. The first-order condition reduces to ln(t/(1−t)) = −(ln 2 + ¾ ln ¾ + ¼ ln ¼) = −0.1308, hence t ≈ 0.467.

**3. An invariance lemma.** Suppose the message M is treated as an event on which Judy conditionalizes. Then the posterior odds on Red against Blue equal the prior odds multiplied by P(M|Red)/P(M|Blue), where these are prior-conditional probabilities of M. So P(Red) is unchanged exactly when M is equally expected in either sector. Reading likelihoods off the posterior in 2(a) — legitimate because the prior is uniform — gives (3, 1, 2, 2) up to a common factor: the within-Red ratio is 3:1, as the constraint requires, and the two sector totals are equal at 2. The sector-neutral answer is thus precisely the answer supplied by a message that is neutral between the sectors.

**4. The diagnosis.** The posterior in 2(b) implies likelihoods proportional to (1.402, 0.467, 1.065, 1.065). The within-Red ratio is still 3:1, but P(M|Red) ≈ 0.935 while P(M|Blue) ≈ 1.065, a likelihood ratio of about 0.877. The downward drift in P(Red) is therefore not a harmless artefact of the constraint; it amounts to assuming that the message was somewhat more likely to have been sent had Judy been in the Blue sector. Nothing in the case as described supports that assumption.

**5. When is neutrality correct?** Posit a latent variable θ: the objective distribution of the company across the two Red areas. Judy's position within Red depends on θ; which sector she is in does not, since θ is a fact about Red's geography while her sector is settled by an independent chance. The message is a noisy report of θ. Then M is independent of the sector variable, P(Red) is invariant, and P(second company | Red, M) = E[θ|M], which equals 3/4 if the report is fully reliable. Sector neutrality is not an ad hoc desideratum but a consequence of a structural independence assumption.

**6. Conjecture.** Neutrality fails when the source's access or its incentives depend on where Judy actually is — for instance, a headquarters that broadcasts conditional probabilities only when it has independent reason to think she is in Red. In such a model a shift in P(Red) is legitimate, and its size is fixed by the reliability parameters. If so, the disagreement between the camps is not about which updating rule to adopt but about which generative model of the message to adopt. Distance-minimization rules are silent on that question, which is why their verdicts on the Red/Blue margin are unearned.
