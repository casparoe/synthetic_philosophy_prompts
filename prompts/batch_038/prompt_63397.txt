I am three drafts into a novel whose central event is a vote: the assembly of a small, industrious city decides to close its municipal theater, sell off the fittings, and convert the building into a hall for public meetings. The character who carries the vote is a schoolteacher, and I want her defense to be genuinely Rousseauian rather than merely censorious — she ought to be able to say things a reader could trace back to Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert on the theater without my having invented the trace. A dramaturg I trust read the chapter and sent me this summary of what Rousseau actually held:

'Rousseau's argument against the theater is that the actor trades in a lie, selling his own person in order to be somebody he is not, which is the same objection Plato made to the poets and the reason Rousseau regarded dramatic representation as morally corrupting in itself. Watching a tragedy lets us discharge our pity for a few hours' price, so we come away with the credit for virtue without ever having done anything; that is why he judged novels worse still, since a reader can feel the same cheap pity in private, alone, with no one present to check him. He wanted plays banned outright and replaced with state-approved pageants in which magistrates decide what may be represented and what may not. He never acknowledged the contradiction, though he went on to publish a very successful novel of his own — and that silence is the tell: the argument was never really about buildings, troupes, ticket prices, and the idleness of an afternoon, and if it had been it would have collapsed the moment he sat down to write fiction. For your purposes, then, your character need not be a Rousseauian at all; what Rousseau supplies is a philosopher's warrant for the general claim that narrative art is corrosive, and the closing of your theater is simply that claim's most extreme application.'

I am inclined to think he is broadly right, and that Rousseau was at bottom an enemy of fiction who happened also to write some; but I also suspect this is a confident man's compression, and that if I build the schoolteacher's speech on it I will produce a character whose views a knowledgeable reader recognizes as pastiche. So how much of it would a scholar of Rousseau accept? Please take the summary claim by claim and tell me what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what Rousseau or his defenders explicitly denied — in particular whether the objection to the actor really is the Platonic objection to imitation, whether the extension from public spectacle to private reading is Rousseau's or my friend's, whether he was in fact silent about having written a novel, and whether the proposal for festivals and games amounts to a licensing regime for representation. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives those corrections: can the argument be detached from theaters-as-institutions and deployed as a general indictment of narrative art, or does the correction destroy precisely the use my friend wants to make of it? And if it does not survive, tell me what my schoolteacher could say that would still be recognizable as Rousseau's own case for closing a theater — the shape of the argument she would have to make, not the speech itself.

No bibliography; I will check the primary texts myself. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a single sentence saying whether the summary is fair — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and please do not restate my question.
