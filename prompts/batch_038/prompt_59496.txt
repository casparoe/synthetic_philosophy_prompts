I should say at the outset that my own field is medieval logic, so I may be missing something obvious in what follows; but our faculty affairs committee has been asked to approve a change in the way course releases are allocated, and after a week of turning it over I still cannot say precisely where the trouble lies, which is not a comfortable position for someone who does this for a living. At present the two annual releases are distributed by a rotation the chair administers, which everyone agrees is clumsy and which has generated the usual resentments. The dean proposes to replace it with an internal market. Faculty accumulate service credits — for committee work, for advising graduate students beyond the funded years, for outreach and prison teaching and the like — and each September we submit sealed bids, in credits, for the releases available; the two highest bidders receive them and are debited accordingly. A colleague objected at the first meeting that this will reliably favour senior faculty who have banked credits over two decades, faculty without children or without elderly parents, and those whose teaching loads are already light enough to leave slack for accumulating credits in the first place.

The dean's reply was not that these effects are acceptable but that they are not effects at all, since the mechanism makes no claim about who deserves a release. It asks each of us only to state what a release is worth to us, and whoever states the highest number has thereby shown that it is worth the most to her; on this account there is no further fact about what she values that the bidding could be getting wrong. She added that a critic who calls the outcome unfair owes us an alternative account of the true distribution of how much people care, and that any such account would have to be assembled out of what people report feeling, which is not evidence of anything. Our representative from the economics department, whom I like and generally trust, thinks my residual unease is sentimental: a device that requires people to put their own accumulated resources behind what they say they want is, he argues, about as good an expression of how much they want it as one could reasonably hope for, so the whole objection collapses into a complaint about who starts with how many credits, and has nothing whatever to do with any deeper question about what valuing something consists in.

Three questions, then. Is the dean's argument sound, or is it doing something more interesting than simply being wrong — I have the persistent sense that a substantive and highly contestable claim is being presented to us as a piece of methodological hygiene no reasonable person could refuse, and I cannot pin down the moment the substitution happens. Is my economist colleague right that what bothers me is only the unequal starting distribution of credits, or is there a separate defect in the inference from what someone bids to how much she cares, one that would survive even if every member of the faculty held an identical balance? And if there is such a defect, what could I say on Thursday that engages the proposal on its own terms rather than amounting to a general complaint about economism — ideally something that concedes what the mechanism genuinely does accomplish, since our present rotation is indefensible and I do not want to be cast as its defender.
