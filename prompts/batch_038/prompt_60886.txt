Retired about four years now — I ran a trace-analysis lab for most of my working life — and I've fallen into philosophy fairly hard late, mostly through a Thursday-night discussion group at the public library. Last week somebody brought in the sleeping beauty problem: woman is put under on Sunday having been told the whole protocol, a coin is flipped, she's woken Monday whatever the coin does, woken again Tuesday only if it came up tails, wiped of the memory each time she's put back under, and asked at every waking how confident she is the coin landed heads.

Two of the regulars went at it for forty minutes and I could not referee. Their moves, as best I can reconstruct them:

1. Walt (halved his way in from a career in insurance) said she should say 1/2, because when she wakes up she hasn't learned anything she didn't already know — she knew she'd be woken and asked, whatever the coin did.
2. Naomi (statistics PhD student) said 1/3, because if you run the thing over and over, two out of every three wakings happen in a tails world, and she's in a waking.
3. Walt said that's just counting interviews. Run it a thousand times and the coin comes up heads five hundred times; her question is about the coin, not about how many times somebody bothers to ask her about it.
4. Naomi said fine, settle it with money: offer her even-money bets on heads every time she wakes and at 1/2 she bleeds.
5. Walt said that argument double-counts — a bet placed at every waking pays out twice on the same coin flip, so of course it loses. Settle one bet per experiment instead of one per waking and 1/2 breaks even.
6. Naomi said she can't settle one bet per experiment, because from where she's sitting she doesn't know whether this is her single shot or one of two — which is precisely what's at issue, so Walt is assuming his answer.

Then I stuck my oar in with a modification of my own, and this is where I'd most like you to work. Say the amnesia drug isn't perfect. The experimenters have calibrated it and told her on Sunday: on her first waking in a given run she gets a strong déjà vu feeling 1 time in 10, and on her second waking she gets it 9 times in 10. Nothing else distinguishes the days.

My arithmetic, which I'd like checked rather than trusted: if she wakes and has the déjà vu, the thirder machinery gives 1/11 for heads and Walt's distribution gives 1/6. If she wakes without it, I get roughly 9/19 and 9/14. Two things struck me. First, nobody's saying 1/2 or 1/3 any more, the evidence has swamped the original disagreement. Second, the no-déjà-vu case looks backwards — the thirder lands closer to Walt's 1/2 then the halfer does.

They then disagreed about what my variant proves. Walt said it proves nothing, because there Beauty genuinely learns something new (a fact about which waking she's in), whereas in the original she doesn't, so the two cases aren't comparable and you can't argue from one to the other. Naomi said the original also involves indexical learning of exactly that kind, only weaker, and that my numbers show the whole quarrel is a quarrel about priors over centered hypotheses, which strong enough evidence will wash out.

So: go through all eight moves and rate each one, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence of justification, and say who came out better overall. Then tell me whether I've checked my own sums right, whether the déjà vu version actually decides anything between the two of them, and whether that weird no-déjà-vu result means something or is an artifact of how I set the calibration numbers. No references needed, I'm not writing this up, but if there are names for the moves they made I'd like the names.

Last thing, and I mean it: say what would move you off your verdict — which of those moves, if it's stronger then you first judged, would flip your overall call — and where your own reasoning here is likeliest to be soft. Thirty-five years of arguing with instrument data has left me with a permanent itch about soft spots.
