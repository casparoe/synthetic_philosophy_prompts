For the past nine months I have served on the ethics council of a county behavioral health authority, and for most of that time the council has been deadlocked over the preamble to our revised Consent, Capacity, and Involuntary Evaluation policy. The operational clauses were settled months ago. What remains contested are two framing sentences. Clause 1 defines the conditions the policy covers as mental disorders, glossed as syndromes reflecting dysfunction in psychological, biological, or developmental processes. Clause 4 permits emergency evaluation without consent on the ground that such a disorder has temporarily impaired the person's capacity to judge their own need for care. One member, an attending psychiatrist of twenty-two years, holds that both clauses are simply true and that hedging them would cost patients care they cannot yet ask for. Another member, a peer advocate who was held involuntarily at nineteen and who reads Thomas Szasz as a serious philosopher rather than as a crank, holds that Clause 1 is a metaphor performing covert political work, and that Clause 4 is circular: the diagnosis establishes the incapacity, and the incapacity then authorizes the diagnosis. I have come to believe that on some points they are arguing past each other and on others they are arguing squarely against each other, and that nobody on this council has ever said which is which.

The chair has asked me to bring the document the two of them should have produced together. Please draft it: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed Szaszian and a committed mainstream biological psychiatrist, written as a text both could sign.

To keep the exercise honest, please work through the following stipulated case, which I have constructed from an amalgam of files the council reviewed, and refer back to it wherever a claim needs anchoring:

M.R., twenty-seven, is brought to an emergency department by her roommate after four days without sleep, having spent most of her savings, and telling neighbors she has been given the solution to a problem no one else can see. She acknowledges the sleeplessness and reports feeling better than she ever has. Blood work is unremarkable; she denies substance use; a maternal aunt had a comparable episode at a similar age. She is held under an emergency evaluation. On day five, after two doses of an antipsychotic, she sleeps eleven hours, then asks to be discharged, saying the problem no longer seems important. She declines ongoing treatment, moves out of state, and her family loses contact with her. Eighteen months later she is rehospitalized with a closely similar presentation.

The deliverable should contain the following parts.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — statements both camps can sign. These must be substantive claims about psychiatry, not shared sentiments; "both of us want patients to flourish" is not an admissible entry. I expect at least one claim concerning the diagnostic manual's own concession that no definition fixes precise boundaries for the concept of mental disorder, at least one concerning the difference between diagnostic reliability and diagnostic validity, and at least one concerning the historical expansion and contraction of the diagnostic catalogue. For each agreed claim, note briefly how each side glosses it differently — the agreement should be real but not uniform.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the disagreement stated in neutral language; the formulation the Szaszian would accept as characterizing their own view; the formulation the psychiatrist would accept as characterizing theirs; a triage tag; the test, case, or argument both accept bears on it; and each side's prediction about that test, recorded in advance.

The triage tag is the analytic device I most want applied rigorously. Every disagreement must be assigned to exactly one of three registers: the ontological register (is there a mind-independent kind, or a dysfunction, that the term picks out?); the semantic and pragmatic register (does the term earn its keep by doing work in clinical communication, prediction, and research?); or the political register (should the term license state power over a person who objects?). Where a disagreement dissolves once the registers are separated, say so plainly, identify which register the residue sits in, and supply the stipulation that would end the verbal part of it — for instance, an agreement to treat illness as a term of art whose extension is fixed by clinical utility rather than by lesion. I suspect at least one of their longest-running arguments is verbal and at least one is not, and I want the document to say which.

3. A short section identifying the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one party. For each, name what would count as settling it and what each side predicts. One crux should concern whether M.R.'s response to medication, and its recurrence across populations, is evidence of a common underlying process or evidence only that sedating compounds sedate. Another should concern the eighteen-month interval: what, if anything, persisted in M.R. between episodes, and on what basis either side claims to know. A third should concern Clause 4 specifically — whether the capacity judgment it authorizes can be made independently of the diagnosis it is invoked to support.

4. A falsification ledger: for each side, two findings that would move them toward the other, stated concretely enough that a third party could later check whether they had occurred, and one argument each concedes is the strongest case for the other's position, phrased so the other side would recognize it.

5. An appendix titled Sentences We Could Not Sign, containing three sentences that one party drafted and the other struck, each with the objection recorded. This is where I most need your care: if every sentence in the document is one both would happily endorse, the exercise has failed.

Two constraints. First, do not adjudicate — the document must not covertly resolve the debate, and any place where the balance of argument seems to favor one side should be flagged as an unresolved residual rather than settled. Second, no bibliography, no citations, and no invented studies with named authors, journals, dates, or effect sizes; where an empirical finding matters, describe the design generically, as in a randomized comparison of two dosing strategies. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words of statement plus the tables. I will be reading it alongside the two council members it is meant to represent, and I need each of them to be able to look at their own column and recognize themselves.
