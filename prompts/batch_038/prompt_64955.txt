I write a fortnightly ideas column for a science desk — mostly neuroscience and automation, occasionally something that drifts over into philosophy. Next month's piece is about skill under machines: the pilot who hasn't hand-flown an approach in years, the radiologist who's stopped reading films without the model's opinion, the translator who now only edits. Inevitably the comments section will fill up with people who are all saying sensible things and none of them talking to each other, because they've got different answers to a question that never gets asked out loud: what is it to know how to do something?

So I want a self-sorting sidebar quiz. Ten questions or thereabouts, each one a concrete case, each with two or three options, written so a reader can answer it without any idea of the labels involved — no "do you think knowing-how reduces to knowing-that?", nobody clicks that honestly. Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the positions actually on the table in that debate: Ryle's line that intelligent practice can't rest on prior contemplation (his chess player who plays well but can't state the rules he's obeying is the shape I mean), the intellectualist response that knowing-how is a species of knowing-that picked out by its content, the views that put the ability or the disposition to succeed back at the centre, the hybrids that say it's propositional knowledge plus a way of grasping it, the virtue-epistemology version, and the deflationists who think the whole thing is a fight about grammar.

Build it from the space, not from the list of names. Give me the questions in order with what each one is doing — which cells it rules out and which it splits — then show me the cells, then drop the views in. Any cell with no defender gets marked unnamed plus one line saying why: is it incoherent, is it just unappealing, or has nobody got round to it? I'd rather be told about a live gap than have it smoothed over.

Branch points I specifically want in there:

- whether you can know how to do something you're currently physically unable to do (the paralysed pianist sort of case), and whether the answer changes if she can still describe and judge performances perfectly
- whether someone who has read everything and never touched the thing knows how, versus the old-timer who can't explain a single move she makes — and note who each of those two cases is meant to embarrass
- whether knowing-how comes in degrees and can be lost by inches, or is on/off
- an AI that passes every test you care to set on the task, with an extra option for readers who say it still doesn't know how and are then on the hook to say what's missing. If a leaf lets them off that hook without paying, I want you to say so.
- whether you can lose the know-how and keep every fact, and what that shows about how the two are related
- and a last question that sorts readers on the actual piece: does the experienced professional now working through the machine still have the skill, has she handed it over, or was the skill never the sort of thing anyone could have in the first place?

After the key, tell me which two or three leaves my quiz can't really seperate — the pairs where the answers come out identical and I'd be fooling readers — and what question would break the tie. And since I'll be writing the last paragraph off the back of this: which leaf do you reckon most of my readers land on, and does that leaf give me anything usable to say about the pilots, or does it leave the story where it started?

Finish with one line in exactly this form: Verdict: [name of the leaf] — [usable / not usable].
