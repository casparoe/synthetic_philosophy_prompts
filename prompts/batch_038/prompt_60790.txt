I am preparing a position statement for a workshop on skill and knowledge, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit of the package I intend to defend. My work sits at the interface between epistemology and machine learning, which is why the last two commitments concern artificial agents; please treat them as part of the same system rather than as an afterthought, since my aim is a single account that handles both human and machine skill.

Here is the set I plan to assert:

(1) Intellectualism: to know how to F is to stand in the knowledge relation to content that represents a way for one to F, entertained under a practical mode of presentation. Ryle's knowing-how/knowing-that distinction does not mark a difference in kind among mental states.

(2) The regress argument that Ryle deploys is sound and I take it as a standing constraint on any theory of skill: if every intelligent operation required the agent to contemplate a proposition governing it before acting, no operation could ever get under way.

(3) I accept that quantifying existentially over ways threatens to make knowledge how trivial — on some way of getting to the moon, everyone knows how to get to the moon — but I refuse to defuse this by restricting the quantifier to contextually salient ways. If my account cannot explain the trivialization worry in some other manner, I would rather abandon the account than borrow a contextualist fix that lets ordinary ascription data decide the issue.

(4) Knowing how is a species of knowing, so it inherits the familiar analysis: it is factive, it is incompatible with Gettier-style epistemic luck, and it can be acquired without ever having been exercised. I affirm that someone who has studied hang-gliding only from manuals and instruction, and has never left the ground, knows how to hang-glide.

(5) A practical mode of presentation is not itself a propositional or conceptual item. It is a nonconceptual manner of bearing the relation to the content, and it is precisely what separates the skier's knowledge from the sportswriter's equally accurate knowledge of the same way of skiing.

(6) An agent knows how to F only if it would reliably succeed in F-ing, in normal conditions, if it tried. I read this as a requirement of competence rather than of current opportunity, and I invoke the distinction between an inner competence and the shape and situation required for its exercise — so I maintain that the concert pianist whose hands are paralyzed still knows how to play.

(7) A chess engine that plays at superhuman strength, but whose behavior is fixed entirely by evaluation of positions and weight lookup, with no beliefs and no capacity to assent to anything, does not know how to play chess. I stipulate that it would reliably succeed if it tried, and I still deny the ascription.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Specifically: identify which of these commitments pull against one another and which merely appear to; state each tension as precisely as you can, including which sentence in which commitment does the damage; and order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list. For each genuine tension, give the cheapest revision that restores consistency and say what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Tell me separately whether either of my two case verdicts — the manual-trained hang-glider in (4) and the chess engine in (7) — is one that my other commitments actually entitle me to, and if not, which verdict has to be surrendered and why. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most damage, and by saying whether what remains is a package a person with my ambitions could coherently hold.

Two constraints on your response. Write it in American English. Do not supply references, a bibliography, or a survey of the literature; you may name a position or an author where that is the most economical way to identify it, but the deliverable is an assessment of my own commitments and nothing else.
