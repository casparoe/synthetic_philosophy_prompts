I'm one of nine members of a national commision that reviews how full-time undergraduate study gets paid for, and we've built up about a decade of recommendations that people now quote back at us. The record so far: we voted to abolish tuition at public universities for our own citizens' first degree and to fund it out of general taxation, and in the same session we rejected funding it with a graduate tax instead; we rejected a surcharge on high-earning degrees like medicine and law; we rejected means-testing the fee so that only students from the top fifth of household incomes would pay it; we rejected charging part-time students who work full-time while studying; we voted through a big expansion of grants for rent and food, on the stated grounds that the sticker price of instruction was never what kept people out; we kept the small remaining living-costs loan on income-contingent terms — repay only above an income threshold, index to inflation, no real interest, write off after thirty years; and we rejected wiping the whole outstanding stock of those loans as a windfall to people who'd already been through the system, while approving write-offs for borrowers who took on debt under the old high-fee regime before the change. We also went on record that fairness has to be judged on the whole package, taxing and spending together, not on the spending side alone.

Next month's paper does something we've never had to rule on. It keeps first degrees free for citizens, then charges (a) students from outside the country and the region the full economic cost of their place, and (b) citizens studying a second undergraduate degree half of that cost — with (b) also payable income-contingently, so nobody has to find cash up front — and ring-fences both revenue streams to double the maintenance grant.

Two of my colleagues are certain this is just our old principles applied to new people; two are certain it's the exact thing we rejected three times over. Can you lay out the candidate rules that are actually consistent with the whole record above, say which survive every precedent and which get knocked out by one, and then say what each surviving rule implies for (a) and for (b) separately? After that: what should we vote for maximum self-consistency, which single precedent is the closest analogue to each clause, what principle would we be committing ourselves to with a yes and with a no, and what one further proposal we could put in front of us next year would cleanly separate the rules that are still tied? For what it's worth, the member who usually swings the vote keeps saying the Nordics already do (a) — no fees for their own, full fees for students from outside the EU.
