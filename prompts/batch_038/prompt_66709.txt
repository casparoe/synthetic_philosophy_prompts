So this has been eating at me for about three weeks and I figured I'd lay it all out somewhere. I run the weekend shift at my campus's food pantry, and I'm also on one of those pledges where you commit a fixed percentage of whatever you earn to whichever charities the evaluators rate best. Last month somebody I've worked alongside for two years said — not even unkindly, more like she'd solved a puzzle — that she'd finally figured me out: that I don't actually care about any of the people we help, I just care about being the kind of person who helps, and that those are two different things. I wanted to say something back and I couldn't, and the reason I couldn't is that I'm not sure she's wrong. Then my ethics seminar spent two weeks on moral worth and acting for the right reasons and every single case we discussed felt like it was secretly about me, so now I'm trying to go by gut and see what my gut actually adds up to.

Here are my verdicts, one by one. (1) The guy who never overcharges customers purely because word would get around and he'd lose business: no credit whatsoever, even though the customers get identical treatment. (2) The other shopkeeper who doesn't overcharge because ripping people off just isn't something you do, who doesn't agonize about it and gets no glow from it: full credit. (3) The Huckleberry Finn case, the kid who helps the man escape and is completely convinced the whole time that he's damning himself for it — full credit, maybe more than the easygoing shopkeeper, which bothers me because on his own account he's sinning. (4) Somebody who gives a lot of money to what turns out to be a total scam, having believed every press release and checked nothing, who would have found a real one if they'd spent ten minutes looking: I want to say barely any credit, but the person was trying, and I can't tell if I'm punishing them for being stupid rather than for being bad. (5) The guy who gives the same percentage I do off the same spreadsheet, and tells you honestly that if you asked him about any individual person he's helped he'd feel nothing, only the aggregate moves him: I give him less credit than somebody who gave the same amount because they saw one specific kid's face and couldn't shake it — even though the second person would have walked past the other nine hundred and ninety-nine kids. I know that pairing is bad. I feel it strongly anyway. (6) The person who visits their sick friend every week and, when thanked, explains they came because it was the right thing to do: the visits are great, something's wrong with the visitor. (7) The man who gets his wife out of a car that's going into a river and afterwards explains that he did it because it was his duty, or because anyone decent would have: I don't think he loses any credit, but I also don't want to shake his hand, and I suspect my reaction here isn't about moral worth at all and I can't tell what it is about. (8) My mom, who has been depressed my entire life and still got my brother and me fed and out the door to school every single day, hated every minute of it, and never once mentioned it to us: more credit than the easygoing shopkeeper, more than me, more than anyone else on this list. I have no idea how that squares with giving the shopkeeper full marks for finding it effortless. (9) Somebody who goes vegetarian because their partner did and they trust her, who has never once engaged with a single argument, and who'd follow her off a cliff on most things: right act, and I genuinely dont know what to say about the person.

The rough rule I keep circling is that credit comes from responding to the things that make the act right, rather than from responding to the rightness of the act. That seems to handle the two shopkeepers, the spreadsheet guy, the hospital visitor, and my mom. But it makes the scam donor look great, since he was responding to what he took to be desperate need, and it gives me no way to rank the one-face donor above the calculator when the calculator is clearly responding to far more of the relevant stuff, and I think it's just flatly wrong about Huck, who isn't responding to rightness at all — he thinks he's fighting it.

So: is there a principle, or a small number of rival ones, that gets most of this? I want it stated precisely, clause by clause, with which of my cases is doing the work on each clause and what breaks if you delete the clause — not a vibe, not a paragraph about how rich and context-sensitive the topic is. Then tell me what it says about the two I haven't sorted out, the scam donor and the person deferring to a partner they trust. Then give me one or two cases of your own that you think I'd get wrong and that your principle is committed to, so I can check whether I actually believe the thing. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save — I have a strong suspicion it's either the mom case or the Huck case, and I'd much rather be told which one to give up than watch you contort the theory to keep both. If the honest answer is that I'm running together two separate questions, like whether the act was good versus whether the person is good, or what deserves praise versus what I'd want in a friend, then say so explicitly and show me how the list splits down the middle, because that might actually be what my pantry coworker was getting at.
