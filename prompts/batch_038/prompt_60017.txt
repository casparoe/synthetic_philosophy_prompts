I co-organize an interdisciplinary reading group at a small research institute — roughly a dozen people split between philosophy, sociology, and economics graduate students — and we have just finished our first session on the social construction of kinds. It was a disaster in an instructive way: every single participant used the phrase "socially constructed" at least once, and it turned out that no two of them meant anything like the same thing by it. One meant that the kind's extension depends on what people believe; another meant only that the kind was historically contingent and could have failed to arise; a third meant that the kind is unjust and should be abolished; a fourth used it purely as a term of abuse for "not real." We burned two hours discovering this. Before the next session I want to hand out a pencil-and-paper instrument that sorts each member by what they would actually say about cases, so we can spend the evening arguing about the disagreements we really have rather than the ones we merely think we have.

Please write that instrument. I'd like a quiz of twelve to fourteen questions, each one a short, concrete, self-contained hypothetical with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key at the end. Two constraints on the questions. First, none of them may name a philosopher, a book, or a term of art, and none may ask the reader to endorse a thesis in the abstract — no "do you think race is socially constructed?", no "does money depend on collective acceptance?" The reader should be able to answer every question having never encountered the literature, and should discover their own position only in the key. Second, and this is the design feature I most want: each question should be a domain-neutral template instantiated for two or three of race, gender, and money, so that the reader answers the same case separately in each domain and the key can detect domain-sensitivity. My own suspicion is that most of the group is a realist about money, an anti-realist about race, and confused about gender, and I'd like the instrument to make that visible to them before they announce it.

The questions should be built to probe the axes on which I think the group actually diverges, and I'd like the key organized around them: (a) whether the reader takes the dependence on attitudes to be *causal* or *constitutive* — a good diagnostic is a case in which everyone stops believing, after which some readers should say the kind's effects persist even though nobody accepts it any more, and others should say the kind itself has ceased to exist; (b) whether the kind does genuine explanatory work, supporting generalizations and counterfactuals, or is only a label for a heterogeneous grab-bag; (c) what the reader thinks gets constructed — an idea or classification, an object that could not exist without the classification, or a *subject*, a person whose properties and self-understanding shift in response to being classified, with the further question of whether that responsiveness is what makes human kinds different from, say, chemical ones; (d) whether anything's acceptance or design is needed at all, since there is room for a kind to be fully real without anyone believing in it or intending it, either because of standing patterns of differential treatment or because it emerged from decentralized interaction; (e) how the reader handles borderline cases — is there a fact of the matter about whether a given person or token belongs, and does the answer differ between the three domains; and (f) whether the reader thinks their metaphysics settles the further questions of inevitability, badness, and whether we would be better off reforming or abolishing the kind, or whether those are independent. Please include at least one question whose only job is to catch people who slide between "constructed," "not real," and "bad," and say in the key which slide the question detects.

For the key, build the logical space before you place anyone in it: a grid whose axes are (a) and (b), with the combinations enumerated first, then the actual positions located in the cells. Every occupied cell should be given a name, and I want at least the following to appear somewhere in the grid — eliminativism or error theory about the kind; the view on which the kind is real but exists in virtue of constitutive rules and acceptance; the view on which the kind is real and social yet not attitude-dependent, because it is constituted by structural position and differential treatment; the looping or interactive-kind view; a natural-kind or commodity view, which for money should look like the position that the money thing is just a valuable substance and the convention is incidental; a spontaneous-order view on which nobody designed the kind and nobody's acceptance was required; and a view on which the whole metaphysical question is misplaced and construction talk was evaluative from the start. You may add leaves you think belong and mark any leaf "unnamed" if nobody really defends it. For each cell that comes out empty, diagnose why — is the combination incoherent, or unstable under pressure, or politically unattractive, or merely unexplored? Finally, flag honestly any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate, and say what further question would separate them; note wherever the same template gives a natural answer in one domain and a misleading one in another. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, and please keep the scoring itself doable with a pencil in five minutes — the handout will be photocopied and used cold, so it has to work with me saying nothing at all.
