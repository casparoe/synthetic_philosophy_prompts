I've been sitting on a city reparations task force for about a year now — mid-size place, mostly housing and urban renewal stuff, and mostly we argue about money — and somewhere around month eight I realized my own views are a mess and I can't tell whether they're a mess or just complicated. So I want to try and figure out what principle, if any, actually sits behind my verdicts, because when I list them out they don't look consistent to me and I don't have a good story for why some of the pairs that look identical get opposite answers from me.

Here's the list. (1) In 1954 the city cleared a Black neighborhood by eminent domain to build a hospital; the owners were paid well under market because of how the area got appraised; the hospital is still there and the city still owns the land. I think the descendants of the displaced families are owed something, and I think the number should track what the land is worth now, not what was paid in 1954. (2) Same decade, other side of town: a white family got a federally backed mortgage that a Black family with the same income was turned down for, and their descendants have home equity today they otherwise wouldn't. I do not think those descendants personally owe anybody anything. (3) But a bank that ran the same lending policy and is still open and still profitable — I do think it owes, and not just because "institutions are different from people," rather because it's the same actor that did the thing. (4) Meanwhile the developer who built the covenant subdivision died in 1979 and his heirs inherited the proceeds, and I think the heirs owe nothing even though it is literally the same money. I can see that (3) and (4) pull apart on actor-identity, but then I have no answer for why the city in (1) owes when nobody who voted for the clearance is still alive or still in office. (5) Slavery itself: no parcel to point at, no surviving firm to name, no claimant who would have been born but for it — and I think this is the strongest claim on the list, stronger than the eminent domain one that at least has an address. (6) The $20,000 Congress paid surviving internees in 1988 felt exactly right to me and it never bothered me that some of the recipients were comfortable professionals. But when I picture payments to descendants of enslaved people I instinctively want them collective — housing programs, endowments, that kind of thing — and sort of keyed to who's actually worse off, and I can't say why the same repair-the-wrong logic changes delivery method. (7) The "none of you would exist if the injustice hadn't happened" argument strikes me as a lawyer's trick and doesn't move me at all. But "my family arrived in 1993" does move me, enough that I concede there's no personal claim — while also thinking that person has no standing to object to their taxes paying for it, and I'm not sure those two fit together. (8) A farm taken in 1870, worked continuously since by people with no connection to the taking, bought in good faith in 2005: I feel the claim has basically dissipated. But 1954 is only seventy years ago and I feel no dissipation there whatsoever, so if my rule is "it fades with time" I should be ceding ground in the case I care about most, and I'm not going to. (9) I also say in meetings both that money without an apology is worthless and that an apology without money is insulting.

The principle I keep reaching for is some kind of ledger — a wrong happened, somebody's still holding the gain, nobody's been paid, so the gain gets disgorged to the losers' line — but that's obviously not it, since (5) has no gain-holder I can point to and gets my strongest verdict, and (2) has a gain-holder I can point to and gets no personal claim. So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidate ones, that actually delivers these verdicts? I'd want it stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a stranger and they'd get the same answers I do, with each clause tagged to the case that forces it. Then tell me what it says about three cases I haven't decided: a former colonial power that no longer governs the territory but whose museums still hold objects taken during the occupation and whose courts still apply property law inherited from it; a case where the wrongdoers' descendants are now themselves a persecuted minority, worse off on every measure than the victims' descendants — does the claim survive, shrink, or flip; and a taking committed by a state that no longer exists, whose successor was founded in explicit repudiation of everything that state stood for. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts the best account can't keep — I'd genuinely rather have a simple principle that tells me one of these intuitions is wrong than a baroque one that saves all nine. Which would you drop first, and what does dropping it cost?
