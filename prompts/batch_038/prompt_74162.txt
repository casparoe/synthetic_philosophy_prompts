I am preparing a contribution to a graduate seminar on the ethics of distribution, and the dialectic we have been handed runs in three steps. First, telic egalitarianism holds that equality between people's levels of well-being is intrinsically valuable, so that one outcome can be better than another in some respect simply in virtue of being more equal, even when it is better for no one. Second, the leveling-down objection says this is intolerable: if equality were intrinsically valuable, then a world in which everyone is equally miserable would be to that extent better than a world containing far more well-being unequally distributed, and no one supposes that making everyone worse off could be an improvement of any kind. Third, prioritarianism is offered as the refuge: the moral weight of a benefit depends only on how well off the recipient is, with greater weight the worse off they are, and no weight at all on relations between people's levels. My co-presenter will be defending that third step, and I should like to do more in response than recycle the standard complaints about the priority view's indifference to relations.

Please argue for the following position: the leveling-down objection, even granting that it is sound against telic egalitarianism, does nothing to establish prioritarianism, because the content of the priority view depends on a scale of well-being with a non-arbitrary zero, and no such scale is available. The line of reasoning I have in mind goes roughly as follows. The prioritarian ranks outcomes by summing a strictly increasing, strictly concave function of individual well-being levels. Utilitarianism, at a fixed population, is indifferent to how the unit and the zero of that scale are chosen: multiplying every level by the same positive constant, or adding the same constant to every level, leaves the ordering of outcomes untouched. Prioritarianism is not indifferent to the second of these operations. Take a square-root weighting and two outcomes for two people, in the first of which both are at 10 and in the second of which they are at 19 and 2. The first is ranked higher, 6.32 against 5.77. Now add 100 to every figure, which — if well-being is measurable only up to positive affine transformation — is merely another way of numerically representing exactly the same facts about how well these two lives are going. The ranking reverses: 20.98 against 21.01. On the interval-scale assumption, the priority view therefore assigns two incompatible orderings to one and the same pair of worlds, which is a defect of a quite different order from the one Parfit presses against the egalitarian.

I would like the case developed in full rather than sketched, and I would like it developed as an argument rather than a survey. Specifically:

- State the leveling-down objection precisely enough to keep "better in some respect" clearly distinct from "better all things considered," and identify exactly what the objection must establish in order to make prioritarianism the default rather than one contender among several. Note that the most it obviously yields is that equality cannot be the only value, and explain what further premise is needed to get from there to the priority view.
- Defend the premise that a uniform upward shift is a re-representation and not the description of a different world, and explain why concavity of the weighting function does not by itself deliver invariance. If you think it matters, distinguish re-zeroing from pure rescaling and say which of the two operations does the damage.
- Address at least the following three replies, saying for each whether it fails outright or merely forces a qualification the position can absorb: that hedonism or some similar account of well-being supplies a natural zero; that the prioritarian is entitled to build the choice of scale into the substantive content of the view, so that nothing is ill-defined and the only cost is a demanding metaphysics of well-being; and that if scale-dependence is disqualifying it also threatens every view sensitive to absolute levels, sufficientarian thresholds most obviously, so the argument proves too much to be aimed specifically at the priority view.
- Close by stating the upshot for the choice between the two views. Does the argument merely strip the leveling-down objection of its dialectical force, or does it leave the egalitarian with some positive advantage? In particular, say whether gap-based egalitarian measures fare any better under a uniform shift than weighted sums do, and whether that asymmetry, if it holds, is something my co-presenter could reasonably turn back against me.

You may assume I know the definitions of the views involved, so please spend your words on argument rather than exposition. Be candid about where the position is most vulnerable instead of papering over it, but do not abandon the thesis: I want the strongest sustained case for it. Roughly 1,300 to 1,700 words is what I am aiming at, and there is no need for citations, references, or a bibliography.
