I have been reading Orwell on political language, and his category of meaningless words comes close to a pattern I keep meeting without quite covering it. I want to know whether the difference has a name of its own.

Orwell's picture, as I read it, is of a word drained of content and left signalling nothing but approval or hostility. The cases I have in mind differ in a way that seems systematic rather than incidental. Take a pejorative applied to an economic tendency. Asked what it amounts to, people produce examples, and the examples pull in opposite directions: one endorses central planning, another denounces planning as the very thing in question. Offer a definition and your interlocutor accepts it enthusiastically, then goes on to apply the term to a case the definition plainly excludes — without embarrassment, and without any sense that the meaning has moved. Nor is the term treated as an expression of feeling. It is put forward as a factual claim about its object, and you are invited to examine the evidence yourself. Two colleagues of mine agree on every relevant fact about a proposed policy and still disagree about whether the term applies to it, and both insist they mean the same thing by it. Pressing the question is received as pedantry: not as an objection from an enemy of the term, but as a failure to grasp something everyone else has already grasped.

So the word is neither empty nor merely expressive, and it is not vague in the ordinary way — it survives being pinned down, and the pinning is not resisted but absorbed. The nearest concept I have found is Gallie's essentially contested concepts, but there the parties contest the meaning, whereas here a definition is conceded and then quietly dropped. Does an established concept fit what I have described? If not, name and define it, and tell me where its edges lie: what a term would have to do differently for me to call it a near miss rather than an instance. I want the carving, not just the label.
