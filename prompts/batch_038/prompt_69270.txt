For two years I sat on a university committee that split a fixed budget by having each unit's representative supply utilities over candidate allocations, which we then summed with fixed weights. It produced a defensible number, and it left me unsure that I could explain to my own students what the procedure actually licensed. I am now setting the final for a graduate seminar in social choice and welfare economics, and I want one essay question on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — 20 marks, answerable in 45 minutes — that cannot be passed by restating the theorem or listing its hypotheses.

Please embed in the question a short fictional passage: a classmate's five-line derivation from the theorem to interpersonal comparability of utility levels and to equal social weights. Candidates must say what that passage assumes rather than establishes. The crux I want tested is precise: given that each individual von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function is determined only up to positive affine transformation, which further assumption fixes common units, which fixes common zero points, and which makes the weights equal — and whether the equiprobability route to equal weights supplies any of these independently or smuggles them in.

Then give a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme of five must-hit points with marks allocated, each accompanied by one line naming the wrong turn that costs the point.
