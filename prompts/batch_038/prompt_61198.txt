i sit on the appeals panel for a borough's discretionary support fund, and we've got a case thursday that has split us down the middle. Some background on the scheme, since it's our own pilot and not something you'll have seen written up anywhere.

Eighteen months ago we replaced the old qualifying rule (twelve months continuous residence in the borough, full stop) with what the officers call a membership ledger. Every household accrues points: roughly one per month of residence, points for council tax actually paid, points for hours of registered volunteering logged through the council's own portal, and points for finishing the evening civics-and-English course. The fund unlocks at 40 points.

The appellant arrived 26 months ago, so she's miles clear on residence, but she's sitting on 30 points. The reason is that she's the full-time unpaid carer for her mother, who has advanced MS — so she works maybe eight hours a week when she can (very low council tax contribution), she can't do registered volunteering because she isn't out of the house between eight and six, and she has twice enrolled on the evening course and twice had to drop out when her mother was admitted to hospital. The officer refused her, mechanically and correctly, ten points short.

Half the panel thinks the refusal is obviously unjust: care is contribution, arguably the most socially useful thing on the ledger, and a scheme that scores a man working forty hours a week in a warehouse and paying his council tax above a woman doing the same hours of care for free has misdescribed what reciprocity even is. The other half — including, awkwardly, me on some days — thinks that's precisely the argument the ledger was built to defeat. The old system failed because caseworkers made sympathetic one-off exceptions, which is why outcomes varied wildly by officer and by how well someone could tell their story in a room; the points were supposed to end that. Overturn this on special circumstances and we've rebuilt discretion with extra steps and a spreadsheet. And the fund is capped: every exception is a household jumping a queue ahead of people who did exactly what they were told to do. One of them, arrived the same month as the appellant, works full time, finished the course, and is on 47.

So: draft the memo i'll circulate before the meeting, 800 words ish, headings are fine, British English please — i keep having to convert your spellings before forwarding anything. It needs a bottom line the panel can vote on (uphold / overturn / overturn-and-amend-the-scheme), the reasons for it, and a genuinely strong version of the rule-following side rather than a strawman we knock over in one paragraph.

Two specific things i want in there. First, be explicit about whether the caring objection is a claim that the ledger measures contribution badly, or a claim that contribution is the wrong currency for membership altogether — those have completely different remedies and i'm fairly sure the panel has been arguing past each other for three weeks because nobody's noticed it's conflating them. Second, engage with the wrinkle in the pilot's own terms: the panel may adjust a score "where the published criteria systematically disadvantage a household for reasons outside its control". So we do have a hook, but by its wording it only reaches systematic patterns, not this one household's bad luck. Either use it and say what makes her case systematic rather than merely unlucky, or explain why it doesn't apply and what we should do instead.

No citations, no further reading — nobody on this panel will look at them and i don't want a bibliography padding out the word count. Just the argument.
