So, I'm putting together a graduate seminar for next spring on anti-party thought — Rousseau on factions, the sortition people, a bit of Ostrogorski — and Weil's short pamphlet arguing for the general abolition of political parties is going to be one of the week's texts, set against some Schattschneider so the students get the orthodox political-science answer in the same room. I've taught the Weil before, but always by way of the mysticism and the factory-year material, and I'll admit I've mostly presented the pamphlet to my students as a very intelligent crank's tantrum. This time I want to take it seriously as a piece of institutional design, becuase I think I may have been underselling it and also, maybe, misreading it in a way that matters.

Here's the reconstruction I've been working with, and I'd like you to tell me where it goes wrong. As I read her, the claim is definitional rather than empirical: a party is a machine for manufacturing collective passion; it exerts continuous collective pressure on the minds of its members; and its real end — as opposed to whatever programme it professes — is its own unlimited growth. From which she gets the move I find most striking, that a member confronting any proposal no longer asks whether it is true and whether it is just, but instead asks how the party and its people will regard it, so the criterion of judgement gets silently swapped out; hence parties being totalitarian in embryo, and hence abolition rather than reform. My gloss on all this, and the one I want to defend in the seminar, is that the mechanism doing the work is enforcement: the reason the member asks the wrong question is that they are watched and sanctioned — the whip, the threat of deselection, the ambitions of people who want a ministry. If that's the right gloss, then a pile of fairly ordinary procedural fixes should neutralise the objection and Weil ought to grant it.

Which is what I actually want to put to you, and I suspect you'll tell me I'm wrong. Does her argument survive transplantation into a system where those levers are simply gone — open lists and free votes on the floor, caucus positions settled by secret ballot, no discipline written into standing orders, candidates selected and deselected by local members rather than the central office, public funding so nobody has to chase donors? In that world a legislator cannot be punished for asking whether something is just and then acting on thier answer, so either the critique collapses into a mid-century complaint about Third Republic machine politics, or it's doing something other than what I think it's doing. Two follow-ups while you're in there. First, if the enforcement reading is right, isn't her own constructive proposal — candidates standing as bare individuals with no labels, the voter alone in front of the ballot thinking only about truth and justice — just a weaker dose of the same medicine, which would make her half a proceduralist herself? Second, I've always assumed the fallback she wants once the parties are gone is some purer channel for the popular will, though I couldn't swear she ever says anything of the kind, and something in the way she talks about obligations rather than rights makes me uneasy about that assumption. So: where exactly does my reconstruction break, what would she say to the free-vote-no-discipline hypothetical, and is there a version of her worry that the procedural fixes actually make worse?
