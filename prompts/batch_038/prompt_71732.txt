ok so this is a weird ask but bear with me. my cousin is raising a seed round for what is — and I want to be exact about this because he gets extremely defensive — a St. Petersburg lottery. the game: flip a fair coin until it lands heads, pay out 2^n dollars if heads first shows on flip n. he's selling tickets to it, not to degenerate gamblers but to a regional pension fund, packaged as an "uncorrelated return overlay." the expected value is infinite, he says, therefore any finite ticket price is a bargain, therefore a fiduciary who declines is knowingly leaving free money on the table. he has a slide that says that. he has a law degree and no probability training beyond what he's absorbed from being argued with, and he is much better at arguing than I am, which is why the two straight lectures I've given have accomplished nothing.

my parents are weighing the friends-and-family allocation. I want to send him something funny instead of another lecture.

Can you write me a parody pitch deck for this company — invent the company name and invent a founder, do not use my cousin or me, this has to be sendable rather than an attack — roughly 650-700 words, formatted as slides with two or three bullets apiece plus the speaker notes he'd deliver out loud. maybe eight or nine slides.

the thing I care about most: the humor has to come from getting the mathematics right, not from the founder being a goof in a fleece vest. he should be sharp, articulate, and completely correct about everything except the one thing that matters. so the expected value really does diverge and he should say so cleanly, and then each subsequent slide should quietly concede, in fine print or a footnote or a "risk factors" slide he clearly thinks is a formality, exactly the fact that sinks him. deadpan throughout, no narrator wink, nobody ever says the word fraud.

things I want worked in, in your own words — and please don't have him cite anyone by name or put any real text in quotation marks, he wouldn't and neither should the deck:

- a slide admitting the house can only pay what it actually has, and that once you cap the bankroll the divergent expectation collapses to roughly log2(bankroll) dollars — so with a trillion-dollar backer the fair price is around forty bucks. he should present this as excellent news and a sign of capital discipline.
- an "outcomes distribution" slide that is scrupulously honest: half of all players collect two dollars, seven eighths collect eight or less, and the entire average is being held up by results with probability somewhere around one in a billion.
- one buried acknowledgment that averaging across a million players who each play once is a different operation from following one player who plays a million times, and that the pension fund only gets to live through the second one.
- the diminishing-marginal-utility objection handled in a way that convinces him he's won it: run it with logarithmic utility, the ticket comes out worth about seven ducats, ducats being a legacy unit retained for comparability with prior art.
- a backtest slide where the simulated mean came out finite only because the payoff column stopped growing past about 2 to the 53, presented as empirical confirmation of the thesis.
- a slide noting the fund's own consultant recommends buying zero tickets, which the founder reads as an opening negotiation position.
- the counterparty problem: somebody has to be on the other side of an infinite-expectation bet, and at some point the deck should reveal who that is without ever naming them.

someone who has taught this paradox should laugh and then feel slightly ill.
