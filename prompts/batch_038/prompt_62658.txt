I am enrolled this term in a seminar on the quantitative study of organized violence, and most of our hours have gone into the construction of the standard datasets rather than into results. Three of the construction conventions now look to me less like bookkeeping than like substantive philosophical positions, and I would like a careful reconstruction of what each one commits us to.

First, the counting rule. The best-known conflict datasets identify armed conflict by reference to battle-related deaths. One widely used convention sets the floor at twenty-five battle-related deaths in a calendar year between two parties, at least one of which is a state; another reserves the term war for episodes that reach a thousand battle-related fatalities within twelve months. Only deaths caused by combat operations are counted. Deaths from the famine, epidemic disease, and breakdown of medical care that a war brings in its wake — which in a number of conflicts have outnumbered the battle deaths themselves — fall outside the count by definition.

Second, the denominator. Claims about the long-run trend are ordinarily expressed per capita, as battle deaths per million of world population, partly because the global population has grown several-fold since these series begin in the early nineteenth century.

Third, the unit of analysis. Everything is indexed to episodes with beginnings and ends, so the headline quantities are the number of conflicts active in a given year and the deaths those conflicts produce.

What conception of violence does this apparatus presuppose, and which rival conceptions does it rule out by construction rather than by argument? A few specific puzzles I cannot resolve on my own:

Does counting combat-caused deaths only presuppose that harm requires a striking agent, and that the distinction between doing and allowing carries genuine moral weight? I keep noticing that the same literature is perfectly willing to explain falling numbers by diffuse, indirect causes — commercial interdependence, administrative capacity, shifting norms — while refusing to admit indirectly caused deaths into the measure. Is that an inconsistency, or is there a defensible asymmetry between the causal claims we make about trends and the causal claims we permit into the numbers?

Does per-capita normalization presuppose that what matters is each person's risk of being killed rather than the total amount of killing, and hence a particular way of aggregating harms across persons? What would have to change if one held that the wrongness of large-scale killing does not scale linearly with the number killed — that the destruction of a city is something other than the sum of its individual murders?

Does defining war by a minimum death toll presuppose that violence is a kind with sharp boundaries, and that the morally significant unit is the episode? A strategy that split one large war into a hundred sub-threshold campaigns would appear in the data as the disappearance of war.

Now suppose a classmate proposed replacing realized deaths with imposed risk: violence measured as the probability of death that a party's conduct imposed on an identifiable population, whether or not anyone in fact died. A standoff resolved by deterrence would then register as violence inflicted rather than violence avoided, and a siege lifted after surrender, before anyone starved, would register as fully violent. What would that measure presuppose, and does the choice between it and the existing conventions quietly settle a question in moral philosophy — whether what a course of conduct produces is part of the description of the conduct itself — that the datasets otherwise profess to leave open? Can either measure be called empirical in a sense in which the other cannot?

Finally, and most importantly for my purposes: is there a fact of the matter about whether violence is declining, independent of the measure, or is the trend claim partly fixed by the operationalization, such that the world is becoming less violent relative to one construction and more violent relative to another? Please distinguish the cases in which rival constructions genuinely disagree about the world from those in which they describe the same world in different vocabularies; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, since I suspect several incompatible theories of violence could license the same codebook; and tell me what would have to change, both in dataset construction and in the public use of these numbers, were the presupposed conception replaced by one of its rivals.
