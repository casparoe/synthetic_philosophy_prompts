You are a philosopher of rational choice who also works on the mathematics of repeated gambles: someone equally at home with the normative debate about what makes a decision rational and with the arithmetic of compounding outcomes. Please work through the situation below carefully before answering. If it helps, begin by sketching, for yourself, the two or three different readings of my colleague's argument, and where each of them goes right or wrong; then commit to one in what you write to me. I am not looking for references, further reading, or a survey of positions. I want your reasoning, and I would rather have a definite answer that might be wrong than a balanced answer that is useless.

Here is the situation. It is a practical one, though I have come to suspect it is not only practical.

I run a small independent press that publishes literary translations. Every year I face the same choice. I can spend the year on our safe list — reissues, steady sellers, backlist maintenance — which grows the press's capital by about three percent, reliably, every year without fail. Or I can spend it chasing the rights to difficult, out-of-print authors: a flop costs us roughly forty percent of our capital, but when one of them lands it roughly two and a half times it. By my count, the difficult books land about one year in three. I have always treated it as all or nothing — the whole year goes one way or the other — though I suppose nothing forces that.

For nine years I took the difficult book. It landed three times and flopped six. My colleague points out, correctly as far as I can tell, that my average yearly result across that stretch was about twenty-three percent, against three percent for the safe list, and that the policy I chose was therefore the better one and I have simply been unlucky. He says I should not judge a policy by a single run of it; that if I keep making the same choice often enough the odds will take care of me; and that in the relevant sense there are a thousand versions of me running this press in parallel, and across them I am doing very well indeed. I can only live this one, in sequence.

Meanwhile our capital is about three quarters of what it was when I started, we have drawn on the bank line twice, and the bank has told me that if capital falls below a certain floor it will not renew the line, at which point the press closes. I cannot borrow beyond it. Had I taken the safe list all nine years, we would be roughly thirty percent larger than we started. I am sixty-one; in ten years my daughter takes the press over, and she inherits whatever is left standing.

Please address the following.

1. Was the policy I chose the better one? Show me the arithmetic that settles it, and state explicitly what each of us is counting when we describe the very same nine years in such different terms. Then give me the shortest argument I could put to him that does not depend on his sharing my values.

2. Was there a version of this choice I should have been making instead — something other than all-in or all-out? If so, give me a rule I could actually apply in January, with a number in it, and tell me how much it costs me to be wrong about that number in each direction. Are the two directions equally dangerous?

3. My figure of one year in three is estimated from those same nine years and from nothing else. Does that change the rule in part 2, and if so, which way, and why?

4. Does a rule that is built for an indefinitely long sequence still govern a sequence with ten rounds left and a handover at the end? And separately: does the bank's floor change what the best policy is, or only how much margin I have to keep around it? I have an intuition that it does more than the second, but I cannot defend it.

5. Someone told me that everything in parts 1 and 2 is no discovery at all — that a person who cares about the long run and has the right attitude toward risk arrives at the same answer by ordinary expected-value reasoning, so the only real question between my colleague and me is what our attitudes to risk are. Someone else told me the gap between us is not about attitudes at all, but a mistake in how he is counting, one that a change of attitude cannot repair. Which is right? This matters to me more than it probably should, because it decides whether my colleague and I are having a disagreement about mathematics or about temperament.

6. Finally, and please answer this one honestly rather than diplomatically: I do not experience the press as a sum of money. Some of my best years were flops, because a book came out that I am glad exists and that nobody else would have published. Does that mean the entire way of scoring the question — in terms of how capital grows — is the wrong way to score it? Or is it a way of refusing to look at a real cost that the difficult books impose on the people who depend on the press? Tell me which, and tell me how you would decide between them if you were in my chair in January.
