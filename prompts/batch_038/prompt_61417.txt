Consider a practice in health law that puzzles me. A competent adult can execute a psychiatric advance directive — a so-called Ulysses clause — pre-authorizing treatment she expects to refuse during a future manic or psychotic episode, and instructing clinicians to disregard whatever refusals she voices at the time. Many jurisdictions treat such a directive as binding on the treating team over the patient's contemporaneous objections. Yet the same body of law generally allows a directive to be revoked at any time and in any manner by a patient who has capacity, and where capacity is unclear clinicians are commonly told to err toward present wishes. Adjacent to this, commercial law prices a change of mind instead: early-termination fees, non-refundable deposits, surrender charges on annuities.

What view of agency, and of consistency across time, does the directive practice presuppose? Must honoring a Ulysses clause commit us to treating the patient as one temporally extended agent whose good is fixed by her earlier, considered judgment — so that a later reversal is a mistake to be corrected rather than fresh information to be respected? Which rival accounts does that rule out: well-being accruing to stages or moments rather than to whole lives, or the later, ill self as in some sense a different person? Is the free-revocation rule consistent with the same account, or does it quietly reintroduce the rival? And bearing in mind the familiar argument that an agent who can reverse her own prior choices at no cost can be led around a cycle of trades and drained, is the law's asymmetry between the patient and the contractor a philosophical inconsistency, or a difference in what each institution is for?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, identify where several competing theories would license the same rules, and say what would have to change in clinical practice if we adopted a stage-relative view of welfare.
