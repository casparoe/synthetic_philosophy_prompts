I'm helping to convene a reading group on reparations for historical injustice, and I've been asked to prepare a two-page handout for next Thursday's session. In the last meeting, a member of the group who reads a lot of corporate law pressed the following analogy, and it carried the room. A nation, he said, is like a going concern: a firm whose management turns over, whose shareholders come and go, and whose name may even change, but which remains one and the same entity, answerable on the contracts its predecessors signed. Nobody thinks it is a defense for a board to say that its members were not in office when the loan was taken out; the firm pays out of its assets, and the current shareholders bear the loss in the value of what they hold. He then concluded that the most common objection to reparations — that nobody alive today enslaved anyone, so nobody alive today owes anything — is simply a confusion about who the debtor is. The debtor was never the individual citizen; it was and is the polity, and citizens stand to it as shareholders stand to a firm, absorbing its liabilities along with its assets.

I don't want a verdict on reparations from you, and I don't want a list of books or articles — the handout has to stand on its own for people without access to a library. What I want is an audit of the analogy. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly, ideally as a compact table: what in the firm plays the role of the polity, and what plays the roles of the charter or constitutive documents, the shareholders, the management, the firm's retained assets, the creditor, the legal machinery that makes continuity determinate, and the mechanism by which liability reaches the members. Then sort the disanalogies into two piles — those that actually threaten the inference he wants to draw, and those that are merely decorative — and say why each belongs where it does. I'm especially interested in candidates like these, though I don't know which of them matter: that corporate membership is purchased and can be exited by selling, whereas citizenship is largely involuntary and exit is costly; that a shareholder's exposure is capped and proportional to a holding, while a citizen's is not; that a firm's obligations are discharged from a pool of assets under a legal system that will enforce them, whereas in the interstate case there is no such enforcer; that the original creditor is dead, and it is not obvious who holds the claim now, since a claim in contract does not simply pass to descendants the way a debt owed to a firm persists; that a firm's identity is a matter of registration and can be adjudicated on a Tuesday, while a polity's continuity across regime change, civil war, and territorial reorganization is a substantive political question rather than a fact about a register; and that the benefits of the historical wrong are not, like cash on the books, traceable and retained, but diffused into everything and therefore into nothing in particular.

Finally, tell me what the analogy quietly imports that the target case lacks — in particular, whether it assumes that the debt is one of restitution of identifiable gains rather than compensation for harm, and whether it assumes a legal framework that makes obligations transferable in the first place — and then judge whether the conclusion survives once the damaging disanalogies are on the table. Does it license a duty to pay money to living descendants, or only a duty to disgorge gains that can be traced, or only some weaker acknowledgment? And if the firm is the wrong vehicle, sketch what a better one would be: something like a trust with successive beneficiaries, an unlimited-liability partnership, a household that passes on both its property and its name, or a river held to be the same river despite the replacement of its water — and say what each of those would license or block that the corporation does not. Please keep the whole thing to 900 words maximum, table included, and put your bottom line about whether the analogy can bear the weight he put on it in the first three sentences.
