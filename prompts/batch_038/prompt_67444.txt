I'm putting together a small pamphlet of poems about philosophy of mind for our reading group and I need one on derived versus original intentionality, but I want Dennett's two-bitser doing the work rather than the Chinese Room — everyones written a Chinese Room poem. Could you write a ghazal, five to seven couplets, in the voice of the two-bitser itself: that four-state contraption whose states mean whatever they've been hooked up to mean, and which can be reassigned to mean something else entirely without anything changing inside it?

The couplets should stand alone the way ghazal couplets do, held together only by a refrain that returns at the end of each second line, and that refrain has to be the part the device doesn't contain — the reading somebody gives it. So no couplet should strictly need the refrain to make sense on its own; the form is supposed to enact the fact that the aboutness is bolted on from outside. I'd also like the ambiguity to be load-bearing rather than decorative: the same state can be taken two ways and theres no further fact about the device that settles which. Then the closing couplet, where the speaker names itself, should be the one place Searle gets a foothold — something has to be doing the reading — without the poem sliding into a refutation of Dennett. Leave it a bit uncomfortable. Please don't put any real lines from either of them in quotation marks, and write it in British English.
