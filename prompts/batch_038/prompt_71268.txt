Context first: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy or econ beyond one intro micro class ten years ago, and I've spent the last six months building the chargeback system for our shared GPU cluster. Five product teams share it. The old system billed raw GPU-hours, which made the team running nightly batch training look wildly expensive and the team doing latency-sensitive serving look cheap, and it started a fight every quarter. I went looking for a principled split and ended up implementing the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, mostly because I could explain it to a PM without math: you compute each team's fallback (what it would get if the shared cluster broke up and it had to provision on its own) and each team's ceiling (what it would get if it had the entire cluster to itself), then you pick the allocation where every team has traveled the same fraction of the distance from its own fallback to its own ceiling. The thing that sold me on it over the Nash version is a monotonicity property — if we add capacity in a way that only raises one team's ceiling, that team can't come out worse off — and I knowingly gave up the "irrelevant alternatives" condition to get it, which I expect the market designer in the room to notice.

Two implementation details that are probably going to matter. The feasible set isn't actually smooth: nodes come in chunks of eight GPUs, so I compute the KS point and then round to whole nodes. And I estimate each team's ceiling by benchmarking it alone on the full cluster over a weekend, using throughput-to-value numbers the teams report to me. Also worth knowing, though nobody has said it out loud yet: my own team is the largest tenant, and I'm the one who wrote the benchmark harness.

Thursday I present it to the Capacity & Chargeback Committee to get it adopted. The committee is two economists — one does market design and is a Nash bargaining partisan, the other came over from finance and doesn't believe you can compare one team's utility to another's at all — our FinOps controller, who has to turn this into actual invoices and hates any number that moves month to month, three engineering directors whose teams pay the bill, and an SRE lead who mostly cares whether the thing survives capacity fluctuations and spot pricing. Nobody has read anything, theres no paper to hand around, just the design doc I wrote, and I couldnt cite my way out of a corner if I tried.
