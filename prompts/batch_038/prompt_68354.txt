Please take on the role of a philosopher of history who is also fluent in how climate scientists now do event attribution, and who is willing to write for an attentive reader with no academic training. Think carefully before answering. I would like you to begin with a short brainstorming pass — a list of the candidate diagnoses you consider, and for each the single consideration that would tell most strongly for or against it — and only then commit to a view and argue for it.

For about six years I have been part of a monthly discussion group that works through popular history books and documentaries. No one in it is a professional anything; I run a small print shop. What has come to trouble me is that the group divides in exactly the same way every time a work attributes some large human outcome to a non-human cause: the eruption of Tambora in 1815 and the harvest failures of the year that followed; the plague of the fourteenth century and the loosening of serfdom in England; a prolonged cooling stretch and the migrations it supposedly pushed along. After several cycles I have concluded that neither camp is gaining ground and that the arguments themselves have stopped changing.

The first camp, which dislikes being called environmentalist but has no better name, says that human affairs have always been conducted inside an environment that pushes back; that ignoring ice cores, tree rings, sediment, and ancient DNA is a kind of species narcissism; and that when they say the drought caused the collapse they mean something perfectly ordinary — the same thing anyone means who says the flood took out the bridge.

The second camp says this is not explanation but substitution. History, they argue, is about what people did and why; a volcano has no intentions and cannot be an actor; a drought does not depose a dynasty, hungry and frightened people do; and promoting the environment to the grammatical subject quietly licenses the oldest and ugliest inference in the subject, namely that some peoples' fates were settled by their geography before they had any say. They add a selection complaint: for every society that comes apart during a bad climatic stretch there is another that endured the same stretch and left no dramatic story to tell.

Nothing here turns on disputed evidence. Nobody in the room doubts the eruption, the temperature reconstruction, or the genetic findings about the plague bacterium. Yet nothing moves, and I have begun to suspect the group is not having one argument.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that we are asserting three different claims inside one sentence. First, sufficiency: without the environmental event, the outcome would not have happened. Second, odds-shifting: with it, the outcome became much likelier, though neither certain nor necessarily unavoidable. Third, significance-fixing: the environmental condition is what set the stakes, narrowed the options, and gave the human choices their shape. My sense is that the first camp is usually asserting the second claim, the second camp hears the first, and both actually believe the third is what history is for — so the word "caused" slides among them without anyone catching it.

With that in mind:

1. Is my three-way split correct? Amend, merge, or reject it as you see fit, and tell me plainly whether it is doing real diagnostic work or is merely decoration.

2. What kind of disagreement is this — verbal, substantive, or several distinct questions travelling under one label? Specifically, when the second camp insists that a volcano cannot be an actor, is that a conceptual claim about explanation, a methodological rule about where to look for evidence, or a moral objection to determinism dressed as a point about causes?

3. Is a hidden empirical question doing the work? Attribution science produces statements of roughly this form: an event of this type was made several times more likely by this forcing, together with a separate figure for how much of the risk was present anyway. Could anything structurally similar be applied to a historical case, given that the past cannot be rerun and the records are patchy and unevenly distributed across regions and classes? If yes, describe what the historical analogue of that calculation would concretely consist of and identify where it breaks. If no, explain why the disanalogy is fatal rather than merely inconvenient.

4. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction the group could adopt, a reformulation of the question, an empirical or formal result, a discipline about how sentences must be written — or should I expect none? If none is available, I want the argument for that conclusion, not a shrug.

Please ground the diagnosis in what the two camps actually say as I have reported them, not in a general survey of the field.
