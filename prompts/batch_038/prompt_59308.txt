I've been asked to write the framing chapter for a handbook section on how public investment agencies should set stop rules — the thresholds at which a partially funded project gets terminated rather than refinanced — and before I wade into the literatures that all claim this terrain (organizational behavior, auction theory, behavioral economics, evolutionary biology), I want the specification rather than the candidates. So: what would a theory of escalation of commitment have to do to count as adequate, where adequate means good enough that an institution could rely on it when deciding whether to cap losses by rule, move decision rights to a fresh party, require an up-front precommitment, or simply trust experienced managers to keep going? Give me the requirements one at a time, each with the case or consideration that motivates it: the phenomena it must explain, the situations it must get right, the distinctions it may not collapse, and the results — experimental, strategic, and comparative — that it cannot contradict.

So you can see where I'm coming from, here are the things I'd expect the list to be built around, and I want you to tell me which of them are genuine requirements and which are the prejudices of one camp wearing a requirement's clothes:

- The asymmetry between entry and continuation in Shubik's dollar auction. Entering at the minimum bid looks like a bargain; every later raise looks locally right to the trailing bidder, who converts a certain loss into a chance to come out ahead; and the sequence is jointly ruinous. A theory must say where the error is located — in the entry decision, in the continuation rule, or in the assumption that both must be assessed by the same standard at all.
- Stationarity. In the standard mixed-strategy analysis the probability that the trailing bidder keeps going does not depend on how deep the bidding already is, precisely because past bids are sunk. The ordinary description of escalation is the opposite: it accelerates. Either the theory explains the acceleration, or it explains the intuition as an artifact of how cases get selected for retelling — and I want to know which.
- Asymmetry of position. The trailer, not the leader, is the one with the structural motive to continue, since folding forfeits the bid while leading allows a graceful exit. An account that predicts symmetric escalation has to say what it is capturing that the payoff structure misses.
- Finite bankrolls. With a hard ceiling on what either side can lose, the trap looks as though it should unravel by backward induction; the online bidding-fee ("penny") auctions that had exactly such ceilings, plus automated bidding agents, still ran episodes in which the fees paid for an item exceeded its retail value. What work does a cap do, and what does it leave untouched?
- The psychological regularities. Continuation rises with the size of the sunk investment, with personal responsibility for the initial decision, and with public accountability for it; it falls when the continuation decision is handed to someone who did not make the original choice; and it does not reliably fall when people are warned about the trap in advance or when they are experienced and paid for accuracy.
- The comparative datum that non-human animals show the same pattern, which some read as evidence that it is adaptive rather than an error — and the question of whether a theory of human escalation owes an account of that or may treat it as beside the point.
- The near neighbors it must separate rather than absorb: perseverance that is a correct response to genuinely new information; paying to keep an option alive when present expected value is negative but the option is not; overbidding as an investment in a reputation for never folding in a repeated game; contractual or treaty lock-in; and, hardest of all, holding to a plan one adopted earlier in the resolute-choice sense, where the observable behavior is indistinguishable from escalation but where we want to call it integrity. I suspect this last one is where the real work is, because it exposes a criterion problem: if escalation is defined as persistence that turned out badly, the theory is unfalsifiable, and if it is defined ex ante, we need to be told in terms of what.
- The reference-point worry. Any account built on loss aversion or anticipated regret must be able to say what fixes the reference point independently of the behavior it is meant to explain, or it can absorb every outcome after the fact.

Format, and the part I most want: for each requirement, a sentence or two of motivating case, then a mark — non-negotiable, meaning that a theory failing it is not a theory of this phenomenon; negotiable but costly, meaning it can be given up at a price you should state; or camp-specific, meaning one literature demands it and another reasonably refuses, in which case say which camp and why the demand is a prejudice. Then, if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and show the conflict. My own guess is that Bayesian updating on the information actually available to the decision maker, consequentialist preferences over final outcomes (no utility from consistency, self-justification, or completion as such), and the observed sensitivity of continuation to sunk-cost magnitude, responsibility, and accountability with objective prospects held fixed cannot all stand together — and I would like to be told which of the three a good theory gives up and what it costs. Name the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of otherwise respectable candidates. Close with a short scorecard, a few lines each, for the three families I expect to be reading: escalation as equilibrium strategic behavior, escalation as a preference or framing distortion, and escalation as an institutional and agency phenomenon turning on who decides, who is blamed, and who pays. No references and no reading list, please — I want the specification, not the bibliography — and where a requirement rests on an empirical claim you are not confident about, flag the uncertainty rather than smoothing it over.
