I sit as one of three lay members on the Governance and Standards Committee of a unitary authority in the north of England, population roughly 310,000. The committee has eleven members: five councillors, three lay members including me, the monitoring officer, and two academics co-opted for this particular piece of work. We have spent a year designing a permanent citizens' chamber — sixty residents drawn by lot from the electoral register, with a supplementary draw from GP-registration data to reach people who are not on the register at all, then weighted so that the chamber broadly mirrors the borough on age, tenure, ethnicity, ward and whether there are children in the household. It would meet four times a year, be paid for its time, and be supported by a secretariat drawn from our own officer staff. Nobody on the committee now doubts that the chamber itself can be assembled fairly; what we must settle at our meeting on 14 March, and what full council will be asked to accept in June, is how much authority it is to have.

Three options are live and the committee is split roughly three ways. (a) Advisory only: the chamber makes recommendations, the council must publish a reasoned response within ninety days, and that is the end of the matter. (b) A suspensive power: the chamber may return any council decision within its remit to the council once, with written reasons; if the council re-passes it unchanged on a simple majority it stands, but if the chamber returns it a second time the decision lapses unless re-passed by two-thirds. (c) Concurrency: over a defined list of matters — libraries and leisure, the allocation of housing sites in the local plan, the active travel network — the chamber's recommendation becomes council policy automatically unless the council rejects it by two-thirds within sixty days. Two things constrain the choice. First, our monitoring officer advises that the council cannot lawfully bind itself irrevocably in advance, since that would fetter the discretion of future councils, so any binding effect has to be built as a procedural requirement about how decisions are taken, reasoned and recorded, rather than as a transfer of decision-making power; she adds that the chamber can have no role whatever in setting the precept or in matters where we are under a statutory duty, such as setting a balanced budget or discharging safeguarding functions. Second, and this is what has actually deadlocked us: under all three options the chamber's agenda is set by the council. Officers draft the question, officers compile the briefing pack, officers decide which matters get referred. One of the co-opted academics argues that this makes even option (c) a check controlled by the party being checked, and has tabled an amendment under which the chamber may call in any council decision on its own motion, or take up a topic on a petition of three thousand residents — which would give it genuine agenda-setting power but would also, as he concedes, make it a rival to the elected chamber rather than a supplement to it. The campaign group briefing councillors against the whole scheme takes the opposite view: they say the chamber is a fig leaf, that authorities commission assemblies when they already know the answer they want, that officers will write the brief so that the recommendation writes itself, and that the only honest options are (a) with the council committing in advance and in public to which recommendations it will and will not accept, or no chamber at all. They have a point about our own record: the one-off assembly we ran on the high street two years ago was set a question drafted by the cabinet member whose policy it duly endorsed.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to all eleven members a week before the meeting. It should recommend one of the three options, with or without the agenda-setting amendment, and it should be decisive — the council cannot adopt all of them. Beyond the recommendation I need: the criteria by which the chamber's powers should be judged at all, so that the two factions who lose can respond to reasons rather than to a preference; an honest account of how far a purely procedural requirement can do the work of a substantive power given the fettering-discretion advice, without collapsing back into advisory-only or forward into something the council cannot lawfully promise; a definite position on the amendment, including what happens if the chamber and the council each claim to be responding to the same complaint from the same residents; and a serious treatment of the fig-leaf objection, engaging it at its strongest rather than noting that we will of course write balanced briefings. Two smaller points should be dealt with in passing: whether the whole arrangement needs a sunset clause and on what terms it could be withdrawn without making the chamber's independence notional, and what the chamber's remit should be where a blocking decision would leave us unable to meet a statutory duty. I am a former schoolteacher, not a constitutional lawyer, so please write in plain institutional prose of about 1,000 to 1,300 words, in my voice as a lay member, with no footnotes, no bibliography and no reliance on named theorists as authority — use the arguments rather than the citations, and refer to real-world assemblies only briefly and only where you are confident of the facts. Close with the specific motions the committee should be asked to vote on, in the form they would appear in the minutes, and write the whole thing in British English.
