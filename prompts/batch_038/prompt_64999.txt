I hold a chair in practical philosophy, but before turning to philosophy I spent the better part of a decade as a health economist: building decision-analytic models, negotiating endpoints with clinicians, and writing cost-effectiveness dossiers for reimbursement agencies. That vocabulary remains more native to me than the vocabulary of normative ethics, which I now teach, and I find that it distorts my reading of the debate over procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics in ways I cannot quite pin down. I would like you to perform the translation deliberately, so that I can see where it breaks.

Render that debate into the working vocabulary of health technology assessment, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report.

The material to be translated: Savulescu's original argument for procreative beneficence in Bioethics (2001) — the claim that where a couple can have any of several possible children, they have a significant moral reason to select the one expected to have the best life; the later formulation with Kahane (2009) in terms of the best chance of the best life and expected well-being; the work the non-identity problem does in making that reason impersonal rather than owed to any identifiable person; Habermas's objection in The Future of Human Nature that irreversible genetic programming by another person upsets the symmetry obtaining between persons who recognize one another as free and equal; the disability critique's insistence that any ranking of possible lives presupposes a contested account of what makes a life go worse; and the enhancement–treatment distinction, which liberal eugenicists variously preserve and dissolve.

The target vocabulary is the one I actually use: comparators and the choice of comparator arm; incremental versus average effectiveness; clinical versus surrogate endpoints; composite endpoints and the weights implicit in them; quality-adjusted life years and the minimally important difference; incremental cost-effectiveness ratios and willingness-to-pay thresholds; time horizon and discounting; intention-to-treat versus per-protocol analysis; probabilistic sensitivity analysis; value of information; distributional cost-effectiveness analysis with equity weights.

Three rules for the exercise. First, do not smuggle. It is easy to render procreative beneficence as the selection of the embryo with the greatest expected QALYs and call that a translation; each time you produce a rendering of that kind, say explicitly whether it preserves the content of the claim or substitutes a neighbouring claim that my framework happens to be able to express. Second, attend to structure rather than surface. What interests me most is this: in HTA the population is fixed before the analysis begins — the comparator arm, the denominator, the identity of the beneficiary are all settled by the protocol — whereas in procreative beneficence the identity of the beneficiary depends on the very choice under evaluation. If my framework contains any analogue for an evaluation whose subject is constituted by the decision, name it and say whether the analogy preserves or destroys the impersonality of the reason; if it contains none, say so plainly rather than reaching for the nearest loose fit. Third, be concrete about at least three technical loci where you expect failure: how the choice of time horizon interacts with a comparison of whole lives; whether reading the best-chance formulation as an expectation commits its defenders to a risk neutrality they may not want; and whether distributional cost-effectiveness analysis with equity weights offers a better target for the view than any scalar maximand does.

Then the residue report. Take the six items above one at a time and classify each as translating cleanly, translating only with a distortion you specify, or having no expression in the target vocabulary at all. Finally, adjudicate. To what extent does the residue indict my framework — is HTA structurally incapable of registering claims about who will exist because it is a decision tool rather than a theory of value? And to what extent does it suggest that procreative beneficence is less perspicuous than its exposition implies — for instance, that it is covertly a population-level claim about the allocation of reproductive technology, misfiled as a claim about individual parental duty? I want a considered verdict, not a balance of considerations.
