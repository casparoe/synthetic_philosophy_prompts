Right so, I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days and I still run a monthly film night for about a dozen people who are terminally online in exactly the same way I am. We're doing Schindler's List next month, which is a slightly grim choice for a Tuesday in November, but I want to send round something beforehand that isn't the usual this-is-an-important-film handwringing. My angle is going to be the inefficacy objection, and I'd like you to write the piece: call it 1,500 to 2,000 words, aimed at people who've read a bit of Parfit but wouldn't recognise a film studies term if it bit them.

The setup in my head is this. The standard line in these debates is that your individual act makes no difference to a collective harm — one flight, one burger, one vote, one person's share of something enormous — and then the replies come in about expected harm, thresholds, complicity, and whether you're even the right kind of thing to be blamed for something you didn't cause. Schindler's List runs that argument with the stakes cranked right up, and it seems to me to make a move the papers mostly don't. Schindler's contribution is a rounding error against six million dead. But for the eleven hundred-odd names on the list he wasn't a rounding error at all, he was the entire difference. So what is the film claiming about whether morality tracks aggregates or particular identifiable people? And does the ending judge him on act-consequentialist grounds — the breakdown where he works out what his car and his party badge would have fetched and concludes he could have had ten more — or is the ring, with its business about saving one life, pushing back on precisely that arithmetic? I genuinely think the film is arguing with itself in that scene and I'd like it teased out rather than tidied up.

Second thing. The girl in the red coat. Half my group will immediately call that scope neglect and the identifiable victim effect and say the film is exploiting a bias; the other half will say it's the only device anyone has ever invented that makes a statistic feel like anything. Is either side right? And is something stranger going on given that she dies anyway — you get the salience without the rescue, which reads to me like the film's own comment on how little good it does to feel for one child.

Third, leverage. He can only do what he does because he's already inside the machine: bribes, black market, a factory, a friendship with Göth, that balcony conversation about power being pardon rather than killing. For most of the first act he's a straightforward beneficiary of the system. So is the film's real point not about marginal contribution at all, but about where luck happens to place you in the causal structure — and if so, does the actual philosophical debate have room for that, or is the film smuggling it in? Don't let him off either: I want the dirty-hands material confronted, including the useless shells from Brünnlitz and the fact that his workers were still, formally, slave labour.

Then two bits of honesty I want up front rather than buried. Say clearly where the analogy breaks — this isnt a climate film, and there's a real question whether dragging the meat-eating and one-vote literature past a genocide is something a decent critic should refuse to do; argue it both ways and land somewhere. And don't reduce the film to an illustration of my framing: tell me where it resists the reading, where I've got it wrong, and where the honest answer is that it isn't making an argument at all. End with what the film gives the debate that argument alone doesn't.

No bibliography, no reading list, no plot recap — everyone's seen it twice. Philosopher names are fine where they're doing work. And please don't put dialogue in quotation marks, just paraphrase it; its exactly the sort of thing people misremember and I don't want to fact-check anyone's recall of a Spielberg script.
