I'm on a small market-surveillance team at a financial regulator, and about half my week goes into writing up "everyone suddenly did the same thing" episodes for people who aren't economists: a retail pile-in to one microcap, twenty funds all quietly moving to the same leverage at once, a whole city of landlords flipping to short-term lets inside a quarter. My boss has started asking me to put a mechanism label on each one before she reads the rest of the memo, and I've realized I've been doing it by vibes. I want to build an actual instrument.

So: write me a decision tree that starts from "we have documented a cluster of correlated behavior, here's what we can see" and ends at a named mechanism. Around eight branch questions, no more. The hard constraint is that every node has to be answerable by someone who has never heard any of the labels and who only has what I have — adoption timestamps and order flow, whether the payoff to acting depends on how many others act, whether there's a public announcement or news item sitting at the front of the cluster, whether dissent is visible and whether dissenters get punished, and (rarely, expensively) a survey of participants where I can ask what they privately believed. Please give each branch question twice: once as the abstract thing it's testing, and once as what it looks like in that data, because the abstract version is useless to my colleagues.

The leaves have to include, at minimum: a genuine information cascade in the textbook sense (rational people rationally discarding their own signal once the public history is decisive); the boring null, where everybody independently responded to the same public information and there is no interdependence at all; a convention or coordination equilibrium where nobody is learning anything from anybody; preference falsification, where private attitudes never moved and only the public expression did; sanction-driven conformity that survives even when the payoff case evaporates; signaling, where the correlation is partly about separating from a type rather than joining a crowd; a composition or selection effect, where the group didn't converge so much as sort itself; and manufactured correlation, where a common source of persuasion is doing the work that peer influence looks like it's doing. Add one or two more if the logical space obviously demands them. Each leaf gets the name, the one observable signature that best distinguishes it, and its most awkward commitment — the thing it predicts that would embarrass us if it turned out false.

Then the part I actually care about: a probe battery. Suppose I can afford five interventions or natural experiments — reveal the private signals of the first few movers, drop one visible dissenter into the middle of the sequence, flip the payoff so being early is punished instead of rewarded, make the action unobservable to peers, remove the sanction for abstaining. Give me a matrix of leaves by probe, with a one-line prediction in each cell. Where two probes are useless against a given mechanism, say so rather than filling the cell with mush.

Finally, be honest about the failures. Flag every pair of leaves this tree cannot actually separate with observational data alone, and say what extra information would break the tie if it's even breakable. And walk the answer-combinations: some sequences of yes/no answers will correspond to no mechanism anyone has ever defended. List the interesting empty cells and diagnose each one — is it incoherent, is it unstable so it never persists long enough to be observed, or is it just unexplored and someone should go look? Typos in my brief are your problem now, sorry.
