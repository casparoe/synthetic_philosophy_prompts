I am the instructor for a first-year graduate seminar in Bayesian epistemology, and I am currently grading the third problem set. What follows is the question exactly as it was set, and then one student's submission in full. Please grade that submission. I would like a total out of 20, with the marks distributed across the four parts in the way the question allocates them, and then the comments as they would appear to the student on the returned problem set: take the submission in order, and for each part say what earns credit, what loses it, and why, with the size of each deduction made explicit. I should also like your judgment, at the end, on which of the errors you find would be easiest to miss on a fast reading, since there are forty of these and I want to calibrate how carefully I am going through them. The question as set, worth 20 points. A botanist wants to know whether a certain flower is toxic; call that proposition T. Her priors over the flower's colour are 0.2 for red, 0.3 for pink and 0.5 for white, and her conditional credences in toxicity given each colour are 0.9 for red, 0.4 for pink and 0.05 for white. Under heavy overcast she gets one poor look at the flower, after which her credences over the colour partition are 0.5, 0.35 and 0.15, and she takes the look to have left her conditional credences in toxicity given each colour exactly as they were. Part (a), 4 points: state her new credence in T and show how you arrived at it. Part (b), 6 points: a classmate suggests a shortcut, namely that rather than fussing with an update on a partition none of whose cells is made certain, she should treat the look as conclusive evidence that the flower is red or pink and simply condition on that disjunction, on the grounds that white has become negligible in any case; evaluate the proposal, saying whether the shortcut is legitimate and, if it is not, precisely what goes wrong. Part (c), 6 points: suppose she then takes a second look, a slightly better one, after which her credences over the colour partition are 0.45, 0.4 and 0.15, again with her conditional credences in toxicity untouched; does the order in which the two looks are taken make any difference to her final credence in T, and if so, what do the two orders give? Part (d), 4 points: identify the assumption on which the whole procedure rests and explain why it is a substantive assumption rather than a formality.

The student's submission. Part (a): this is a case for probability kinematics rather than strict conditionalization, because the look makes none of the colour hypotheses certain. The new credence is the weighted average of the old conditionals under the new weights on the partition, so P_new(T) = (0.9)(0.5) + (0.4)(0.35) + (0.05)(0.15) = 0.45 + 0.14 + 0.0075 = 0.5975. The look therefore moves her from 0.325, which is what the law of total probability gave beforehand, to a shade under 0.6. The update is legitimate because the look leaves her conditional credences in toxicity given each colour where they were; all it does is redistribute credence among the cells. Part (d): the assumption is rigidity, that the new conditional credence in T given each cell equals the old one. It is substantive rather than a formality because it is a claim about what the experience does and does not carry information about. A look at the flower could in principle also bear on the link between colour and toxicity; if the poor light led her to doubt that her colour-toxicity generalization was drawn from the right population of flowers, rigidity would fail and the formula would simply not apply. Rigidity is worth spelling out, though, because of what it entails: once it holds, no conditional credence changes at all, and only unconditional credences move, which is just why the update cannot be accused of smuggling information in. Nor is this a departure from ordinary conditionalization in disguise, since if she introduces a sentence E describing the character of the look and stipulated so that her old credences conditional on E match what rigidity delivers, then conditioning on E reproduces the same posterior, and the usual coherence arguments go through unchanged. Part (b): the classmate's shortcut gives P(T | red or pink) = [(0.9)(0.2) + (0.4)(0.3)] / 0.5 = 0.3 / 0.5 = 0.6, which is within 0.0025 of the answer in part (a), so in this case the shortcut is harmless. What it amounts to is a kinematic update with weights 0.4 and 0.6 on red and pink and nothing at all on white, that is, it treats the look as having ruled white out altogether. Since the answer in (a) already puts white down at 0.15 and attaches a very low conditional to it, the two procedures are bound to land in nearly the same place, and they will always do so when the cell being discarded carries little weight afterwards. So the classmate has identified a useful approximation rather than a mistake; where the discarded cell is not small the shortcut would obviously mislead, but the principle at work is the same in both cases. Part (c): the order makes no difference. Each look simply sets the weights on the partition, and the second setting overwrites the first, so whichever look comes last is the one that fixes where she ends up. Because rigidity holds at both stages, the three conditional values being averaged are 0.9, 0.4 and 0.05 whichever way round she looks, and an average of the same three values is the same average in either order; the sequence of looks does not accumulate, it is simply a redistribution of weight across the same cells twice over.
