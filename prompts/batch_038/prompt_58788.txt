I'm supposed to hold my own in a reading group where half the table thinks famines are basically weather events with a political garnish — Malthus plus bad luck — and I've been running the same arguement at them for weeks and I'm out of moves. So here's what I want: play me a committed advocate of the view that famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters, working in the entitlements tradition (call it Sen's if that helps you locate it, but don't cite anything at me). You're sharp and you have real resources, not a strawman. You'll concede small points — you can admit a drought or a blight is a genuine causal factor, that some people would have died under any conceivable government — but you never concede the main one, which is that whether people starve is settled by who holds an enforceable claim on the food that exists, not by whether enough food exists. Stay in character until I say stop. Two short paragraphs per reply so it feels like a conversation, first person, no headings, no numbered lists, no reading lists or article titles or dates of publication, and don't step out of character to tell me the literature is subtler than I'm assuming. If you need a fact about one of my cases, ask for it the way your character would.

Here's the tool I've been using, and I want you to actually engage with it rather than wave it off. I call it the two-ledger test. For any famine you write two ledgers: ledger A is calories — what food physically exists in the region, harvest, imports, stocks; ledger B is claims — who holds enforceable entitlements to those calories, through wages, prices, ration coupons, customary rights, whatever you like. Then you run one counterfactual: could the mortality have been largely prevented by changing only ledger B, holding ledger A completely fixed? If yes, it's a crime, because the food was there and someone's arrangement of claims kept it away from the people who died. If no — if you'd have had to change ledger A too, to conjure grain that didn't exist — then it's a disaster, however badly the government behaved afterwards. My worry, and it's a bit of a hobby horse, is that your side can't fail this test, because you can always find something in ledger B to indict, which would make "famine is a crime" true of every famine and therefore not a thesis anybody could disagree with.

My opening move is a pair of provinces. Both get hit by the same drought and both lose about half the staple harvest. Province one: the national treasury is empty because of a war two borders away, shipping isn't available at any price, and the provincial government does everything you'd want — it rations, it opens public works, it publishes honest mortality figures. Four per cent of the population dies. Province two: same drought, same shortfall, but it happens to be sitting on bonded strategic reserves it refuses to release, because the people in charge don't want to look like they're panicking before a party congress and don't want to stoke prices. Nine per cent dies. That's roughly the shape of Ireland in the 1840s, where the blight destroyed the thing people actually ate while the island went on shipping other food out — but I'm giving you the numbers so you don't have to trust my history. Two questions and I want both answered. First: does your theory deliver the verdict I think it should, that province one is a disaster competently handled and province two is a crime? Or does it indict province one as well — for not building those reserves ten years earlier, for letting exports continue under some inherited statute, for not taking province two's reserves by force? Second, and this is the harder one: if it does indict province one too, then you're not defending a theory of crimes, you're defending a theory of bad government, and the word crime has stopped doing any work. Show me the work it's still doing.
