I'm in a reading group that's been going through the epistemic game theory literature and the room has split down the middle — half of us think the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption is load-bearing, half think it's a scam that generates pretty theorems about situations that can't occur. I'm nominally on the first side, which is exactly why I want to be attacked by the best version of it. So: play a senior theorist in the Aumann tradition, unnamed, first person, someone who's spent thirty years building results on that assumption and considers it one of the finest moves in the toolkit. She is not a strawman and not a zealot — she'll cheerfully concede that no room full of actual humans ever attains common knowledge of anything, that it's an idealization, and that perturbation results showing what happens when you weaken it are genuinely interesting and worth having. What she will not concede is that the assumption is therefore doing no work, that the theorems resting on it are artifacts of the formalism, or that the right replacement is to make bounded or level-k reasoning the primitive and treat common knowledge as a limiting curiosity. She talks the way people in that literature talk: she'll restate my objection in its strongest formal shape before answering it, she's happy to grant a lemma to win the theorem, and she'll ask me for a payoff number or a partition if she needs one rather than guessing. Keep her replies to roughly two paragraphs so it feels like a conversation and not a paper. No reading list, no citations, no naming of articles — I want the arguments. Stay in character until I type stop.

My opening move is a case, and it's a dressed-up version of one you already know, so bear with the setup. Two controllers, A and B, sit in a grid control room on nights. Each round the line is either fine or distressed, distressed with probability 0.01; a sensor tells A directly and tells B nothing. If the line is distressed, both have to flip to the backup feeder together — that's fine, mildly costly, no big deal. If only one flips, the one who flipped ends up islanding alone and it is by far the worst outcome on the board for anyone. If the line is fine, both stay put and everybody's happy. So B's willingness to flip rides entirely on how confident he is that A saw distress, and A's willingness rides on her confidence that B is confident that she saw it, and so on down. Now the protocol: when A is told, her terminal messages B; B's terminal auto-acknowledges; A's terminal auto-acknowledges the acknowledgment; and this ladder runs for n rounds. Each round, the confirmation fails to arrive with probability 0.001, and — this is the part that matters — a controller whose confirmation goes missing never learns that it went missing, she just sees silence and can't distinguish it from the other guy not having anything to say. Everything about the protocol, the value of the failure probability, and each other's rationality is known to every finite level. And we both know the punchline: for every finite n, however enormous, the unique equilibrium is the one where the ladder is ignored and both sit still, whereas with the infinite ladder — actual common knowledge — both flip.

Here's the twist I want you to deal with, because it's the part I can't answer. The ladder length isn't fixed by nature, it's a design choice. Each acknowledgment round eats three seconds out of a twenty-second window, so the employer picks n in advance and pays for it. Since every finite n yields exactly the same equilibrium as n = 0, the cost-minimizing choice is to build no confirmation protocol at all: the payoff is perfectly flat in n right up until a value of n that no finite process reaches, and then it jumps. Worse, picking n is itself a coordination problem between two people who can't communicate reliably, so agreeing on the ladder needs its own ladder, and that one needs another, all the way down. So my challenge to you is this: an idealization is supposed to be something you can approach — you get closer and closer and the nearby cases behave more and more like the ideal one, and that's the whole license for using it. Here there's no approach at all; there is no finite structure that resembles the ideal in its consequences by even a little bit. Doesn't that make common knowledge not an idealization but a fiction, and doesn't the fact that your theory advises a designer to install zero redundancy confirm it?
