So here's a thing that happened in our Friday workshop and I've been chewing on it ever since. Two different people used the exact same phrase — "information is physical" — within about twenty minutes of each other, and they used it to push in nearly opposite directions. First a grad student working on the thermodynamics of computation invoked it while explaining why Maxwell's demon isn't a threat to the second law: the demon has to record its measurements somewhere, that record is a physical thing, and wiping it costs something on the order of kT ln 2 per bit at the temperature of the surrounding bath, so the books balance once you include the demon's memory in the accounting. Then right after, a visitor from another department used the identical four words as a premise against substrate independence in philosophy of mind — the thought being that if information really is physical, then it can't be the abstract pattern that matters for consciousness, it has to be the particular stuff the pattern happens to be realized in. Nobody blinked. I want to know how one sentence can be load-bearing in both arguments, and whether it's actually load-bearing in either.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan might be standing for, ordered roughly from platitude to provocative? I'm thinking of at least: a weak embodiment claim (whatever information there is, is instantiated in some physical degrees of freedom, so there's no free-floating information); the Landauer-style claim tying logically irreversible operations to a minimum dissipation; a bookkeeping claim (you can't do the statistical mechanics of the demon while ignoring the demon, since its memory is part of the physical system); something like the Wheeler-style inversion, on which the physical is supposed to bottom out in information rather than the reverse; and an epistemic reading on which thermodynamic entropy just is an observer's missing information. For each, tell me (a) what exactly is being asserted and what would count against it, (b) whether it's trivially true, substantive and well supported, contested, or simply confused, and (c) which notion of information it needs — Shannon, semantic, algorithmic, or thermodynamic — because I suspect most of the slippage happens right there, between senses of the word.

Then the payoff I actually want: which reading does each of my two speakers need, and does the reading they need follow from the one they almost certainly had in mind? My hunch is that the philosophy-of-mind use is a bait and switch — the embodiment platitude is true but does no work against multiple realizability, while the stronger reading that would do that work isn't licensed by anything about erasure costs — but argue me out of it if I'm wrong. Also flag any reading on which the phrase isn't making a claim so much as waving off an objection, and say where the demon literature itself isn't settled: I know there are philosophers of physics who think the Landauer principle either restates the second law or overreaches, and there are ways of dissolving the demon that never go through erasure at all. Format it as a numbered menu I can hand out at the next session, keep the notation light (half my reading group hasnt opened a physics text since undergrad), and please write it in American English.
