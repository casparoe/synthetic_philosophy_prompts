I'm in year four of a philosophy of mind PhD and my quals paper is about whether the fight between multiple-drafts people and what I'll call determinists about phenomenal timing is substantive or just two vocabularies yelling at each other. My committee wants the disagreement stated exactly, and I've decided the only honest way to do that is to write the document an adversarial collaboration between the two camps would have produced. So: draft that document.

The two parties, as I'm setting them up — sharpen them if you want, but don't strawman either one:

Party A, a committed multiple-drafts person. Content-fixation happens in many places at many times, there's no finish line in the brain past which arrival order equals order of presentation, reports are constructions rather than readouts, and the Orwellian and Stalinesque ways of explaining the color phi case are observationally equivalent, so whatever is left over — whether the pre-report state was "already experiential" — isn't a disagreement about anything.

Party B, the determinist about phenomenal timing. Also rejects the Cartesian theater, also perfectly happy with reconstructive memory and distributed parallel processing, but holds that for a subject at a time there is a determinate fact about what it's like for them then, fixed by what their brain is doing and not by what anyone says afterward. Their complaint is that making the writing-it-down-in-memory step criterial for consciousness either changes the subject or collapses experience into dispositions to report.

Note they agree on a lot, which is the whole point. I want the agreed-claims section to be long and non-vacuous — the stuff both would actually put their names to, about the neurobiology, about what first-person reports are and aren't good evidence for, about what the filling-in and change-detection results show, about what would count as a theater and why neither of them thinks there is one. Then the residual disagreement, narrow but real.

For the cruxes I want them hanging on a paradigm I made up, so please work with it rather than substituting a familiar one. Two brief dots, green left, red right, about four degrees apart, 70 ms onset to onset; subjects get apparent motion and on a free report say the dot turned red somewhere around the middle of the path. On each trial we collect three things. (1) A location report of where the color changed, a slider along the trajectory, collected either 50 ms after the second dot or 2 s after, randomized across trials. (2) A cross-modal timing judgment: a short click plays at a jittered time around the physical middle of the trial and the subject says whether the dot changed color before or after the click — this is meant to peg the experienced change time to an outside clock without asking for any narrative. (3) A mask manipulation: on some trials the second dot is followed by a pattern mask at variable lag, which delays when its color becomes identifiable while leaving the motion intact.

Record the predictions in advance. My guess is Party B says masking shifts both (1) and (2) later by the same amount and that within a subject the two measures should track each other trial by trial, because one real event moved and both probes see it move; if they diverge systematically, that just shows one probe is broken and the job is to figure out which. Party A, I think, says the two probes are separate content-fixations, so "when did it change" only has an answer relative to a probe, and systematic divergence between (1) and (2) is precisely what you should expect rather than a measurement failure to explain away. If you think that's wrong, or that my paradigm is bad — for instance that the click judgment is itself just another report and so can't play the independent-clock role Party B needs — put that in as a party objection, that's fine, it's probably one of the cruxes anyway.

Also have both sides say what they're committed to about the reverse dissociation: a subject who reports no color change at all but whose click judgments place a change at the midpoint. Does either side have to call that subject wrong?

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with columns for the disagreement, Party A's own wording of their side, Party B's own wording of theirs, whether it's verbal or substantive, and what would have to be true for it to dissolve. Then the cruxes, each with the test or case both accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction written down beforehand. Then a short closing section on what each side would count as evidence against their own view, and whether there's anything both agree would actually move the debate. Anywhere you think a supposed disagreement is purely verbal, say so and name the word doing the work.

No citations and no reading list, I know the literature, I just don't have this document. And don't referee it — I don't want a verdict on who's right.
