I'm a staff software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or molecular biology, and next month I'm giving a forty-minute seminar talk to a university systems-biology group: mostly experimental molecular biologists, two computational people who work with sequencing data, and one philosopher of biology whose work concerns talk of "information" in the life sciences. My thesis is a deliberately narrow engineering claim rather than a grand one: DNA is a code in the literal, technical sense — an explicitly stored, copyable, versioned data format with a decoding table and a tolerated error budget at the level of individual symbol reads — but it is not a program, and the standard objection that "the cell is hardware and software at once" simply begs the question by assuming the program reading I'm rejecting. My supporting points are that the codon table functions like a serialization schema with documented variants (mitochondrial and ciliate tables reassign codons, which behaves like a schema migration), that wobble at the third base position is a deliberate redundancy rather than sloppiness, and that the decoding machinery — ribosome, tRNAs, and the cytoplasmic state supplied by the egg — is inherited rather than encoded in the sequence, so development is interpretation of data by an external machine, not execution of the data itself.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the seven or eight objections this audience would actually raise — not the ones a general audience or a software crowd would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk, phrased in the vocabulary an experimental molecular biologist or a systems person would use, each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is softest. Tell me which of my three supporting points a specialist would consider already conceded or badly overstated, what I should give away in the first five minutes to keep the room with me, and which single objection I should hope nobody asks.

Finally, please be explicit about the state of the underlying debate: separate what is settled among people who work on this material from what is genuinely contested — in particular, whether "code" and "information" language in biology is taken to be literal or merely useful shorthand, and whether my interpreter-is-inherited framing has any standing as a recognized position. Don't present a contested reading as consensus, and flag any point where my argument quietly depends on one side of an open question.
