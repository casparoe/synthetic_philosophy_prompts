I am marking the take-home essays for a final-year module on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and before I finalise the marks I would like an independent assessment of one script. I have deliberately not read it closely, so please treat it as unmarked and give me your own reading rather than a sanity check on mine.

The question as set (20 marks; 6 for (a), 6 for (b), 8 for (c)):

McCarthy and Hayes observed, in their 1969 paper Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence, that a formalism in which actions are characterised by axioms stating what they bring about does not by itself license the conclusion that everything else stayed the same. To obtain that conclusion one must add, for every fluent and every action that does not affect it, an axiom stating that the action leaves that fluent alone.

(a) Explain what is objectionable about this requirement: say precisely what is supposed to become unmanageable, and for whom. Then explain how this objection differs from the one pressed later by philosophers working outside AI, namely that an agent must be able to tell which of its beliefs bear on a situation in order to act in it at all.

(b) A standard formal response replaces the many persistence axioms with a single axiom per fluent, giving the conditions under which that fluent holds after an arbitrary action, so that persistence becomes a theorem rather than an assumption. Explain how this is meant to work, and explain what it establishes about the objection in (a).

(c) Other formalisms let an agent assume by default that nothing interferes with the ordinary course of events, and revise that assumption on acquiring evidence of interference. A colleague maintains that neither the response in (b) nor these default-and-revision formalisms bears on the second objection in (a), since that objection concerns an agent's grasp of relevance rather than the size of its axiom set. Assess this claim. Say in particular whether it applies with equal force to the default-and-revision formalisms, and whether the second objection is best understood, as some have held, as a version of Hume's problem of induction.

The student's answer:

(a) Actions in the situation calculus are axiomatised by their effects, but effect axioms say nothing about what is unaffected, and logic will not supply that for free. One therefore adds a frame axiom for each fluent and each action that leaves it alone. With F fluents and A actions this requires on the order of F × A axioms, and in practice the growth is worse than that product suggests, since any fluent worth representing can plausibly be affected by many actions. The objection is a bookkeeping objection: the axiom set cannot be written, maintained, or reasoned over, and every addition to the ontology obliges one to revisit the whole set.

Dennett's sequence of robots is usually taken to show that the difficulty goes beyond bookkeeping. R1 hauls a wagon out of a room and is destroyed by a bomb it had no reason to think was on the wagon; R1D1, built to work out the implications of its intended actions before acting, is still generating implications when the bomb goes off; R2, built to ignore the irrelevant ones, is still separating relevant from irrelevant implications when it too is destroyed. As I read the story, however, the moral is a computational one. R1D1 fails for want of processing speed, and R2 fails because it must search a large space of candidate inferences before it can act. The second objection — that an agent must be able to tell which of its beliefs bear on the situation — is therefore an objection about search cost rather than about the number of axioms, and it does not by itself show that the logical approach to cognition is mistaken. It has been suggested that the objection is really Hume's problem of induction in another guise, since to know which beliefs bear on a situation is to know which regularities will continue to hold.

(b) The response in question writes, for each fluent F, an axiom giving necessary and sufficient conditions for F's holding after an arbitrary action: F holds just in case some action made it true and nothing made it false, or F held before and nothing made it false. Persistence is then a corollary rather than an assumption, and where one previously needed F × A axioms one now needs F. The mechanics are an improvement. The philosophical significance is routinely overstated, though. The single axiom contains exactly the information contained in the axioms it replaces, compressed rather than eliminated, and the number of axioms still rises with the size of the ontology. The bookkeeping objection said that the axiom set becomes unmanageable; an unmanageable set of F axioms is still unmanageable. The objection in (a) is thus answered notationally, not substantively.

(c) I think the colleague is wrong. Minimising what changes, and treating interference as something to be believed only when there is evidence for it, is precisely a way of acting without knowing in advance which facts matter: the agent requires no catalogue of relevant considerations, because relevance is settled by whatever evidence of interference actually turns up. In this respect the default-and-revision formalisms are stronger than the response in (b), which still requires the axiomatiser to state, fluent by fluent, when it holds. Adding further interference predicates whenever a counterexample appears keeps such a formalism open-ended rather than closed, and minimising change delivers exactly the conclusions we want in ordinary cases. It follows that the second objection is not really a different objection from the first: both concern how much an agent has to keep track of, and they differ only in whether the accounting is done by the axiomatiser or by the inference procedure. Whether that accounting problem is Hume's is another matter; if it is, it is a problem for any account of how present knowledge licenses expectations, and not a difficulty peculiar to artificial intelligence.
