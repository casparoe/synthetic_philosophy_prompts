I retired three years ago from a career in experimental physics, and I now audit a philosophy seminar for lay students at the university here. This term's topic is the old distinction between knowing how to do something and knowing that something is so, and our tutor has been handing down a verdict on one case a week without ever stating her principle. So far:

1. A violinist who plays flawlessly but cannot say what her hands are doing, and whose explanations when pressed are muddled and partly wrong — she knows how to play.
2. A man who has read everything about hang-gliding, could pass any written examination on the subject, and has never left the ground — he does not know how to hang-glide.
3. A competitive skater who is confidently wrong about the mechanics of one of her jumps, insisting that the take-off must come from the opposite edge to the one she actually uses, yet lands it cleanly every time in competition — she knows how to perform it.
4. A baker whose loaves come out superbly perhaps once in twenty attempts, with no idea why and no capacity to produce one to order — he does not know how to bake them.
5. A woman who rode as a child, has not been near a bicycle in forty years, and pedals off steadily when one is handed to her — she knows how.
6. A club chess player who, in one family of endgames, finds the strongest move within seconds while playing like a beginner everywhere else — he knows how to play those endgames.

Next week's case is this. A neurosurgeon has had a stroke; his hands no longer obey him, and he could not now carry out a single step of an operation he has performed two thousand times. His articulable command of it, however, is undimmed: he can dictate the procedure in order, sketch the approach, and at the operating table he identifies and corrects his trainees' errors instantly and reliably. Does he know how to perform the operation?

Predict her verdict. Then treat the list the way I would have treated a set of measurements: state the candidate rules that accommodate all six precedents — I expect at least two, drawn from the rival camps in this debate — and say which one you would bet on and why. Tell me which precedent is the closest analog to the surgeon, and whether the resemblance survives scrutiny or is misleading. Finally, say what further case she should be given to break the degeneracy. Please reason from my list rather than from a survey of the literature; I have read the survey, and it does not tell me what she thinks.
