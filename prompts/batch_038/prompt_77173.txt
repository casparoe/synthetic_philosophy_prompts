I'm a staff engineer on the platform team at a payments company. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I've tried to write my reasoning out explicitly enough that each step can be checked rather than guessed at.

**Background.** Our compliance function imposes a requirement on the audit trail of any system touching a regulated payment flow. In substance: every fact about the system's behaviour during an incident must be recoverable from the audit trail. Last month a consumer with a full queue silently discarded a message at 03:12:07. Instrumentation on that path sampled one message in a hundred, so no entry for the discard was ever written; our 90-day retention has since expired, so the trail for that window is now frozen — it is settled, once and for all, what it contains.

Let **d** be the proposition *the message was discarded at 03:12:07*, and let **R(p)** mean *the audit trail records that p*. I mean this as a claim about content rather than strings: R(p) holds if a reader of the trail could reconstruct p, not only if p appears verbatim. Let **s** be the conjunction d ∧ ¬R(d).

**The argument.**

1. *Completeness.* For every proposition p about the system's behaviour, if p is true then it is possible that R(p). Nothing about a fact can make it in principle unrecordable: sampling rates, retention windows and buffer sizes are engineering choices, not barriers in principle. Had we instrumented and retained properly, the trail would have recorded p.
2. *Accuracy.* If R(p), then p. The trail never misrepresents the system; an entry recoverable as saying p is present only if p. This is part of the same requirement — a trail that can lie is treated as worse than no trail.
3. *Granularity.* If R(p ∧ q), then R(p) and R(q). If the trail lets you recover a compound fact, it lets you recover each part of it.
4. *s is true.* The first conjunct was established in the incident review from the consumer's own metrics. The second holds because sampling meant nothing was written and the retention window has closed.
5. From (1) and (4): it is possible that R(s).
6. Suppose R(s). By (3), R(d) and R(¬R(d)). By (2) applied to the second, ¬R(d). But R(d) was just derived. Contradiction.
7. So R(s) is impossible, contradicting (5). Hence (1) is false: there is a true proposition about the system's behaviour that no trail could record, however engineered.
8. Nothing in (4)–(7) was specific to logs. Any recorder that is accurate and granular is exposed to the same argument — post-mortem documents, design records, an investigator's notes. So the general claim that any fact could be captured by some record is false.
9. Therefore requirements of the form "the audit trail must be capable of answering any question about the incident" demand something impossible, and, completeness being unattainable in principle, a gap in a trail is not a defect that could have been avoided.

**Why I can't accept it.** Two things. First, our incident report contains the sentence *at the time of the incident nothing in the audit trail showed the discard* — true, recorded by someone, and precisely the kind of proposition (8) says cannot be recorded. Second, (9) is not something I could take to compliance: it implies that every missing log entry in the industry was unavoidable, whereas most of our gaps are instrumentation we chose not to build and could have built.

So something in (1)–(9) is wrong, and I cannot tell whether it is a step that does not follow or a premise I should never have granted. Please tell me:

(a) Whether the inference from (1)–(4) to (7) is valid as stated, given how I defined R. If it is not, identify the exact place it fails.
(b) If it is valid, what the argument actually establishes — I note that a contradiction between (1), (2), (3) and (4) does not by itself tell me (1) is the culprit — and which premise is cheapest to give up. I want the cost priced for the practice, not in the abstract: what would the requirement have to say instead, and what could auditors no longer demand?
(c) Whether (8) and (9) follow from (7) at all, or whether I have slid from a claim about one proposition to a claim about records in general.
(d) What to make of the sentence in our incident report, since it looks like a counterexample to (8) sitting in a document I wrote myself.

If it helps to know my priors: I would most like to keep (2), because accuracy feels non-negotiable in this domain, and (4), because I know the discard was real and unrecorded. I would rather weaken (1) or (3). Tell me plainly if that preference is mistaken.

I am not asking you to match this to a named result and recite the standard menu of replies to it; I want a diagnosis of my steps. Please state how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the one point in your answer of which you are least sure. Please write in British English.
