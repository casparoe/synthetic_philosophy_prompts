Background first, because it matters for why I care: I'm a retired physical chemist, 37 years of enzyme kinetics, and since I stopped going into the lab I've been reading philosophy with a small group that meets Tuesday mornings. We're on Dennett's crane/skyhook distinction. For our purposes a skyhook is any explainer that puts a mind at the bottom — a designer, a purpose, an already-intelligent something — and uses it to account for the lesser things above it. A crane is the opposite: a mindless process that, given time and the right substrate, builds the intricate thing you wanted explained, so that the intricacy is earned rather than assumed.

One of the group, an ex-philosoper, turned up last week with the following, and half the room thinks it's devastating. I think it's got a hole in it but I've lost the thread twice now trying to find it.

(1) A crane has to run on something. Any process that builds order needs a substrate already structured so that its outputs accumulate instead of washing out.

(2) Natural selection is the flagship crane. But selection only runs on a substrate that already has heritable variation, differential replication, and enough stability that a gain survives to the next round.

(3) Those are just the features the crane was supposed to generate — order where chemistry offered none. So the flagship crane borrows its explanandum from its own substrate.

(4) Generalize. For any crane you nominate, there's a deeper level at which it either doesn't run at all, or runs only on a substrate that already carries the order it was invoked to produce.

(5) And you can't halt the descent with a brute bottom, because a bottom level whose laws and constants simply contain the capacity to throw up eyes and enzymes and sonnets is a skyhook with the agent rubbed out — the intricacy is still down there, it just isn't attributed to anybody.

(6) So there are no cranes. Every explanation either borrows all the way down or lands on a disguised skyhook.

(7) Which means the distinction has no teeth. It can't indict dualism, or nativism, or teleology, or fine-tuning, because the identical indictment lands on quantum mechanics, on the second law, on the periodic table, on the Arrhenius equation I made my living from.

(8) And since (7) would condemn four centuries of explanation that demonstrably works, the only coherent conclusions left are the radical ones: either the world isn't explicable at all, or the intricacy at the bottom really is somebody's.

I can't accept (8). I've watched transition-state theory predict a rate constant to within a factor of two, blind, before the measurement was made — that isn't nothing, and I don't think the word "nothing" is available to me. If I had to rank, I'd keep (1) and (2), which strike me as flatly true, and I'd rather give up (4) or (5) than anything else. Something between (4) and (5) keeps feeling like its carrying more weight than it looks like it's carrying, but when I say that out loud I'm told I'm just squeamish.

So: which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost — not "you lose elegance," but the specific explanatory work that goes dark once you reject it? Then tell me why that step looked fine in the first place, because I want to know what's making it seductive; that's the part my friend keeps winning arguments with. Then take the three camps in the room in turn — the Dennettians, the brute-laws people who are perfectly happy to stop at (5), and the one member who says (5) is a proof of God — and say what each of them owes by way of a reply to this argument and whether they can pay it. Finally, run your diagnosis past a case I trust, so I can check it isn't a verbal fix: quantum mechanics accounting for why the noble gases won't react. Is that a crane, a skyhook, or neither, and does your answer to the argument say the right thing about it?
