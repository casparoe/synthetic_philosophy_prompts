Take Air Florida Flight 90 — the 737 that hit the 14th Street Bridge and went into the Potomac on January 13, 1982 — and analyze it through sphexishness, the digger-wasp idea Dennett uses in his frame-problem essay. In the wasp case you can point at a triggering stimulus (paralyzed cricket at the burrow mouth), a fixed routine (drag it in, go inside, check, come back out), and a feedback loop that restarts instead of updating: move the cricket a few inches while she's inside and she drags it back to the entrance and begins again, indefinitely. Now do that mapping for the cockpit, rigorously, and tell me where the concept stops doing work.

I know the crash, so don't retell it to me. The facts I want you to work from: heavy snow closed Washington National that morning; the aircraft was de-iced and then sat waiting while snow kept falling; the crew used reverse thrust to push back in the snow and positioned behind a departing jet to try to melt ice with its exhaust; the engines' internal ice protection was left off; and the takeoff was never rejected even though the crew had picked up signs of a power problem on the ground and could see contamination on the wings. The NTSB found pilot error and listed roughly those items.

First question: what exactly is the stimulus, what is the routine, and where does the system receive the signal that should have caused a re-plan and then fail to use it? Second, and I think harder: whose design is in question? The captain, the first officer, the checklist, the aircraft, the airline's procedures? Sphexishness is supposed to be a property of one organism's control architecture, but here the flexibility looks like it was installed in the wrong seat — the captain had a documented history of poor marks for checklist usage and flight procedures, while the first officer was a former fighter instructor pilot whom colleagues described as the type who would speak up when he knew something specific was wrong. And the first officer was the one flying, not the one in command. He did speak up, several times, over the whole sequence, and nothing reset. So: can sphexishness be distributed across a crew and an artifact, or gated by an authority structure so that the capacity to re-plan exists but can't act? Or does stretching it that far cost the concept all of its content and turn it into a label for any bad outcome?

Then the free-will payoff, both directions. Does this case give the Dennettian compatibilist anything — was the captain unfree in the way a compulsive or an addict is supposed to be, or free-and-wrong, or free-and-sphexish? Does "he could have done otherwise" get any grip at all here, given that the otherwise was available, verbalized, and rejected? And the skeptical reading: people who think human flexibility is a story we tell ourselves love accidents like this as evidence that we're wasps with a bigger cortex. Does this one support that, or does it cut the other way — since the wasp's rigidity is supposed to follow from a narrow control system, whereas here you have an intelligent animal made rigid by the machine and the social structure it's embedded in?

Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than making it speak. I'd especially like to know what sphexishness has to say about the tradeoff — flexibility buys you aborted takeoffs you didn't need, and under workload and time pressure rigidity is sometimes the better design, so the wasp isn't obviously worse off than a re-planning competitor. Also tell me whether the idea of humans offloading judgment onto instruments and procedures, so that the rigid element ends up sitting in the machine instead of the head, is a real extension of the concept or a different idea wearing its name. (If you happen to recall the further detail about ice on the engine inlet probes making the thrust indications read high, use it; if not, just work with the plain fact that the crew had reasons to distrust what they were being shown and pressed on anyway.)

Finish with a verdict on the theory: is this a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case sphexishness was never built to handle? No citations, no bibliography, no reading list — you won't have web access and I dont want guessed-at sources. Argue from the concepts.
