Two and a half centuries of commentary on Hume's essay on taste have produced a disagreement that, as far as I can tell, nobody has ever lost — and I have come to suspect that the participants are not actually having the argument they think they are having. The charge runs like this. The essay offers two candidate standards: on the one hand, the general principles of composition, said to be founded on experience and on what has been found to please in all countries and all ages; on the other hand, the joint verdict of the qualified judges, those possessing "strong sense, united to delicate sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, and cleared of all prejudice." But the second appears to make the standard whatever such judges say, and there seems to be no independent way to identify such judges except by observing whose verdicts we already endorse — and what we endorse is largely the durable consensus of the canon. Critics, verdicts, and standard therefore define one another, and the objection concludes that the account of the true judge is empty: it cannot be used to settle a case we are genuinely uncertain about, since the only evidence that a judge is qualified is that he gets the right answers, and we do not know which those are.

The defenders reply that this misdescribes the reasoning entirely. On their reading Hume is a naturalist offering something more like the calibration of an instrument than a definition: the qualities of the true judge are inferred inductively from cases in which agreement among experienced, widely read, unprejudiced observers has in fact persisted, and the persistence of that agreement is itself the evidence for the qualities, not a consequence of assuming them. The rules of art are then too coarse to decide particular cases — which is precisely why authority is vested in persons rather than precepts — and the objection simply assumes that a standard must be a procedure that can be executed in advance of judgment. That assumption, they say, is what Hume is rejecting.

Two features of the essay complicate both readings, and I do not know what to do with them. First, Hume concedes that some variation — of temperament, of age, of national manners — is not corrigible by practice or comparison; but if the qualified judges may blamelessly diverge, then in exactly the contested cases their joint verdict may be undefined rather than merely hard to ascertain. Second, when Hume wants to illustrate delicacy he reaches for the episode of Sancho's kinsmen, whose reports of a taste of iron and of leather are vindicated by the discovery of a key on a leathern thong at the bottom of the hogshead. There, delicacy is confirmed by something outside the verdicts themselves. Whether aesthetics admits any analogue of the key — a discoverable feature of a work that certifies a critic's sensitivity independently of his agreement with the canon — seems to me to be where the dispute really bites, though neither side puts it that way.

My working hypothesis, which I may well be getting wrong: the quarrel is not about what makes an aesthetic judgment correct, but about what the word 'standard' is being asked to do — whether it names a criterion of correctness or an institution whose say-so settles disputes — compounded by a further divergence about whether the standard must be applicable prospectively to unfamiliar work or may be wholly retrospective, a ratification of the settled canon. If that is right, both sides have read the text accurately and have been answering different questions under one heading.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the essay or of the commentary, and not a list of who has said what. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal, a difference about what counts as evidence for the qualities of a judge, a difference about which cases count as data, a hidden question about the applicability of rules to particulars, or a sign that the notion of a standard of taste as Hume frames it is ill-posed. Argue from what each side actually claims. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, a novel kind of case, a demonstration that one side's question cannot be answered as posed — or argue that no progress should be expected, and say why the impasse is stable rather than merely difficult.
