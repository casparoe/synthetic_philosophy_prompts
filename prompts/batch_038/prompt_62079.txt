**The free energy principle as a variational meta-principle: maybe the unfalsifiability is the point**

1. My understanding of the machinery. An agent carries a generative model of how its sensory states come about. Surprise, here, is just the negative log probability the model assigns to the sensory state that actually occurs — so it's model-relative, not a property of the world. Variational free energy is that surprise plus a non-negative gap (the divergence between whatever approximate posterior the agent is using and the true posterior given the model), which makes it an upper bound on surprise. Perception minimizes free energy by changing beliefs; action minimizes expected free energy by changing the world so the input matches what the model expects. Two features of expected free energy matter for what follows: it splits into an epistemic term (information gain about hidden causes) and a pragmatic term (fit with the model's prior preferences over sensory states), and it is always evaluated over a policy, i.e. over some stretch of time.

2. Two familiar objections, and what I think is really going on. The dark room objection says that a surprise-minimizer should sit motionless in a dark empty room, where nothing unexpected ever happens. The loophole objection says an agent could minimize surprise by tampering with the model itself — adopting one on which whatever happens was expected — rather than by getting better at predicting; Friston's reply, as I read it, is that a persisting system can't do this because its priors are what make it the kind of thing that persists. My diagnosis: both objections are aimed at a quantity (momentary surprise) that the theory doesn't actually tell you to minimize, and the answers both work by quietly specifying two things that were left free — the content of the prior preferences, and the time horizon over which expected free energy is computed. Once you fix those, the objections dissolve, but so does most of what the principle was claiming.

3. The analogy I want to push. The principle of stationary action doesn't predict anything either. Hand it no Lagrangian and no endpoints and it's silent; hand it those and it's equivalent to the equations of motion. Nobody concludes that Lagrangian mechanics is unfalsifiable and therefore worthless — we understand it as a schema that organizes and constrains the candidate dynamics, with the empirical content living in the L you choose. Maximum entropy in statistical mechanics is the same shape: the principle is nearly content-free, and all the physics is in the choice of which observables you hold fixed. I want to say the free energy principle is a third member of this family, with one important difference: its free parameters aren't a Lagrangian but the generative model's prior preferences and, above all, the precisions — the inverse-variance weights on prediction errors at each level. My hunch is that the precisions are where all the actual psychology lives, and that the interesting empirical claims in this literature (aberrant precision weighting in psychosis, that sort of thing) are claims about precisions, not about free energy.

4. Consequences. If that's right, then demanding that the principle itself be falsifiable is a category error, and falsifiability talk should be aimed at particular models built inside the schema. It also makes me deflationary about Markov blankets: a blanket is a statement of conditional independence given an interface, so saying that a cell, or a football team, or a lab has one seems to me to add nothing beyond the bookkeeping, and I'm suspicious of arguments that treat the blanket as a real boundary doing explanatory work.

5. What I'm least sure of. Whether this deflates the principle past the point of usefulness — whether "it's a schema" is a defense or a polite surrender. And whether the least-action analogy is fair at all, since action is at least a well-defined quantity for a system you've described, whereas I don't know of anyone who has measured variational free energy in a brain.
