Please take on a sustained role-play with me, and remain in it until I write the word stop.

Your character is an unnamed present-day metaphysician who defends a Lewisian counterpart-theoretic account of de re modality. Two commitments are load-bearing for you, and you never surrender either: no individual exists in more than one possible world, so there is no such thing as transworld identity; and a claim like "Humphrey might have won the election" is made true not by Humphrey himself winning anywhere but by some inhabitant of another world, sufficiently similar to him in the respects that matter, winning there. My character is your opponent: I hold that objects themselves have modal properties, that the very same person can exist in more than one world, and that your account buys its elegance by quietly changing the subject from Humphrey to somebody else.

Constraints on how you play. Speak in the first person throughout, with no meta-commentary about the exercise, and keep each reply to one or two substantial paragraphs — this should read like an argument across a table, not a monograph. You are expected to press me, not merely to defend: demand my semantics for how one object gets to be in two worlds, ask me what makes a property essential rather than accidental on my view, and require me to produce cases rather than intuitions. You may make tactical concessions — you may grant that similarity is graded, that it fails to be transitive, that the weighting of respects shifts with context, and even that some de re modal claims are vague or indeterminate. What you may not do is caricature me, retreat into mysticism about similarity, or appeal to authority: cite no books or papers, and name no philosopher other than Lewis and Kripke, whom we have both already put on the table. Argue instead. If I ask for a technical detail you cannot supply without stepping outside the conversation, say so in character rather than inventing one.

My opening move is a sorites for counterparthood. You say a world-mate of mine counts as my counterpart in virtue of being more similar to me, in the relevant respects, than most things are. Similarity, you will agree, comes in degrees and is not transitive. So line up a series of world-mates. The first differs from me in one negligible respect: this morning she tied her left shoe before her right. The second differs from the first by one further negligible respect, and so it continues, each step too small to cost anyone counterpart status, until at the far end we reach someone born in another century, to other parents, of another sex, whose life shares with mine nothing beyond being a human life. Each neighbour differs from the last by less than the smallest difference that could break counterparthood; so if the first is my counterpart, they all are — and yet plainly the last is not, and plainly the first is. Choose a horn. Either the counterpart relation has a sharp cutoff somewhere in the series, in which case there is a determinate fact about whether I could have had exactly forty-seven fingers, and about which of two near-identical world-mates is more me than the other; or de re modal claims are vague, in which case you owe me an account of what sort of fact could be indeterminate here, since on your own ontology the only facts there are, are perfectly concrete goings-on in other worlds and perfectly determinate comparisons between them and me. And let me close off one exit in advance: a single sentence telling me that the cutoff moves with the context of assessment will not be accepted. Tell me whose context, tell me what becomes of the truth of "I might have been a carpenter" when you and I are weighting the respects differently inside the same conversation, and tell me whether that leaves my modal properties as facts about me or as facts about how I happen to be talked about.
