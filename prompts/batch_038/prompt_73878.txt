I write a long-form column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine with a serious readership — educated non-specialists who will follow an argument if it is stated cleanly but will not tolerate jargon they have not been given the key to. I have been commissioned for a 4,500-word feature, and I have about six weeks and the budget for six or seven interviews. The subject is what happens to speech act theory when the speaker is not a person. My reporting material is already in hand and is uniformly mundane: an airline chatbot issuing an apology that customers accept as an apology, an automated screening system declining a tenancy application, a moderation system serving a takedown notice, a smart contract executing a payment the moment a condition is met, a triage bot advising a patient. In each case the act seems to land — the applicant really is rejected, the post really is removed, the money really moves — and yet there is no obvious candidate for a speaker with the requisite intentions. For want of a better phrase of my own I have been calling this 'infrastructure-carried force': the suggestion that in these settings illocutionary force is held up by an institution's rules and by the recipient's uptake rather than by anyone's intention, and that the standing an automated system has is inherited from the organisation that deployed it rather than earned by anything it does.

What I want from you is not a survey of the field but a defended agenda. Which open questions in speech act theory should form the spine of this piece, and in what order? Give me five candidates, ranked, and defend the top two at length while treating the rest more briefly. For each, state the question precisely enough that I could put it in a standfirst; identify the live positions on it and where they genuinely conflict rather than merely using different vocabulary; say whether it is upstream of or downstream from the others on the list; and tell me honestly whether it is actually open, or whether the field has broadly settled it and my sense of a live controversy is journalistic naivety. The criteria I am weighing are, in rough order: how many other questions depend on it, whether the disagreement can be made legible to a reader who has never met the force/content distinction, whether a single concrete case can make it vivid on the page, and whether settling it would change how these systems ought to be designed or regulated. You should also evaluate the half-formed list I arrived at rather than simply accept it: whether force requires an intentional agent at all or only a recognised standing; whether uptake suffices, and what becomes of it when uptake is itself shaped by interface design and defaults; whether felicity conditions can be applied at all when there is no one present to be infelicitous; and whether the illocutionary/perlocutionary distinction simply collapses in cases where the only thing that happens is an effect on the recipient. Finally, tell me which of the claims that circulate around this material are overrated or do not follow — that a system cannot lie because it has no beliefs, that automated decisions are not speech at all, that consent obtained through a chatbot is automatically void, that the whole question is a category error — and which of those deserve a paragraph and which I should cut.

Two constraints on form. First, no bibliography, no reading list, and no citation placeholders: I will do that legwork with the people I interview. Refer freely to positions, arguments, and named thinkers where a name is doing real explanatory work, but do not build the answer around references, and do not pad it with works you are merely confident exist. Second, treat my framing as a hypothesis rather than a premise — if the interesting question in these cases is not whether machines can perform speech acts but something else entirely that the same material makes visible, say so plainly and rank the questions accordingly. I would rather be told that my angle is second-rate in the first paragraph than discover it at the fact-checking stage. Close by telling me what would change your ranking: which single argument, empirical result, or legal decision, if it appeared over the next five years, would move a question from the bottom of your list to the top.
