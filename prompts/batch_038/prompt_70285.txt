After thirty-five years in experimental physical chemistry, I have taken up philosophy in retirement, and I find I cannot stop asking what would falsify a claim. The claim I have come to accept is the money pump argument in its general form: an agent whose preferences cycle — who will pay a cent to move from C to B, from B to A, and from A back to C — is practically irrational, since a patient counterparty can drain her for the pleasure of her own intransitivity. What would have to be true for that to be wrong?

I want the defeaters rather than the slogan. In particular: does an agent who foresees the cycle and declines the first trade — the sophisticated-choice reply — show that the argument begs the question against intransitivity, or does it merely push the work onto a diachronic principle that the argument was supposed to establish? Are commitment devices, transaction costs, and the tacit assumption that choices are made sequentially and myopically doing covert work? And are accounts on which an agent chooses in light of anticipated regret, cycling in what she reveals yet never becoming pumpable, genuine counterexamples or mere redescriptions?

Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a defender of the argument would herself concede, and tell me whether the argument has been qualified into safety — and whether that is a problem for a claim about rationality. Please commit to a verdict on the sophisticated-choice reply rather than surveying it, and give your reasons.
