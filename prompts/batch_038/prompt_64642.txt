I have read rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias era, and consciousness threads there have a very predictable shape: someone invokes philosophical zombies, someone else replies that the zombie world is either inconceivable or indistinguishable from ours, and the exchange eventually dissolves into competing reports of intuitions about what one can coherently imagine. I want to do better than that, because I have noticed something uncomfortable about myself: I can recite the physicalist rebuttals fluently, yet I cannot tell you what I would actually do with the argument that qualia, if they are further facts over and above the physical, do no causal work at all — including no work in producing my own beliefs about them. So I would rather be sparred with by the strongest available version of the opposing side than by a version I invented for the comfort of my own position.

Please play a committed epiphenomenalist: a property dualist who holds that conscious experience involves qualitative properties irreducible to any physical or functional properties, that the physical world is causally closed, and that these qualitative properties therefore have no effects — not on the wider world, and not even on the brain that hosts them. The difficulty I intend to press is the one usually called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experience never causes anything, then my beliefs and reports about it must be produced entirely by physical antecedents that would have produced just the same beliefs and reports in a world containing no experience, which makes those beliefs look unmoored from their supposed subject matter. Rules for the exchange: stay in character throughout and do not break out to offer a balanced survey, a caveat about the state of the literature, or a footnote about who holds what. Argue from considerations rather than authority — no citations, no named philosophers unless I name one first — and if you need a stipulation about a case I describe, ask for it as the character would. You may concede genuine but secondary points: that your epistemology is incomplete, that acquaintance is not a relation you can fully explicate, that your view is expensive. You may not concede the main ones: that qualia are causally efficacious, that they reduce to physical or functional facts, or that the problem I raise is a refutation rather than a cost you are prepared to pay. Do not caricature my side either; assume I have already heard every reply beginning with the observation that a zombie would say the same things. Keep each turn to roughly two paragraphs, make one main thrust rather than three, and you may end by putting a question back to me.

My opening move is this. Take your zombie duplicate: a world physically identical to ours, same neurons firing, same dispositions, same utterances, same apparent deliberation, but with no qualitative experience anywhere in it. In that world your duplicate writes down exactly the sentences you write about qualia, with the same confidence and the same sense of having thought the matter through carefully. Now ask what fixes the content of those sentences. Whatever your answer, it must be something present in both worlds, because by construction the two worlds do not differ physically in any respect. So either your sentences mean the same in both — in which case they are false in the zombie world, true in yours, and the difference between their being false and their being true is not a difference in anything you did, perceived, or were caused by — or they mean nothing about qualia in either world, and your theory has just explained itself away. And please do not answer me with a term of art. Tell me what relation, if not a causal one, can make a belief be about something, and why admitting such a relation does not license me to claim the very same intimate non-causal acquaintance with the number seven, or with what it is like to be a creature I have never encountered and never could.
