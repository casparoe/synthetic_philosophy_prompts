Been going back and forth on Dennett's three stances / the real patterns thing for a couple weeks now, and I've realized I don't actually have a handle on when I take an ascription literally versus when I know I'm just talking loosely. So here's my running list of verdicts, mostly from arguing with my housemate about it. Tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Things I say without any hesitation, fully literal:
- My Roomba is "hunting for dust" and gets "confused" in corners. Fine.
- Stockfish "wants the center" and "is worried about its back rank." Fine — and I'd never explain one of its moves in terms of voltage levels even if someone handed me the physics, which seems like a strong claim about what's real.
- A slime mold "solves the maze," i.e., finds the short route to the oat flakes. Real pattern, no question.
- An ant colony "forages" and "recruits." The colony has the pattern; no individual ant does. Real.
- "The company decided to acquire its supplier." I would bet actual money on this, and if you asked me how it's settled I'd point at the board minutes.

Things I refuse to say literally:
- My calculator "believes you typed 2+2 and wants to print 4." Silly. Except the design stance works perfectly there, and it works the same way it works for the Roomba, which I just said was fine. This one bugs me.
- A thermostat "believes the room is too cold." Same problem.
- A storm "wants to head north." I know this is fake because the intentional talk buys me nothing over a weather map.
- A face in the toast. Not a real pattern — even though describing it is enormously shorter than listing every pixel, so compression-by-itself can't be the test.
- A head-and-shoulders formation in a price chart. Also not real.
- "The market expects a rate cut." Not real, and my reason is that the same prices admit rival intentional readings — you could equally read them as fear of inflation. But then why isn't the company's decision hostage to the same objection?

And the pair I genuinely can't reconcile:
- When a chat model says it "wants to make sure it understood your question," I treat that as a real belief-desire pair; it predicts what it does next. When the same model, same weights, says it "believes it has a body" or "feels bad about earlier," I say that's not a belief at all, just a training artifact. Same mechanism, same stance, opposite verdicts.
- Related: an image classifier "sees a cat" and that's fine by me, but stick an adversarial perturbation on a gibbon photo and I flatly deny it "sees a panda" — even though the design-stance explanation is identical in both cases.

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is broken: a pattern is real iff (a) describing things that way is much shorter than an atom-by-atom or pixel-by-pixel account of the same stretch of events, and (b) the short description keeps working when you interfere with the system, not just when you fit it after the fact. I think (b) is what's supposed to kill the toast and the chart pattern, and maybe rescue the slime mold. But I don't see how it separates the two chat-model cases, or the Roomba from the calculator, and I'm not even sure (a) and (b) are doing the same job — it's possible I'm conflating "this stance predicts well" with "this pattern is real" and those turn out to be two different questions, which is exactly the thing I want you to settle for me.

So: state the principle (or a small set of competing candidates) precisely, clause by clause, and say which of my cases each clause is there to handle. Then tell me what it delivers on cases I haven't listed — a self-driving car that classifies a white truck as sky, my smart speaker "hearing" a command from the TV, a chess engine's evaluation of a position that is objectively lost, a person under general anaesthetic, and me explaining a choice I made ten minutes ago with a reason I probably invented on the spot. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts the best principle simply cannot save — if one has to go, I'd rather you pick the victim than hedge.
