I'm a senior double-majoring in history and philosophy, and this term I'm in a historiography seminar — the kind where you read about how history gets made instead of actual history — while also doing an independent study at the county records office two towns over, where I'm transcribing 19th-century institutional paperwork for credit. We just did Trouillot's *Silencing the Past*, and the four-moments idea, where silences can enter at the making of sources, at the assembling of the archive, at the retrieval into narrative, and at the assignment of retrospective significance, is one of those things I now can't turn off. My problem is that it doesn't tell me what to actually do with a gap when I'm sitting there with a ledger. My roommate's a stats major and he says this is obviously a missing-data problem and I should just ask whether the data is missing at random or not, which sounds smart but he has no feel for the cases, so I want to write out my own gut verdicts and find out whether there's a principle underneath them or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Most are test cases I made up to check against the real ones I'm working with, so nothing here is load-bearing factually.

Here's where I land. (1) A monastery's cellarer accounts log wine deliveries weekly for forty years, near-exhaustively; one named vineyard disappears from 1512 to 1515 and then comes back. I treat that gap as decent evidence they genuinely stopped buying from it. (2) A colonial district court enrolls every litigant's deposition for thirty years, and in one district there is not a single deposition by a woman. I flatly refuse to read that as "women didn't testify" — I read it as produced. Structurally that's the same shape as (1): habitual, near-exhaustive record, one category conspicuously absent. I feel the difference intensely and can't articulate it beyond "people aren't wine." (3) An estate ledger records the births of enslaved children with obsessive care, because they were capital, and never records a death. Here I think the birth-exhaustiveness licenses nothing about deaths, and worse, the thoroughness is itself explained by a valuation interest, so I don't get to argue "this book is meticulous, therefore its omissions mean something" for anything outside what the interest covered. That seems to wreck (1), since the cellarer also had an interest, in tracking stock. (4) A mission station's baptismal roll for one year is less than half the years on either side. I take that as real decline, precisely because the missionary had every incentive to inflate his numbers — under-reporting cuts against his interest, so it's credible. But in (3) I said motive-driven recording disqualifies the inference, and here motive is doing the opposite work, and my stated reasons don't separate the two. (5) Fire-insurance maps show one city block empty for twenty years; it later turns out the block was frame shanties nobody bothered to insure or map. My verdict is that concluding vacancy would have been wrong *even before* I learned that, because the map's coverage class was insurable property and a shanty was outside it by design. So there's a clause about the intended domain of a recording practice that I can apparently only ever learn after the fact, from somewhere else. (6) A university email system from 2016 on retains everything, and all forty thousand messages from one office are searchable; there is nothing at all about a controversial closure decision. I take that as evidence the decision happened in a channel I can't see, not as evidence there was no decision. Total-capture archives seem to invert the inference and I don't know why they should. (7) An official purged under an authoritarian regime: the name scraped out of group photographs, the personnel file destroyed. I think the erasure is affirmative evidence the person existed and fell, and I'd date the fall from the last appearance. So a deliberately produced silence is doing *more* evidential work here than an accidental one in (1). (8) The one I keep going back and forth on, and it's from my actual project: an orphanage admissions ledger, 1901–1930, gives a father's name for maybe 15% of entries. Three readings are all live — genuinely unknown, known but withheld by the mother, or known but never asked for by the clerk. My verdict is "can't tell," and I don't know if that's principled humility or a cop-out.

My tentative principle is that a gap licenses a negative inference only if you can independently establish that the recording practice's coverage class included the sort of thing that's missing, and that nobody with the power to prevent or destroy the record had an interest in its absence. Which I suspect is unusable, because both conditions can only be established from other records that went through the same production process — so I seem to need a complete archive to justify reasoning from an incomplete one. I've been calling this the bootstrapping problem for want of anything better and I half-suspect it's fatal. So: what principle, or small set of candidates, actually gets (1) through (7)? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and give me its verdict on (8) — including whether a principle that handles the others is even permitted to say can't-tell there, or whether something in the ledger's own structure could break the three-way tie. Then test it on a case I haven't given you a verdict for: a 1970s union local's minutes name every member disciplined, except that during one eight-month stretch the minutes get unusually thin and generic. Tell me what the principle says before you guess what I think. And be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one I should give up, plus whether the missing-at-random framing genuinely maps onto this or is a red herring that's making me feel more rigorous than I am. Please write in American English.
