I run a small specialty printing business — twelve people, letterpress work and short-run packaging. For five years nearly all of our board stock came from one merchant, on terms they set themselves because the volume was worth courting at the time. Two things have happened since the spring. In April a pallet arrived with the wrong grain direction, which meant we scrapped a client's run and ate about four thousand dollars. In September a delivery promised for a Tuesday came the following Monday, we missed a deadline, and we had to discount the job. Both times the merchant's rep explained that the fault lay with the mill or the haulier rather than with them, and both times they issued a credit covering a fraction of the loss — a gesture, not a settlement.

Here is the part I keep turning over. Our contract runs to the end of next summer, and the merchant knows we are not renewing it, because I told them so myself in March; we are consolidating onto a national supplier with better rates. That leaves me eleven months of orders still to place with them. Since that conversation I have noticed small changes. Samples that used to arrive overnight now take a week. A rep who used to answer within the hour now takes a day. The September credit was smaller than the April one for a larger problem. None of this is pinnable and none of it is a breach of anything.

I have had two pieces of advice and they point in opposite directions. My accountant says to deduct the full loss from the next invoice without discussion — his word for it was a signal, and that if I don't send it now I'll be sending it every month until the contract expires. My operations manager says the opposite: with eleven months left there is nothing worth protecting anymore, so a fight buys me nothing and puts the eleven months of deliveries I still depend on at risk; better to stay pleasant and quietly qualify a backup merchant as insurance.

What I want is not a decision dressed up as reassurance. I would like someone to work out, as carefully as they can, whether these two pieces of advice rest on different pictures of what my next eleven months actually look like, and which picture is the more realistic. Does the fact that we both know when the relationship ends change what either of us should be doing now? And if it does, does that change run backwards — that is, does knowing the last order is eleven months away make their September slippage the sensible thing for them, and make my April patience the sensible thing for me, and then make the order before that sensible to skimp on too, all the way back to this week? Or is that kind of reasoning too tidy to describe real people at a real company, who will be dealing with each other again in some form, whose reps are looking for their next job, and whose conduct toward me is visible to my competitors and to other merchants?

Then there is the thing I cannot settle, which is whether the two failures were them testing me or two ordinary accidents in a bad year. How much should my answer depend on which of those it was? And is there anything I can build into the next two orders that would tell me, without also telling them what I am testing for?

I know this is a fairly ordinary business problem and that I may be overthinking it. Please be plain about where you are confident and where you are guessing, and finish by naming the single point in your answer that a better-informed person would most likely dispute.
