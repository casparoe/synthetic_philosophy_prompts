Before you write anything evaluative, spend one short paragraph reconstructing what you take my thesis to be and what would have to be true of Pascal's texts for it to hold. **Draft: Pascal's Quietism Is Not a Prudence Claim**

1. The fragments on justice and force are usually read as a cynical realism: justice without force is impotent, force without justice is tyrannical, and since we could not make justice strong we have instead made strength just, so that the two are joined and peace, the highest of goods, results. On that reading Pascal holds that legitimacy is a mask worn by power. I want to argue that this reading gets the direction of his claim wrong, and that the error is not merely interpretive but has consequences for how much work his position can do in political theory.

2. Distinguish two ways in which force can stand to a standard of justice. On what I will call a *target-directed* account, justice is identifiable independently of enforcement, and force is legitimate insofar as it tracks that standard; a failure of enforcement is a performance failure of an apparatus pointed at something already there. On an *output-directed* account, there is no independently articulable standard at the level of positive law at all: what justice amounts to, for enforceable rules, is fixed by which rules the prevailing force in fact enforces. Force does not track the standard; it selects among candidates and thereby makes one of them the standard.

3. My claim is that Pascal is output-directed about positive justice and target-directed about justice as such, and that the two attitudes are held apart from each other by his doctrine of the three orders. What I take to support the second half: his insistence that laws are obeyed because they are laws and not because they are just, and that whoever obeys them on account of their justice is obeying a justice of his own invention; his repeated use of rivers and mountain ranges as the boundaries of truth and error, so that the same rule is just on one bank and unjust on the other; his treatment of custom as the entire foundation of a law's authority for no other reason than that it is custom; his remark that civil war is the greatest of evils; and, more broadly, his view that we no longer have the true — that natural law exists but our reason has corrupted it, and that true justice has been lost to us rather than merely disputed among us. Pascal does not deny that there is such a thing as true justice. He denies that we are in a position to identify it.

4. The consequence, and the real point of the paper, is this. If positive justice is output-directed, then reform is not merely dangerous but unintelligible from inside the political order. A reformer's complaint must appeal to a standard that is not available to him; and if he can state one, he is already appealing to something outside the order of custom, and Pascal offers him nothing of that kind except the order of charity, which is not a political resource. So his conservatism is not a claim about the costs of instability. It is not that civil war is the worst of evils and therefore we should keep the existing order — that would be a prudential calculation, revisable in light of better facts about how costly upheaval actually is. It is rather that the existing order is the only thing there is to compare it to.

5. The obvious objection: Pascal plainly thinks we can recognize particular laws as unjust, or his remark about the river bounding justice would be empty, since the remark depends on our seeing that the identical rule is just here and unjust there. I want to reply that recognizing an injustice is not the same as possessing an actionable standard. I admit that this reply is thin, and whether the paper survives it is one of the things I most want your judgment on.
