ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on grounding — the idea that some things are the way they are in virtue of other things, like how a statue is grounded in the lump of clay, or how a moral fact (if there are any) would be grounded in natural facts. And basically everyone explains it with buildings: the world is a structure, the derivative stuff is the upper floors, the fundamental stuff is the foundation. A friend of mine used exactly that picture to argue there has to be a bottom layer. His version went something like: a building with no foundation is just stuff hanging in the air, so a world where every fact is grounded in some further fact, all the way down with nothing at the bottom, isn't a world that's held up at all — it's turtles all the way down, and that's not a coherent arrangement, it's nothing. He added that the reason people keep arguing about infinite regresses of grounding is that they havent noticed the metaphor is doing the work for them.

I want to push back but I dont really know how, partly because the building picture is genuinely how I picture it too. Can you audit it for me? Lay out the mapping explicitly — what plays the foundation, the upper floors, the load, the act of building, the architect if there is one, the ground under the foundation — and then go through where it breaks. I care most about which of the breaks actually threaten his argument and which are just cosmetic. Like: does it matter that a real foundation gets laid before the floors in time, when grounding is supposed to be an "in virtue of" relation and not a causal one? Does the metaphor sneak in the idea that the bottom layer has to be doing something active, holding things up, when maybe fundamentality is just the absence of anything further to appeal to? Does it assume the floors are separate objects stacked together, so that the world has to be decomposable into discrete levels at all?

Then two things I've been chewing on that I'd want you to take seriously rather than wave away:

1. I read that some people think the whole universe is the fundamental thing and the parts are the derivative bits, which feels like the foundation being on top — the building hanging from its roof. Does the building picture have any room for that, or does it just quietly assume bottom-up? If it can't accommodate it, is that a strike against the metaphor or a strike against that view?

2. I keep picturing an arch instead: the stones lean on each other, no one of them is special, the whole thing stands because of the shape, and if you pull any single stone out it comes down. If mutual grounding is coherent — and I know the objection is that grounding has to be asymmetric and irreflexive, so nothing can help hold itself up — is the arch a better picture? What does the arch get right that the building gets wrong, and what does the arch also get wrong, especially about there being no fact of the matter about which stone is doing the supporting?

Finish with an actual verdict: does the foundation argument for a bottom layer go through once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? And if the building picture should be retired, what would you put in its place for someone like me who needs some image in their head in order to think about this at all — or is the honest answer that there isn't a good one and the image is the problem?
