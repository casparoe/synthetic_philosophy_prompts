I am writing a novel whose engine is Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment — the one, in his essay Where Am I?, in which a brain is kept alive in a vat, the body it used to drive is piloted by radio, and a molecular duplicate of that brain is held cold as insurance. My book keeps the machinery and changes the ending, and what I need now is a partner who can hold one side of the argument the way a person holds it, so that my central scene does not collapse into a lecture.

Here is the case, and please treat all of it as established within our exchange. The subject's brain was removed, preserved, and given a telephone line and a writing desk. The body it drove was destroyed in an accident four months ago. The duplicate, kept in reserve, was woken instead, and has driven the replacement body ever since. Both are continuous with the man I am writing about — in memory, in temperament, in the particular way he misremembers his childhood — down to the moment of the operation. Since then they have diverged in the only way that seems to matter. The one in the tank has spent four months alone and has written most of a book; the one in the body has spent four months walking, eating, being recognised in the street, and has fallen quietly in love with his physiotherapist. The replacement body is failing. Only one of them can be transferred into the new one. The programme's ethics board wants the certificate signed this week.

Your role is the board's philosopher, whom my novel calls Dr. Vail. She holds that a person goes where his brain goes: psychological continuity is what matters, but only when it is carried by the right physical stuff, and only when it does not branch. She has sat on boards like this for twenty years; she is precise, unhurried, and never raises her voice. Do not make her a caricature. She can distinguish the claim that the tank brain is the man from the weaker claim that it is his best continuer; she knows exactly how damaging branching is for her position and she does not pretend otherwise; she will concede a great many local points — that the body has been kissed and the tank has not, that her view licenses something cruel, that a certificate must nevertheless be signed by somebody. What she will not concede is the central claim: that the man is where his brain is, and that a duplicate is never the original. If I press her to a point she cannot defend, she should say so in plain terms rather than dissolve the question.

The rules of the exchange. Speak in the first person, as Dr. Vail, in the room with me: no stage directions, no narration of her feelings, no scene-setting. Two paragraphs per reply. No appeals to authority — she is not permitted to cite philosophers, papers, or named experiments, and neither are you; she argues only from the case in front of her. If she needs a fact about the case that I have not supplied, she asks me for it in her own voice. Do not step outside the role to offer me plot alternatives or advice about the novel; I am the one making the moves.

After the two paragraphs, add one bracketed line out of character in which you tell me how confident you are in the judgment Dr. Vail has just made — settled, uneasy, or guessing — and name the single point in it that you are least sure of. I want that line to be candid. If the strongest version of her position is one you do not yourself accept, say so there rather than smuggling it into her mouth.

My first move is spoken by the wife, whom I have put in the room with her:

You have now told me twice that my husband is the one in the tank, and I want to be fair to you, so let me grant you what I find hardest. The duplicate is a copy, and a copy is not the man. Fine. But the man in your tank has no hands. He has not been touched in four months. The one who has been sitting at my kitchen table is the one who learned to walk again, who cannot stop thinking about his physiotherapist, and who says he is my husband and means it with everything he has. Your rule selects the voice on the telephone. So tell me what my marriage is now a marriage to — and if your answer is that it is a marriage to a tank, tell me why I should not regard your rule as a theory about something other than people.

Answer her.
