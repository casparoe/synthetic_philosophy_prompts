A large retail sportsbook runs its trading desk roughly as follows. It quotes prices on every outcome with a built-in margin, so that the implied probabilities across all outcomes of an event sum to well over one hundred percent. It moves those prices chiefly in response to where its customers' money is going, sometimes with no change whatever in the underlying prospects of the teams. It lets customers settle an open bet early at a price the firm calculates itself, and that price is worse than the bet's current fair value on any reasonable estimate. And it quietly restricts or closes the accounts of customers who consistently beat its closing prices, while leaving the accounts of persistent losers wide open. A separate class of arbitrage bettors does the mirror image: they combine prices across competing firms to lock in a riskless profit, and are banned for it.

Philosophers have a family of results — call them the coherence theorems — holding that an agent whose dispositions to accept and offer bets cannot be represented by one consistent set of probabilities is liable to be made a sure loser, and that this liability is what makes incoherent belief irrational rather than merely eccentric.

Reconstruct the theory of belief and practical rationality that this industry presupposes. Specifically: do the quoted prices express the firm's degrees of belief at all, or something of a different kind entirely — and if the party that reliably profits is the one that refuses to treat its own prices as two-way commitments, what does that reveal about the idealizations the theorems require? Is the ordinary customer who accepts the margin being systematically exploited in exactly the way the theorems describe, and if so, why is his guaranteed loss not thereby evidence of his irrationality — especially given that the same customers buy insurance at a loading, which also guarantees an expected loss, and nobody calls that a failure of reason? Say what separates the two cases, or whether nothing does. Then distinguish, feature by feature, what the practice strictly requires of the implicit theory, what merely sits comfortably alongside it, and what is incidental to any theory at all. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in the firm, in the customer, or in the theorems themselves — if we took seriously the claim that only coherent agents are rational bettors.
