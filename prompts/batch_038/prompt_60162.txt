Adam Smith's impartial spectator and Harsanyi's aggregation theorem are both offered as procedures for converting a plurality of partial standpoints into a single impartial verdict, but they operate on very different raw material: Smith asks the agent to imagine how a well-informed bystander would feel about the sentiments actually on display, whereas Harsanyi requires that a social ordering over lotteries satisfy the expected utility axioms alongside the Pareto condition. Compare the two devices as accounts of impartiality. How does each fix the weight to be given to one person's gains and losses relative to another's, and what must each assume in order to do so? In answering, consider whether Harsanyi's theorem buys its determinacy at the price of leaving the aggregation weights ethically underdetermined — the Pareto condition constrains only their sign, not their relative magnitude, and equal weights require a further premise the theorem itself does not supply — while Smith's spectator acquires traction on those weights precisely by inheriting the partialities of the community that formed it. Is that trade-off a real advantage for either side, or do the two procedures fail to be genuine rivals because they are answering different questions?
