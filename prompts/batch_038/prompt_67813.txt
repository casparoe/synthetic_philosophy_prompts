I am the staff analyst for a state legislative committee reviewing a lobbying-reform bill whose central provision would prohibit any lobbyist or lobbying firm from accepting compensation contingent on an outcome: a percentage of an appropriation secured, a fee payable only if a contract is awarded or a bill passes. At yesterday's hearing one member opposed the prohibition on the ground that the same conduct could simply be disclosed — contingent arrangements filed publicly within forty-eight hours — and then, in his words, sunlight is the best disinfectant, so the committee should not ban what the public can see and judge for itself. I have to draft the staff brief on this objection, and I am not sure how to take the maxim, because it seems to slide between several quite different propositions.

Could you set out the distinct claims it might be making? I want a menu rather than a single gloss. For each candidate reading, state the mechanism it presupposes — reputational deterrence, better-informed voting, enabling some third party such as a reporter, prosecutor, or watchdog to act, or nothing more than the existence of a public record — then say whether it is a platitude nobody would dispute, a substantive claim that could be true or false, or one you think is actually false, and finally whether it would support this member's conclusion that disclosure can substitute for prohibition.

Please separate the readings along lines such as the following. Whether sunlight means data merely available or data actually processed, summarized, and attributable to named individuals. Whether the wrong publicity is supposed to cure is one that depends on secrecy at all, or one whose harm survives being fully known — the sponsor's stated worry is not that contingency fees are hidden but that they distort what the lobbyist is paid to want. Whether the maxim asserts that disclosure suffices or only that it helps. What disclosure is claimed to be best relative to: bans, caps, licensing, structural separation, enforcement staff. And whether the relevant audience is in a position to act, given that the losers from an inflated appropriation are diffuse taxpayers who will never read a filing.

Also treat the possibility that publicity is counterproductive here — that filing requirements push compensation into arrangements indistinguishable from ordinary consulting, expose small or unpopular clients, or hand rivals commercial intelligence — and say which readings survive that objection and which do not.

Close by identifying the reading the member most likely means, what would have to be true for that reading to carry his conclusion, and what he would have to concede if it does not. I do not need case law or a bibliography; I need the logical geography drawn precisely enough that the committee can see where the equivocation, if any, occurs.
