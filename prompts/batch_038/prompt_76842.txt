I am a fourth-year doctoral student in intellectual history, and my committee has asked me to submit a written statement of my methodological commitments before the prospectus defense. What follows is my honest draft. I suspect it does not hang together, but I cannot locate where it fails, and I would rather be told plainly than reassured.

The dissertation reconstructs a group of mid-nineteenth-century European reformers whose projects failed. My framework is loosely Nietzschean — the second Untimely Meditation, on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — though the commitments below are mine, not his.

(1) *Fidelity first.* A reading that is useful to me but misdescribes the archive is simply a bad reading, and I would abandon it. I do not believe narrative can be neutral; I do believe it can be checked, and that checkability is what makes the result history rather than rhetoric.

(2) *Anti-presentism.* It is a mistake to judge these people by standards they could not have possessed. My chapters ought to be usable by a reader who shares none of my politics.

(3) *Quarantine.* My politics live in my choice of topic and of cases — I work on failed movements because I think my generation needs to see failure at close range — and nowhere else. Interpretation is where they must not appear.

(4) *Monumental ambition.* Several of these figures were genuinely admirable, and I intend the book to make readers want to be like them. A portrait that is merely accurate and not moving has failed at something, and I will not apologize for wanting the moving.

(5) *Critical ambition.* I also refuse to write hagiography: part of the job is to expose what these projects cost, and especially what they cost the people they claimed to serve.

(6) *Availability.* Where the sources show that contemporaries said the thing clearly and were ignored, I treat that as evidence that the reformers could have known better, and I criticize them accordingly. This is how I propose to square (5) with (2): the standard applied is theirs, not mine.

(7) *Antiquarian loyalty.* I feel a real obligation to the small institutions, families, and local archives that preserved this material against indifference, and I regard that loyalty as a virtue in a historian rather than a bias.

(8) *No authority from greatness.* That an admirable person believed something is not evidence for it. I am not in the business of arguments from authority.

(9) *Inheritance.* I have read the warning that the critic of the past is himself a product of what he condemns, and I think I can live with it: I claim no standpoint outside my own formation, only a willingness to be made uncomfortable by it.

What I need from you is an audit, not a lecture. Specifically: (a) identify which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another and which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging; (b) for each genuine tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision looks like, and what that revision costs — in terms I could defend to a committee that includes both a strict archival historian and a critical theorist; (c) tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether any commitment here is doing hidden work, that is, presented as the solution to a problem while actually being the site of the leak. Please do not recapitulate the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes for me; I have read the essay and so has my committee. Write the response in American English.
