I'm a retired invertebrate paleontologist — thirty-odd years describing fossil clams and brachiopods out of drill cores and arguing about where to draw the species boundaries. Only picked up history and philosophy seriously in the last few years, so bear with me if this is a naive question.

Here's the thing that keeps nagging at me. I've been reading about "collapse" — the Classic Maya lowlands, the eastern Mediterranean palace societies around 1200 BCE, Easter Island. And in nearly every one of these studies, the quantity that actually gets measured and dated isn't people. It's the last monument carrying a date. It's the king-list that just stops. It's the fact that nobody raises a new temple or carves a new statue for a generation. It's the imported prestige goods dropping out of the deposits. Then somebody lines that curve up against a drought signal from a stalagmite or a lake core and concludes: climate caused the collapse, and here's roughly when. And yet in the discussion section of the very same paper they'll say — quite correctly — that the Maya didn't vanish, that millions of Maya are alive now, that the northern cities went on prospering, that what ended was a particular kind of kingship and a particular habit of writing dates on stone.

So what does that measuring practice presuppose about what a society *is*? I want the implicit ontology reconstructed, not a survey of the debate. What has to be true of societies for the last dated inscription to be the death certificate? Is the presupposition that a society is identical to its political-administrative apparatus, or something subtler about complexity being what makes a society that particular society? Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out — a society as a population, as a set of transmitted practices, as a body of people who recognize each other? And is the field being inconsistent, or is the discussion-section disclaimer perfectly compatible with dating the collapse to a century? Point out where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories would license exactly the same measurements.

There's an idea from my old trade I want you to test. We used to distinguish true extinction from pseudoextinction: a lineage doesn't die, it just changes enough that the diagnostic characters we named it by are gone, so its "extinction" is partly an artifact of the taxonomy. Is collapse-talk doing the same job — is a society pseudoextinct when it stops being diagnosable by the traits we used to name it? Or does the analogy break, and if so where: societies don't have to have descendant lineages, regional depopulation is not a renaming, and nobody is proposing that the Classic Maya evolved into something.

Also: if you swapped in a different ontology — say, a society is its members and their wellbeing — what would have to change in practice? Would skeletal stress markers, stature, settlement counts even be resolvable to a century, and does the resolution problem undercut the whole project? And finally, is there a smuggled value claim in treating loss of complexity as loss, period.

Two things I'd ask of you beyond the analysis. Don't invent references for me, I'll chase those down myself. But do tell me what would change your mind: which argument or piece of evidence would push you to a different verdict about what the practice presupposes, and where you think my framing — an old taxonomist looking for a taxonomic error — is most likely to be leading you somewhere wrong.
