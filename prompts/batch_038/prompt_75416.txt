I spent thirty-eight years in a national measurement laboratory, occupied chiefly with the calibration of instruments and with the question of what entitles one to call a reading accurate. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I have lately been working through the literature on antinatalism. A chapter in a trade-press book on pessimism that I picked up summarizes David Benatar's asymmetry argument, from Better Never to Have Been: The Harm of Coming into Existence (2006), as follows:

'The asymmetry is easy to state. Pain is bad and pleasure is good; but the absence of pain is good even when there is nobody around to enjoy that good, whereas the absence of pleasure is bad only when there is somebody it deprives. Set the two scenarios side by side — the child who is born and the child who never is — and the comparison settles itself. The born child has some bad and some good; the never-born child has a good (no pain) and nothing that counts as a bad (no pleasure missed). A good plus a non-bad beats a good plus a bad, so coming into existence is always a harm and procreation is always wrong. Benatar does not flinch from this: he concedes that most of us are glad to have been born, but dismisses that as a product of optimism bias and adaptation, and since the arithmetic comes out the other way he holds that humanity should stop reproducing and let the species lapse — as promptly as decency permits. Anyone who says life has been good to them has simply never done the sums.'

I should like a careful audit of this paragraph rather than a general introduction to the debate. Please take it sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a defender of Benatar's view would sign off on, which are fair popular simplifications, and which are outright misstatements — in particular, whether "procreation is always wrong" is his claim or an inflation of it, and whether he really endorses a prompt lapse of the species. I should also like to know whether the paragraph is right to make the argument depend upon the empirical pessimism about optimism bias and adaptation, or whether the asymmetry is meant to stand on its own even if a typical life contains a surplus of pleasure; and whether the charge implicit in the final sentence — that Benatar contradicts himself about people's gladness at having been born — has any force, or whether it works only by running together two questions he keeps apart.

Two further matters I cannot settle for myself, and on which I would value your judgment. First, in my own trade we never credited a system with a benefit merely because a hazard failed to materialize: a containment failure that did not occur was recorded as an event that harmed nobody, and the absence of a victim is not itself a beneficiary. Does that analogy show the third clause of the asymmetry — that the absence of pain is good even when no one enjoys that good — to be empty, or is there a principled reply that preserves it, and if so, does it become an impersonal value claim rather than a claim about anyone's interests? Second, when I lay the four clauses out as a two-by-two table, the conclusion appears to require that "not bad" be treated as something weighable alongside "good" within one column, while the two goods sitting in different columns are never traded off against one another. Is that legitimate bookkeeping, or does the argument depend on it in a way that begs the question? Once the paragraph's errors are corrected, is there still an argument there that deserves my attention?
