I am at work on a novel whose third act turns on a single transaction, and I have found that I cannot write the scene until I know exactly what the two sides would have agreed on had they been more honest with each other than my characters are. So I am asking you to draft the document they never produced.

The transaction. A state has decommissioned a nineteenth-century penitentiary. A memorial trust, subsisting on small donations and one modest state appropriation, proposes to convert it into an archive and museum. A corporation whose founding capital came substantially from convict leasing in that same state offers to endow the site in perpetuity, to open its own corporate records to the trust's historians, and to fund a permanent education staff. In exchange it asks for its name on the education wing, one seat of eleven on the board, and an undertaking that the interpretive materials will describe the historical practice in language its general counsel has reviewed. The trust's director is prepared to sign. A descendants' council and two of the trust's own historians hold that the deal cannot be taken without destroying the thing it pays for.

The two camps. On one side, a position I will call the trade-permitting view: broadly, that where parties with different values can each obtain something they care about more than what they surrender, the exchange is morally available; that such trades are how pluralistic institutions get built at all; and that refusal is a costly purity whose bill is presented mainly to people who were never asked to be pure. On the other side, a position I will call the trade-refusing view: broadly, that some goods are not the sort of thing that can enter an exchange without ceasing to be what they are; that a memorial's authority rests precisely on its not being purchasable; and that a trust which lends its cooperation to the rehabilitation of a corporate memory becomes a party to it, whatever the endowment buys afterward.

I want the joint statement of a real adversarial collaboration between these two — a text both could sign, not a truce that flatters either. Please give me:

1. A numbered list of at least eight claims both sides accept. These must be substantive rather than diplomatic: specific enough that a reader who knew only the two labels could not have predicted them. Include at least one claim about what is genuinely lost if the deal is refused, at least one about what is genuinely altered if it is accepted, and at least one that each side would find mildly expensive to concede.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the question at issue; the trade-permitting formulation, worded so that side would sign it; the trade-refusing formulation, likewise; whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal; and, if genuine, what would settle or at least move it. No formulation may smuggle the other side's conclusion in as a premise, and no column may be written in language designed to be found objectionable.

3. An explicit diagnosis of which disagreements are verbal, and which words are doing the damage. My suspicion is that one side keeps hearing compromise where the other keeps saying sale, and that neither has noticed; if you agree, show where the substitution occurs, and if you think the substitution is only apparent, say so and argue it.

4. Three cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. For each, name the case, argument, or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance of the result. At least one crux should concern something checkable in the world rather than a matter of principle — for instance, what becomes of interpretive independence and of the funded content after comparable agreements have been signed and lived under elsewhere.

5. A closing section in which each side states what it would have to give up in order to have been wrong, followed by whatever the two can jointly say about the penitentiary: a recommendation if one is reachable, and if not, the exact remaining shape of their disagreement, including whether it reduces to a further empirical question on which they expect to differ.

Write the document as though it were to be signed at the end of a long and tiring day by two people who have come to respect each other and still cannot agree: somewhere between twelve hundred and eighteen hundred words, no apparatus of scholarship, no bibliography, no named authorities — the reasoning alone, in prose my characters could plausibly have written. Where you find that you cannot draft a formulation one side would genuinely accept, say so plainly and explain the obstruction, rather than supplying a sentence that looks neutral and is not.
