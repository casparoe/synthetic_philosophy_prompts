Two months until I have to hand in a PhD proposal on cognitive penetration of perception, and my supervisors main criticism of the draft is fair: chapter one is going to be a pile of definitions rather than an argument unless I can say exactly what would have to be true — and shown — before the thesis claim is worth making. So help me write the desiderata. What does a study, or a theory, have to deliver before it has earned the right to say that cognition penetrates perception?

Fix the purpose like this, since adequacy is relative to it: I want penetration in the sense that would matter to the epistemology of perception, i.e. the sense in which, if it's real, a perceptual seeming stops being an independent check on what you already believe and starts inheriting your biases. That means I care a lot about the gap between a belief merely causing a change in what is experienced, and a belief figuring in the experience in a way that makes the experience itself evaluable, as good or bad reasoning. My worry is that half the literature slides between those two without noticing, and I want the requirement stated sharply enough that I can tell which side a given result falls on.

For each desideratum, give me the consideration behind it plus a concrete case that satisfies it and one that fails it — from the kinds of results people actually point to: value-and-size judgements with coins, the hunger studies where food looks bigger (and the follow-ups that couldnt reproduce the effect), ambiguous figures where the label you've been given changes which one you see, memory colour effects where a genuinely grey banana looks faintly yellow, the shooter-bias style paradigms. Then sort the whole list into three piles: genuine requirements; methodological prejudices of one camp that have hardened into requirements (I'm thinking of the insistence that every effect be shown to survive demand characteristics, and the preference for stimulus-driven measures over reports); and things that just restate what penetration means and therefore can never be evidence for or against it.

The part I most want: I think at least two of the requirements cant be jointly met. If a perceptual effect has to be measured, and every measurement on offer is a judgement about what someone saw, then demanding an effect that can't be attributed to judgement is demanding something no experiment could deliver — which would make the debate unfalsifiable by construction rather than merely hard. Tell me whether that's right, or whether there's a route out (adaptation aftereffects, eye movements, matching tasks, some response-time design I haven't thought of), and if there is a route out, say which requirement it costs you. If the collision is real, the list is my argument and I need to know precisely where it bites and which side it hurts more.

Finish with a few paragraphs, no more: which single requirement does the most damage to the most-cited results in the area, and does a modularist or a liberal account come out better against the list as a whole. Skip the history and the name-checking, I've read the surveys already.
