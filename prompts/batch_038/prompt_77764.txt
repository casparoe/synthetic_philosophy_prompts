I am preparing a session for a first-year seminar in the philosophy of mathematics: twenty-two students, one hundred minutes, most of whom have taken calculus but no proof-based course and no game theory. The unit's theme is what a proof actually gives you, and this session is devoted to existence proofs that carry no witness — the family of strategy-stealing arguments, of which the standard illustration establishes that the first player can force a win in Chomp on any rectangular bar bigger than a single poisoned square, without ever naming a winning first move.

Please design the in-class activity. Students should run into the argument by playing, and should be able to state it themselves before anyone says the words "strategy stealing."

The session needs to deliver three things at once. First, the felt difference between proving that a winning strategy exists and having one: at some point the students must be in a position where they are certain who wins and utterly unable to act on it. Second, a working grasp of the hypotheses the theft depends on — not as a list recited back to me, but as conditions they can see being satisfied or violated in the games in front of them. Third, an encounter with the objection that the argument proves too little to be worth calling knowledge, which is the philosophical point I actually want the hour to serve.

Requirements for the design:

1. **The game.** State the rules completely and unambiguously. You may use Chomp, a Hex-like game, a variant, or something of your own devising; whichever you choose, fix the exact board sizes we will play and check for those sizes that the conditions the argument needs genuinely hold. A single game must finish in under four minutes so that several rounds fit, and it must be playable with graph paper and two colours of counters.

2. **Two boards, two proofs.** Pair a size at which students can plausibly find an explicit strategy for themselves — a mirroring or pairing line, say — with a size at which the theft is the only route available to them, and build the comparison into the structure of the session so that they register the difference in informational yield between the two proofs of the same kind of claim.

3. **Stage the theft.** Include a round in which one player literally copies the other's discovered strategy after making an arbitrary opening move, and hits the case where the copied prescription has already been rendered redundant or unavailable by that opening move. Engineer this moment deliberately rather than hoping it arises; students' reflex will be to conclude that the copying has broken down, and the activity should get them to see that redundancy is not breakdown.

4. **Sabotage.** Small groups must construct a symmetric, finite, deterministic, draw-free game in which the second player nevertheless wins, and must identify which hypothesis their game breaks. You should supply at least one such game yourself, with a proof sketch that the second player wins it, and an account of why it is instructive rather than a degenerate parity gotcha. If you judge that no genuinely instructive example exists, say so and describe the nearest approximation.

5. **Stakes.** At some point students commit in writing to a prediction about a board larger than any they have played, and are then shown that the proof they have just accepted licenses the prediction while giving them no means of executing it.

6. **Debrief.** Map specific moments of play onto specific hypotheses, and state those hypotheses in the exact form the activity teaches. Flag where the popular one-line formulation is loose: what symmetry has to mean here, what "an extra move can never hurt" has to mean, whether the conclusion is that the first player wins or only that the second player does not, and how the presence of possible draws changes what follows.

7. **Failure modes.** Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with concrete fixes — at least five, including the two or three students who solve everything immediately, the pairs that stall into silence, the timing overrun, and the misunderstanding I most expect: that a proof of the existence of a winning strategy has been mistaken for a winning strategy.

8. **Honest limits.** On every board we can realistically play in a classroom, exhaustive search is in principle within reach, so the sense of non-constructivity cannot come from the board in front of them. Tell me what the activity therefore cannot reproduce, and what I have to supply by hand in the closing minutes.

I do not need a bibliography or citations; refer to games, arguments, and positions by name where that is useful. Please give me a minute-by-minute timeline alongside the rules, materials list, group sizes, and the facilitator script for the transitions only — not for the content, which the play should carry.
