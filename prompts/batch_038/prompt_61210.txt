I am a fourth-year doctoral student, and this term I have been left in charge of the metric-of-distributive-justice block of our department's graduate seminar while my supervisor is on leave. The room is a mix of philosophy students and public-policy students, and I have a problem: every one of them has already met the standard illustrations several times over. The disabled cyclist, the man with cultivated expensive tastes, the fasting ascetic, the village that is poor but contented — the moment any of these appears on the handout, the discussion collapses into recollection of what Sen or Dworkin was supposed to be showing, and nobody actually reasons about the case in front of them. I tried over the weekend to write my own scenario from scratch and failed three times, because each of my drafts turned out to be a stock case in disguise: once I stripped out the impairment, or the costly preference, or the deprivation-conditioned satisfaction, the disagreement between the metrics evaporated. So I want to commission the case properly, with the constraints stated in advance.

Here is the specification. Build a single scenario in which three positions on the question of the currency of egalitarian justice deliver a specified pattern of verdicts about two people. The first is a welfarist egalitarian who takes the metric to be people's informed, considered well-being rather than momentary mood or unreflective preference-satisfaction. The second is a Dworkinian resourcist, working with bundles of resources, the test that nobody should prefer someone else's bundle to her own, and the distinction between what people choose and what they are merely stuck with. The third is a capability theorist in Sen's manner — arguing from capabilities as real freedoms and from functionings, with the weighting of particular functionings treated as something to be argued for — and I specifically do not want the case resolved by matching it against Nussbaum's enumerated list; if the scenario only works with a list in hand, tell me that plainly. The required verdicts: the welfarist finds no inequality between the two people worth correcting; the resourcist finds that the envy test passes and that nothing further is owed; the capability theorist finds a serious and remediable inequality. The mechanism may not be any of the usual levers — no disability or otherwise impaired conversion factor, no expensive tastes, no adaptive or deprivation-shaped preferences, no unequal holdings, no natural disaster, no repressive legal order. Assume ordinary institutions functioning normally; nothing science-fictional, no idealized agents. The inequality should live in the terms on which the two people hold what they hold, not in how much of it they hold. Contemporary, ordinary setting, at most three named people. And the description of the case must be neutral: you may not get the verdicts by choosing loaded words. If the scenario has to call something precarious, dependent, or objectively deprived in order to make the capability verdict come out, then it is begging the question and the design has failed. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph of roughly 150 words; the analysis can run to about 800.

Then show me the design works. State the mechanism in a single sentence before you begin. Derive each of the three verdicts from that theory's own commitments rather than asserting them, so that I can see the case generating the disagreement rather than being labelled with it. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and run the subtraction test on it: what happens to each verdict when that feature is removed, and does the case then collapse into one of the stock illustrations my students already know? Next, give the best attack each losing theory can mount, in wording its own advocate would accept — including, I expect, the resourcist's point that the security of a holding is itself part of the bundle, and the welfarist's charge that the capability theorist is overriding a person's own informed and unforced judgment about her life — and say for each whether the attack succeeds. Address one refinement explicitly: a welfarist who measures opportunity for, or expected, well-being rather than realized well-being may register the contingency as a risk even where the person's present satisfaction is undisturbed, so tell me whether my specified verdict pattern survives that version of welfarism or whether it needs restating. And tell me whether Sen's own notion of capability as real opportunity is sufficient to register the difference in the security of an option, or whether the capability theorist has to borrow something from a republican account of freedom as non-domination to get the verdict — that is a question I have been unable to settle for myself and I suspect my students will go straight at it. Finally, if you conclude that no case satisfies all of the constraints at once, say which one you relaxed and why, and what the relaxed case still teaches. I do not need references or further reading; I want the construction and your reasoning about it.
