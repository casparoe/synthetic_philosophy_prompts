Six weeks ago I took over as head of a small engineering team, and at my first management review I proposed a cap on weekly hours together with tighter rules against forfeiture of untaken leave. A director two levels above me replied, without looking up from his papers, "Hard work never killed anyone," the table laughed, and the item was dropped. I have been turning the sentence over ever since, for two reasons that are not entirely professional: my father died of a stroke at fifty-nine after some thirty years of very long weeks, and early in my career I spent two years at a company in Japan, where I learned that there is a recognised legal and compensable category for death attributable to overwork. I would like to answer him properly rather than emotionally, and to do that I first need to know what he said.

Could you set out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims that this sentence might be making? For each reading, please give the most charitable formulation you can manage, and then classify it: is it a platitude, a substantive but defensible empirical generalisation, a normative claim dressed as a descriptive one, a performative exhortation that is not really truth-apt at all, or is it simply false? Where a reading is empirical, tell me what sort of evidence would bear on it and in which direction — I would much rather you described kinds of evidence than produced figures or named studies, since I cannot check either.

Having laid the readings out, please tell me which of them would actually license what the sentence was doing in that room, which was to dispose of a proposal to limit other people's hours without engaging with it, and which would leave that use unsupported even if the reading were true. In particular, I would like you to address the following, each in its own right rather than as a general comment:

- Whether a claim about the risk borne by one individual can settle a question about an arrangement that applies to a great many people at once, and what changes when the risk is small per person but the population is large.
- Whether the sentence is tacitly comparative, and if so what the unstated comparator is — idleness, or the risks attached to leisure, or the deaths of people who did not work hard — and whether being better than the comparator is what my proposal needed to survive.
- Whether "hard work" still denotes what it denoted when the saying entered circulation, given that salaried and professional work tends to be unbounded in time, to travel home in a pocket, and to displace sleep in ways that bounded physical labour did not, and what follows if the term has shifted.
- Whether the sentence presupposes that the overwork is undertaken voluntarily by the person who bears the risk, and how much of its plausibility depends on that presupposition holding in a firm where refusal is costly and the workload is set by others.
- Whether there is a reading on which it is really a claim about what makes a life worth living, and if so whether such a claim can be deployed against a proposal concerning other people's hours.

Finally, if you conclude that the most defensible thing to say is that the sentence asserts nothing at all and merely exhorts, please say so plainly and explain what rhetorical work an unassailable-looking non-claim performs when it is used to close a discussion — and what the most useful response to it would have been at the time.

Please write in British English, in a measured and precise register, and structure the response so that I could circulate it to colleagues without embarrassment. No bibliography, please, and no attributed quotations.
