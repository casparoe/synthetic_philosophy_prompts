I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate and MA seminar on personal identity, and I would like you to draft one essay question together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

**Background.** The closing weeks of the seminar cover views on which a person's self is a pattern capable of partial, degraded, or scattered instantiation in substrates other than that person's own brain. Our centerpiece is Hofstadter's proposal, in *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007), that a person installs what he calls a "gemma" of herself in the brains of those who know her well, and that these persist after her death. I want the exam question built around the following excerpt. Reproduce it verbatim in the question as given here; do not extend it, paraphrase it, attach a page or chapter number to it, or supply any further quotation from Hofstadter or from any other author.

> "It seems to me, therefore, that the instinctive although seldom articulated purpose of holding a funeral or memorial service is to reunite the people most intimate with the deceased, and to collectively rekindle in them all, for one last time, the special living flame that represents the essence of that beloved person, profiting directly or indirectly from the presence of one another, feeling the presence of that person in the brains that remain, and thus solidifying to the maximal extent possible those secondary personal gemmae that remain aflicker in all these different brains. Though the primary brain has been eclipsed, there is, in those who remain and who are gathered to remember and reactivate the spirit of the departed, a collective corona that still glows. This is what human love means. The word 'love' cannot, thus, be separated from the word 'I'; the more deeply rooted the symbol for someone inside you, the greater the love, the brighter the light that remains behind."

**Requirements for the question.** It must be answerable in fifty minutes in roughly one thousand words, and worth twenty-five marks. It should be unanswerable by reciting that, for Hofstadter, the self is a pattern or a strange loop, or by narrating the circumstances that prompted the passage. To that end, present students with two stipulated cases involving a deceased person, P, and require them to adjudicate between them:

- *The Archivist*, who never met P and corresponds with no one who did, but has spent twenty years reconstructing P's psychology from her diaries, letters, and marginalia, and now carries an extraordinarily detailed, stable, and frequently activated model of how P would judge, feel, and act.
- *The Estranged Daughter*, who lived with P for eighteen years and so hosts a gemma built by direct interaction, but who has since P's death been actively and deliberately revising that model in a hostile direction, pruning what she cannot bear.

The question should then ask students (i) which host, if either, instantiates more of P, and whether the answer turns on informational richness, causal history, intimacy, or the host's ongoing endorsement of the model; (ii) what has to be true of the relation between P and a host brain for that host to contain a *part of* P rather than merely a *representation of* P; and (iii) whether the reasoning that licenses gemmae at the same time strips the "primary brain" of its privileged standing — that is, whether P's own self-symbol was a representation of P in the same sense as the archivist's reconstruction, and what the view must concede if so.

Require in addition that students engage at least one of two difficulties: *promiscuity*, since if richness plus informational lineage suffices, then novelists, biographers, and stage actors seem to extend P, which threatens to reduce the account to a graded claim about resemblance rather than a claim about persistence; and *divergence*, since two hosts revise their gemmae independently, so a person scattered across them would appear to change her mind with no communication between the parts, and the student should say what could supply the missing co-personhood relation if anything could. Finally, ask students to reckon with the tension between this metaphysics and the passage's own closing claim: if the archivist hosts more of P than the daughter does, survival has come apart from intimacy, and the consolation the passage offers is not the one its metaphysics can support.

**Model answer.** About nine hundred words. No citations, footnotes, or bibliography; it may name Parfit, Lewis, Merricks, or Gilmore, but any principle it relies on must be stated in its own words rather than attributed to a text. It should take a definite position on the two cases and defend it, not survey options.

**Marking scheme.** Five must-hit points with marks totalling twenty-five, each accompanied by a one-line note on the way candidates most often botch it. Follow this with a short paragraph explaining what separates a first-class answer from a competent second-class one, naming the specific moves the better answer makes. Close with three or four responses that would read as sophisticated but should lose marks — for instance, treating "gemma" as an avowed metaphor and declining to do the metaphysics, or answering only the question about grief.
