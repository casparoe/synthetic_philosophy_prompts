I chair a working group at a medical humanitarian NGO that is rewriting how we select and deploy field staff. The people we are writing rules for will hold sole custody of cash, medicines, and fuel at remote posts, sometimes for months, with no supervisor on site and only intermittent radio or satellite contact. Two consultants have been asked to advise us, and I want the exchange between them written out as it would actually go if both were at the top of their game.

**The two consultants**

- **Ada Ferrand**, a philosopher of character in an Aristotelian vein. She holds that virtues and vices are real, reasonably stable dispositions of the whole person; that they are built by practice, by the praise and blame of a community, and by the roles a person is trusted with; and that a hiring process which declines to ask about character has not stayed neutral but has settled the question by default.
- **Tomás Reyes**, a psychologist-philosopher sympathetic to the situationist critique. He holds that the cross-situational consistency global traits are supposed to explain is not visible in the data; that conduct tracks features of the setting — who is watching, what the role permits, how the choice is framed, whether a first small step has already been taken — far more than we admit; and that the reliable lever is therefore engineering the post so that honesty is the path of least resistance.

**The deliverable they must argue over**

Three decisions, with a fixed budget that forces a trade-off among them:

1. whether to keep a structured integrity interview plus reference checks in selection;
2. whether to spend that money instead on restructuring posts — dual custody of the cash box, rotation of assignments, a no-blame reporting channel, ambient record-keeping of stock movements;
3. whether to run any character-forming programme for staff already hired, and if so what it could plausibly consist in.

**Two findings from our own records.** Treat these as given; they are ours, not the literature's. Over twelve years:

- Staff who scored in the top decile of the integrity interview diverted stock at the same rate as everyone else, but were roughly three times as likely to *report* a colleague's diversion.
- Posts with two staff sharing custody of the cash box had lower diversion than single-custody posts, but only during the first year after the second custodian arrived. By year three the gap had closed.

Both consultants must say what they think each finding shows. The second one is the hard case and should not be resolved neatly by either side: it is awkward for the trait view and awkward for the situation view, and I want to see each of them try to claim it and each of them fail to some degree.

**Requirements on the dialogue**

- Neither consultant may be a strawman, and neither may be permitted to win by the other forgetting an obvious reply. Where one makes a move that has a standard answer, the other gives the standard answer, and the first then has to say something new.
- Real back-and-forth: at least four turns in which one speaker explicitly takes up a specific claim the other just made, rather than advancing their own next prepared point.
- At least once each, a consultant states the other's best argument in a form the other accepts as fair.
- The following must come up, and be handled rather than name-checked: (a) the state of the evidence, including that some of the classic demonstrations in this literature have replicated poorly, and that personality psychology's own settled view is that dispositions predict aggregates of behaviour much better than single acts; (b) the self-referential worry that designing situations is itself a prediction about how people will behave in situations not yet encountered; (c) what explains the small minority who dissent under strong situational pressure, and the rejoinder that dissenters may differ in the settings they select themselves into rather than in anything enduring; (d) whether "trust the person" and "trust the structure" are even the right way to carve up the choice, or whether our three decisions are actually interdependent.
- Do not let it degenerate into one side reciting famous obedience and bystander experiments and the other reciting the doctrine of the mean. By the middle of the dialogue they should be arguing about Monday: what goes on the interview schedule, who holds the second key, what the rotation cycle should be.
- No citations, no bibliography, no named studies with dates. If an experiment is invoked, describe it. Assume the reader cannot look anything up.
- I will tell you where I start: I find the situational evidence compelling and the Aristotelian response evasive. Build the pressure accordingly — but Reyes must pay a real price for his position by the end, and I should be able to see what he has had to give up.

**Format.** Use markdown headings for exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **The two positions at their strongest** — about 200 words total, one paragraph each, written as the consultants would write themselves.
2. **The dialogue** — 1,400 to 1,800 words. Speaker labels only; stage directions in square brackets if unavoidable, and sparingly.
3. **Concessions and sticking points** — about 300 words, as two labelled lists: what each consultant gave up during the exchange, and the two or three disagreements that survived it intact.
4. **The recommendation filed** — about 250 words: the joint recommendation on decisions (1)–(3), or, if they cannot agree, a joint recommendation plus a minority report of no more than 120 words.
5. **Design note** — about 150 words: identify the two places in this exchange where a model writing it is most likely to produce something false or shallow, and say what you did to avoid that.
