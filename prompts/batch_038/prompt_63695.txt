I am putting together a handout for a seminar on the metaphysics of causation in the Arabic philosophical tradition, and I want it in the form of a dialogue rather than an expository summary, because my students' reflex on encountering the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers is to file it under skepticism about induction, Hume six centuries early, and I want a text that makes that assimilation impossible to keep holding. Write the dialogue between two speakers, each argued at full strength and neither allowed to be a strawman: a mutakallim defending the position of the Incoherence, namely that the connection between what is habitually called a cause and what is habitually called an effect is not necessary, that observing one together with the other establishes only concomitance, and that God creates the effect upon the occasion of the apparent cause in accordance with His habit; and a faylasuf defending the Peripatetic position that natural causes are genuinely productive, that the necessity of their operation is what makes demonstration and therefore knowledge possible, and that God's causality is not a competitor to theirs but the source of it. They need never to have met: set the exchange in an undated hall before an audience of students, and give the faylasuf the full resources of the reply composed in Andalusia a generation later, Ibn Rushd's among them, without requiring him to cite it. Keep stage directions to a minimum.

Both speakers must be pushed past the two moves students always stop at. The mutakallim may not rest his case on mystery or on the inscrutability of the divine will, and he must answer the charge that his position renders the world unintelligible in principle and that locating the effect at the occasion of the apparent cause explains nothing rather than something. He should be granted the concession al-Ghazālī himself makes, that God could create in fire a nature or power by which it burns provided that power remains dependent on God's continued willing, and then be made to face the question whether that concession is a surrender to the philosophers or a redescription of their claim. He may deploy his school's atomism, bodies composed of indivisible parts and accidents that do not endure beyond the instant but are recreated moment by moment, but he must then meet the objection that a world remade at every instant leaves the notion of a habit with nothing to hold on to. The faylasuf, for his part, may not simply repeat that without causes there is no knowledge: he must say what a nature is over and above a reliable description of what a thing characteristically does; he must concede that the necessity he posits is never itself an object of the senses, and identify the faculty that apprehends it together with his grounds for not mistaking a settled habit of thought for a feature of things; and he must concede that natural causation is secondary and derivative rather than independent of the first cause. I also want the exchange to pass through two cases of my own devising. First, suppose God's customary arrangement were reversed across the board, cotton consuming fire, bread wasting the one who eats it, water inflaming rather than quenching; ask each speaker what makes the actual pairing natural rather than merely habitual, and press him on whether his answer has any content that his opponent's lacks. Second, a man wills to raise his hand and the hand rises; on the theologian's account God creates both the willing and the rising, and the act belongs to the man only by acquisition, kasb. Have the faylasuf press that a willing which is merely accompanied by its object, never productive of it, cannot separate an agent from a bystander who happens to will at the right moment, and have the mutakallim return the pressure by asking what secondary causes add to the divine act, or whether they demote God to a guarantor of what natures would have accomplished in any case.

Aim for roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words in alternating turns, and require each turn to take up something the previous speaker actually said rather than to advance to the next item on a list. Neither side may win by redefinition or by declaring the quarrel merely verbal, though if one of them really does think the quarrel is verbal, he must say so and argue for it. Each must make at least one substantive concession, plainly marked and not immediately diluted by a qualification. Argue only with the conceptual resources of the two traditions, nature, form, potency, essence, accident, atom, agent, will, wisdom, habit, sign, intellect, demonstration, and do not import Hume, Kant, Malebranche, quantum mechanics, or contemporary analytic machinery; no verbatim quotation of scripture or of any book appears anywhere in the handout. End without resolution: the final two turns should have each speaker state in a sentence or two what would have to be true for his opponent's account to be correct, and why he holds that it is not.
