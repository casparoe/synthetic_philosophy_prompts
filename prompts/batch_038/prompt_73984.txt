I teach political philosophy and this year I am directing a graduate seminar on the legitimacy of the European Union. We have been working through the familiar apparatus: pooled sovereignty, the input/output legitimacy distinction, the no-demos thesis, and the family of defences that portray the Union as a regulatory state whose warrant lies in what it delivers rather than in how its deliverers are chosen. One of my doctoral students has submitted a reconstruction of an argument she encountered in the secondary literature, and I find myself unable either to endorse it or to say precisely where it fails. It runs as follows.

(1) The decisions of a political body are democratically legitimate only if those subject to them can, by voting, remove the decision-makers responsible for them.
(2) Those subject to the monetary policy of the European Central Bank cannot remove the members of its Executive Board by voting: the Board is appointed by the European Council for a single non-renewable eight-year term, and no European electorate casts a ballot on the matter.
(3) Therefore the ECB's monetary decisions are not democratically legitimate.
(4) The same holds of the Court of Justice, whose judges serve renewable six-year terms by common accord of the national governments and may be deprived of office only on the unanimous judgement of their own peers; so the Court's rulings are not democratically legitimate either.
(5) Generalising, no decision of any institution insulated from electoral removal — an independent central bank, a constitutional court, an arm's-length regulator — is democratically legitimate.
(6) But (5) is unacceptable: the decisions of the Bundesbank and of constitutional courts across Europe were and are treated as legitimate by the very populations subject to them, and a theory that condemns them wholesale is a theory to be rejected rather than embraced.
(7) Hence (1) is false: removability at the polls is not a condition of democratic legitimacy.
(8) And so the much-lamented democratic deficit of the Union dissolves; its insulated institutions stand or fall exactly as their national counterparts do, and no special problem arises for European integration.

Each step strikes me as tolerable in isolation, and the conclusion strikes me as intolerable. My instinct is that the damage is done in the second half of the argument, but I cannot tell whether that is an insight or merely a reluctance to part with the opening premise. If I must rank my commitments, I would sooner keep (2) and (3) — the Bank does seem to me a genuine problem case — than keep (6), and I would sooner keep (6) than keep (8).

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me which step you reject and why; explain how that step contrived to look innocuous while doing the work you claim it does; price the repair you regard as the strongest rival to yours; and say, finally, whether anything deserving the name of a democratic deficit survives your operation, and if so what distinguishes it from the delegation problems that every constitutional democracy already lives with. I should like your own settled view with reasons attached to it, rather than a balanced inventory of options that leaves the choice to me. Since I intend to circulate the answer as a handout, please keep it to exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction, no closing remarks, and no headings.
