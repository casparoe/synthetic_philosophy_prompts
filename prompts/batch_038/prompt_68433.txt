I am the story editor and senior researcher on a six-part television series covering the Greek world from the Persian invasions to the death of Alexander. My background is current-affairs fact-checking, where there are documents, bylines, and living witnesses to call. Here there are none, and I have found that the habits I brought with me misfire in specific ways: I keep counting two authors who tell the same story as two sources, and I keep treating a vivid, concrete detail as evidence of a well-informed narrator.

Each episode script reaches me carrying between eighty and two hundred assertions of historical fact, and I have roughly twenty minutes per contested assertion before the producer's read-through. Our network's standards department requires every claim — in narration, in on-screen text, and in dramatic reconstruction alike — to be assigned to one of three tiers, ESTABLISHED, PROBABLE, and REPORTED, each with a fixed wording template, and requires that nothing sit in the top tier unless it has survived a recorded challenge. Complaints go to the regulator. An academic reviewer has already accused a previous series of ours of laundering one late source into a certainty by repeating it across three episodes, and he was right.

Write a decision procedure my team can actually run. I want numbered questions in a fixed order, each with a stated pass condition and a stated consequence for failure — this question answered one way moves the claim down a tier, answered the other way cuts it. The procedure must explicitly handle:

- **Dependence between sources.** How we determine whether two surviving accounts are genuinely independent, including the case where the only witness to an event is a much later compilation or abridgement of a history that is itself lost, and the case where two apparently separate authors turn out to be drawing on a common earlier original. I need a rule for when descent collapses two sources into one, and what remains when it does.
- **The standing conventions of ancient historical writing.** Composed speeches and debates, army and casualty figures, stock characterisations of tyrants and of foreign peoples, and the habit of explaining an outcome by a single clever stratagem. Tell us what a source's report licenses us to say about what was said, believed, or intended, as distinct from what happened.
- **Material and scientific evidence.** Inscriptions, coins, excavated remains, and absolute dating: what each can corroborate, and how to resist the move from "the find confirms the place and the period existed" to "the find confirms the story."
- **Silence.** When the best-placed surviving source says nothing about an event, and how much weight that absence can bear, as against the fact that most of what was written is simply gone.
- **Direction of error.** Where the procedure terminates in "cut the claim," where it terminates in "downgrade and reword to the template," and where the claim may stand as written.

Mark the steps that are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure, and say so plainly rather than hiding them inside a threshold. Say where the procedure hands the question to our outside academic consultant, who answers email about twice a week and cannot be used for anything on a production deadline, and where it hands the question to the producer because what is really at issue is the series' dramatic needs rather than the state of the evidence.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show the answers question by question:

1. Our tentpole sequence asserts that a major battle was decided by a deliberately feigned retreat. It rests on one narrative history written about four centuries after the event, plus a shorter, vaguer passage in an earlier surviving author who mentions the withdrawal but not the intention behind it. I want the tier this can occupy and the exact wording the narration is permitted to use.
2. Our scriptwriter has composed a speech for a general on the eve of that battle, and defends it on the grounds that the ancient historians composed speeches themselves, and that his wording tracks the arguments a surviving history attributes to this man. Decide whether this is a claim at all, and if it is, which tier it falls into and what on-screen disclosure, if any, discharges our obligation.
3. The script states the population of a besieged city as a round figure, supported by an inscription recording grain distributions and a literary number for the size of the garrison that is very likely conventional. The producer loves this figure because the siege sequence is built around it. I expect the procedure to kill the number; show me exactly which question does the killing and what we are allowed to say instead.

Finish with a short statement of the procedure's limits: the kinds of claim it will misclassify, the cases where a correct tier assignment would still mislead a careful viewer, and what it cannot settle at any length.

Keep the whole thing under 1,100 words, questions in terse numbered form rather than prose, worked cases compressed to the answers that matter.
