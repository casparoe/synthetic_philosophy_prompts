So I'm stuck on Carroll's tortoise and the usual gloss isn't getting me anywhere. The lesson everyone draws is that inference rules can't be turned into extra premises, or you get a regress. But there's two ways to describe what's actually wrong with the tortoise and I cant tell which one the argument licenses. (1) He's missing a belief — the next conditional — and the regress shows that no finite stock of beliefs will ever do it. (2) He has all the beliefs he needs; they just aren't hooked up to his inferring, so the defect is in what the beliefs *do* in him rather than how many of them there are.

Can you explain why (2) stops the regress instead of just hiding it one level down? And then the bit I really want: if what separates us from the tortoise is only causal wiring, then following a rule of inference looks like nothing over and above having a disposition, and dispositions don't seem to make anybody *right* — being built to sneeze at pepper isn't sneezing correctly. So does (2) leave us entitled to call the tortoise irrational, or only differently built? Explain the conceptual issue itself, not a survey of who said what.
