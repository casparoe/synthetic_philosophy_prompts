For a paper in my physics-and-philosophy seminar I want to use the storm that struck southern England on the night of 15–16 October 1987 as a test case for the claim that deterministic chaos drives a wedge between determinism and predictability. The facts I will assume: hours before the storm arrived, a BBC forecaster reassured viewers that no hurricane was coming; that night brought sustained hurricane-force gusts across Kent and Sussex, around twenty deaths, and the worst wind damage England had seen in centuries. The official review afterwards located the failure in gaps in the Atlantic observation network and in the coarse resolution of the numerical model, not in any limit of principle, and both were substantially upgraded. Years later, a re-analysis of that very storm identified a narrow descending airstream — since named a sting jet — that the 1987 model could not represent at all.

First, map the episode onto the chaos-theoretic picture with some discipline: name the system, the initial state, the tolerable error, the relevant error-doubling time, and who counts as the predictor, and say what that picture implies the forecasters were up against. Second, tell me which diagnosis the episode actually supports — sensitive dependence on initial conditions, or a missing mechanism plus missing data — keeping in mind that the useful horizon grows only logarithmically with the accuracy of the initial state, so halving the error buys roughly one error-doubling time of extra lead rather than a proportional extension. Does that arithmetic shift where the fault lies? Third, run the case back against the theory: does 1987 confirm the standard story, or does it show that the story struggles to separate unpredictability in principle from ordinary model error, so that blaming chaos risks becoming an unfalsifiable excuse? Is predictability here a property of the atmosphere, or of the atmosphere together with a given instrument network, a given model, and a given tolerance for being wrong? Finally, mark where the theory goes silent — including on whether determinism is even at issue, and on why forecasters afterwards grew more willing to over-warn.
