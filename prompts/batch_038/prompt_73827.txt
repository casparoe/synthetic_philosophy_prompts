Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend — a Bayesian in the strict sense, and considerably better at formal methods than I am — about whether a person facing a genuinely novel decision can be required to have a precise degree of belief. Our test case was a small regional bank deciding whether to extend credit against a technology that has never been deployed at scale anywhere: no relevant frequencies, no comparable past episodes, and no prospect of learning by repetition, because the decision is one-off and irreversible. My friend's position was that the bank's officers must have numerically precise credences whether or not they would state them, since otherwise their choices could be exploited in ways a coherent set of beliefs would not permit, and that everything Keynesians describe as irreducible uncertainty dissolves into either a shortage of evidence, which the posterior absorbs, or a reluctance to declare one's priors aloud. My position was that this misdescribes the situation: that the difficulty is not that the number is unknown to us but that no such number is fixed by the evidence, and that a theory which supplies one anyway confers spurious authority on what is in fact an arbitrary stipulation. We got nowhere, and what I now want is the specification of the dispute rather than another round of it.

So: before I commit myself to any account of rational belief under uncertainty, I should like to know what such an account has to deliver. Working from Keynes's early theory of probability — probability as an objective logical relation between a hypothesis and the body of evidence bearing on it, the insistence that many such relations admit of no numerical measure and of only partial comparison with one another, the separate notion of the weight of an argument, and his later claim that in certain matters there is no probability at all, so that agents fall back on convention and on a spontaneous urge to act rather than on calculation — and from the Ramsey-style rejoinder that a degree of belief must be precise and coherent if it is to guide choice at all, set out the desiderata. I have a candidate list of my own, which I suspect is padded, and I want you to prune it rather than defer to it: (i) a no-spurious-precision requirement, that an account must not manufacture a determinate credence where the evidence determines none; (ii) a separability requirement, that the strength of a belief and the quantity or relevance of the evidence behind it must not be collapsed into a single dimension; (iii) a usability requirement, that the account must tell an agent deliberating now, from inside her own standpoint, how to proceed; (iv) a requirement to explain persistent and apparently reasonable disagreement among well-informed forecasters, which must not be written off as universal irrationality; (v) a requirement to explain why agents resort to convention — supposing the present to resemble the future, or doing what the majority does — and whether that resort is a rational failure or a rational resource; (vi) a coherence requirement of the sort my friend appealed to, that the account must not license belief-states vulnerable to sure loss; (vii) a requirement that it handle non-repeatable decisions for which frequency data is unavailable in principle; and (viii) a requirement that it say how confidence should affect the size of a commitment and not merely its direction. Some of these, I now think, are not desiderata at all but partisan commitments dressed up as neutral standards: my friend maintains that (iii) is precisely such a prejudice, and that a theory of rational belief owes nothing to the felt convenience of the believer. I am not sure he is wrong, and I would like the question settled rather than assumed.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no parting remarks. Each item is to be one of the five requirements you judge genuinely non-negotiable for any adequate account, selected from, revised, or where necessary substituted for my candidates, and each should run to a couple of sentences: state the requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste, and add a clause saying which of the three contenders — the Keynesian objective-relation view, the coherent-credence view in the Ramsey and Savage tradition, and a two-dimensional view that keeps credence and evidential weight apart — fails it or survives it. Within those five items, mark whether the requirement is non-negotiable or the local preference of a camp; identify the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates; and say plainly whether any two of the five are jointly unsatisfiable, since if they are that result matters more to me than the list itself, and I want it unmistakable rather than hedged. Finally, as I intend to argue with my friend again and would prefer not to lose twice, attach to the relevant item an admission of which of the five you hold least firmly, what argument or evidence would lead you to withdraw it or replace it, and where your reasoning about it is most likely to have gone wrong.
