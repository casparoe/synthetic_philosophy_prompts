In mid-twentieth-century cosmology two rival accounts of the large-scale structure of the universe coexisted for a generation: the steady-state theory of Bondi, Gold, and Hoyle, and the evolutionary or hot-big-bang account that grew out of the Friedmann–Lemaître models and Gamow's school. I would like a case analysis of that rivalry through Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes — not a history of cosmology, and not a general exposition of Lakatos, but a disciplined mapping of the framework onto the episode, followed by an honest reckoning of where the framework strains under it.

Begin with the mapping. State what you take to be the hard core of each programme, what its negative heuristic placed beyond criticism, and how the positive heuristic drove the sequence of auxiliary adjustments that constitute the protective belt. For the steady-state side this should include the perfect cosmological principle, continuous creation of matter, the later C-field formulation, and the quasi-steady state cosmology with its periodic creation events that Hoyle and his collaborators advanced long after the original proposal had been widely regarded as dead. For the big-bang side it should include the cosmological principle without its perfect version, a hot dense early phase, primordial nucleosynthesis, and the protective belt that accumulated afterwards — dark matter, an inflationary epoch, and a cosmological constant or dynamical dark energy. Be careful about which of these adjustments Lakatos would count as content-increasing and which as content-reducing, and say why.

Then apply the criteria of progress to the two episodes usually treated as decisive. The first is the counting of faint radio sources by Ryle's group, read as showing that the distant universe differs from the local one; note that the steady-state proponents contested these counts for years on grounds of source confusion and misidentification, and that improved surveys reproduced the excess. The second is the mid-1960s detection of the microwave background. Here the details matter and cut in both directions: an approximate temperature for relic radiation had been estimated within Gamow's programme years earlier and then largely forgotten, so that the detection was in a real sense anticipated and yet corroborated nothing for anyone at the time; Dicke's group at Princeton had independently predicted the radiation and was building an instrument to find it when the detection was announced by accident; and Hoyle and Wickramasinghe subsequently offered a steady-state-compatible account of the same radiation as starlight thermalized by metallic whiskers. Say what Lakatos's distinction between theoretical and empirical progress makes of these episodes, what his requirement of novel facts does with a prediction that had been forgotten, and whether he has the resources to judge which response to an anomaly — absorption into the belt or concession of refutation — was rational when it was made rather than only in hindsight.

Finally, tell me where the framework breaks or falls silent. Lakatos insists that programmes are appraised comparatively rather than singly and that the appraisal lags the events by decades, so that degenerating character is visible only retrospectively; work out what that implies for cosmologists who kept developing steady-state theory after the microwave background was found, and whether the framework can avoid making their persistence rational by default. Consider the harder worry about circularity: if inflation counts as progressive because it was later confirmed by the acoustic structure of the background anisotropies, while the quasi-steady state proposals count as ad hoc, what test distinguishes them other than knowing which side won? And consider a third difficulty the framework seems poorly equipped for — cases where an anomaly was dissolved not by either programme's theorizing but by better instrumentation, as when the apparent discrepancy in which globular clusters seemed older than the universe was removed by improved parallax measurements and revised stellar models rather than by any new cosmological postulate. Close with a verdict: is this episode a confirmation of Lakatos's account, an anomaly for it, or something his framework never contemplated — and where, precisely, does it decline to speak?
