I am in the final year of a joint degree in philosophy and environmental policy, and as part of my dissertation module I have been seconded for a term to a small grant-making trust whose trustees have become persuaded that the welfare of wild animals is systematically neglected within conservation ethics. The trust has roughly £450,000 to commit over five years, which in practice means either two three-year doctoral studentships or a single postdoctoral programme with field collaborations; it cannot do both. The trustees are, however, uneasy about the question the whole agenda turns on, namely whether habitat destruction reduces or increases wild animal suffering, and uneasy for two reasons. One is that it can apparently be answered in either direction by the same facts, depending on how one aggregates. The other is that a confident answer of the wrong sort could be taken up in bad faith by developers and by opponents of protected-area designation, and the trustees do not want to fund work whose most likely public effect is to weaken the case for conserving habitat. They have asked me to draft a research agenda rather than a summary of the debate, and I would like your help with the hard part of that.

Given that budget, that horizon and that institutional predicament, which open questions should the trust's money actually go to, in what order, and why? I want the questions stated sharply enough that a student could tell whether they had made progress on one, not merely gestured at as topics. Two candidate cruxes have come up in our own discussions, and I would like you to assess them rather than adopt them. The first is what I have been calling the displacement-and-density crux: animals surviving habitat loss are not simply a proportionally smaller version of the original population, but are redistributed into the remaining fragments, where per-capita resources may be scarcer, edge exposure greater and predation, parasitism and intraspecific aggression rates different; if that is so, habitat destruction could reduce the number of animals alive while raising the average badness of each remaining life, and the verdict would then reverse depending on whether one aggregates totals or averages, or on whether one takes an impersonal or a person-affecting view of the lives averted. The second is the juvenility crux: in most taxa most individuals never reach adulthood, dying as eggs, larvae or juveniles, so if the capacity for pain arrives relatively late in development, or if death at that stage involves very little experienced suffering, then any estimate of wild animal suffering that scales with census population size is badly distorted, and the quantity worth estimating is not how many animals there are but how many animal-years are lived by animals that are actually in a position to suffer.

For each question you rank — I would expect four to six, no more — tell me why it sits at that position: which other questions depend on its answer, how tractable it is within five years using methods that genuinely exist (field demography, welfare biomarkers, behavioural indicators, philosophical argument about population ethics), how neglected it is relative to how much turns on it, and what specific finding would count as progress rather than as a restatement of the difficulty. Then tell me what you would deprioritise and why. In particular: is the headline question of whether the average wild animal life is net positive or net negative worth attacking directly at all, or is it so hard to measure that it should be approached only through proxies, and if through proxies, which ones? And should the risk of adverse uptake by opponents of conservation change the ranking itself, or only the communication strategy wrapped around it? I am not after a survey of the positions people hold or a list of sources; I want to see the ordering defended, the criteria you used to weigh importance against tractability and neglectedness made explicit, and some account of what evidence over the next few years would most plausibly make you revise it.
