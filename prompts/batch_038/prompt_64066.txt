Write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 words between two political philosophers assessing a concrete admissions scheme. The scheme, which the state they live in has just proposed: it will admit any displaced foreigner for whom five citizens file a joint voucher, and those five are legally liable for the newcomer's housing and support for ten years. The state admits no one on its own account and funds no resettlement. There is no quota, no waiting list, and no assessment of anyone's persecution or need — only the question of whether anyone vouches.

One interlocutor holds that a political community may permissibly control entry, because it is an association whose members collectively set the terms of their shared life; the other holds that freedom of movement is a basic interest whose restriction demands a justification the excluded themselves could accept. Each believes the voucher scheme exposes a fault line in the other's position rather than resolving the question.

Both must be equally sharp and neither a strawman, and every turn must answer the actual point just made instead of restating a view. Have them press hard on: whether a voucher converts admission from something owed into a gift, and what that does to the standing of the person admitted; whether the scheme is better or worse than a compulsory quota from the standpoint of the applicants nobody wants to vouch for; whether ten years of liability to private sponsors is a dependence that a rights-bearer ought not be placed in, or the only form of admission that makes the duty to take people in fall on identifiable persons rather than on a diffuse public; and whether, with no persecution test anywhere in the machinery, the scheme has simply lost track of who counts as a refugee.

End without a winner, but with an explicit diagnosis of which of these disputes is now substantive and which two may only be talking past each other.
