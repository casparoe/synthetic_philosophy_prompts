I spent thirty-eight years in a physical chemistry laboratory, most of it on gas-phase kinetics, and retired two years ago; since then I have been reading moral philosophy at the rate of roughly a book a month, in a small group that meets in my old department's library. My scientific habits have not abandoned me: I want to know what a claim predicts and what would count against it. The question I have gotten stuck on is the so-called inefficacy objection — the argument that an individual act cannot be expected to make any difference to a large collective harm, and therefore cannot be wrong or required. I know the standard moves on both sides: the reply that each act nevertheless carries a small but nonzero expected harm because production and distribution respond in discrete batches; the reply that what matters is not causing but participating, or being complicit in a practice; the reply that the objection proves far too much, since it would license abstaining from elections as well; and the Sorites-flavored point that a heap of individually harmless acts can be harmful without any one of them being so. What I lack is a historical case in which the quantities are actually on the record.

For that reason I want you to analyze the American response to the chlorofluorocarbon problem between 1974 and 1978, and I will supply the facts I take to bear on it so that you need not go hunting. Molina and Rowland published their chlorine-catalyzed ozone destruction mechanism in 1974 and urged restraint in the non-essential uses of CFC propellants — deodorants, hairsprays, household cleaners. There followed several years of public alarm, a National Academy of Sciences review, and a large voluntary consumer shift: by the EPA's own accounting when the federal ban took effect on 15 October 1978, fluorocarbon released from aerosols had fallen from roughly 511 million pounds in 1973 to about 300 million pounds the year before, and the agency remarked that consumers might notice no difference because so many producers had already moved to other propellants or mechanical pumps. The 1978 rule covered only non-essential uses, exempting items such as inhalation pharmaceuticals and certain insecticides; Sweden, Canada and the Netherlands went different routes, one of them a labeling requirement. Industry resisted regulation through a trade coalition for some years before the largest American producer, as I recall, came to support a phase-out once substitutes were in development — do not lean on my recollection of those dates, the chronology is not what I am asking about. The Montreal Protocol was signed in September 1987 and is now universally ratified. I am not after the chemistry or the policy history, which I know better than most summaries of it; I am after the ethics of the individual contribution.

Let me offer a device of my own, which you should treat as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a position I am defending. I have been sorting collective-harm cases by what I call their causal geometry. A case is smooth when the harm is a continuous, monotonic function of aggregate emissions, so that no individual act corresponds to any discrete change in the outcome; batched when the causal chain runs through discrete steps — a retailer restocks by the crate, a plant runs a campaign, a legislature needs a quorum — so that one act carries a small probability of triggering a large jump; and signaling when the act does its causal work not on the harm at all but on the beliefs of other agents, altering what producers take demand to be and what neighbors take to be normal. My suspicion is that the literature argues as though each problem had a single geometry, whereas a real episode may be smooth with respect to the stratosphere and batched or signaling with respect to the market, and may change geometry over time as substitutes, regulation and norms shift. Take that seriously and tell me where it breaks. Concretely, then: which geometry did the household that set down an aerosol deodorant and picked up a pump spray actually occupy in 1975, in 1977, and after the ban, and what does that imply about whether the act was obligatory, permissible, or supererogatory under each of the four replies I listed? Where is the objection simply silent — the exempted uses, the labeling route, the products marketed as ozone-friendly afterward that had never contained CFCs at all, the consumer who changed her sprays while buying the same propellant in her refrigerator? And run the analysis in the other direction as well: does this episode confirm one of the replies, constitute an anomaly for the objection as such, or present a configuration the debate never anticipated? Commit yourself. I do not want a survey of positions with the choice left to me; tell me which reply survives this episode, which you think it kills, and what your verdict implies for a case I have not raised — the individual decision to fly, or to eat meat — depending on whether its geometry is the same or different. Where you are unsure of a historical detail, flag it as I have flagged my own, but do not let uncertainty about the facts become a way of declining to take a position on the argument.
