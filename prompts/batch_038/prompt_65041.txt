I'm a third-year doctoral student in the history of the book, and for two semesters now I have been sitting in a social epistemology seminar that keeps returning to the same toy model: a line of decision-makers, each of whom receives a weak private signal of her own and can also observe everything the people ahead of her chose. Once two or three of them agree, the public record carries more weight than any individual's private evidence, so the fourth and the fifth and the fiftieth rationally go along — and from that point on, their choices convey nothing at all, even though to anyone watching from outside the unanimity looks ever more impressive. I can do the problem sets. What I cannot do is feel the model, and I've come to think a short story is the only thing that would fix that. The trouble is that nearly every story I've read on this theme cheats in one of two ways: it makes the person who goes along with the crowd a coward or a fool who deserves what she gets, or it gives us a clear-eyed hero who breaks the spell, is vindicated, and teaches the village a lesson. I would like to commission the opposite, and I'd like to be quite specific about the setting, because it comes out of my dissertation material rather than out of the seminar.

The setting is a monastic scriptorium somewhere in the Latin West in the twelfth century, and you should invent both the house and the text — please do not attach this to a real abbey, a real chronicle, or a real author, since I would hate for a listener to go looking. The text is a chronicle, and the disputed item is a single character in a number: how many days a town held out, or how many men crossed a river at a certain ford. One early scribe, copying from a damaged or ambiguous exemplar, alters that character for a reason that would seem unimpeachable to anyone who shared her knowledge — she has been to the place, or she has heard an old man recite the passage, or she knows that the number as written would make nonsense of the march she has just spent a week copying. Her correction is then recopied. Years later, a second scribe at the same house is charged with producing a clean copy and can consult everything the library holds: five exemplars, and all five read the same way. She has her own reason to doubt — a reason of exactly as much force as the first scribe's reason had — but she is outnumbered by objects on a shelf, and she knows she is outnumbered, and she writes what they say. Here is the turn I want the story to exploit, and it is the part the seminar never mentions: the five exemplars are not five independent witnesses. They are five descendants of one woman's decision, and no one in the chain is ever in a position to see that, because a copy looks like a witness.

Please write it as three or four short sections in close third person, each from inside a different scribe's head at a different point in the chain — the one who makes the change, the one who counts the shelf and defers, and one much later who has in her hands something that ought to break the whole thing. I want the reader to be able to reconstruct the epistemic structure entirely from what the characters notice and do, so the story must never explain itself: no use of words like consensus, signal, evidence, authority, or tradition in any diagnostic sense, no dialogue in which anyone articulates the mechanism, and above all no author's note at the end telling me what it meant. Each scribe's reasoning should be good reasoning, given what she can see; if any of them is stupid, the story has failed. Let the parchment, the ruling, the cold, and the gall ink be present but subordinate — I want the epistemics right rather than the atmosphere thick. For the last section, the outcome should be genuinely uncertain and in any case costly, and I want the final pages to make vivid how little it would have taken, at almost any point in the chain, for the whole thing to have come out differently — and how invisible that fact is to the people standing in it. Give it a title, aim for roughly two thousand words, keep the prose plain, and let the number at the center of it be a specific number that the reader can hold in mind throughout. I'm reading this aloud at an interdisciplinary workshop where half the room has never heard of herding and the other half thinks it needs no illustration, so it has to work for both.
