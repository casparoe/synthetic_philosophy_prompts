For a history-of-science book I've been reading in our school library, there's a chapter arguing that great discoveries were going to happen anyway, and it makes its case out of two examples. Here is the passage, which I've copied out because I want to check it before I repeat it in an essay:

"Newton and Leibniz each worked out the calculus on their own, and Darwin and Wallace arrived at natural selection within a few years of one another without ever having compared notes. When the same idea turns up twice, independently, at roughly the same moment, the reasonable conclusion is that it was in the air: the discovery was waiting to be made, and either man would have got there eventually even if the other had never been born. Which is why the priority quarrels look so silly in retrospect — the Royal Society's committee report of 1712 settling the calculus dispute in Newton's favour, the fuss over whose paper was read first at the Linnean Society in 1858. Nobody was robbed, because there was nothing to take: the loser would have been scooped anyway, and history has in any case credited both men. Ideas of that size belong to their century, not to the person who happened to write them down." (A footnote adds that sociologists of science have a name for cases like these, "multiples," and treats that as further support.)

I'm inclined to accept the conclusion, but the sentence "nobody was robbed" makes me uneasy, and I don't know enough about either episode to say why. Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a historian or philosopher of science would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong? In particular: do the two cases actually establish what the author says they establish, is the "in the air" inference as strong as presented, and does his conclusion about credit still stand once the 1858 arrangement is described accurately? I'd also like to know whether the word "multiples" in that footnote does the work he thinks it does.
