For the past eighteen months I have been taking a GLP-1 agonist for type 2 diabetes. The metabolic effects were expected; what I did not expect was how thoroughly the drug appears to have turned down the volume on wanting itself. Food is the obvious case, but the change is broader: the pull to buy things, to check my phone, to win arguments, to start ambitious projects has gone quiet alongside it. My wife thinks I have been flattened. I do not feel flattened. I feel better than I have at any point in my adult life, and — more disconcertingly — I cannot identify anything I gave up to get there.

Trying to work out which of those two descriptions is accurate has pushed me into the philosophy of well-being, where I have encountered a family of views that treat the absence of craving as doing all the positive work: tranquilism, antifrustrationism, and various minimalist alternatives to the usual pleasure-minus-pain accounting. My difficulty is that the stock thought experiments were designed by people who had not had their appetites pharmacologically lowered, and when I apply them to my own situation several of them issue the same verdict on my life. I cannot yet tell whether my relief is evidence for a tranquilist axiology or just an artifact of it.

I run a reading group of eight, only two of whom have any philosophy training, and I would like to give them a self-diagnostic quiz that sorts them by what their intuitions commit them to rather than by which label appeals to them. Please write it, to the following specification.

1. Begin with a preamble of no more than a page setting out the axes the quiz tests, stated as questions rather than as names of theories: whether a satisfied desire adds anything to a life beyond the disappearance of the want; whether a frustration the subject never learns of is bad for her; whether a moment containing no craving for change can be improved by the addition of pleasure; whether the number, strength, or content of desires matters; whether goods such as achievement, friendship, or understanding can be traded off against craving.

2. Then nine questions. Each is a concrete case of sixty to a hundred words with two or three answer options, at least one of which may be that the alternatives are equally good for the person living them. No question may use the vocabulary of any theory — no talk of intrinsic value, preference satisfaction, utility, or welfare — and no option may be recognizable as the signature answer of a named view. Cases must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the positions.

3. Include at least five of the following, in your order and phrasing: a desire frustrated after death or otherwise never known to the subject; a desire extinguished before it could have been satisfied; a craving-free but unremarkable life compared with a life of large desires all of which are satisfied, matched as closely as you can for felt quality; a person whose dementia has removed the craving for the project she spent her life on, while leaving her pleasant; a state with no felt need for change that is not experienced as pleasure, such as deep absorption in work; a choice between removing one intense craving and preventing five mild ones; and a comparison between a being who never forms desires and one whose desires are few, easily met, and all satisfied. At least two cases should turn on a medication that quiets an appetite, but phrased neutrally, and no case should be written so that the tranquilist answer is obviously the sympathetic one — I want the instrument to be capable of telling me I am wrong.

4. Follow the questions with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto: tranquilism; antifrustrationism; global preferentism; an informed or idealized-desire version of preferentism; hedonism; suffering-focused or negative hedonism; objective-list views; and hybrids. For each, state which answers are genuinely diagnostic and which are merely consistent with it.

5. State explicitly which pairs of positions the nine questions cannot separate, and supply one further question that would separate tranquilism from hedonism and another that would separate tranquilism from antifrustrationism.

6. Where a pattern of answers is unstable rather than merely unusual, say so and diagnose what the respondent is groping toward instead of simply marking them inconsistent.

7. Finally, walk through the combinations your axes permit that no standard position occupies, and say briefly for each whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I am particularly interested in whether satisfaction can have positive value purely as a means of removing further cravings while unfelt frustration still counts as bad, or whether that combination is a confusion.

Please do not include citations, a reading list, or attributions to particular authors; the group will not chase references, and I want the instrument to stand or fall on the cases alone. My own expectation is that I will come out as some variety of tranquilist and that my wife will come out as a preferentist about me. I would like to be able to check both predictions.
