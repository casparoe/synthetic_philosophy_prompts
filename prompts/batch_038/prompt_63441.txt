I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long piece about what a legal system looks like once it stops believing in desert. My case is Norway's handling of Anders Behring Breivik, and I want it analyzed against the Dennett–Caruso dispute over just deserts — the exchange published as *Just Deserts: Debating Free Will* (Polity, 2021), in which Caruso defends free-will skepticism and the public health-quarantine model he develops with Pereboom, while Dennett defends a compatibilist, competence-based account of responsibility and treats the skeptic's proposed reforms as an overreach.

Here are the facts I will be relying on, so that you need not reconstruct them. On 22 July 2011 Breivik killed seventy-seven people — eight in a bombing in Oslo, sixty-nine at a Labour Party youth camp on Utøya. Two court-appointed psychiatric reports disagreed about him. The first, delivered in December 2011, diagnosed paranoid schizophrenia, which under Norwegian law would have meant compulsory psychiatric care rather than prison; after that report drew heavy criticism, a second team was appointed and concluded in April 2012 that he was not psychotic. The Oslo District Court accepted the second report and, on 24 August 2012, found him sane and guilty, sentencing him to twenty-one years of preventive detention with a ten-year minimum, extendable indefinitely for as long as he is judged dangerous. He admitted the acts, pleaded not guilty on grounds of necessity, and his defence called witnesses in order to establish that ideological extremism is not a mental illness; he had made plain that he wanted to be held responsible rather than dismissed as mad, and most of the bereaved wanted the same outcome. In January 2022 a court rejected his request for early release. Norway's prime minister at the time answered the attacks by promising more openness and more democracy, and the court documented the killings in graphic detail so that the record would be complete on the day he asked to be let out.

Now the analysis I want.

First, the mapping. Under Caruso's model the operative questions are dangerousness and the least restrictive measure proportionate to it, which makes much of that trial — the fight over schizophrenia, the finding of sanity, the notion of guilt at all — look like a costly distraction. Say whether that is right. If the sanity question is not a distraction, say what covert work it is doing inside a framework that has no use for it: a proxy for treatability, for responsiveness to reasons, for something else? Then run the same exercise for Dennett, whose picture has responsibility attributions functioning as part of the social technology that produces and sustains the relevant competences. What does he make of a court spending ten weeks establishing that a man who confesses everything was nonetheless answerable?

Second, the convergence problem. Both frameworks seem to license indefinite detention here: desert-based because of the gravity of what he did, threat-based because the threat has not lapsed. Tell me whether that is a real convergence or an artifact of sitting at the extreme end of the distribution — and specify the kind of case in which the two come apart, together with what Norwegian practice would have to look like for the difference to become visible.

Third, test a distinction I have been using in my drafts. I distinguish practices that are 'desert-justified' — defended by appeal to what the offender deserves — from practices that are merely 'desert-shaped' — whose internal machinery still asks who is to blame, how badly, and whether he knew what he was doing, even after the official justification has been rewritten in forward-looking terms. Is the Norwegian system desert-justified or desert-shaped, and does the distinction earn its keep, or does it collapse into a redescription of the same institutions?

Fourth, the anomaly. Breivik demanded to be held responsible, and the bereaved wanted him found sane. Neither position appears to have a slot for a defendant who insists on culpability. Say what each can do with that fact, and whether it is an anomaly for one side, for both, or for neither.

Throughout, mark clearly where you are extrapolating from what these philosophers have said to what they would say about a case they have not addressed, and say where each theory is simply silent rather than pressing it to speak. Close with a verdict I could put into a single paragraph for readers who have never encountered the phrase basic desert: does this episode confirm the skeptic's claim that a humane non-retributive system can actually be built, show that what Norway built is retributive in everything but its self-description, or is it a case neither side anticipated? No bibliography and no citations, please — I have supplied the facts I need.
