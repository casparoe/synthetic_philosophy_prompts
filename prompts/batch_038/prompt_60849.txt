And here's the student's answer, verbatim apart from my fixing the line breaks:

> I'll use Guthrie's agency-detection account, Boyer's account of supernatural concepts, and Durkheim.
>
> Guthrie (1993) argues that treating the world as if it contained agents is a perceptual bet: under uncertainty it's cheaper to mistake the rustle in the grass for an animal than to mistake a predator for the wind, so a system biased toward over-detecting agents gets favored. Barrett (2004) names this HADD, the hyperactive agency detection device, and religious belief is what you get when the device fires where no agent is present and the firing gets culturally elaborated into gods and spirits. Notice this already shows the byproduct people are half agreeing with the adaptationists: HADD is adaptive, so religion does have a biological function, and the only real dispute is whether that function is what selection was aimed at directly.
>
> Boyer (2001) adds a filter. Most of what a culture throws up is forgotten; what gets transmitted is the minimally counterintuitive — concepts that violate one or two of our intuitive expectations about a category (a person who is invisible, a tree that listens) while staying ordinary in every other respect. These stick better than either fully intuitive or wildly counterintuitive items, so they spread. Boyer draws the conclusion that religious belief is irrational, since it's manufactured by memory and inference biases rather than by evidence.
>
> The standard reply is the genetic fallacy: where a belief comes from is one thing, whether it's true is another. I think that reply is right as far as it goes but it's also a way of making the whole field irrelevant, and Boyer would reject it, since on his view the mechanism producing the belief has nothing to do with whether there are gods. So the causal account does bear on rationality, just indirectly.
>
> Durkheim gets filed as a sociologist rather than a cognitive scientist, but he's really an early adaptationist: in the Elementary Forms he argues the totem is the clan's symbol for itself and that ritual is how the group feels its own force. That mechanism, he thinks, is what made large-scale cooperation possible — the same claim Norenzayan makes about moralizing high gods. So for Durkheim religion is explained by what it does for the group and its truth is beside the point.
>
> So the statement in the question is too strong. Natural causes don't prove falsity, but if the causes are mechanisms that fire whether or not the relevant agents exist, then the belief isn't tracking anything, and that is a reason to hold it less confidently.
