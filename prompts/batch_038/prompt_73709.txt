I'm a junior and I run the "giving club" at my school, where once a month we collect money and send it to one of those charities that does mosquito nets or deworming or whatever. For our last meeting we read the pond guy — the philosopher who argues that if you'd ruin your shoes to pull a drowning kid out of a shallow pond, you have to send the money too — plus some of the people who say that view asks way too much of actual humans. I thought I agreed with the pond guy until my friend Dara kind of wrecked me at the fundraiser last week, so now I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe by writing down my gut calls on cases and seeing if there's a principle hiding under them. Can you do that with me?

Here's what I think so far. Sneakers: yes, obviously I have to ruin my $300 sneakers to save the kid in the pond, its not even a sacrifice worth talking about. Identical money, different kid: if the kid is 6,000 miles away and I can save them with the same $300, I feel like donating is a really good thing to do but I don't think I'd be a *bad* person for not doing it, and I can't tell you why those two feel different when the arithmetic is the same. Envelope: taking $300 out of the club's envelope is clearly worse than just not donating $300, even though the money ends up in the same place either way, and that bugs me because I don't think doing-vs-not-doing is supposed to matter if you're a consequentialist. Job: taking a high-paying job I'd hate in order to give away half my income feels genuinely admirable but not required — Dara said that's just the pond case with extra steps and I had nothing to say back. Everything: if I had to give until I was down to food and a mattress in order to save more people, I feel like that's too much, but not because it's unfair to *me* exactly, more like a world where everybody's required to do that is worse in some way I can't name. Little brother: my brother wants $300 of stuff for his birthday and it would make him way happier than the same $300 makes a stranger, and I feel I owe him something I don't owe the stranger — and I don't think that's just bias, tho I can't prove it. Second ask: if I already gave my $20 this month and a different fundraiser asks me again the same week, I think the second one has less of a claim on me, like the obligation has a monthly budget, and I have no idea where the budget would come from. Pledge card: our club sells cards where you commit to $20 a month for the year, and once I sign one, skipping a month feels like a broken promise, which feels different from and worse than never donating in the first place. Offset: my school's debate team flew to a tournament and the school paid for a carbon offset, and part of me thinks paying to undo harm you caused isn't the same as donating to prevent harm you had nothing to do with, but another part of me thinks the offset thing is a cop-out, so I'm contradicting myself there.

So: is there a principle, or a small handful of candidates, that gets all of these or most of them? State it precisely, not vibes — I want something I could actually run on a new case, like a test. Tell me what each clause is doing and which of my verdicts forced it in. Then apply it to three cases I didn't list: (a) there are a hundred other people standing around the pond who could save the kid just as easily — does my obligation shrink, split, or vanish; (b) it's not life and death at all, just the choice between making a stranger's life noticeably better and keeping my own situation mildly annoying but bearable; (c) does the pledge-card obligation survive if the club quietly dissolves and nobody would ever find out. And be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle can't save — if it has to overturn one, pick which one and argue for dropping it rather than bending the principle into a pretzel to keep everything. Also, if my "budget" intuition and my "promises are different" intuition turn out to be the same intuition wearing two hats, tell me that. Please write the whole thing in American English, I'm at an international school and half my classmates use British spellings and it messes up my notes.
