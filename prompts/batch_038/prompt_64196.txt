I'm doing a short "here's what I actually believe" talk at an informal ethics night run by the compliance team I work on (I'm not a philosopher, I just read some). Topic is lying vs. misleading, and I want to get my position written down and audited before I say it out loud in front of people, because I have a nagging sense that parts of it don't want to live in the same house. Here's the whole package:

(1) I would never lie. I'll mislead all day if I have a reason to, and I don't lose any sleep over it.

(2) A lie, for me, is saying something you believe to be false to someone who's entitled to take it as your word. Whether they actually end up believing it is beside the point — a lie nobody swallows is still a lie, and a true statement that fools somebody isn't one.

(3) What makes lying wrong isn't the damage it does. It's that you got someone to run their reasoning off your say-so while you weren't standing behind what you handed them. You spent credibility that belongs to the whole practice of telling people things, and you spent it on something you don't endorse. So the wrong doesn't get worse as the harm gets worse.

(4) I also think that when I deliberately arrange for somebody to draw a conclusion I never said out loud, I've communicated that conclusion just as much as if I had said it. That's what I was after. I'm not going to pretend otherwise to myself.

(5) Case: a man shows up at the office looking for a colleague of mine and I'm pretty confident he means him harm. He asks where my colleague is. "He left at four" is fine, if he left at four. "He's not here" is not fine, even though it would save my colleague some trouble.

(6) Case: a client asks whether a deal we're both looking at is solid, and I know its shaky. I say "the paperwork is in order and nothing has been filed against it." Both true, and I know exactly what he'll hear. I'd sign off on that one and sleep fine.

(7) Case: my sister asks what I think of her kitchen renovation and I say "it's very bold." Not a lie, and not the kind of misleading I'd feel bad about either, because she isn't entitled to my decorating opinion as my word.

(8) One more: years ago I told a client something I was certain was false, and it turned out to be true. Nobody was hurt, nobody was even misled. I still count that as a lie and I still think I did something wrong.

So — which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which ones only look like they do until you poke at them? I want the real conflicts ranked by how much they'd cost me, and for each one the cheapest thing I could drop plus what I'd be giving up by dropping it. Then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most. Please answer in American English.
