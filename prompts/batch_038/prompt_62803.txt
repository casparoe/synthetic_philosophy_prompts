I am auditing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of religion this term, and in last week's session a classmate disposed of the entire ontological-argument tradition in a single line: existence is not a predicate. The remark was received as decisive, and we were on to Plantinga's modal reformulation within ten minutes. I left unconvinced but unable to say why, and on reflection what troubles me is that the sentence is short enough to be doing several incompatible jobs at once, and that its apparent force seems to vary wildly with the argument it is aimed at. Directed at the Proslogion's reductio against the fool it sounds devastating; directed at Descartes' Fifth Meditation claim that existence is contained in the concept of a supremely perfect being it sounds merely programmatic; and directed at Gödel's axiomatisation, where necessary existence is defined in terms of essences and then asserted to be a positive property, I cannot even tell that it addresses the right target, since the contested premise there concerns which properties are positive rather than whether a first-order existential predicate is legitimate.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a defence of any single reading, the distinct precise claims this one sentence might be taken to express? I would like each candidate stated as a thesis with its own subject matter: a claim about natural-language grammar (whether "exists" functions syntactically as a first-level predicate of objects); a claim about logical form (whether existence is properly represented only as a second-order property of concepts, or as a quantifier plus variable binding, and what free logic with its distinction between existing and being denoted contributes here); a claim about conceptual content (whether adding existence to a concept enlarges or otherwise alters that concept); a claim about metaphysics (whether there is any genuine property of being instantiated, or of being self-identical, or of being identical to something); a claim specific to the modal case (whether necessary existence differs in logical kind from plain existence, so that a slogan about the latter simply does not reach the former); and a claim that is not about truth at all but about dialectical burden. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude no one in the room would deny, a substantive and defensible thesis, a thesis contested among people who would all cheerfully repeat the slogan, or flatly false. Please also say whether the slogan's best-known philosophical ancestry actually licenses these readings, or whether the line has drifted away from the argument that originally produced it.

Then map the readings onto the arguments. Which of them, if true, genuinely block Anselm's reductio? Which block Descartes? Which, if any, touch Gödel's version, given that its conclusion is derived from axioms about positive properties plus a definition of God-likeness inside quantified modal logic — and if none of them do, say so plainly and identify what a critic of Gödel would have to add to the slogan to make it bite, for instance an objection to the coherence of positive properties, to the necessary-existence definition, or to the modal system itself. Finally, tell me which reading my classmate was most likely invoking, what the strongest version of their move looks like once the rhetoric is stripped out, and what one question I could have asked in seminar that would have forced them to specify which thesis they were asserting.
