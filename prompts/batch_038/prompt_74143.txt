You are a political philosopher who also writes and delivers a great many wedding speeches; work out the argumentative and comic architecture before you write, but keep the working to yourself and show only the result.

My sister is marrying a man whose entire career has been spent defending the claim that a distribution of holdings is just if it arose by just steps, however lopsided it looks at the end — and who has, with perfect consistency, paid for the whole wedding himself. I am giving the toast. The room is about two hundred people: her side is schoolteachers, nurses, and a brother who organizes a tenants' union; his side is graduate students and faculty who will catch every reference.

Build the toast around Nozick's basketball-player case — the fans who each pay a small admission fee until the player ends up with a fortune no single one of them decided to give him — and make that case do the structural work rather than serving as an allusion. Two failure modes to avoid: her brother is listening for a moment in which the toast endorses the theory, and the groom is listening for a moment in which it mocks it; it must contain neither, and must still be funny rather than evasive. It also has to turn, near the end, to my sister, who considers all of this absurd and has never read a page of it. Four to five minutes aloud.

Begin with the toast itself — no title, no preface, no commentary. After it, add a short note saying where the speech takes liberties with Nozick's argument or leans on a contested reading of it (for instance, whether the example is being used to make a point about historical versus patterned principles, or about liberty as such), how confident you are in the reading you have adopted, and which single element of the note you are least sure of.
