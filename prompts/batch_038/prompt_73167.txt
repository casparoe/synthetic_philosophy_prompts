I've been on our Intergovernmental Commission for about fourteen months now and I'm starting to worry about something. We're a mid-sized federation, seven regions, and the commission was set up after the new constitution to redraw who decides what — we're not a court, we make recommendations that parliament almost always waves through, so over time we've built up our own little body of precedent without ever writing down the principle behind it. We were told at the start to read up on the Swiss, German and EU practice, and if I'm honest half of us have just been pattern-matching off the EU's subsidiarity language (including the bit where it only really bites in areas the centre doesn't already own exclusively) without anyone admitting it out loud. So far the chair's test — "who pays, and who can vote them out over it" — and the junior member's test — "does the effect cross a border" — have pointed the same way on every single case, which is precisely why I think the next one is going to blow up.

Our record so far, roughly in order. Fertiliser runoff from an upstream region poisoning a shared river basin: we recommended federal minimum water standards, regions implement. Licensing of restaurants and building trades: left wholly to the regions, despite the national chains lobbying hard for a single licence. Disability income support, after the smallest and poorest region proved it simply couldn't fund a decent level: federal floor on the amount, regions free to top up and to run delivery however they like. School-leaving qualifications, even though families move across internal borders a lot: we refused to centralise, and instead recommended mutual recognition plus a standing joint body that meets twice a year, with curriculum and language of instruction left entirely regional. Pandemic powers: federal authority to declare and coordinate, regions execute, and any declaration lapses automatically unless renewed. Corporate tax base: federally defined, because the regions were undercutting each other into a hole, but regions keep a rate inside a band. A region's ban on short-term lets operated by a national platform: allowed, on the basis that the housing effects land locally, notwithstanding the platform's warnings about a fragmented market. Electoral rolls and voting rights: fully federal, zero regional variation, unanimous and quick. And a region that wanted to require fluency in the regional language for every public-sector job including postal sorters: we recommended against, on the grounds that it burdened people's freedom to move and work far more than it protected the language.

Now the case in front of us next month. The region of Vessane wants to require that every public body in its territory — hospitals, schools, the regional rail operator — buys meat, eggs and dairy only from suppliers meeting a Vessane animal-welfare standard considerably stricter than the federal one. It's Vessane's own procurement budget, so the extra cost falls on Vessane taxpayers and nobody else's. But the nationwide suppliers' association says it splits the internal market, that a supplier certified in another region can't bid for a Vessane contract at all, and — this is the part that's got me — that Vessane's own farmers will simply sell their non-compliant produce across the border while Vessane imports compliant meat from abroad, so the animals it's trying to protect mostly end up being raised and slaughtered somewhere else anyway. Vessane's reply is that the federal standard is a floor and nothing preempts it, and that if its voters want to pay more for their own hospitals' sausages that's their business. So: which of our earlier decisions is the closest analogue, and what do you reckon we'll actually vote? I'd like you to lay out the candidate rules our record is jointly consistent with — I can see at least three and I suspect they aren't the three you'd pick — say what each one implies for Vessane, then give me your best guess with reasons, and separately what you'd recommend if the goal is consistency with our own precedents rather than getting the animals the best deal. And since we're about to adopt a principle more or less by accident, tell me what each choice would commit us to, plus the further case I should table next year that would force us to show our hand about which rule we're really running.
