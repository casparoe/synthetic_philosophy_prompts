I came to distributive justice from decision theory rather than from political philosophy: fifteen years of rationalist blogs and forums, and only in the last few years the primary literature, mostly by way of the Stanford entry and some Dworkin, Arneson and Cohen. I have now written my settled position down as a numbered list because I intend to publish it as a long post, and I want it checked before I do. To be explicit about what I am after: not a tutorial on luck egalitarianism, and not readings. I want the tension map.

1. The question distributive justice asks is whether an inequality traces to choice or to circumstance. Disadvantage nobody chose must be neutralized; inequality that flows from choices people actually made may stand, however large it becomes.

2. Responsibility is fixed by the agent's epistemic position, not by their causal control — by whether they could reasonably have foreseen the risk on the evidence they had. So of two people who take the same gamble, the one who had good reason to think the risk small and lost anyway is owed something, while the one who had no such reason and happened to win is owed nothing. I accept that responsibility can come apart from control in both directions.

3. The currency is resources rather than welfare, with the sum owed for a given handicap set by the kind of hypothetical insurance calculation Dworkin uses. A welfare currency ends up paying people for their tastes, and I want no part of that.

4. Accordingly I do not compensate expensive tastes, whether cultivated or innate: in both cases the taste belongs to the person, and if the state subsidizes it as though it were a disability, it treats him as a container of unmet desire rather than as the owner of his own preferences. I do compensate expensive medical needs — someone born with a condition requiring costly treatment has a claim on the pool. Both verdicts strike me as obvious, and I do not think they need any further principle behind them.

5. The objection that this view abandons the negligent — that we would leave the uninsured reckless driver to die rather than treat him — fails because it applies the theory at the wrong level. The object of justice is the design of a scheme running across a complete life, not the adjudication of single transactions, and a well-designed scheme never allows anyone's own option luck to leave them destitute.

6. Nevertheless I reject a guaranteed minimum or floor of any kind. A floor is not responsibility-sensitive, it transfers from the prudent to the imprudent, and it is not egalitarian either, so it delivers nothing I actually want.

7. I am a pluralist about the value of equality: a move from a large inequality to perfect equality in which everyone is worse off is, in one respect, an improvement. That does not by itself settle what to do, but it is not nothing.

8. None of this is threatened by determinism. What makes an outcome mine is that it issued from my own weighing of reasons, not that the weighing was itself uncaused, so the choice/circumstance line survives intact.

Go through the set pair by pair and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, ordered by severity rather than reported as equally troubling. For each, say which commitment has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me — including whether it damages something load-bearing elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which pairs only appear to conflict and dissolve on inspection, and why they dissolve, since I would rather not fix those. Finally, name the single change that would buy me the most consistency overall. And if any one of these items turns out to be ambiguous between two readings that give different verdicts on the same case, flag it and say how the ambiguity propagates through the rest of the list.
