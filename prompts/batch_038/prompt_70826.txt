Here is the situation. I am a second-year graduate student in a philosophy-of-history seminar. A senior student cut in with the old proverb — I'll use the almanac version Franklin recorded, since it's the one I can reproduce:

"For want of a nail the shoe was lost;
for want of a shoe the horse was lost;
and for want of a horse the rider was lost,
being overtaken and slain by the enemy,
all for want of care about a horse-shoe nail."

(People usually run it further, to a lost battle and a lost kingdom.)

He has now deployed it three times, and I suspect a different claim is doing the work each time:

1. To argue that since every historical outcome has indefinitely many causes, singling out "the" cause is just an arbitrary decision about where to start counting — so the structural/contingent debate is verbal, and both sides are describing the same network from different entry points.
2. To argue that historical outcomes are routinely overdetermined, in the sense that independent sufficient causes were available in quantity, so the war would have come about anyway without the assassination.
3. To argue that counterfactual history is trivially permissive: since any small change ripples outward, any counterfactual is "true enough," and there is no fact of the matter about which ones are worth pursuing.

He insists all three are just the proverb. I don't think they are, and I don't think the proverb obviously supports any of them — but I cannot yet say precisely what it does assert, which is why I'm asking.
