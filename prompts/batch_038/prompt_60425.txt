So, situation. I'm a novelist and I've got maybe eighteen months of research runway before I have to actually start drafting. The book follows one family in a mid-sized German city from the summer of 1914 through the early sixties, and the spine of it is the argument about who was responsible for the war, conducted by three generations who each think they already know the answer. The last third lands right in the Fischer controversy — the grandkid is a grad student and the whole country is suddenly fighting about whether the Kaiser's government wanted the war or sort of slid into it.

I'm not trying to write a thesis and I don't want the philosophical equivalent of a Wikipedia skim. What I want is a ranked agenda for how to spend my thinking time, because I've got about four hours a week of real reading-and-notetaking and the stuff on national responsibility is enormous. So: which of the open questions about blaming a nation are worth those four hours, and in what order? And just as important, which ones are self-contained scholarly arguments that won't change a single sentence of prose, so I can skip them without feeling bad?

Here's what I've already been chewing on — partly so you know where I'm at, partly because I suspect I've got bits of it wrong:

1) The clause gap. The treaty article everyone calls the war guilt clause doesn't actually use the word guilt, as far as I can tell — it's framed as responsibility for loss and damage, which sounds like a financial/causal claim, and the moral reading got bolted on afterwards by the reception, to the point where one of the men who drafted it later regretted the wording. Is there a real philosophical distinction between being held liable for damage you caused and being blamed? Can a nation be liable without being blameworthy, or blameworthy with nothing to compensate? This one feels load-bearing for me because a minor character signs the document.

2) What I've been calling the ledger problem. If Germany is responsible, is that responsibility just the sum of what the individual decision-makers are each responsible for, or does the collective verdict float free of the total — real even when no single person's culpability would add up to it? My hunch is that the interesting case is where the sum is small and the collective verdict is big, but I can't tell whether that's coherent or whether I'm just confusing myself with arithmetic metaphors.

3) Blame-addressability. States don't blush. So who does the blame actually get delivered to — the government, the citizenry, the grandchildren? I've been separating blame-eligibility (can a collective agent be a proper target at all) from blame-addressability (can anybody receive it in a way that does something), and I'd like to know if that's a distinction with any content or just a novelist's private invention.

4) Sleepwalking versus aiming. Can someone be more blameworthy for an outcome they didn't intend when the not-intending was itself their fault — the muddled bureaucrat who never forms the intention, versus the one who forms it cleanly? My instinct is that the muddled one gets off too easy in the literature and I want to know if there's an argument available for the opposite of that.

5) A form question, which might be the one that actually decides the book. There's no omniscient narrator and no character who sees the whole July Crisis; every chapter is inside one head, and each of them holds mostly true beliefs and still gets it catastrophically wrong. Which positions on collective agency would license that structure, and which would make it a lie? If the collective really is an agent over and above the individuals, am I formally denying that by never letting anyone see the whole thing?

Rank these for me — plus anything big I've missed, which I'm sure there is — with the reasoning exposed rather than a summary at the end. Which are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone with no philosophy training (I read a lot, I'm not an idiot, but I don't do formal machinery), and for the ones you'd deprioritize, tell me honestly why: overrated, doesn't actually follow from the rest, or only matters once something I've ranked low is settled. And tell me what I'd run into in the first two or three months of reading that should make me re-rank the whole list.

Don't give me a bibliography or citations — I've got a librarian and a library card and I'll handle that end. I just want the questions and the ordering defended.
