I teach a philosophy-of-AI course and I keep reaching for the same picture whenever the shutdown problem comes up: an off switch for an advanced agent is like the emergency stop on a press brake — big red mushroom head, hardwired upstream of anything the machine can reach, works no matter what cycle its in. A colleague over in the policy school has picked the picture up from me and is now using it in testimony, arguing that a mandated kill switch is more or less a sufficient safeguard because we already know how to build and certify these things for industrial equipment. I'm pretty sure I handed him the analogy.

Can you audit it? First the correspondence table — what plays the button, the cutoff circuit, the operator, the machine's job, the certification regime — then the disanalogies. I have a partial list going: the press has no model of the button, no stake in whether tomorrow's shift happens, its cutoff is physical rather than something implemented in the machine's own control software, and you can test an e-stop without the machine learning anything about the test. Tell me which of those actually break the policy inference and which are cosmetic, and whether anything distinctive about corrigibility survives the mapping at all — or whether the analogy is quietly doing all the work by assuming the agent is indifferent.

Last part: is there a better picture I could use that keeps the safety-engineering framing without smuggling in that indifference? I'd rather replace the analogy than just retract it in front of him.
