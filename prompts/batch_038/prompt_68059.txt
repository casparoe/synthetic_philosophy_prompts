ok so I had a two hour arguement with a friend last night — we started on the Exodus and somehow ended up on Troy and King Arthur — and there's one specific move he kept making that I couldn't name at the time, and its still bugging me.

The move: you point out that some part of a story is impossible. The sea splitting, the plagues, gods picking sides, a wizard. And instead of the story losing credibility it somehow gains some. The reply is basically "fine, throw the miracle out, but what's left is a perfectly plausible memory of a group of slaves walking out of Egypt, and that's the historical core." Same with Troy: once the golden apple and the goddesses are discarded, what remains — a bronze age fight over a real hilltop town — gets treated as *more* likely than it looked when the story was still full of gods. And Arthur: remove the sword and the lake and you're supposedly closer to some actual post-Roman warlord.

What bothers me is the direction of the update. Everywhere else I can think of, a source you catch inventing one detail gets *less* belief for the rest of what it says. A witness in court, a friend describing their weekend, a news piece where one quote turns out to be made up — the caught fabrication stains the remainder. But here the fabrication is being spent, like currency, to buy credibility for the leftover bits. It's as if the impossible elements were a shield, and by knocking the shield away you've done the story a favour.

He said that's just how you read ancient narratives and that I was being naive about it, and I didn't have the vocabulary to push back. So:

- is there an actual established name for this? specifically the move where you subtract the incredible parts and then treat the residue as *supported* rather than merely *not refuted yet*. not looking for a bibliography or a list of stuff to go read, just whether the concept exists somewhere with a label.
- what's the nearest named thing to it? I have a vague sense there are whole traditions about turning myths into accounts of real people, and about stripping the supernatural framing off old texts, and I bet those are cousins of this but not the same move. I want to know exactly where they come apart.
- is it ever legitimate? there must be situations where catching an error in a source makes the rest of it more believable and not less, and I want to know what's different about those situations versus this one.
- and if nothing fits, make something up. Name it, define it, and give me the boundary cases — because what I actually want is to be able to tell when someone is doing *this* versus when they're doing something defensible, like just refusing to treat a single mistake as disqualifying.

a bare label won't help me. I want the carving: what the thing is, what it isn't, and where its edges are.
