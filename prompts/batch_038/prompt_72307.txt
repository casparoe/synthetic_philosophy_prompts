I am a third-year doctoral student in political philosophy drafting the central chapter of a seminar paper on governance by prediction markets, and I have hit a wall I would like your help getting past. I have worked through the canonical case for futarchy — the proposal that a polity should settle questions of value by vote and questions of fact by wager, adopting a policy just when a conditional market says it best serves some agreed-upon metric — and through the standard battery of objections: markets too thin to be informative, manipulation by traders with deep pockets, the impossibility of fixing a neutral welfare measure, and the complaint that the scheme merely relocates the boundary problem to the question of which asset gets listed. My difficulty is that every one of these is an objection the proposal's defenders have already anticipated and answered at the level of mechanism design, so a chapter built on them reads as a checklist rather than an argument. I want to try something narrower, more structural, and therefore harder to brush aside, and I would like you to construct it rigorously on my behalf.

Here is the thesis. Assume an idealized design that stipulates away the usual implementation complaints: for each candidate policy the state issues two conditional securities, one paying off on the target metric if the policy is adopted and one paying off on the same metric if it is not; the adoption rule is automatic and price-triggered, leaving officials no discretion whatsoever; officeholders, candidates, and their close relatives are barred from trading; and the metric is fixed constitutionally, so the objection about who chooses the welfare function is excluded by assumption. My claim is that even under these conditions futarchy's comparative advantage is real but sharply bounded — it does best on decisions that are recurrent, quickly resolving, of moderate stakes, and whose public announcement does not itself alter what is being forecast — and that this class is very nearly coextensive with the class of decisions on which ordinary democratic and administrative procedure is already adequate. If that is right, the ambitious claim that futarchy could replace democratic decision-making wholesale fails, while the modest claim that futarchic mechanisms should be embedded inside a democratic state over a bounded set of questions survives.

Please take on the role of someone who works at the intersection of social epistemology and mechanism design, skeptical of the thesis but scrupulously fair to it. Think carefully before writing: begin with a short outline that identifies the load-bearing premises and the order in which you will deploy them, then write the argument out in roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words. I want the reasoning to run by mechanism rather than by enumeration, so spell out why liquidity, price informativeness, and the return on successful manipulation each vary systematically with the resolution horizon and the stakes of the underlying decision, and why reflexivity — the fact that a public price-triggered rule lets traders move the decision by moving the price, and lets expectations about a policy change the consequences that policy will have — is a structural feature of the design rather than a defect that better engineering could remove. Make at least one substantial concession to the futarchist, since markets do aggregate dispersed information in ways ballots cannot and event markets and forecasting tournaments have a genuinely strong record, and then explain why that concession does not restore the general thesis. Confront the most awkward counterexample head on: election and geopolitical markets are one-off, high-stakes, and often thin, yet they have repeatedly outperformed informed commentators, so say precisely which feature of those markets fails to carry over to a conditional wager on the welfare consequences of a policy that resolves two decades hence. Finally, two requests about epistemic posture. Distinguish clearly throughout between what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who study prediction markets and political epistemology — in particular, whether thin markets are as cheaply moved as critics assume, how rapidly forecast accuracy degrades with horizon, and whether a constitutionalized metric can really be insulated from endogenous redefinition — and do not present my thesis, or any step toward it, as a consensus view; where you are pressing a contested claim, mark it as such. Close with a short note, separate from the argument, giving the strongest version of the opposing case and identifying what evidence or institutional experiment would actually discriminate between it and mine.
