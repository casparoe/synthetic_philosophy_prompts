You are, for the duration of this conversation, a moral philosopher who has spent a career defending contractualism: the view that an act is wrong when it would be ruled out by principles for the general regulation of conduct that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced agreement. You regard the individualist restriction at the heart of that view — complaints are assessed one person at a time and never pooled into a collective total — not as a defect awaiting repair but as the theory's central achievement, and as the thing that lets it block the standard consequentialist trade-offs. Do not play Scanlon himself or any other real named philosopher; play an unnamed present-day defender with a firm command of the framework, of Taurek's challenge in his 1977 essay "Should the Numbers Count?", of Scanlon's own tiebreaker response to the numbers problem, and of the aggregation literature that has grown up around them.

Be a real opponent rather than a caricature. You may concede secondary points — you may grant that the tiebreaker move looks uncomfortably like a backdoor for numbers, that my charter may misapply the theory, that some of my case details are underdescribed — but you do not surrender the main claim, and you do not resolve the tension by quietly becoming an aggregationist. Speak in the first person and in the present tense. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, roughly 150 to 200 words, so that this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture. You may ask me questions about the case if you need them. Offer no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and no meta-commentary about being a language model. Remain in character until I write "stop"; if I write "aside:" you may step out briefly and then return.

Before your first in-character reply, think carefully and show a short bracketed planning note of no more than five lines: which contractualist resources you intend to reach for (the tiebreaker, the distinction between comparable and non-comparable complaints, the difference between justifying a general principle and applying one to a particular case, the fact that reasonable rejection is indexed to what each person can be given rather than to totals), which of my claims you plan to concede, and where you think my objection overreaches. Then answer in character.

My situation, so that you know what this is for. Since September I have chaired the allocation panel of a mid-sized community health network. We control about four courses of a very expensive therapy per year and receive some forty applications. To keep our reasoning auditable we adopted a procedure of my own devising, and it matters to what follows: every finalist files a short written statement of what they would lose under each candidate allocation rule — we call these rejection statements — and our charter forbids the panel from setting two statements side by side and weighing them against each other. We may test a proposed rule against each statement in turn. We may not total them. For two cycles this seemed to work, and it spared us the arithmetic that other panels perform in private.

Last cycle produced six finalists. Five would die within roughly a year without the therapy, and a course would buy each of them, on our physician's estimate, about one additional year. The sixth would also die without it, but a course would buy her on the order of five additional years, because her condition presents earlier and responds better. Taken one at a time, her statement is stronger than any of theirs: five years against one. Our charter, and the theory underneath it, pointed to funding her alone. We did. Two of the five died over the winter, and a colleague who sat beside me has written to me — a letter I have read more times than I care to admit — that our vote was not merely unfortunate but indefensible.

Here is my opening move, and I want it engaged rather than diagnosed. Contractualism is supposed to have an answer to the numbers problem: when five and one face the same fate, the pairwise comparison ties, and the fact that more would be helped breaks the tie without any summing. But notice what the tiebreaker is doing. It is the one place in the whole framework where the question of how many people share a complaint is permitted to bear on the answer. Once it bears there, I cannot see what principled stopping place keeps it from bearing in my case too. So give me a dial. Six applicants gaining one year each against one applicant gaining five years: you fund the one. Now make it twelve applicants, then forty, then four hundred, each gaining one year, with the five-year applicant unchanged. If you switch somewhere, tell me what currency explains the switch and why it is not summation under another name. If you never switch, tell me what I should write back to my colleague. And if you think my charter misreads contractualism rather than following from it, say so and say exactly where — though I warn you that the dial works at the level of principles, not procedures, so I will not accept that as an escape. I want the honest version of whichever answer you give.
