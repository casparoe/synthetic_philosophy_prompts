Three months ago I was appointed to the planning commission of the mid-sized New England town where I have lived for eleven years — the first seven as a renter, the last four as the owner-occupant of a house inside the district that the proposal now before us would affect. At last week's hearing on a draft amendment that would allow fourplexes by right throughout that district, eliminate minimum lot sizes and parking minimums, and leave every existing house exactly as it is, two of my fellow commissioners argued for close to an hour. Neither is foolish. I have read the transcript twice and come away unable to say which of them won which point, and I am the member assigned to draft the findings that will accompany whatever the commission decides. I would like a careful adjudication of the exchange as it actually ran, move by move. I have tried to state both at their best; I will not tell you how I am inclined to vote, because I do not want the adjudication fitted to my preference.

Ellen — thirty-one years on the street, retired school administrator, owner-occupant:

1. The code has said one dwelling per lot in this district since 1961, and this commission refused petitions to change it in 1974, 1989, and 2006. When a public body writes a rule down and then reaffirms it three times, people arrange their savings and their lives around it, and the claims that such planning generates are not mere preferences. What is proposed is not a revision of policy but the withdrawal of a promise, and it lands on the people who relied on it.

2. Ravi — six years in the district, condo owner, two years on the commission: The 1961 rule is not a moral zero point. It was itself a decision, made by a body nobody in this room voted for, that took a stick out of every owner's bundle at the time — the right to build more than one unit — and took it out in order to control who could live here. If reliance counts, the reliance of the owners who petitioned in 1974, 1989, and 2006 counted too, and was overridden each time. You cannot privilege one political decision as the baseline and score every later one as a loss without saying what makes that one special.

3. Ellen: That proves far too much. If the outputs of any rule may be reopened because the rule or its predecessor had an unjust origin, no reliance interest is ever secure — including renters' reliance on rent regulation and tenants' reliance on their leases. A defective rule can still generate real though defeasible claims, and the right response to a bad rule is prospective change with notice and transition, not the assertion that its beneficiaries have no claim at all. I am not asking to freeze the code. I am asking that a change of this magnitude be phased, and that owner-occupants of thirty years be treated differently from investors who bought here last spring.

4. Ravi: Then the deeper point. The people with the strongest claim on this district are not in this room — the nurses and line cooks who work here and drive eighty minutes, and the adult children of the families on this street, none of whom can afford to come back. The right to the city, in Lefebvre's sense, is a right of access to and participation in the urban fabric, held by whoever is caught up in it, not a right of incumbents to fix its character in perpetuity. On that framing the question is not what was promised in 1961 but who is entitled to a say in what this place becomes — and your account answers that question by reading off the list of people already inside.

5. Ellen: You have used one phrase for two different claims. Access is about distribution: how much housing exists and at what price. Participation is about authority: who votes. They come apart, and here they point in opposite directions, because those with the strongest access claim have by your own description never lived here and have no standing in our procedure — and giving them standing would abolish the town as a polity. Access against whom, in any case? The amendment entitles no one to a dwelling. It changes what owners may build, and the price effects you are counting on are diffuse, contested, and at the scale of one district in one mid-sized town probably undetectable. Meanwhile the participation right you invoke is precisely what I am exercising at this hearing, under rules you propose to change while I am using them.

6. Ravi: Grant the uncertainty about local prices; it damages your position, not mine. Under the existing code the exclusionary effect is not uncertain at all — it operates every year and we know its direction, since a district where nothing may be built except a four-bedroom house on a tenth of an acre has a floor price, and a floor price decides who lives here. Only the magnitude of reform's benefit is unknown. Where a rule produces a harm we are sure of and a change whose benefits are merely probable, the burden sits on those who want the rule kept. And your participation point concedes my conclusion: you are in this room because you live here, and the reason you live here rather than one of the people I named is a rule you did nothing to earn.

7. Ellen: There is no harm we are sure of that is not measured against the very baseline we are disputing. To say the code excludes people already assumes they had a claim to be here, which is the question. As for earning: pressed consistently, that principle dissolves every claim in the room, including your appointment to this commission, the salary that paid for your condominium, and the value of the lot you have held for six years. If unearned advantage voids standing, none of us may speak and this hearing is a sham.

8. Ravi: I never said unearned advantage voids standing. I said it defeats the extra weight you give your expectations when they are set against the claims of people who are not here. That is a point about the strength of a claim, not the eligibility of a claimant — and it is the point you have not answered.

What I would like from you: (i) rate each of the eight moves — clearly mistaken, partly right, clearly right, or right for the wrong reasons — with a sentence or two of justification for each rating; (ii) wherever a move depends on an empirical claim about supply, prices, or commute times, name the claim and say whether the move survives if it turns out false; (iii) identify every place where the two are using the same words for different things, and every place where one assumes the point at issue; (iv) tell me who is more right overall, and, once the bad moves on both sides are stripped away, what the surviving disagreement is actually a disagreement about — noting in particular that this amendment withdraws nothing from anyone's own lot and only removes restrictions on what owners may choose to build; (v) tell me what neither of them said, including whether a dispute of this shape is the kind of thing a zoning hearing can settle at all. End by distinguishing the considerations that could honestly be written into a municipal findings document from those that are philosophically important but unavailable to a commission acting under a state enabling statute. Please give me a verdict rather than a survey: I can produce a survey myself.
