ok so this is a work thing but it's turned into something bigger and I'm way out of my depth.

I'm a backend engineer at a ~350 person company. We have an employee hardship fund — people donate, company matches, we recieve about $45k a year — and I'm the one who built the intake form and the scoring spreadsheet because nobody else wanted to. Committee of 5 volunteers, I'm on it.

This year HR pushed through a new rule: the fund is "for misfortune, not for the consequences of your own decisions." So my form now asks people to explain what happened *and* what led up to it, and I built a fault multiplier into the score. Roughly 1.0 if it was out of their control, sliding down to like 0.4 if they knowingly did the thing that ended up hurting them.

Two requests came in last month and the pool can only really cover one properly.

First one is easy. Support engineer, partner got laid off in March, then their kid needed two surgeries. Nobody's fault. Score 1.0.

Second one is what's tearing the committee appart. A dev who, about 18 months ago, turned down a solid counteroffer to stay on a risky internal project with a big bonus attached if it shipped. He did the math in a doc, he showed it to us, and honestly the numbers were good — he wasn't being dumb, he was making a reasonable bet. It didn't ship, the bonus evaporated, he'd already put a deposit on a house assuming it was coming, and now he's about to lose the deposit and probably the house. His fault score is 0.4.

Arguments in the room:

- One person says if we pay out for bets that lose, we're taxing the cautious people to subsidize the gamblers, and the fund gets eaten alive. Also: if it had shipped he'd have kept the upside and not asked us for anything.
- Another says the whole fault thing is ugly. We're making someone confess their worst decisions to five coworkers to earn the right to help. And half of these "choices" get made by people who grew up with no cushion and no one to catch them, so calling it a free choice is a stretch — you can trace any decision backwards far enough that it stops being theirs.
- Then there's the matching condition: the company only matches if we use "documented, objective criteria," and the fault score is the only number on the form that looks objective. "How badly do you need this" is a vibes judgment made by five people who already know the claimant.

What I actually want help with:

1. Is the fault rule defensible at all, or is it wrong in principle? I keep feeling like its wrong but I can't get past "that seems harsh," which isn't an argument.
2. Does it matter that his bet was a *good* bet given what he knew at the time? Should the score be grading the decision or the outcome, and if the decision, what does that do to the multiplier?
3. If I scrap the rule, what should the form do instead — just delete the cause question entirely? That swings into the other problem, where someone who's been bailed out three times for the same thing gets identical priority to someone hit by a bus, and the donors notice.
4. Is there any way to satisfy the "objective criteria" condition without interrogating people's life choices? Or is the condition itself the thing I should be pushing back on, and how would I argue that to HR without sounding like I'm lobbying to hand out free money?

Please don't just tell me to loop in HR or talk to a lawyer. I want the actual reasoning about whether we're allowed to do this.
