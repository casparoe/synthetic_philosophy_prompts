Four of my novels have been built out of a single piece of machinery: the distance between what a character says she wants in scene two and what the plot eventually demonstrates she wanted all along. I have begun to suspect that a body of work in moral philosophy and AI alignment is running the same machinery with more apparatus and less feel for it. I want that suspicion tested, not flattered.

My native vocabulary is that of character construction, and I am thoroughly fluent in it: the conscious objective and the unconscious motivation; the distinction practitioners call want versus need; the arc and the turn; free indirect style, in which a sentence carries a character's own idiom and a more capacious understanding of her at the same time; the retrospective narrator who reports on an earlier self from a position of greater knowledge; unreliable narration; dramatic irony as a structural property of point of view rather than a device for withholding facts; backstory as the constraint that determines what a character could not have known; and the implied author, the single intelligence whose presence is felt behind every character's separately rendered mind. I read close philosophy slowly and without pleasure. Do not assume I recognize a name.

The material I want translated: the proposal known as coherent extrapolated volition, on which an aligned system should not be given what people say they want but what they would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together"; its insistence on extrapolating rather than aggregating; its expectation that individual extrapolations converge rather than cancel; the qualification that the system should act only on the coherent parts and, where several attractors remain, keep the satisficing options open rather than choosing among them; and the fear that one person's extrapolated will, admitted early, poisons the whole. Alongside it, the older family of idealized-preference accounts: the test of whether a desire survives vivid and repeated confrontation with all relevant available information; the account that asks what a fully informed and fully rational version of an agent would want her actual, non-idealized self to want; the dispositional treatment of value as what we would desire under idealized conditions; and the standing objection that all such procedures quietly substitute the theorist's own preferences for the agent's and call the result the agent's.

Deliver, first, the translation proper, component by component, without importing assumptions from my craft vocabulary and passing them off as correspondences. Where a notion looks easy in craft terms because an author may simply decree a character's deeper need, say so and mark the authority CEV cannot borrow. Where craft has a resource the philosophers appear to lack, say that too: I want to know whether free indirect style, or the retrospective narrator's second-order relation to her earlier self, genuinely models extrapolation without substitution, or whether I am enjoying a resemblance.

Deliver, second, the residue ledger, in three registers: what translates cleanly; what translates only with distortion, naming what the distortion costs; and what has no expression whatsoever in my vocabulary.

Deliver, third, a verdict on the untranslatable items. For each, is the residue a poverty in craft terms, or a sign that the original claim was doing less work than its phrasing implies?

Finally, address one hypothesis of mine as a hypothesis. In a novel, the coherence of many separately rendered minds is guaranteed from outside, by one intelligence that is none of them; the ensemble never has to negotiate. My suspicion is that the convergence claim in extrapolated volition borrows this trick unnoticed, and that its apparent success at extrapolating many minds at once is an artifact of implied authorship. Tell me whether that diagnosis holds, where it misdescribes the proposal, and what I should stop saying if it fails. If some part of this material is best characterized in my own idiom as a plot device rather than a theory, say so plainly.
