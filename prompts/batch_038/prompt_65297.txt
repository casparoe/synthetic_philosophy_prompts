There's an idea — I think Dennett calls it a free-floating rationale — where a thing has a reason for being the way it is but nobody has that reason. Termite mounds ventilate themselves and no termite has a theory of convection. A price coordinates supply and demand and no one intends it. A fraud model trained on call-center logs reliably flags the customers who complain the most, and the company sincerely says that isnt what it's checking for and would never sign off on it.

Write me an essay on whether that idea earns its keep. One side says the rationale-talk is a useful fiction: reasons are essentially somebody's reasons, because to have one is to be able to cite it, be guided by it, and be criticized for ignoring it, and a mound can do none of those, and neither can a model whose operators disown the pattern. The other side says that just smuggles in a picture of agency, and that what makes a rationale a rationale is that it explains why the thing keeps working, whether or not anybody is home.

Weigh them against each other and land somewhere. I want an actual verdict, not a shrug plus an it-depends-on-what-you-mean-by-reason — and if you think the disagreement is verbal, show me which version of it isn't. Then say what turns on the answer: does anything follow for blaming a company whose system does what nobody meant it to, or for asking what a statute is "for" once its drafters are dead?
