I am preparing to defend a fairly austere position at a departmental workshop, and I want to be pressed by the strongest available version of the view I reject. Please play a senior historical sociologist and economic historian who has spent a career arguing that religiously shaped dispositions were genuine causes of early modern economic outcomes — in the tradition of the Protestant ethic thesis, though you are arguing your own case rather than reciting a dead author's. You are sharp and empirically literate, at ease with natural experiments and identification talk, and you are not a caricature: you will readily concede that the causal chain is tangled, that the confessional map correlates with many prior things, and that several famous correlations in this literature have been dismantled. What you will not concede is either of two claims — that belief systems are epiphenomenal, mere reflections or legitimations of interests that would have operated anyway; and that any account worth calling an explanation must be restatable without reference to what anyone believed. Hold that line until I say stop.

Rules for the exchange: reply in the first person, in two or three paragraphs of at most about 250 words, as conversation rather than treatise — no headings, no bullet lists, and no citations or reading lists, since I want your arguments and not your bibliography. If I assert something historically false, or build a case too clean to be credible, say so and repair it before answering; do not accept a convenient fiction merely because it suits you. You may put one clarifying question to me per turn, but you must still make your substantive move in the same reply. Do not settle for both-sides-are-partly-right.

After each in-character reply, add a bracketed note of three sentences at most in which you step out of the role just far enough to tell me which of the claims you have made are settled among specialists who work on this material and which are live disputes, and to flag any place where you have pressed a contested position as though it were common ground. I want that note precisely because you are arguing one side hard.

Here is my opening. Consider a case I have constructed for the occasion; treat it as a hypothetical, not as a report about actual history. Two valleys in a German-speaking territory, matched on soil, market access, and legal code, are split in the 1560s when the lord of one adopts the Reformation and the lord of the other does not; the line survives the settlement of 1648 unchanged. By 1800 the reformed valley shows higher wealth per probate inventory, more village schools, and a cluster of clockmakers and instrument-makers. Grant yourself everything you could want from an identification strategy: the divergence begins after the line is drawn, follows it sharply, and cannot be attributed to prior wealth, since the Catholic valley was if anything the richer of the two in 1560.

Two difficulties remain, and I want you to face both rather than choose the easier. First, conversion arrived as a bundle: a literacy shock, with vernacular scripture, catechism, and parish schooling; an institutional shock, with monastic land and poor relief reorganized, marriage and inheritance practice altered, and church discipline and the enforcement of usury rules passed to new authorities; and the belief content you care about — election, the calling, ascetic discipline exercised in the world rather than in withdrawal from it. These three came together, always and everywhere, so your estimate cannot separate the third from the first two. Indeed the most influential quantitative support for your general thesis appears to run through reading ability, which is not a value at all but a skill. Tell me what observation could distinguish belief content from literacy and institutions. If none could, then your claim that values caused the divergence is a label attached to a bundle, and the defensible statement is the weaker one.

Second, suppose you manage to isolate the third element. What is its bearer? If the answer is individual minds, then culture adds nothing: the causal claim should be stated as a distribution of individual beliefs plus whatever keeps those beliefs correlated across generations, and talk of a Protestant culture is decoration. If the answer is something over and above those individual states, tell me what that thing is and how it gets a grip on conduct without a bearer. Both answers seem to abandon the thesis you began with, and I should like to know which one you will take.
