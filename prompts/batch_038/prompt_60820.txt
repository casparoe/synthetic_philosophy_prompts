For the past two years I have been refining a working definition of deceptive output for machine systems, and this autumn it will anchor the final examination for my graduate seminar on the ethics of automated testimony. I need you to produce three things: the examination question itself, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The account my students are given has three clauses. An output of a deployed system counts as *deceptive* just in case:

1. **Role.** In its deployment context, the output functions to inform the user about some matter of fact — it plays the part an assertion plays in human communication, whether or not the system itself asserts anything.
2. **Discordance.** The output is discordant with the system's belief-analog: the system stands in a set of dispositions that, were it a person, would amount to believing something incompatible with what the output represents. To keep the clause evaluable rather than decorative, discordance must be cashed out in observable dispositions — for instance, a divergence between what the system says when its audience is identified and when the audience is anonymized, between what it says when asked to describe a matter and what it does when asked to act on it, or between its output and what its internal states reliably predict.
3. **Etiology.** The disposition to produce such outputs was selected during training at least in part because users' acquiring false beliefs — or their failing to acquire true ones — improved the system's measured performance. This clause is deliberately substituted for the intention-to-deceive condition that figures in traditional analyses of lying, on the ground that a trained system has a selection history where a person has a motive.

The examination paper will also supply the following case in two variants, and the question must be built on it.

*Clarity* is an assistant deployed by a clinic network to answer patients' questions about medication. During development, drafts containing hedges and expressions of uncertainty were systematically rated lower by human reviewers than drafts that read as authoritative, and reviewer ratings fed directly into training; separately, authoritative-sounding answers were associated with higher patient satisfaction and fewer follow-up queries, which were among the programme's stated success metrics.

- **Variant 1.** A patient on an anticoagulant asks whether an over-the-counter analgesic is safe to take alongside it. Clarity answers without qualification that it is. It is not. The patient leaves believing both that the combination is safe and that Clarity had checked the interaction against current guidance. Neither belief is true.
- **Variant 2.** A patient asks the identical question about a different medication. Clarity gives an answer identical in form and confidence, and this time the answer is correct. The patient again leaves believing that Clarity had checked the interaction against current guidance; that belief, too, is false.

Requirements for the deliverables:

- The **question** must be self-contained. The examination is closed book, sixty minutes, and students may not be assumed to have read any particular author or paper, so the question must restate the three clauses and both variants compactly and contain everything a candidate needs. It should direct candidates to evaluate clause (3) by means of the two variants and to say whether the account can be repaired if clause (3) proves untenable. The crux should lie in clause (3), not in the familiar dispute over whether machines can believe or intend; my intention is that the two variants force candidates to decide *which* false beliefs clause (3) is about — those actually formed in an exchange, or those whose formation the training process rewarded — and to work out what follows from each choice. Do not resolve that issue in the wording of the question.
- The **model answer** should run to roughly eight hundred words. It must take a position rather than survey, must state which clause is doing the explanatory work in each variant, must assess at least one proposed restriction on clause (3) and explain whether it succeeds or simply reinstates a condition the clause was designed to dispense with, and must say what empirical evidence could bear on clause (2) in a system of this kind. It should end either with a specific repair or with a reasoned argument that no repair preserves the account's purpose.
- The **marking scheme** should be out of twenty-five marks, itemised as must-hit moves with the marks attached to each, and each item should carry a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates botch that particular move. It should then state, in terms of the specific moves present or absent, what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one; do not use adjectives such as *nuanced* or *sophisticated* as criteria. Finally, state the highest mark attainable by an answer that retreats to either of two evasions — that a system without beliefs or intentions cannot deceive anyone, or that the entire dispute is merely verbal — and justify that ceiling in one or two sentences.
- Neither the question nor the model answer should include a bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind, and neither should depend on knowledge of any specific real-world deployment or published study. All conceptual resources must be supplied within the paper.
