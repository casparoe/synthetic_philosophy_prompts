so, I've been going back and forth on democracy vs epistocracy for the better part of a year and I finally tried to write down what I actually believe, mostly because I suspect it doesn't hang together and I'd rather hear that from you now than from a room full of people in a couple weeks. I'm presenting this at a reading group and they've asked for my position, not a survey of the literature. Here's the list, in no particular order:

(1) I'm an Arrow skeptic in a fairly strong sense. I don't think there's any fact about what "the people" prefer or want that sits there prior to the rule you use to aggregate individual views. The procedure constructs the collective outcome; it isn't tracking something already on the shelf.

(2) My case for democracy, such as it is, is instrumental and not the jury-theorem kind. I think democracy is defensible because among the procedures actually available to a large modern state it does a decent job of landing on outcomes that are roughly good for the people who have to live under them. Please don't reach for Condorcet here — I don't think its conditions ever hold in a real election, since there's no clean binary question, no common interest, and no independence between voters. So "the many are smarter than the few" is doing zero work in my view.

(3) Given (2), I'm genuinely open to epistocracy. If it turned out that some restricted electorate — screened for knowledge, or weighted votes, or a filtered subset of officeholders — reliably produced better outcomes, then (2) commits me to preferring it. I dont have any deep attachment to everyone getting a vote as such.

(4) I don't think political equality is intrinsically valuable. Equal say matters only insofar as it produces better results or blocks certain kinds of abuse. If it stops doing those things it stops mattering.

(5) But I do hold a publicity condition on legitimacy: the basic rules for deciding who decides have to be justifiable to everyone subject to them, in terms those people couldn't reasonably reject. Not that everyone actually accepts them — just that they'd have no reasonable grounds to turn them down.

(6) And here's the one I added recently, which I think is load-bearing, so let me spell it out. Call it the feedback proviso: I'll only treat a group's competence as a reason to hand it power if that competence would survive the change in public life that handing it power would cause. Competence isn't a fixed property of a set of heads. Narrow the electorate and the campaigns change, the coverage changes, which issues get treated as serious changes, what it pays the remaining voters to figure out changes — so the competence of a narrow electorate under epistocracy isn't the competence you measured while they were sitting inside a wide one. You can't just take the measured gap and cash it in.

What I want from you: which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? A friend tells me (6) rescues (3) from the obvious objection and lets me keep everything else, and I'd like to know whether thats right or whether (6) is doing something to my view that I'm not seeing. Some of these probably only look like tensions — if so, tell me what I've misdescribed rather than just reassuring me, and tell me why the appearance arises. Rank the real ones by severity; I don't want a flat list where everything is "in some tension with" everything else. For each, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me to make it, and then say which single change would clean up the most.

No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write it in a letter. A page or a page and a half is plenty. I'd rather see the reasoning than get a verdict, so walk me through the pairs one at a time instead of summarizing up front.
