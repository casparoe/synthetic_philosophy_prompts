Every country that keeps national accounts draws a boundary around what counts as production, and the boundary has a peculiar shape. Goods that a household makes and consumes itself fall inside it: the maize grown on a family's own plot, the milk from its own cow, the firewood it cuts, the vegetables from its garden are valued at the prices those goods would fetch at market, and the imputed figures enter GDP alongside everything the mills, farms, and offices produce. Services that a household performs for itself fall almost entirely outside it. The cooking of that maize, the cleaning of the house, the tending of the children who eat it and of the grandparents who no longer can — none of it appears anywhere in the accounts. There are two exceptions on the services side only: the housing services that owner-occupiers are treated as renting from themselves, and the work of domestic staff who draw wages. The reasons usually offered for the exclusion are practical rather than philosophical — the difficulty of valuing such work, its supposed self-containment, the comparability of national aggregates — and the hours so excluded are consigned to time-use surveys and occasional satellite accounts, while the headline figure, GDP per capita, remains the yardstick by which living standards and "development" are officially compared across the world.

Set that practice beside two familiar claims. The first, from anthropology, is that foraging bands were not permanently at the edge of starvation but secured an adequate diet in a few hours' work a day and enjoyed considerably more leisure than the farmers who succeeded them. The second, from the skeletal record, is that the shift to agriculture raised total output and supported far more people while lowering stature, worsening nutrition and disease load, and widening inequalities of rank and property. Note that the boundary is drawn around own-account production of *goods* rather than around farms, so in principle a day's gathering would be imputed just as a homegrown squash is; in practice the machinery for such imputations exists for agriculture and for little else. Measured by the official yardstick, the sequence foraging → subsistence farming → wage labour registers as uninterrupted improvement, since each step moves activity from what the accounts ignore or impute to transactions they count directly, and the metric itself contains no term for hours worked, for health, or for the share of the total that reaches any particular person.

What theory of value, and of well-being, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct it as precisely as you can: what does it treat as making a life go better, and what does it treat as mere cost or mere absence of production — is leisure invisible to it, or visible only negatively, as output forgone? Then separate what the practice strictly commits anyone to from what it merely fits comfortably with. It is arguable that an accounting convention aimed at market-linked activity carries no theory of the good at all, and that the philosophical burden lies entirely with those who read GDP as welfare; if so, say where the line between the two readings falls and whether anything internal to the practice — its imputations, its exceptions, its use as the criterion of development — settles the question. Test the goods/services line for coherence: if a household's own housing services and a hired cook's services both count while an unpaid parent's count for nothing, which principle is doing the work — market linkage, third-party separability, the possibility of contracting the task out? Check each candidate against the cases it is meant to explain. Identify which rival accounts of value the metric excludes rather than merely fails to display: views on which well-being consists in the satisfaction of the desires one actually has; views on which it consists in health, friendship, and uncoerced activity; and views on which what matters is the total amount of value in the world rather than the average per head. The Neolithic case turns precisely on that last distinction — many more lives, each somewhat worse on several dimensions — so say whether per-capita GDP embodies a covert average view, a total view, or neither. Finally, tell me what would have to change if we replaced the implicit theory with one on which freedom from toil and length of the working day are constituents of a good life rather than inputs to be economized: would the revision have to be made in the accounts themselves, in the satellite ledgers beside them, or only in the uses to which the headline figure is put? And under that replacement, would the agricultural revolution come out as a gain, a loss, or something whose sign depends on whose ledger one is reading?
