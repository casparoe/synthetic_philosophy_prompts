I am drafting a seminar paper in political philosophy on immigration and the welfare state, built around a distinction that a senior colleague pressed on me. He described the welfare state not as an instrument of equality but as an association of reciprocal risk-pooling: a cooperative insurance scheme whose obligations run only among those who have in fact pooled their risks, through contributions, shared exposure to common shocks, and mutual expectation of support. On this picture, membership is constituted by the pooling itself, not by residence, descent, or whatever paper status the state happens to issue.

From that premise I have drawn four conclusions, and I want to be told where they go wrong, because I suspect at least one of them is doing considerably more work than it can bear.

First: prospective migrants are not parties to the scheme, so the scheme owes them nothing; excluding them is not unjust, merely ungenerous. Second: admission is therefore supererogatory, an act of the members' charity that they may decline without wronging anyone. Third: cosmopolitan arguments from equality, if taken seriously, must be asking us to owe outsiders more than we owe our own contributors, since the resources are finite and every unit extended outward is a unit withdrawn from the pool. Fourth: critics who call border enforcement discriminatory are misusing the word, because the state is not treating non-members worse than members — it is simply not treating them as members.

The difficulty I cannot resolve concerns the fourth conclusion. If exclusion is merely a refusal to include, and if a refusal to include cannot wrong someone who was never a party to the arrangement, then the detention, removal, employer sanctions, and interdiction that actually maintain a closed border appear to fall outside the account altogether: on my own premises they are none of the excluded person's business. That is an uncomfortable place to end up, and I cannot see which step licenses it.

So I have two questions. Is there any version of the reciprocal risk-pooling account that survives contact with the enforcement of borders, or does the account simply commit me to holding that coercion directed at outsiders cannot wrong them? And where does the reasoning break: at the claim that pooling is prior to membership, at the slide from not being a party to the scheme to being owed nothing, at the arithmetic in my third conclusion, or in the treatment of exclusion as an omission rather than as an act? I would rather be told that the framework is incoherent than be handed reasons to soften its conclusions.
