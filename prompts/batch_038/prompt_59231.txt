I'm putting together a small workshop on reduction and emergence, and I've spent the last month trying to reverse-engineer the classification scheme a colleague of mine has been working out in the first chapters of a book she's circulating. She sorts every candidate case into exactly one of three boxes: real reduction (the higher-level talk gets genuinely recovered from the lower level), genuine emergence (the higher level does something the lower level can't), and elimination (the higher-level posit just isn't there — no such thing). Her verdicts so far, with a snippet of her reasoning where I remember it:

- Temperature of a dilute gas / mean molecular kinetic energy: reduction. "The bridge law here is a definition in a lab coat."
- Caloric: elimination.
- The second law — irreversibility out of time-reversible microdynamics: emergence. "You only get it by letting the particle number go and keeping the limit."
- Phase transitions and critical exponents: emergence. "No finite system has a real singularity, and universality does work no micro-story does."
- Mendelian genetics into molecular genetics: reduction. "The gene is a stretch of sequence; everything the old theory wanted gets said again in the new vocabulary." She knows this one is contested and doesnt care.
- Élan vital: elimination.
- Covalent bonding out of quantum mechanics: reduction. "The bond is what the wavefunction says it is."
- Geometrical optics out of wave optics: emergence. "Caustics are precisely where the limit misbehaves."
- Superconductivity: emergence.

Two loose ends in the reasoning. She never says whether "nothing left over" for her means nothing left over at the level of concepts or nothing left over about particular facts, and I've stopped asking. And she never says what makes something elimination rather than a failed reduction.

Here's the case I want to spring on her: molecular handedness. The physics as I understand it, no need to correct me — a molecule and its mirror image have the same energy to any accuracy chemistry cares about, the weak force makes an absurdly tiny difference and there's no mechanism that picks one out; and the full molecular Hamiltonian doesn't really have states with a definite localized geometry in the first place, you only get a shape, and so a shape with a handedness, by clamping the nuclei or by leaning on the environment. Meanwhile handedness does enormous explanatory work, and its generalizations hold across molecules that have nothing else in common.

Plus a wrinkle I want to use. For some molecules the two mirror forms interconvert freely — the barrier is low enough that it tunnels or racemizes on a picosecond timescale, and you simply cannot bottle a left-handed one. For others the interconversion time is longer than the age of the universe.

So: what does she say about handedness, and does she say the same thing about the fast-interconverting version as the slow one? I want the candidate rules that fit every verdict above — I can see at least two or three in there, and I want them stated crisply enough that they actually differ somewhere — then what each of those rules predicts about both versions of my case, then your best guess at her answer and why you'd bet on it, then, if the fast/slow pair doesn't separate the rules, the single further case that would. And flag any of her existing verdicts that sits uncomfortably with whichever rule you end up favoring.

Reason it through; don't send me a bibliography or a list of who's said what, that's not what I'm after.
