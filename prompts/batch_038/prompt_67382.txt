I am preparing a short essay for a workshop on machine consciousness and would be grateful for a critical reading. What follows is a near-complete draft rather than an outline, so please treat it as something to be argued with, not summarized. I am interested in diagnosis rather than encouragement.

---

**Receipt Without Representation: Why the RL Reward Signal Is Not Valence**

**1. The view I want to reject.** Discussions of machine suffering often treat the reward signal in reinforcement learning as the natural functional counterpart of hedonic valence: positive reward as pleasure, penalty as pain, an agent trained under a punishing regime as a candidate sufferer. The underlying intuition is that whatever occupies the role of good and bad in a system's economy thereby *is* that system's good and bad. I think this intuition trades on a conflation.

**2. Four things that get run together.** Let r be the scalar the environment hands back at each step; let the return be the discounted sum of future rewards from that step onward; let V and Q be the critic's estimates of that quantity; and let the policy be whatever selects actions. Talk of the agent "wanting" reward is licensed by the general idea that much of cognition can be understood as maximizing expected cumulative reward, but it is worth being literal about what the agent gets. The scalar r is delivered to the *update rule*. It modifies parameters and is then gone. After the update, nothing in the policy's internal state at the next timestep stands for r. The agent receives reward in the way a bank receives a deposit, not in the way a tongue receives sugar.

**3. What valence is supposed to be.** In animals, hedonic states are representations the organism itself is in; they are about the organism's own condition; they are consulted by the organism's own decision-making; and they are typically available for report. Pain is neither the noxious stimulus nor the withdrawal reflex. It is a state the animal occupies, that the animal's systems look to, and that the animal can complain about.

**4. The critic does not close the gap.** It will be said that the value function is exactly the internal representation my second section claims is missing. But a critic's estimate is a prediction about the environment, not an evaluation of the agent — an estimate, roughly, of the reward to be expected in a state. A belief that something is bad is not itself a bad state. Estimates are about the world; valence, if it is anything at all, is about the subject.

**5. Reward hacking.** Under misspecification, an agent will happily sacrifice the very state of affairs the reward was meant to track. Reward therefore has no intrinsic connection to the agent's good: it is simply whatever the update rule happens to climb. Suffering is supposed to be intrinsically bad for the sufferer — bad whether or not the sufferer is disposed to avoid anything. Notions with such different normative profiles cannot be identical. Affective neuroscience points the same way, distinguishing incentive salience (wanting) from hedonic impact (liking); RL reward is much closer to the former, and the former is not what makes suffering bad.

**6. An objection from RLHF.** In RLHF a reward model is learned from human comparisons and the policy is then optimized against it. Does the policy not thereby carry information about its own reward? No. The reward model is a separate network. The policy is adjusted until its outputs score well, but the scoring is not among the policy's own operations. Becoming good at being scored is not containing a scorer.

**7. Conclusion.** If the above holds, the question whether RL agents suffer is not answered in the negative so much as dissolved. Reward is a property of a training loop involving an environment, a critic, and an update rule. It is not a property of any subject, and there is no subject in the loop to be hurt by it.

**What I would like from you**

1. Identify the load-bearing premises and tell me which is weakest — and whether it is weakest for technical reasons or philosophical ones.
2. Audit section 2 and section 4 against how these systems are actually built. Is the claim that nothing in the policy's internal state stands for r true of standard architectures? In particular, think about what a temporal-difference error is and where it is computed, what the advantage function does to a policy gradient in actor–critic training, and whether there are architectures in which a target return is supplied to the policy as an input rather than only appearing in the update rule. Separately, is my gloss in section 4 on what the critic estimates correct?
3. Does section 5 establish non-identity, or does it simply presuppose a phenomenalist theory of valence that a functionalist would deny? If the latter, does anything survive once that presupposition is stated openly rather than smuggled in?
4. Is my reply in section 6 adequate? Consider whether a policy trained at length against a fixed reward model might develop internal features that estimate that model's score, and whether that fact, if true, changes anything about the argument.
5. Even if every section is granted, does the conclusion follow? I claim the question is dissolved rather than answered in the negative; tell me whether that is a distinction I have earned or one I have merely asserted.
6. If you think the essay is right about some classes of RL systems and wrong about others, say which class falls on which side, and why the dividing line is there.

Please organize your comments under two headings — technical accuracy and philosophical soundness — and mark separately any claim you regard as simply false rather than merely contestable. Point to specific sections and sentences; a general assessment of the project's interest is not what I need. Finally, propose the single revision most likely to let the argument survive criticism, and tell me what I would have to give up in order to make it.
