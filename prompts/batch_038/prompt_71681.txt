Context: my friend Nadia writes a small newsletter in which she runs what she calls the Weil test on organizations that aren't political parties. The test comes from the party essay Weil wrote in London, published after her death in 1950 as a note on the general suppression of political parties, and specifically from the three criteria Nadia recites constantly: a party is a machine for manufacturing collective passion; it's an organization built to bring collective pressure to bear on the thinking of each of its members; and its first and ultimately only aim is it's own growth without limit. She also leans on the point that belonging to a party always drags people into lying, and on Weil's replacement proposal — no parties at all, just small writing-and-reading circles that people feel close to rather than belong to, with nobody standing for election under any banner.

Her verdicts so far, roughly in the order she gave them:

1. Any national political party, left or right: condemned, no argument offered.
2. Her own reading group, eight people, no roll, no positions, you drift in and out: cleared. She says this is precisely the circle Weil wanted.
3. A big public-sector union with a political action fund and endorsed candidates: condemned.
4. A hospital ethics committee whose rulings actually bind: cleared. Her reason was that it holds no standing doctrine, argues each case from scratch, and puts dissent in the minutes.
5. A six-person faculty caucus that coordinates hiring votes: condemned, even though it has no budget, no public voice, and no enthusiasm worth mentioning.
6. A volunteer search-and-rescue corps: cleared, despite ferocious esprit de corps, rituals, and real social cost if you wash out. Her reason was that the pressure there runs toward competence, not toward opinions.
7. A mutual-aid group she cleared at fifteen members and condemned at eight hundred once it had staff, a fundraising target, and a communications person. She reversed herself on the record and said the reversal was the whole point.
