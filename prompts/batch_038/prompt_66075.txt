I'm the "community seat" on a seven-person patient compensation panel at a mid-size private hospital — basically I'm the one member who isn't a doctor or a lawyer, they picked me because I taught high school math for thirty years and someone thought that counted as being good with fairness. We meet every other Tuesday in a room that smells like the carpet cleaner and I've been on the panel four years now. Next meeting we have to rule on a claim from a sleep study that went sideways, and the panel is split down the middle in a way that nobody can seem to explain properly, including me. I've been reading about it at night on my own and getting more confused the deeper I go, so I want to hand the panel a short memo I actually believe before Tuesday.

Here's the study as best I can reconstruct it. A sponsor ran a trial on 52 volunteers with a sleep disorder. On the monitoring night each volunteer got a short-acting sedative, the same kind they use for colonoscopies so you don't remember the procedure, plus a repeat dose that blocks new memories from forming. Then a fair coin was flipped for each volunteer. Heads: they were woken once, given the study procedure (a contrast injection and a few blood draws, which left people pretty nauseous and sore for a day), and put back under. Tails: they were woken twice on the same night, got the identical procedure each time, and had the memory wiped between the two. At the end nobody was told which arm they'd been in — that was in the protocol on purpose. The charts survived, so we do know exactly who was where: it came out 26 in the once group and 26 in the twice group, and 78 total episodes of procedure across the study. The sponsor settled and put $390,000 in a pool, and our charter says we divide it "in proportion to the harm each volunteer experienced," which sounds decisive and turns out not to be at all. Two of the volunteers in the twice group had the sedative not fully take, and they do have fragmentary flashbacks and are still rattled; the other 50 remember nothing whatsoever.

The fight is this. One camp says: divide per person, $7,500 to each of the 52, because the coin was fair, everybody signed up for a 50/50 protocol, and a volunteer is one life, not a stack of episodes. The other camp says: divide per episode, $5,000 per episode, so the twice group gets $10,000 and the once group gets $5,000 — because if you tally up every moment somebody was awake on that table being sick and sore, two thirds of those moments happened to the twice group, and the harm is in the moments, not in the coin. One of our members is a grad student and he keeps saying this is the Sleeping Beauty problem and that the per-episode camp are the "thirders" and that if you asked a volunteer at 3am mid-study what the odds were they'd been woken twice, the honest answer is two thirds and not one half; the sponsor's rep says that's nonsense, a fair coin is a fair coin, and the odds are one half no matter how many times you slice it. Neither of them has told me what any of that has to do with whose check is bigger, and honestly I suspect the two-thirds-versus-one-half thing might be a red herring that's just making everybody dig in.

So please draft the memo I'll circulate — call it 900 to 1200 words, plain language, addressed to the panel. I need: a recommendation with real numbers, meaning a specific dollar figure for each of the three groups (once-and-remembers-nothing, twice-and-remembers-nothing, twice-with-flashbacks), and it has to add up to $390,000 exactly, so show the arithmetic. Tell me clearly whether the two-thirds/one-half dispute actually changes what we should pay anyone, or whether it's a question about beliefs that has no grip on a question about money — and if it does have grip, show me the step where it bites. Take the two flashback cases seriously rather than folding them into a flat rate, since they're the one place where the "nobody remembers it" argument seems to fail. And then give the strongest honest case for the per-person split — I mean the version the sponsor's rep would actually make, not a straw man, including the worry that if we pay per episode then any future sponsor can manufacture a bigger claim by chopping one procedure into three sedated episodes without adding a scrap of harm anyone can point to — and explain why I'm nonetheless going where I'm going, or if you think that argument wins, say so and recommend the flat split instead. End with the rule I should propose we adopt for the next protocol like this one, in one or two sentences a tired person could apply at 5pm. No citations, no reading list, no telling me to consult counsel — we have counsel, she's useless on this.
