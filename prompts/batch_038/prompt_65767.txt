Please take the role of a philosopher who has both a working command of David Lewis's analysis of convention and real experience sitting on hospital patient-safety committees. Think the case through carefully before drafting: open with a short diagnostic paragraph in which you consider what options are actually on the table and what each depends on, and only then write the memo itself. No citations or reading lists — I know the text and the committee does not.

Three months ago I joined the medication-safety committee of a two-hospital system created by last year's merger; I am its only non-clinical member. Our question is the notation of fractional doses in nursing handoff notes and free-text chart entries. The legacy hospital writes a leading zero and no trailing zero ("0.25 mg," "5 mg"); the acquired hospital omits the leading zero and keeps the trailing one (".25 mg," "5.0 mg"). Pharmacy maintains the first practice is intrinsically safer, since a dropped leading zero can turn a tenth of a milligram into a whole one. But every one of our recent near-misses occurred at the boundary between the two sites, when float-pool staff trained in one practice read notes written in the other. Our EHR vendor can normalize all structured dose fields automatically, for a real but bounded cost; it cannot reach free text. The committee votes next month, and I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation beforehand.

Draft that memo. It should come down on one option — mandate the legacy notation system-wide, mandate the acquired one, mandate nothing and buy the vendor fix, or some ordered combination with a stated timeline — and should say what happens to existing free-text entries and to staff hired under the old practice.

Reason explicitly in Lewis's terms, but do not summarize him. Map the case onto his conditions and say which fail: the mismatch cost looks like a coordination problem, yet the legibility asymmetry means the two candidate regularities do not serve the group about equally well, and the parties' preferences over outcomes are not approximately the same. Then tell me whether this is a convention question at all, or whether the case shows the notion does no work for standard-setting where every candidate rule carries a small intrinsic advantage. Finally, engage honestly with the strongest objection on the losing side, from our most senior charge nurse: a policy document will not change what anyone writes, because clinicians' expectations are fixed by what they see colleagues do and by the ward where they trained; so if we mandate anything we should expect to be ignored, and should spend the money instead.
