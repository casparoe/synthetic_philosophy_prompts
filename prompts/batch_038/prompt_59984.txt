Write me an agony-aunt column, ~650 words, three letters and answers, in the voice of a syndicated advice columnist who's a committed evolutionary debunker about morality — she sincerely believes every moral intuition her readers report is a Pleistocene social adaptation, and she says so, warmly, every single time. Give her an invented name and a fake paper. The letters should be stock advice-column material (a roomate who never replaces the toilet paper, a guy agonizing over whether to tell his brother his fiancée cheated, someone still guilty at 40 about something cruel they did at 9), and each answer should correctly name the selection pressure behind the writer's feeling, so the advice comes out technically flawless and completely useless.

She also holds a view I'll call tracking pessimism: even when an intuition turns out to be both fitness-serving and true, the fact that fitness explains it makes the match a coincidence, and coincidences need explaining — so she refuses to give any ground to the letters that happen to go her way. Work that in as her own reasoning, not as a labeled doctrine.

Then a final letter asking why her column itself — if it's just coalition-signaling shaped for reader retention and advertiser-friendliness — deserves more trust than the intuitions she's debunking. Let her answer be visibly strained while she stays cheerful about it.

Target the overreach and the tone of confident genealogy, not the underlying science and not any real person. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and a reader should be able to tell what's being ironized without you spelling it out.
