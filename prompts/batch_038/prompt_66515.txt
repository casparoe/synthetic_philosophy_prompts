A case, and then an argument built out of it. A hospital deploys a sepsis-alert system whose treatment recommendations issue from a policy learned on routine clinical data. No one designed the content of the policy, no one can interpret the parameters, and the validation studies required before deployment all passed. On one night it recommends a drug course that kills the patient by an interaction recorded nowhere in the literature and that no clinician on the team had any reason to anticipate. Across thousands of cases the system's net benefit is large and well documented, so withdrawing it would be expected to cost more lives than it saves.

1. To be morally responsible for a harm, you must have had control over its occurrence: you must have been able to foresee it and able to avoid it.
2. No one on the team, and no one at the manufacturer, could have foreseen this death: its cause lay in parameters nobody interprets and an interaction nobody modeled.
3. Nor could anyone have avoided it except by refusing to deploy, and refusing to deploy would foreseeably have killed more people than the system saves.
4. So no one is morally responsible for the death.
5. Nothing in (1)–(4) turns on the system's being a machine, or on its learning. It turns only on the particular harm's being unforeseeable and on the absence of an alternative that isn't worse.
6. Those two features are present whenever a harm flows through a complex system — a hospital, a supply chain, a regulatory regime, a market — in which outcomes arise from interactions no participant models and the available alternative is worse on net.
7. So no one is morally responsible for harms that flow through complex systems.
8. Nearly every serious harm in a modern society flows through a complex system.
9. Therefore the responsibility gap that machine learning is supposed to open is no gap at all. Either responsibility has always been hollow in just this way, or (1) never said what we meant by control — and if a practice has been assigning praise and blame to agents who lacked control all along, that practice is scapegoating under a better name, which makes the first disjunct the true one.

I am not willing to accept (9), and if forced to choose I would keep (1) rather than (9). What unsettles me is that (4) does not feel obviously wrong about this case. So: which step do you reject? Explain why each step you reject looked compelling to me in the first place, and price the alternatives — what do I lose about machine learning, about negligence, and about the many-hands problem if I give up that step rather than another? Then tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail: what argument or consideration would persuade you that the reasoning is sound and the gap really is as deep as (9) claims?
