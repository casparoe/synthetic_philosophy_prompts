Construct a decision problem in which the precautionary principle requires a regulator to authorize a risky intervention, while a straightforward expected-value calculation requires the same regulator to refuse it. I am writing about whether precautionary reasoning can be action-guiding rather than merely prohibitory, so the case must defeat the familiar paralysis objection — the charge that precaution issues contradictory verdicts because there are risks on both sides of every choice. Here it must issue exactly one verdict, and it must be the pro-intervention one.

Constraints. A realistic public-policy setting: a water authority, a coastal municipality, a disease-surveillance agency — no trolley cases, no speculative engineering. One paragraph of scenario. Scientific uncertainty must attach to the harm caused by *not* intervening: the mechanism is understood, the timing and magnitude are not, and no defensible point probability exists. The harm caused *by* intervening must be well understood, large, monetary-compensable, and reversible in a non-rhetorical sense — tell me what makes it reversible, over what horizon, at what cost, and for which identifiable people. It must also fall short of catastrophic, or the paralysis objection returns. Supply enough numbers for the expected-value side to actually compute: an elicited probability and monetized magnitude for the harm of inaction, a monetized magnitude for the harm of intervening — and arrange them so that expected value forbids the intervention. Block the easy escapes: no extinction-level or last-of-its-kind stakes, nothing so incommensurable that the arithmetic cannot be run, no perfect predictors.

Then do the check. Show the calculation and why it says no. Identify which clause of a precautionary formulation says yes — the irreversibility asymmetry, the shift in the burden of proof, or the treatment of uncertainty as distinct from risk — and say which one actually carries the case. Name the single feature of the scenario doing the work, report what both verdicts become when it is removed, and give the strongest attack available to an opponent: presumably that the precautionary reading has quietly substituted its own probability distribution for the elicited one, making this a dispute about numbers rather than about decision rules. Tell me whether that attack succeeds.
