I am a professor of philosophy at a research university, and I currently chair an ad hoc faculty committee reviewing the retention office's new student-messaging program. Outside consultants have built a system that sends each undergraduate individually tailored messages about advising, counseling, and course registration. Three features matter for my question. First, the system chooses send times from each student's own activity logs, favoring the hours in which that student has historically been most receptive; for some students this means after eleven at night. Second, the messages assert true propositions — an advising appointment has opened, the add/drop deadline is Friday — but the selection of which truths to assert, and in what order, is optimized for the office's click-through and registration targets, and there is no channel through which a student can ask why they were messaged at all. Third, some messages borrow the student's own phrasing from university records and append social-proof statistics ("84% of students in your program have already met with an advisor"), and one variant opens by remarking on the student's late-night study habits in a way no one solicited and no administrator would attempt in person.

In the ensuing debate over whether this counts as legitimate persuasion or impermissible manipulation, both sides have reached for the same sentence: manipulation is what happens when you get around a person's reasons rather than through them. The vendor's representative invoked it to insist that, since every message states a true reason, the program cannot be manipulation. A junior colleague invoked the identical sentence to argue the opposite: since the messages are timed and framed to produce action without the student ever weighing what is said, the program bypasses reason and is therefore manipulation. The slogan is presently bearing the entire weight of the disagreement, and I am not confident it can.

Please take the sentence apart for me, and lay out the distinct precise claims that could be hiding inside it as a numbered menu rather than settling on one favored gloss. For each reading, specify what "reasons" denotes — evidence bearing on the truth of a proposition, considerations that genuinely count in favor, considerations the target would endorse on reflection, or something else — and what "getting through" is supposed to require: a belief-forming mechanism sensitive to that evidence, an unimpaired capacity to weigh it, an actual opportunity to contest it, or the target's own reflective endorsement. Say also whose reasons and which time slice are in play, since the answer differs if we assess the message as received at midnight by a tired nineteen-year-old or as it would appear to that student on reflection a week later.

Then, for each reading, give me a verdict: is it a platitude that no one in the room denies, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false? And does it support the vendor's defense, my colleague's objection, or neither? I would especially like to know whether any reading permits a message that states nothing but true reasons to count as manipulation, and if so, which of the three features described above would be doing the work on that reading. Finally, tell me which reading each of the two disputants most likely had in mind, whether the sentence can discriminate between them at all, and whether the committee would do better to drop it and argue directly about the features of the program.

I do not need citations, a bibliography, or the names of authors; the committee needs the analysis, and I would rather you not guess at sources. Please write in American English, in spelling and idiom alike.
