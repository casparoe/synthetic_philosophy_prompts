Background: I teach a philosophy-of-physics seminar and over the last couple of years I've been keeping a list on my office whiteboard of "demons" — real ones from the literature plus a few I invented for problem sets — each tagged with my gut verdict on whether it can beat the second law. The list has grown past the point where I can see the pattern in it, so I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. Treat the verdicts as data rather than as claims to argue me out of one at a time; the whole point is to find out what I'm already committed to. Here they are. D1: Szilard's one-molecule engine, demon with a finite memory that gets reset every cycle — cannot win, and I'm perfectly happy with the usual story (resetting costs at least kT ln 2 per bit). D2: same engine, but the demon's memory is unbounded and never reset — also cannot win, except that here the usual story just runs out, since nobody ever erases anything; my colleage says I'm being squeamish, and I don't think I am. D3: Feynman's ratchet and pawl — no measurement, no memory in any interesting sense, and it still can't lift the weight. D4: a demon that measures and then immediately scrambles its own memory by a thermal process — cannot win; feels like D1 with extra steps. D5: a demon that is simply told the microstate by an outside lab that did the measurement and paid the Landauer cost over there — cannot win for the whole arrangement, though I'll grant that the demon's own ledger is clean, which bugs me. D6: a demon that reads a pre-existing record it did not create — say a logged microstate from when the gas was prepared, sitting on a drive nobody paid for — can win, at least locally, and I don't think that's a paradox at all. D7: a demon that opens the trapdoor at random — cannot win, trivially; control case. D8: "the Inheritor," my own invention: a chain of demons, each of which fills a finite memory, passes the entire contents to its successor unrestored, and is then switched off without ever being reset, and so on forever. Verdict is that the chain cannot win, but I have no account of why that isn't just deferring the bill indefinately into a future that might never arrive.

The tension I can't resolve is that D2, D3, and D8 all get a "no" while failing for what look like completely unrelated reasons — no erasure, no information, no end to the chain — and D6 gets a "yes" even though that demon is doing essentially what D8 does, only once. My tentative principle, which I suspect is either wrong or badly incomplete, is roughly this: a demon can only cash in correlation it already has, and it cannot manufacture correlation with a gas while sitting at the gas's own temperature. Call it the correlation-is-the-fuel principle. Test it against the list, and repair it if it can be repaired.

What I want back: the best one or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could hand them to a grad student without any hand-waving about information being "physical," with each clause tagged by which of D1–D8 motivated it and what that clause earns by being there; the principle's verdicts on two cases I have *not* listed — a demon whose memory is deliberately held colder than the gas, and a demon that operates only during the last stretch before heat death, when there is nobody left to collect — and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I'd genuinely rather have one clean principle that forces me to give up a verdict than a baroque one that saves all eight, but sketch what the baroque version would have to look like too. Then end your reply with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: D_ , naming the one item on my list you think I should abandon first.
