I help run a twice-monthly interview series organized by my university's philosophy of science reading group. Our audience is mostly advanced undergraduates and graduate students, and we record in a cramped teaching room with one unreliable microphone, so anything we ask has to be followable by ear: a host who reads out a three-clause question loses the room, and a guest who is allowed to settle in will happily talk for ten minutes about something nobody asked. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a robust account of emergence. On her view, certain properties studied in the special sciences — she usually reaches for examples from chemistry and from the neuroscience of consciousness — are genuinely novel with respect to their physical bases, in that they are not deducible even in principle from a complete specification of the physical facts at the lower level, and they carry causal powers that the base does not already supply. She insists this is not merely a claim about our predictive limitations, and she rejects the suggestion that she is a vitalist or denies that everything is physical. She is a disciplined and engaging speaker, but she has well-rehearsed replies to the standard objections and is very good at converting a challenge into an occasion to restate the positive view.

Could you draft ten to twelve interview questions for me, ordered so that the sequence escalates? I want it to open with a question that forces her to pin down exactly what "not deducible in principle" is meant to rule out, since she otherwise slides between a metaphysical and an epistemic reading of her own thesis, and to close with one that asks her to name the single commitment she would give up if the causal exclusion argument turned out to be sound. In between, the set should cover at minimum: the causal closure or completeness of physics, and the threat of systematic overdetermination; the charge that her novel causal powers are a redescription of what the lower level already does, with nothing left over to explain; weak emergence of the Bedau kind, and what her stronger claim adds beyond incompressible-but-in-principle-derivable behavior; how she decides which properties and which levels are emergent, given that a permissive criterion seems to multiply levels at will while a restrictive one looks ad hoc; multiple realizability, and whether she actually needs it for irreducibility; the worry that downward influence either leaves the physical laws incomplete or smuggles in indeterminism at the base; and the pressure from the phenomenal case, where the familiar choice between eliminativism, panpsychism, and emergentism may push her toward a Russellian-monist position she has never advertised.

For each question, add a one-line note identifying the pressure-point it targets and what an evasive answer to it would sound like, so I can tell in the moment whether she has engaged. For the four questions where you expect her to have the most polished stock reply — I would guess the exclusion argument, the redescription charge, the weak-emergence deflation, and the levels question — also give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, phrased so that the follow-up cannot be absorbed by that same reply. Keep the technical vocabulary intact, but gloss it briefly enough for a listener who has encountered reduction in a survey course and has not read the emergence literature. Please include no warm-up questions about her intellectual biography, her teachers, or what she is working on next, and write the entire set in British English.
