I run a small film-and-philosophy reading group and we're doing Memento next week. Everyone in the group has already got their "it's about memory and self-deception" takes ready, so I want to hand them something less obivous: a reading of the film as a picture of the frame problem. Not the pop version — "computers can't tell what's relevant" — but the technical one from McCarthy and Hayes, plus what Fodor and Dennett made of it: an agent that has all the right beliefs sitting in its store still can't work out which of them bear on the action in front of it, can't enumerate the conditions that have to hold for an action to have its usual effect, and can't afford to check the endless list of things that stay the same. Dreyfus's version too, where the moral is that background coping can't run on explicit rules at all.

Here's what I want from you.

First: is Leonard actually a frame-problem case, or something else? My worry is that the frame problem is about a system with a rich stock of beliefs it can't filter, while Leonard has the opposite trouble — his stock doesn't persist past a few minutes. So is the film illustrating the frame problem, or dramatizing what happens to an agent that has to solve it without the resources the problem quietly assumes? Argue it out, don't fudge it.

Second: the system itself. The polaroids with notes on the back, the tattoos, the Sammy Jankis story he recites to everyone he meets. I want those read as a hand-built frame — a man writing out his own ceteris-paribus clauses one at a time, each rule presumably paid for with a mistake, and a default story he uses to interpret new people. Does the film suggest that project can work? What does Teddy's reveal at the end do to it — the fact that the template story is really Leonard's own past wearing someone else's name? And what's going on in the last stretch, where Leonard, knowing exactly what he's just been told, burns the photographs and leaves himself a false lead pointing at Teddy? He isn't selecting relevant facts from his beliefs any more, he's editing the input. I find that a fascinating datum and I can't tell whether it supports or undercuts the people who think relevance selection is just goal-driven and therefore nothing to worry about.

Third: the reverse structure. The colour scenes run backwards, so the viewer meets every scene with no frame for it and can't tell what matters until much later. Genuine philosophical payoff, or a trick I'm over-reading?

And be honest about where the reading breaks. I suspect there's a version of this where I've turned a grief thriller into an AI paper, and I'd rather you told me so than flattered me. No references or citations, please, and don't try to quote the film — paraphrase the scenes instead. I just want the argument and the reading.
