I'm part of a philosophy-of-mind reading group that has spent the better part of a term on the colour phi phenomenon: the apparent-motion illusion reported by Kolers and von Grünau in the mid-1970s, in which a spot of one colour is flashed at one location and, a fraction of a second later, a spot of a different colour is flashed at another, and subjects reliably report having seen a single spot travel between the two and change colour part-way along the path — a change they insist they witnessed before the second spot appeared. Dennett's use of this case turns on two rival explanations of the report. On the Orwellian story, the subject genuinely had the veridical experience of two static, differently coloured spots, but the memory of it is edited after the second flash, so what gets reported is a reconstructed past that was never conscious in that form. On the Stalinesque story, the content is revised before it ever becomes conscious, so the subject's experience really was of a moving spot changing colour mid-flight, and no veridical experience was had and then lost. Dennett's own gloss is that we may suppose the two theorists agree about everything in the brain and even about how the episode feels to the subject, differing only in whether a particular locus is counted as pre- or post-experiential — and he treats that indistinguishability not as an embarrassment but as a lesson, since both accounts presuppose a finish line the brain does not contain. His critics reply that this is far too quick: there is still a substantive question about when, in the causal history of a report, the subject's experience was fixed, and it is a question about processing order that better temporal resolution and better-designed tasks might yet answer. Roughly that is where matters have stood for three decades, including through the multi-author exchange that Dennett and Kinsbourne provoked in the mid-nineties.

What puzzles me is the shape of the impasse rather than the answer to it. Nobody appears to have moved, and yet nobody appears to be talking past anybody in an obvious way either: both sides accept the psychophysics, both accept that the report concerns a mid-path colour change, and both are physicalists of a broadly functionalist stripe. My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I would like you to test it rather than flatter it — is that the disagreement is not empirical but concerns what a theory of consciousness is obliged to deliver. On that reading, one side treats the question whether there was a veridical experience that got rewritten as a genuine question about the causal history of a mental state, while the other treats experience as fixed by dispositions to report and by whatever leaves traces for later use, so that the question lacks a determinate answer because nothing in the world answers to it. If that is right, the debate has stalled not for want of data but because two different questions are being answered under a single label, and the claim that there is no fact of the matter would be either trivially true or an illicit stipulation dressed as a discovery — which is, I take it, precisely the thing to settle.

So: diagnose the disagreement. Tell me which of the following it really is — a verbal dispute about what experience or consciousness picks out; two substantive but empirically equivalent theories underdetermined by all possible behavioural evidence; two different questions, one about neural processing and one about phenomenology, sharing a name; or a genuine and merely very hard empirical question about temporal order that better methods could still resolve. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says rather than in a general history of the consciousness literature, and be explicit about the load-bearing premises on each side, including any premise the two sides share that is doing most of the work. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, an experiment, or a change of question — or, if you think the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, argue for that and say what the field ought to be doing instead. In particular, please address whether a no-report paradigm could break the tie: a design in which subjects make a speeded forced-choice judgement about the timing or the colour of the illusory event at an experimenter-chosen moment, or an implicit measure such as smooth-pursuit eye movements tracking the illusory path, rather than a retrospective verbal report — and state in advance what each side would make of either result. Two practical notes: I mean the apparent-motion illusion, not the Φ of integrated-information theory, which merely shares the letter; and please write in American English, with no bibliography, reading list, or citations I have no way of checking, paraphrasing any specific paper you lean on instead of quoting it.
