The view I want pressure-tested is a Deweyan account of democratic legitimacy, and I should like to know precisely where it is exposed. As I hold it, legitimacy attaches neither to consent, nor to the fair aggregation of already-formed preferences, nor to the wisdom of outcomes; it attaches to the experimental cycle. A policy is legitimate insofar as it is adopted as a revisable hypothesis, with its criteria of success and failure stated in advance and in public, and with a standing mechanism — a sunset clause, a review, an obligation to report against those criteria — that makes reversal cheaper than persistence. A public, on this picture, is not a pre-existing body of citizens but whatever group of people comes to perceive the indirect consequences of some set of transactions and organises to inquire into them, and much of the business of the state is to make those consequences visible and articulable. The corollary I draw is the one I most want attacked: the central political evil is not oppression as such but fixity, the foreclosure of inquiry, whether by entrenched interest, by ideology, or by experts who have stopped treating their own conclusions as provisional. The test of an institution is not whether it gets things right but whether it can learn from having got things wrong.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward rather than listed indiscriminately, and I want to know which of them a committed defender of my own persuasion would concede as damaging and which they would explain away, and on what grounds. Include the cases the view seems unable to describe at all: emergencies requiring decision before inquiry can conclude; harms that are irreversible and therefore cannot be experimented with; domains where feedback is so slow, noisy, or confounded that a policy cannot be replicated or its criteria evaluated; and the suspicion that criteria of success are themselves political and cannot be fixed neutrally in advance, so that the experimental idiom merely launders a substantive conception of the good. Say also what the view predicts that its rivals do not — the agonist or decisionist who thinks politics concerns the constitution of a friend and an enemy rather than the solving of problems, the epistemic democrat who thinks majorities track the truth, the contractualist who thinks legitimacy bottoms out in reasons no one could reasonably reject — and where those predictions have come out the wrong way.

I would like a candid judgement about insulation. It is easy to redescribe any failure of my view as a failure to have framed the hypothesis properly, identified the right public, or set the right criteria; if that move is available in every case, does the view risk anything at all? The charge is peculiarly damaging here, since the whole claim is that politics should be conducted as experimental method is conducted, and a method that cannot fail is not a method. Say plainly whether you think the insulation is a genuine defect, or whether there is a non-question-begging way of specifying what would show the view false — and if there is, specify it.

What would move me most is an actual case in which the machinery of pre-stated criteria and cheap reversal demonstrably made things worse than a fixity-preserving institution would have, together with an answer to the irreversibility problem that does not simply retreat to the claim that all consequences are in principle traceable.

Please take a definite position and give your reasons, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose between them. Write in British English, in continuous prose of the kind one would put in a considered letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And no bibliography and no citations — I want your reasoning, not a reading list.
