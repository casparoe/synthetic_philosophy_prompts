Retired four years back from a national lab, where I spent most of my working life on detector instrumentation, so I finally have time to read outside my field. Berkeley has got me. Specifically the move in the Principles where he dares you to try to form the idea of something existing unconceived — you try, and whatever you picture you are picturing, so you've failed, and he thinks that settles the question — and the same thing in the Dialogues where Philonous does it to Hylas. People call it the master argument. I'm not convinced it's a master of anything, but I can't say exactly where it slips.

I have a reading group, eight of us, mostly ex-engineers plus one classicist, we meet in my kitchen. Nobody has read Berkeley except me, and at least two of them are idealists who would deny it under oath.

So: write me a decision tree that sorts us. Starting commitment is that you'll grant, for the sake of the exercise, that anything you succeed in conceiving you conceive as somehow present to your mind. From there the tree walks down and drops you at a named position.

Some requirements.

- Ten to fourteen nodes. Each node is one plain question with two or three lettered answers, and each answer says where it goes: another node or a leaf. No node may name a philosopher, name an -ism, or use a term a non-philosopher wouldn't naturally reach for. I want to answer each from gut, not from knowing which way it's leaning.
- At least four nodes should be about the argument rather than the metaphysics: whether failing to conceive something shows it impossible or only shows I can't do it; whether there's a real gap between trying to conceive a thing as unperceived and trying to conceive a thing without perceiving it; whether "matter" is a claim that could come out false or a word doing no work at all; and whether the same reasoning that finishes off matter also finishes off other minds.
- Cases we'd actually argue over at my table: a scaler in an empty basement counting pulses that nobody reads until Tuesday; the tree behind the shutters of a house nobody's been in for a year; the number of grains in a sealed jar; the handwriting in a notebook by someone who has died. Use that last one only if it earns its place.
- Leaves I want, at minimum: Berkeley's own view; the person who says the argument fails but immaterialism might be right anyway on other grounds; flat-footed realism that refuses the setup; the representationalist who keeps the outer world and admits they can't picture it; the view that a physical object is just a bundle of what-you'd-see-if-you-looked conditionals; a neutral or structural leaf where the question doesn't get a grip; and a deflationary leaf for whoever thinks the whole thing is a language trick. Add others if the space calls for it.
- Every leaf gets a name, or "unnamed" if nobody actually holds it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders reliably change the subject away from.

Headings, in this order:

1. **Entry point** — about 150 words on the one concession everyone makes before question 1, and why it's supposed to be harmless.
2. **The tree** — the main event, call it 1200 words. Numbered nodes, lettered answers, destinations.
3. **The leaves** — a short paragraph each, maybe 700 words all told.
4. **Empty squares** — 250 words on answer-combinations the tree makes available but nobody in the history of the subject has taken. For each, say whether its incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
5. **Pairs I can't separate** — 200 words naming the leaves where two people with genuinely different views will land, plus one extra question I could ask at the table to break the tie if it's breakable.
6. **Kitchen-table notes** — 150 words on running this on eight people in one evening without it turning into a lecture, including where people will predictably stall and where they'll fake an answer to look consistent.

Last thing: don't soften it into a survey. I want an instrument that discriminates. If two branches collapse in practice, tell me so in section 5 rather than papering over it.
