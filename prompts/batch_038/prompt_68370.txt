Our reading group has two members who have spent a year arguing past each other about populism, and I've been asked to get them to write down where they actually stand. One is broadly a populist democrat: thinks 'the will of the people' is a real thing in democratic politics and not just a slogan, and that a system which won't let majorities act on it is oligarchy with nice manners. The other is a liberal pluralist: thinks 'the people' is a costume whoever wins the last election gets to wear, that any claim to speak for the whole is a claim to exclude the part that disagrees, and that the social choice results — cycling, agenda manipulation, the impossibility theorems — show there's no unique popular will sitting there to be found.

Write the joint statement they should have produced instead. I want:

1. A numbered list of claims both could genuinely sign. Not vacuous ones — no 'both agree democracy matters.' I'd expect this section to include things about how vote totals underdetermine outcomes, and about what an election or a referendum actually licenses a government to do, phrased so neither side feels shortchanged by the wording.

2. The residual disagreements, each stated in words its own side would accept. So give me the populist's claim the way a populist would put it and the pluralist's the way a pluralist would, side by side, so I can see that they're genuinely contradicting each other rather than talking about different things.

3. The cruxes: sub-questions where settling the answer would actually move at least one of them. My guess is that one is wether the impossibility results show a collective will doesn't exist at all, or only that it can't be read off any particular voting procedure; and another is whether 'the people' is supposed to be a pre-existing aggregate you discover or something that gets constituted in the very act of being claimed. If you think the real cruxes are somewhere else, say so and say why.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or test both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down before the answer is known. Be specific — e.g. a referendum with three mutually exclusive options on the ballot, high turnout, winner takes 51.4%: what does each camp say that result expresses, and what would each say about a rerun with a different ballot order or a different question framing? If you think some crux has no test both would accept and isn't empirically settleable in principle, say that plainly instead of inventing one.

5. Finally, if part of this fight is verbal — if they're using 'the will of the people' in two different senses and would agree on nearly everything once you fix the senses — identify which part, and give the two fixed senses explicitly.

Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section first, then a table with columns for the disagreement, each side's own wording of it, the crux it turns on, and the test plus both predictions. We're handing it to the rest of the group at the end of term to decide what we spend next year on, so it has to read as something both authors would put their names to, not a neutral summary written from outside the fight.
