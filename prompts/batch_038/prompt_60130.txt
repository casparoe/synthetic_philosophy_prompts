I'm running a session on longtermism for a small film-and-philosophy reading group and we're watching Interstellar. I want the discussion to actually engage the argument instead of dissolving into "space is beautiful, love is transcendent," so I'd like you to write me a reading of the film through strong longtermism -- the view that the far future has enormous expected value, that the possible lives of people who don't exist yet deserve at least as much weight as the lives of everyone alive now, that extinction is a uniquely bad outcome because it forecloses all of that, and so on.

Here's the thing that's stuck in my head, and I'd like you to either develop it or demolish it. The film looks like it sets up the textbook longtermist forced choice -- Plan A gets everyone currently on Earth off the planet, Plan B is a crate of frozen embryos that founds a colony somewhere else while everyone at home dies -- but then it quietly deletes the dilemma, because Professor Brand knows Plan A is unsolvable without data from inside a black hole and lies about it to get the mission launched. So the tradeoff isn't a real one; it's manufactured by a deception. Does that make the film a longtermist tract (the embryos matter more than Murph, and the lie was obviously worth it) or a reductio of it (the numbers only get to dominate once someone has hidden the alternatives from the people being traded away)? Sit with that and tell me which reading the film actually supports, and where it resists both.

A few other threads -- say so if any of them are wrong-headed:

- The wormhole and the tesseract turn out to come from far-future humans who survived. That's longtermism running backwards: the descendants rescue their ancestors. What does a bootstrap like that do to the claim that we owe everything to the future, when in this story the future is what saves us?
- Dylan Thomas's villanelle gets recited throughout as the film's slogan, but the poem is about one man watching his own father die. Does the borrowing work, or does the film's whole framing of refusing to go gently into extinction depend on sliding from a private grief to a species-level imperative?
- Structurally, a handful of people at one moment decide the fate of everything downstream. That looks like a hinge-of-history story. Does the film need that, and is it defensible?
- The last act has Cooper stealing a ship to go find Amelia on Edmunds' planet -- a choice about one person, not about maximizing. Murph gets her colony and her family and lets him go. Is the ending person-affecting or impartial? Does the film know the difference?
- The blight is slow rather than sudden, which is much closer to the risk profile longtermists actually argue about than an asteroid would be. Does the film treat it as manageable, and what does that say about its optimism?
