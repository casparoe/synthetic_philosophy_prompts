I've come to hold a position I call the reception asymmetry, and I want a serious attempt to break it. It starts from Du Bois in "Criteria of Negro Art" — the essay printed in The Crisis in October 1926, from a speech he gave that June at the NAACP conference honoring Carter G. Woodson — and his insistence that all art is propaganda and ever must be, whatever the purists say. My addition is this: whether a work functions as propaganda is a property of how it is received and circulated, not of what it declares. A work that announces its partisanship — the protest novel with a thesis, the docudrama built around a verdict, the mural that tells you what it is about — invites its audience to treat it as a claim to be assessed, and thereby partly disarms itself; viewers enter a posture of evaluation and discounting. The work that presents itself as autonomous, formal, universal, or simply beautiful is the more effective propaganda precisely because its stakes never surface as anything anyone could agree or disagree with. So the political work is done mostly by the art that denies doing it, and explicitly didactic art is comparatively weak. I'm delivering a keynote at a documentary festival's curatorial symposium, to an audience split between programmers who think in these terms and scholars of African American literature who will know Du Bois far better than I do, and I need to know how exposed I am before I say it out loud.

Tell me what would count against the view. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want the ranking to be argued rather than asserted. Specifically:

Which observations would be decisive, and which would only be uncomfortable? Documented cases where an avowedly propagandistic work demonstrably changed beliefs or mobilized people; cases where formally austere, disavowing work turned out to do no ideological work at all; and any evidence about whether recognizing a persuasive intent actually makes audiences scrutinize more, or simply makes them distrust and discount, since those point in opposite directions for me. There is an empirical literature on absorption in narrative and on resistance to overt persuasion attempts — say where it bears on the asymmetry and which way it cuts, and be candid if it is indifferent.

What does the view predict that its rivals don't, and have those predictions held up? Its natural competitors are an intention-based account (a work is propaganda if its maker meant it to persuade) and a plain aesthetic-autonomy account (propaganda is a defect in the work, full stop). The asymmetry ought to imply, for instance, something about what censors actually go after: if declared partisanship is what marks the political, authorities should reliably catch the didactic work and miss the rest. Does the historical record of suppression support that, or undercut it? It should also imply something about retrospective politicization — that the works which disclaimed politics are the ones later read as political. Assess both.

Then the internal problems, which I suspect are worse. If propagandistic force is wholly a property of reception, is the position still Du Boisian, or has it slid into a generic ideology-critique thesis about all cultural production, with Du Bois as decoration? Du Bois wanted truth and beauty alongside propaganda, and he objected that his side was policed while the other side was permitted every license to tell its own truth; does the asymmetry contradict that complaint, or explain it? And there is the unfalsifiability worry I can't shake: a didactic work that succeeded was "really" doing covert formal work, an autonomous work that did nothing was "really" autonomous, and every counterexample gets absorbed. Say honestly whether the view risks anything at all. If it doesn't, tell me whether that is fatal for a claim of this kind — a thesis about how art functions politically, not a law of nature — or only a sign that it needs to be sharpened into a comparative form that could come out false. If sharpening is the answer, give me the sharpened version and name one observation that would refute it.

Finally, separate out what a careful Du Bois scholar would themselves concede as counting against me from what only a partisan of art for art's sake would press, since the first is what I need to be ready for on the panel. I'm not after a bibliography or a reading list — I can't check anything you cite, and citations would be decoration here. I do want you to state how confident you are in each of your main claims about the position's exposure, single out the point you are least sure of, and tell me plainly where you think my own framing is muddled rather than working within it.
