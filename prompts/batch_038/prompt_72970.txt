Right — I chair a philosophy-of-mind reading group and two of our members have been talking past each other since October. I've told them the only way out is a joint adversarial-collaboration statement they could both put their names to, and I'd like you to draft it so they can see what that would even look like.

The two camps:

**A** — introspection is a genuine source of knowledge about one's own current mental states: not inference, not theory, not covert self-observation of one's own behaviour, and broadly trustworthy within its proper domain.

**B** — self-ascription is a theory-laden interpretive process that leans on much the same evidence we use when reading other people, plus a handful of internal cues, and is systematically error-prone: confabulated reasons, changes of mind the subject never notices, self-reports of mental imagery that don't line up with what those people can actually do on imagery tasks.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign, and I mean sign without gritting their teeth. No mush. I want claims that constrain something — for instance about what the experimental paradigms do and don't establish, and about which self-reports even A thinks are junk.

2. A table of what's left over: the topic, the disagreement stated in wording A would accept as a fair description of A's own view, the same stated in wording B would accept for B's side, and a final column saying whether the row is a real disagreement or a terminological one.

3. Two or three cruxes — sub-questions where an answer would actually shift at least one of them. Here's my hunches, and push back if you think I'm barking:
   - scope. A may only ever have been claiming reliability for occurrent phenomenal states — the pain, the itch, the way the room looks, the imagery — while nearly all of B's good evidence is about attitudes: beliefs, preferences, reasons, values. If that's right, do the camps even overlap on the phenomenal side, or is the whole fight about attitudes?
   - the causal-process exemption. Every time somebody confabulates a reason, A can say the process that produced the reason was never introspectable in the first place, so nothing whatsoever has been shown about introspection. Does B have a paradigm where the reported state itself, rather than its upstream causes, comes out wrong? Describe it in generic terms — no citations, no author names, no dates, I can't check any of it anyway.
   - the constitution worry. If on B's picture self-interpretation partly constitutes the attitude rather than detecting it, then "is it reliable?" is the wrong shape of question entirely, and one of their rows above should be struck. Say so if you think that's what's going on.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or type of study both would agree bears on it, and each camp's prediction written down in advance — that's the bit that matters, they have to commit to what they'd expect to find before anyone looks.

5. One thing each camp would accept as a genuine defeater for their own view. If you think one of them doesn't have any, say that plainly and explain what it implies about the arguement they've been having for the last eight months.

Don't referee it — I'm not after a winner — and don't lecture me about how the debate is stale. Tone should be dry and joint, like something two people who mildly dislike each other could still read aloud without wincing.
