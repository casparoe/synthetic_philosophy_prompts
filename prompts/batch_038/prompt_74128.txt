I'm teaching a small grad seminar on the philosophy of economics this term and week five is revealed preference — specifically, what "utility" is even claiming once you take the Samuelson program seriously. My TA wants to turn the session into a worked problem instead of a reading discussion, and a colleague over in the econ department told him the students don't need philosophy for that, they just need to run the axioms, which is more or less the thing I want the session to unsettle. So I need a designed case, not one of the stock illustrations. Here's the spec. Give me a finite choice data set for one shopper: three goods, at most four observations, integer prices and quantities, and she exhausts her budget every single time. The data must satisfy the weak axiom as it's normally stated for a finite set — spell out which version you're using, but I mean: whenever bundle j was affordable at observation i's prices and she bought something other than j, then bundle i was not affordable at observation j's prices — so that no pair of observations is directly revealed preferred in both directions. But the data must violate GARP: there has to be an indirect revealed-preference cycle with at least one strict link in it, so no utility function can rank the chosen bundles consistently with the choices. Write out every inequality with the actual numbers and check it line by line; I want to put this on the board and have a student verify it in two minutes, so nothing can depend on rounding or on a tie I have to squint at.

The setting should be an ordinary life rather than a lab, and the failure has to be clean. My own half-built version had a woman buying three staples at a market where the relative prices rotate from week to week, the same ten units every week — three of each plus one bonus unit — with the bonus unit landing on a different staple each time because of some simple habit of hers about which one is mid-priced that week. I could never get the price vectors to come out so that the pairwise check passes and the cycle still closes, and I've given up on it, so either repair my version or throw it out entirely. Either way: whatever you hand me comes with an articulable, stable decision rule that generates the numbers, and the case must not get its bite from her tastes shifting between observations, from her being confused about prices or qualities, or from you just stipulating that she has intransitive preferences. The cycle has to fall out of a rule she could state aloud to you at the checkout. If the rule sounds like a preference ordering in disguise, say so and then tell me whether that's a problem for the case or the whole point of it.

Then the part I actually care about, since this is a philosophy seminar and not an econometrics one. Say what each of three readings of "utility" makes of this woman: the operationalist one, where utility is just a name for consistent choice and there's nothing further there; the representation-theoretic one, where having a utility function just is passing the global consistency test; and the welfare one, where utility is about how her life goes from the inside. Build enough into the scenario that the third reading has something to grab onto — I don't want it bolted on at the end as a remark about how she seems perfectly content. The payoff I'm after is that the first two are supposed to be the same doctrine and they aren't: the pairwise test she passes and the global test she fails are both "consistency of choice," so the operationalist owes us an account of which one constitutes having a utility at all. Make that argument explicitly and tell me which of the three readings is in worst shape afterwards. Then the diagnostics. Which single inequality is doing the work — point at it. What happens if I delete an observation, or turn one strict link into an equality: does the data set become rationalizable again, and does that mean the woman changed? And could you have pulled the whole thing off with two goods instead of three? I have a hunch you can't, that with two goods the pairwise check already forces acyclicity, but I've never sat down and proved it — so if the hunch is right give me the argument in a few sentences, and if it's wrong give me the two-good numbers instead. Last thing: how would someone who wants to keep "rational = has a utility representation" resist calling her irrational — changing tastes, the objects of choice being weeks or menus rather than bundles, the axioms as empirical hypotheses about maximizing rather than constitutive norms of rationality — and what does the best version of that resistance cost a program that started out saying utility is nothing over and above observable choice? No references or reading list, please; I know the literature, I want the case and the reasoning.
