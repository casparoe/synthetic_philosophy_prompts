I'm grinding through the recognition chapter of the Phenomenology (the lordship and bondage bit) and I've hit a wall in the transitional paragraphs right before the two figures actually show up. I'm working from the old public-domain English translation that calls the book The Phenomenology of Mind — pretty sure it's Baillie's, 1910 — so paragraph numbers are the standard ones. Here's the chunk, §182, §184, §185, more or less continuous:

"The first does not have the object before it only in the passive form characteristic primarily of the object of desire, but as an object existing independently for itself, over which therefore it has no power to do anything for its own behalf, if that object does not per se do what the first does to it. The process then is absolutely the double process of both self-consciousnesses. Each sees the other do the same as itself; each itself does what it demands on the part of the other, and for that reason does what it does, only so far as the other does the same. Action from one side only would be useless, because what is to happen can only be brought about by means of both."

"...Consciousness finds that it immediately is and is not another consciousness, as also that this other is for itself only when it cancels itself as existing for itself, and has self-existence only in the self-existence of the other. Each is the mediating term to the other, through which each mediates and unites itself with itself; and each is to itself and to the other an immediate self-existing reality, which, at the same time, exists thus for itself only through this mediation. They recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another."

"This pure conception of recognition, of duplication of self-consciousness within its unity, we must now consider in the way its process appears for self-consciousness. It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes."

And earlier, §178: "SELF-CONSCIOUSNESS exists in itself and for itself, in that, and by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness; that is to say, it is only by being acknowledged or 'recognized'."

The thing I can't get straight is the fit between §182 and §185. §182 states a hard condition: nothing comes of one side acting alone, the whole thing has to be a double process, each doing per se what it demands of the other. Then §185 says the first way this appears is exactly a one-sided split — one party merely recognized, the other only recognizing. So is the lordship/bondage relation a case where §182's condition is simply not met, and therefore a non-starter that has to collapse, or is it a genuine, if distorted, instantiation of the double process? Notice that the bondsman does act — he serves, he works on the thing — so it isn't that only one of them does anything. What seems missing is that his acting isn't done "per se," from himself, if it's wrung out of him by the fear of death. Which pushes me to a question I've been going back and forth on:

There are two ways the standard gloss describes the master's failure. Either (a) he really is recognized, but the recognition is worthless because it comes from someone he himself doesn't count as an independent self — a mere thing-like dependent; or (b) he never gets recognized at all, because coerced compliance doesn't satisfy the reciprocity condition laid out in §182, so what he has is obedience and not acknowledgment. Those sound similar but I think they imply quite different chapters: on (a) the story is about a bad exchange between two real recognizers, on (b) it's about a relation that never gets instantiated and so was never a candidate for satisfaction in the first place. Which one does this text actually support, and what would settle it — is there something in the way §185 is phrased ("in the way its process appears for self-consciousness", "the break-up of the middle term into the extremes") that tells us whether the asymmetry is a failure of the concept or just its phenomenal first draft?
