For eleven years I have opened the third session of my graduate seminar on the norms of sequential choice by putting five short cases to the room and announcing, without argument, the verdicts I take to be correct. This year I intend to turn the exercise on myself. I should like the five verdicts treated as a body of precedent — as a ruling chamber's line of decisions might be treated — and my own consistency tested against a sixth case that one of my students has devised and that has, I confess, unsettled me.

The standing case is the familiar one, and I shall fix the details so that nothing turns on vagueness. There are three bundles, A, B, and C, and the agent's all-things-considered preferences over them are strictly cyclic: A to B, B to C, and C to A. She begins holding C. A broker offers swaps one at a time, each swap requiring the payment of a fee; the fees are stipulated to be trivial relative to the differences in her ranking, so that no swap is ever rendered unattractive by its cost alone. She is fully informed about the bundles, she is not coerced, and her preferences do not change over the episode.

My five verdicts are these.

(i) The agent takes each of the three swaps as it is offered — C for B, then B for A, then A for C — each of which she prefers, considered as it strikes her at that node, to the holding she would otherwise keep. She arrives back at C, three fees poorer. Irrational. When pressed, I add that the sum is beside the point: three cents would indict her as surely as three hundred dollars.

(ii) An agent with precisely the same cyclic preferences sees the whole sequence in advance and works backward from its end. She accepts the first swap, refuses the second, and finishes holding B, one fee poorer and better pleased than she began. Not irrational. I insist here on a detail my students reliably miss: nothing about her preferences has altered. They remain cyclic; they remain, by any test applied to the ranking considered in isolation, incoherent. What differs is the manner of her choosing.

(iii) An agent, again cyclic in her preferences, finds a counterparty willing to hold her to a costless pledge and binds herself in advance to accept the first two swaps and refuse the third. She therefore finishes holding A, her most preferred bundle, two fees poorer. At the third node, abandoning the pledge would by her own then-present lights be the attractive thing to do, since she prefers C to A; she honours it anyway. Not irrational — indeed I tell the class that this is the one case in which the received picture possesses no vocabulary for the virtue on display, and that she ends better off than the agent of (ii).

(iv) An agent whose preferences are strictly transitive — a single coherent ranking, no cycle anywhere in her — faces a sequence in which the option she prefers at the first node, considered only as it presents itself there, forecloses the branch she would have preferred on the whole. She takes it, and arrives at an outcome she ranks below one she was offered at the outset and could have had. Irrational.

(v) An agent with cyclic preferences, and with exactly the disposition of the agent in (i) — had the sequence been offered she would have gone all the way around it — is nevertheless asked to choose only once, between her present holding and a single alternative. She chooses and goes home. No sequence is offered to her, and none ever will be. My verdict: there is nothing in the case about which she could have been right or wrong. The charge has nothing to fasten onto.

Now the sixth case. My student's agent has the cyclic preferences described above, and the broker's three swaps are offered to her in the exploiting order. She accepts the first and declines the second — so that her trajectory through the sequence is, to the letter, the trajectory of the agent in (ii), and she finishes holding B, one fee poorer, better off than she started. But she declines it for no reason connected to the sequence at all. She holds a standing personal policy, adopted a decade earlier for considerations having nothing whatever to do with swaps or brokers or money, of never accepting a second proposal from the same stranger within the same week. No pledge was available to her in this episode; we may stipulate that no counterparty would have held her to one. She had no plan about any of this, and at the moment of refusal she was, my student specifies with some relish, thinking about lunch. When the whole sequence is afterwards laid out before her she is surprised, and readily agrees that had the second proposal come from her sister she would have taken it, and the third after it, and gone around the circle to end where she began with three fees gone.

What I want from you is the following, in roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words.

First, set out the principles of diachronic rationality that are jointly consistent with all five of my verdicts. There is more than one, and I would rather have each stated as a decision procedure an agent might actually run, or as a criterion a critic might actually apply, than as a slogan. For each candidate, show how it delivers every one of the five verdicts, and identify which verdicts do the work of eliminating the candidates you reject — I expect at least two familiar-looking candidates to die, and I should like to be told precisely where and why. Some of these procedures have names; use the names if you are confident of them, but nothing turns on labels.

Second, predict my verdict on the sixth case under each surviving candidate, and say which of my five precedents each candidate treats as the closest analog. I am particularly interested in how each candidate copes with the fact that the sixth agent and the agent of (ii) perform the very same acts and reach the very same outcome, since a view that must say those two agents differ in rationality owes an account of what the difference consists in, and a view that says they do not differ owes an account of why foresight seemed to matter so much in (ii).

Third, tell me what I should say if I wish to remain consistent with myself, and what I would thereby be committing myself to. Be explicit about the cost in both directions: if I acquit the student's agent, what have I made safe, and can I still say what I said in (i) about three cents indicting as surely as three hundred dollars? If I convict her, what becomes of (v), where I held that a merely dispositional liability to be pumped fastens on nothing, and what becomes of (iii), where I praised an agent for acting against her own then-present preferences?

Fourth, construct one further case of your own that would discriminate among the surviving candidates where the sixth does not, or that would discriminate among variants within a single candidate, and state what each candidate says about it.

Please do not offer me references, a bibliography, or a survey of who has held what; I have the literature and I am not asking after it. I am asking whether my precedents hang together, and on which principle they hang.
