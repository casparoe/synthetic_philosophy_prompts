I am working on whether a citizen's baseline attitude toward her own government should look more like the high trust characteristic of the Nordic countries or the ingrained suspicion characteristic of the United States, and I want an essay that treats this as a question about the rationality of an attitude rather than about which polity is better administered.

Please grant two stipulations for the sake of the argument. First, take trust in government as a three-place relation — a truster, a trustee, and a domain of action entrusted — rather than as a single diffuse sentiment, so that "trust" and "distrust" can vary independently across domains such as taxation, policing, benefits administration, and regulation. Second, grant this feedback mechanism: monitoring is costly, so high trust purchases less scrutiny, and less scrutiny raises the probability of exactly the failures that would warrant lowering trust; pervasive suspicion, conversely, buys more scrutiny, which detects malfeasance more reliably but makes collective action slower and more expensive.

The controversy is what that mechanism does to the evidential standing of the two attitudes. Does it make trust self-undermining and suspicion self-confirming, so that the suspicious citizen is better calibrated to the evidence a non-expert can actually gather? Or does it cut the other way, because a suspicious attitude manufactures the evidence it predicts and thereby insulates itself from disconfirmation, while trust is the only attitude that can be falsified by the trustee's own conduct?

In weighing the two sides, please take up: the institutional-quality explanation of Nordic trust, on which trust is a reasonable inference from low corruption and reliable service delivery rather than a cultural given; the objection that such an inference cannot travel to a large, heterogeneous, federal polity with weaker state capacity; the American claim that distrust is a structural feature of a constitutional order built on separation of powers and contestability rather than a national mood; and the possibility that the two attitudes differ not in accuracy but in which kinds of error they are disposed to make.

Commit to a verdict. I want a definite position — which attitude is the more reasonable one, for which domains, and on what grounds — not a balanced survey that leaves the choice to me. State your reasons, identify what you take to be the strongest point on the losing side and explain precisely where it fails, and flag how much of your conclusion depends on contested empirical claims about state capacity or the effects of monitoring, so that I can see what survives if those claims are set aside.
