I'm writing a seminar paper on the 1996 US welfare reform and I want to try a lens that isn't in any of our readings, so tell me if it holds up. The facts as I have them: AFDC gets replaced by TANF, block grants to states, work requirements, a five-year lifetime limit, and financial penalties on states that don't hit their participation rates. Caseloads then fall by more than half over the next decade, and the "leavers" studies find a bunch of people who left the rolls without stable employment — some with no earnings and no benefits at all.

The usual framing is incentive-based: make benefits conditional on working and people work. But I want to read the same machinery as a screening device instead. Compliance is costly, the cost of complying isn't the same for everyone, so who's left on the rolls is fixed by who can absorb the cost — which means the caseload drop might be the screen working rather than the incentive working.

So: set the model up properly — who's the principal, who's the agent, what's the signal, what type is supposed to get sorted out. Then where does the screening story break, especially on whether it sorts on the trait the deserving/undeserving language claims to track? And does the episode end up confirming, wrecking, or just outrunning the model?
