Longtime lurker in the decision-theory and econ corners of the internet, first time I've actually built an argument rather than just complaining in a comment thread, and it seems to have worked too well.

My roommate is an econ major and whenever I push on revealed preference he gives me the usual deflationary line — it's not a psychological hypothesis about anyone's head, it's just a way of bookkeeping observed choice, so there's nothing to refute. I tried to write a reductio of that deflation. The reductio lands, but it also takes out two things I really want to keep, and I can't tell whether my argument is broken or my intuitions are.

1. Nobody ever observes a preference. The only access anyone has to what an agent prefers, including the agent themselves, is what the agent chooses.
2. So the only thing that could make a preference attribution correct is that it rationalizes the choices. There's no further fact to be right or wrong about.
3. For any single choice, some ranking rationalizes it: whatever got picked, put it at the top of the ordering.
4. Therefore no possible pattern of choice behaviour could count as failing to maximize.
5. Therefore "agents maximize utility" is not an empirical claim but a redescription of data, and (2) buys that emptiness at the price of making the whole enterprise contentless.
6. But then the same move should kill the money-pump argument for transitivity, which I have always assumed was about as close to a proof as philosophy gets. If any choice pattern can be rationalized by some imputed ranking, the agent who cycles A over B over C over A and gets drained a dollar per cycle isn't irrational either; we just haven't found the right imputation.
7. And it should kill willingness-to-pay as a welfare measure. If the imputed ranking is free to be redrawn observation by observation, there's no stable fact about whether a policy made anyone better off, so Kaldor-Hicks, cost-benefit analysis, and "he bought it so he wanted it" are all empty in the same way.
8. So revealed preference is empty, and everything downstream of it is empty with it.

I want to reject (8) and keep money pumps, and ideally keep some version of (2) as well, because the alternative looks like pretending we have introspective access to utility. Something between (3) and (4) feels like a switch in what's being quantified over, but I've never been able to say it cleanly, and the people who say Afriat's theorem answers the triviality charge generally just state the axiom back at you.

The case that keeps breaking my own argument: suppose I build a toy agent, call it Shuffler, which at each timestep draws a fresh strict ranking over {coffee, tea, milk} uniformly at random and picks whatever comes out on top. Every individual choice is trivially maximizing. Over a million timesteps the aggregate pattern is about as intransitive as you can get, and if you can trade with it, it's the purest money pump imaginable. But now compare Shuffler to a human whose ranking drifts slowly, or whose shifts are locked to contexts I can't observe or even describe. From any finite data set there's a regime where I can't tell them apart. So is "there is one stable ranking" an empirical hypothesis that could be true of Shuffler for all I can ever know — in which case (6) has some bite and I just can't prove it — or does Shuffler's data actually falsify it, in which case (3) is not doing the work I claimed and I need to know what work it *is* doing? Those two answers seem to require different repairs to the argument and I don't know which is right.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look so obviously fine to me when I wrote it? What does rejecting it cost — do I lose the operationalist reading of preference-talk, or the money pump, or the welfare economics, or some mix? And is anything left over that deserves to be called a substantive thesis about revealed preference once the hole is plugged?

One thing I'd ask: be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely still contested among people who work on it. I've been burned enough times by someone writing up a live methodological fight as if it were a solved problem, and I'd rather get flagged where the field just disagrees than get a confident-sounding answer I can't check.
