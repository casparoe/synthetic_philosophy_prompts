I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of science seminar that has spent the last month on the Duhem–Quine problem, ad hoc hypotheses, and Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes. For my final paper — twelve pages, due at the end of term, and my instructor has already told me she intends to attack it hard — I have decided to defend a position I suspect is badly insulated, and I would like a frank map of exactly where it is exposed before I commit to it in writing. A friend of mine in the environmental economics programme thinks the position is not merely wrong but unfalsifiable in an embarrassing way; I think she is confusing the structure of an equilibrium claim with the structure of an ordinary empirical prediction, but I cannot yet articulate the difference, and I would rather find out that I am wrong now than in the paper.

Here is the position, stated as carefully as I can manage. The Malthusian mechanism, on the reading I want to defend, is a claim about a tendency rather than about an event: whenever per-capita access to subsistence rises above the level that holds a population stationary, population rises until that access is pushed back down again. In pre-industrial Europe the coupling was observable, and as I understand it the responsive variable was chiefly the age at which people married rather than the number of children born within marriage. What the industrial era did, on my account, was not to repeal the tendency but to enlarge the resource base against which it operates, by means of a one-off drawdown on accumulated stocks — fossil carbon, deep topsoil, confined groundwater, mineral phosphate, wild fish populations. It follows that two centuries of rising real incomes are not counter-evidence to the mechanism but rather the mechanism running on a temporarily inflated base, and that the predictions derived from it are asymptotic: no finite interval of escape can refute them, any more than a theory of the tides is refuted by an estuary that happens to have been dammed. Malthus was right, and merely early. The position also carries two auxiliaries that I expect to be the main target: first, that falling fertility in wealthy countries is the preventive check operating in its modern form, trading earlier death for fewer births; and second, that declines in the inflation-adjusted prices of scarce resources — the well-known 1980 wager between a biologist and an economist over a basket of five metals, settled ten years later decisively in the economist's favour — reflect substitution and discovery financed by the same drawdown, and so are not evidence that scarcity has been escaped.

What I want from you is a disciplined account of what would count against this position, not a general critique of neo-Malthusianism. Please separate defeaters that strike the core mechanism from those that strike only the auxiliaries from those that merely postpone the timetable, and rank them from fatal through serious to merely awkward. Say which of them have arguably already occurred: I am most worried about sustained below-replacement fertility in the wealthiest countries across several decades in which their incomes kept rising, and I would like to know whether that is already a refutation of the preventive-check reading or whether a defender can absorb it. Then tell me what a committed defender of this position would herself concede as counting against her — where the line runs between a legitimate auxiliary and a face-saving one, and how I could tell, from the inside, if my own paper had crossed it. Please set the position against the rival accounts it competes with (that population pressure itself induces the innovations that relieve it; that the escape is self-sustaining because rising income permanently reduced the demand for children through a trade-off between their number and the investment made in each; and undifferentiated technological optimism) and say whether my thesis predicts anything they do not — if it predicts nothing distinctive, say so plainly rather than diplomatically. I would also like the position located against the historical cases where being right but early was vindicated, such as the missing stellar parallax that was explained away by an auxiliary about the distances to the stars which turned out to be true, or continental drift, and the cases where the rescue failed, such as Le Verrier's intra-Mercurial planet; what distinguishes the successful rescues from the unsuccessful ones, and which side does this thesis fall on? Finally, address directly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect for a claim of this particular kind — an equilibrium claim about a regime not yet reached — as opposed to a claim about present observations; and if you can rescue only one falsifiable commitment as the load-bearing wall of the paper, tell me which one it should be and what single development over the next couple of decades I ought to be watching.
