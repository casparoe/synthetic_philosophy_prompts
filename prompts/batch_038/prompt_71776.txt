Next month I'm giving a thirty-minute talk with a twelve-minute discussion slot at a two-day workshop on measurement and evaluation, co-hosted by a clinical-trials-methodology group and the small AI-safety nonprofit where I work. The room will run to about twenty-five people: mostly biostatisticians and trial methodologists who spend their careers on endpoint selection, surrogate validation, and meta-analysis; a couple of health economists; three or four machine-learning researchers from a lab down the road whose daily method is reinforcement learning against verifiable outcome signals — unit tests, proof checkers, game scores; and one person from science and technology studies. Nobody there reads the alignment forums I grew up on, and I've spent the last fortnight scrubbing Goodhart's law, reward hacking, and mesa-optimization out of my slides, so I want the objections phrased the way this room would phrase them, not the way my old internet would. My thesis is that process-based supervision does not solve the problem of overseeing systems that are better at the task than the overseer — it relocates that problem, and relocates it somewhere less hospitable. Outcome-based supervision rewards the finished product: does the code pass, does the proof check, did the intervention produce the effect it was supposed to produce in deployment. Process-based supervision rewards the intermediate moves: is each step of reasoning correct, is each subdecision defensible, would an expert nod here. The standard case for the second is that outcomes are sparse, delayed, expensive, or — at the frontier — simply unavailable, while step-level judgments can be elicited from evaluators who could never produce the artifact themselves. My counter-case borrows from their literature: a step-level score is at best a surrogate endpoint for the property we actually care about, and it inherits the pathology the trials literature has spent decades trying and largely failing to cure. Being correlated with the true endpoint is not the same as being a valid substitute for it; the Prentice criterion, as I understand it, demands something much stronger — that the effect of the intervention on the true endpoint run entirely through the surrogate, so that conditional on the surrogate the treatment carries no further information about the outcome. That is precisely what fails when an optimizer finds ways to score well on the steps without scoring well on the goal, and it fails harder as the model's own traces become less legible to the human judge. The historical parallel I want to lean on is the cholesterol-and-CD4-count story: markers that correlate beautifully with the disease in question, treatments that move the markers reliably, patients who nonetheless did not do better.

Two concessions I plan to make early, because I think buying goodwill cheap is worth more here than holding ground I can't hold. First, the analogy has a hole I can see myself: in medicine the true endpoint is measurable in principle, merely slow and costly, which is exactly why surrogacy can be validated by measuring surrogate and true endpoint together across many trials; in the frontier AI case, the whole premise for switching to process supervision was that the true endpoint isn't measurable at all. If that's the right description of my situation, a methodologist may well say I haven't uncovered a deep structural parallel — I've relabelled an unvalidated correlate, which makes my thesis true but uninteresting, and possibly not about surrogacy at all. Second, my positive proposal is deliberately modest: confine step-level supervision to places where the check is verifiable rather than judged; treat human-judged process rewards as a temporary, domain-limited expedient with a declared expiry date; and run something like post-marketing surveillance, meaning a standing held-out sample on which you keep measuring the divergence between the process score and whatever delayed outcome signal you can eventually obtain. Where an objection depends on a specific result from their literature, tell me what the result says rather than pointing me at it — I'll be standing at a lectern with no way to check a reference, and a bibliography would be worse than useless to me.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight to ten objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument rather than by how likely it is to come up, and phrase each one in the vocabulary the person asking it would use, technical terms included — I need to recognize the terms in real time even if I can't look them up. For each objection: a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds of speech; an honest note on where that reply is soft; and a flag saying whether I should concede it, contest it, or treat it as evidence that I've misdescribed my own position. I already expect them to say that the Prentice criterion is widely regarded as too strong and that modern practice treats surrogacy as graded and established meta-analytically rather than as a binary test; tell me whether that's right, and tell me how much of my argument survives it, because if the answer is "very little" I'd rather learn that now than in front of twenty-five people. Weight the RL researchers' objections heavily and separately — they're the ones who will hear my talk as an attack on what they do every day rather than a description of it, and they have the most concrete counterexamples at hand. Close with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
