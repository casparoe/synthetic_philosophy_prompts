I'm TAing a first-year grad seminar in formal epistemology and I'm writing problem set 4, which is the logical uncertainty week. So far they've had Ramsey/de Finetti, the Dutch book argument, conditionalization, and one lecture on why credence 1 in all logical truths is hopeless for an agent with finite time. They haven't seen any of the technical literature, so the problem has to be self contained — everything they need should be sitting in the statement itself.

I want one problem, worth 25 marks out of the 100 on the set, that they should spend about two hours on, and it should be impossible to pass by writing an essay about logical omniscience. Here's the setup I want you to build it out of; keep it close to this, just clean up the wording.

Agent A posts a price P_n(φ) in [0,1] for every sentence φ of first-order arithmetic on every day n. Stipulate: prices are rationals whose denominator divides 2^n (so claims about prices are exact arithmetical facts and nobody has to compute with reals), the map (n, ⌜φ⌝) ↦ P_n(φ) is computable in time polynomial in n and the length of φ, and n ↦ ⌜ψ_n⌝ is poly-time too. By diagonalization, take as given a sequence ψ_1, ψ_2, ... where ψ_n says of itself "the price A posts for me on day n is at most 1/2". Note that ψ_n is settled the instant A posts day n's prices, so there's no waiting-for-the-limit worry about payoffs in this example — that's part of why I picked it. A share in φ pays $1 if φ is true and $0 otherwise. A trader is poly-time, holds at most one share of any given sentence on any given day, and counts as successful if its cumulative profit diverges. Desiderata the statement should name for them: (i) limit coherence — for each fixed φ the prices converge and the limit obeys the probability rules relative to arithmetic; (ii) timeliness — A gets prices right before it has the resources to check them; (iii) no successful trader.

Parts, roughly, adjust as you see fit:

(a) 6 marks. Assuming traders may be discontinuous functions of the prices they observe, exhibit a trader that succeeds against any A whatsoever on the ψ sequence.

(b) 7 marks. Say what minimal restriction on traders blocks that, then derive as much as you can about P_n(ψ_n) under the restricted criterion. My hunch is that "tends to 1/2" isn't the whole answer and that the criterion also constrains how big or how often the errors can be — work that out, and if it holds, make it a must-hit point.

(c) 6 marks. Why isn't this just the liar paradox in disguise? I want them to contrast the day-indexed sequence with a single self-referential sentence priced once and for all, and to show that (i) is perfectly compatible with what (b) forces, then say what that reveals about what limit coherence does and doesn't control.

(d) 6 marks. A computes its own prices, so in principle it knows whether ψ_n is true. Why is that knowledge no use at all here, and does this case show the problem is about bounded resources or about something that would survive unlimited transparency? Plus: is the restriction from (b) principled or a technical trick, and what would settle that?
