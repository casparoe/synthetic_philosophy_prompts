I am reading a popular book on the philosophy of mathematics that devotes several pages to intuitionism, and before I draw on one passage for a short piece I am writing I would like an accuracy audit of it. The passage reads:

"Brouwer's intuitionism begins from the claim that mathematics is an activity of mental construction, so a statement is true only if a proof of it has actually been carried out. It follows that intuitionists reject the law of excluded middle: for them 'P or not-P' is simply false in general, and neither disjunct may be asserted until someone decides the question. This is why Brouwer was able to show, by means of a real number defined out of an unresolved arithmetical problem, that one cannot even say of some particular number whether it is zero or nonzero. Intuitionists also refuse proof by contradiction, since deriving an absurdity from not-P establishes nothing whatever about P. On this view an unproved statement is neither true nor false but something in between, which is why intuitionistic logic is best understood as a species of many-valued logic. The costs are severe: Brouwer had to abandon much of classical analysis, and because truth now depends on what human minds are able to prove, mathematics becomes a private and subjective affair. It is no surprise that intuitionism has won almost no adherents among working mathematicians."

On a first reading this struck me as plausible, and I suspect it is close to the standard account, but two things will not sit still. I have also come across the remark that intuitionistic logic proves something involving the double negation of excluded middle, which seems hard to reconcile with the claim that the law is false for intuitionists; and I have seen Bishop's constructive mathematics described as neutral between classical and intuitionistic interpretations, which does not fit the subjectivism of the closing sentences.

Could you take the passage claim by claim and say which sentences a specialist in constructive mathematics would accept as they stand, which are fair though compressed, and which are misstatements — in particular whether the passage's talk of excluded middle being false is really the same thing as the intuitionist's refusal to assert it, and what intuitionistic logic does and does not license by way of reductio? Where you judge a sentence wrong, please tell me what belongs in its place, since I do not want to inherit the error. Then assess the passage's argument as a whole: does the conclusion that intuitionism makes mathematics subjective, and has consequently failed to attract mathematicians, follow from what is true in it, or does it depend on the parts that are not true?

Finally, be explicit about the standing of your corrections. I want to know which of your points are settled matters — what intuitionistic logic in fact proves, what Brouwer and Bishop actually maintained — and which remain genuinely disputed among people who work in this area, for instance over how far Brouwer's idealism commits intuitionism to psychologism, or over what the correct account of intuitionistic truth is. Please do not let a contested position be presented to me as the consensus.
