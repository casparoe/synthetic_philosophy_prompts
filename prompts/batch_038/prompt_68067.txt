I am in the second year of a philosophy doctorate, and for the past two terms I have been working through normative uncertainty in a reading group. I can run the standard machinery competently: assign credences to the moral theories I take seriously, put their verdicts on a common scale where that is possible, and choose the option with the highest expected choice-worthiness; on the examples we were given in the seminar, it delivers answers I am happy with. The difficulty is that when I write down what I actually think about cases of my own devising, my verdicts diverge from the machinery in a way that looks systematic rather than arbitrary, and I have not been able to state the pattern. I have been asked to present something in the department's work-in-progress series in a few weeks, and I would rather bring a question that is genuinely unresolved for me than a summary of positions. One friend's diagnosis, offered half in jest, is that I simply do not hold the credences I claim to hold, and that everything else is rationalization; I suspect there is something to this, but I cannot tell whether it can be made into a coherent view.

Here are the verdicts, as honestly as I can report them. (a) On charitable giving I divide roughly 60/40 between a fairly demanding consequentialist view and a commonsense view on which aid past a modest threshold is supererogatory. I think I ought to give well above the commonsense threshold; the wide range of the demanding view outweighs my greater confidence in the permissive one. (b) On a particular false statement to a colleague I give 25% to a rigorist view on which it is a grave wrong of roughly the same kind as betrayal, and 75% to a view on which it is morally unremarkable. The two views disagree about whether any distinction is in play at all: the rigorist thinks everything turns on the difference between asserting what one believes false and merely permitting a misleading impression, a difference the other view denies is morally real. My verdict: I feel almost no pull from the rigorist view. I would behave as though its probability were close to zero, and I would not be surprised to learn that I had been treating it that way all along. (c) On diverting a threat onto one person instead of five I am split evenly between a view that forbids diverting and a view that forbids refraining. Here both views agree completely about what the situation is — one agent, five lives, a switch — and disagree only about how it is to be valued. My verdict: the disagreement is fully live, it makes me hesitate, and it disposes me to hunt for a third option both views rank acceptably. On the surface (b) and (c) look alike — substantial credence on each side, substantial stakes either way — yet only (c) moves me, and my hunch is that the difference has nothing to do with stakes and everything to do with whether the theories share a description of the act. (d) On long-haul air travel, one view I give 55% holds that flying and purchasing offsets is a genuine third option, distinct from flying simpliciter; another I give 45% holds that no such option exists, so the choice set contains only flying and not flying. My verdict: I do not average across the two choice sets. I first settle whether offsetting is a thing, and I settle it by asking which view I trust more about what such a transaction could possibly amount to — a descriptive question — rather than by asking which I trust more about what is permissible; and once settled, I experience no residual unease, despite retaining 45% credence in the view I discarded. (e) On procedure, I am evenly split between maximizing expected choice-worthiness and simply acting on whichever first-order theory I believe most. My verdict: I go with the favourite theory, but I feel faintly as though I have broken a rule — which is the opposite direction of trade from (a), where the low-credence, high-range theory won outright. (f) The case I keep returning to: a view I give 5% says that an entirely ordinary purchase of mine makes me a participant in something horrific. The view is internally coherent and its argument is not obviously bad, but its conclusion depends on describing the purchase as participation rather than as a causally remote and negligible contribution, a description the rest of my credal distribution rejects as drawing no real distinction. Compare a second 5%-credence view, which says that the treatment of animals in food production is torture; there, everything in my distribution agrees about what is happening and disagrees only about how to value it. My verdict: in the first case I do nothing and do not feel akratic; in the second I have changed my behaviour considerably.

What I would like is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that generates these verdicts — or that gets as close as possible while telling me plainly where it must break with me. In particular: can the underlying idea be stated precisely enough to be applied rather than gestured at, something to the effect that a theory's effective weight is reduced in proportion to how much of its verdict depends on distinctions that the other theories in my distribution deny are real? If so, which clause of it does each case motivate, and which of my cases come out differently under the simpler alternatives I can think of — plain expected-choice-worthiness maximization, expected-choice-worthiness maximization applied only after fixing an act-description by something like a vote across theories, a favourite-theory rule, and a maximin or robustness rule? I also want the admissibility objection taken seriously: my principle appears to let my credences depend on the decision procedure I am using, when credences are supposed to be inputs to it. Can that be answered — perhaps by insisting that what is really going on is a claim about how to individuate the objects of my uncertainty, so that the rigorist view in (b) is not a rival moral theory at all but a moral theory plus a contested descriptive claim, and I have been miscounting my theories — or is it fatal? Then tell me what the principle predicts where I have no verdict yet: two theories that draw exactly the same fine distinctions but assign them opposite weights; a disputed description that is backed by non-moral evidence, such as settled facts about what my purchase causally does; and a case in which every theory I take seriously agrees on the description, where the principle ought to switch off and ordinary expected-choice-worthiness reasoning to return. Finally, be candid about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, and if it has to overturn (e) rather than (a), explain why that is the cheaper sacrifice. Do not trouble yourself with references; I will supply those once I know what position I am actually defending.
