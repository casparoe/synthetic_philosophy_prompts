I came to *On Certainty* late and from the side. I've spent about fifteen years reading and posting on rationalist blogs and forums, so I arrived with a vocabulary of priors, cruxes, and load-bearing beliefs, and someone eventually pointed out to me that the book is essentially a mid-century treatment of a distinction I'd been gesturing at badly: some of the things I call beliefs don't function as beliefs at all, because they are not items in the web that could be confirmed or disconfirmed, but the frame that makes confirmation and disconfirmation possible. I found the book more useful than I expected, and also more frustrating, because I cannot tell which of my own commitments are hinges and which are merely cached thoughts I have never bothered to check. So I wrote down my verdicts on a batch of cases. I am not asking for exegesis; I don't care whether my reading would survive contact with the secondary literature, and I'd rather you not spend the answer on who said what about the book. I want a criterion I can apply.

My tentative criterion is this. A proposition p is a hinge for me just in case I cannot coherently undertake an inquiry into p without relying on p somewhere along the way: any evidence I would gather, any method I would use to weigh that evidence, and any inference I would draw from it already presuppose p, so the doubt consumes its own instrument. Call this the self-undermining-doubt test. It feels right on the paradigms and I suspect it is wrong at the edges. Here are the verdicts it is supposed to reproduce.

1. The proposition that I have two hands, one of which is currently in front of me. Verdict: hinge. But note that the content is not what does the work — if I were an amputee I would know perfectly well that I had one hand — so what makes it a hinge is that checking is pointless, not that checking is impossible. I also agree with the book rather than with Moore here: holding up one's hands is a performance that fails, not a proof that succeeds, and the proposition is not something I know in a sense that would let me deploy it as a premise against a sceptic.

2. That the planet has been here far longer than I have. Verdict: hinge, and unlike case 1 this one does heavy lifting. I use it constantly — about fossils, about whether my grandmother's testimony can be trusted, about anything historical. So the criterion has to leave room for hinges that are premises in ordinary inference, not only hinges that sit inert.

3. That Napoleon existed. Verdict: not a hinge. This is a routine empirical claim resting on documents and testimony; it feels settled only because no one has ever given me a reason to check. If historians converged on the view that he was a composite figure, I would give it up, and although I would have to reshuffle a good deal of history, nothing in my epistemic machinery would break. High credence is not immunity.

4. That patterns which have held will keep holding. Verdict: hinge by my test. Also the case that embarrasses me, because reading about the problem of induction and about artificially contrived predicates genuinely changed my relation to it: I now hold it as a rule I follow rather than as a fact I assert. A hinge apparently changed shape, which I had thought it wasn't supposed to do.

5. That my memory is broadly reliable. Verdict: hinge, but not a single one. I have successfully demoted slices of it. After reading about confabulation and eyewitness error I no longer trust my memory for exact wording, or for anything before I was five, while I still trust it about whether I ate breakfast. I want a criterion on which hinges can come in graded depths rather than all-or-nothing.

6. Arithmetic. That two plus two is four and its friends: hinge, in the specific sense that if a long hand calculation disagreed with the calculator I would check the calculation. But that my own hand-arithmetic is reliable is emphatically not a hinge: past a few lines I trust the machine over myself and think I should. The general rule is entrenched while the particular faculty implementing it is not.

7. That other people are conscious. Verdict: hinge by the test, since any experiment I could design would rely on treating reports and behaviour as evidence, which is precisely what is in question. But I have also expanded it — I used to think machine consciousness was incoherent and now think it is an open empirical matter. That is more movement in a hinge.

8. A friend of mine holds that God exists. He offers no arguments, does not treat it as a hypothesis, is unmoved by anything I raise, and organizes much of the rest of his life around it. Verdict: this functions as a hinge for him, and — this is the part I want preserved — he is not thereby irrational. I stopped arguing him out of it, not from politeness but because I think there is no argument to be had.

9. A man I once spent months arguing with on a forum was certain that a particular agency had been surveilling him for a decade, that patterns he found in license plates and street signs were reliable evidence of it, and that any attempt to dissuade him was itself part of the operation. I cannot find the structural difference from case 8: immune to counterevidence, not in practice offered as a testable hypothesis, load-bearing for everything downstream, unsupported by argument. My verdict is the opposite — he was deluded and criticizable. And I want the difference to be visible from the inside. A criterion that only separates them when applied by someone who already knows which one is true is no criterion, because then it is doing no work that my prior confidence wasn't already doing.

That pair is the one I cannot resolve.

10. That the simulation hypothesis might be true. Verdict: not a hinge — I can entertain it, and it doesn't self-undermine. But nothing about my behaviour would change if it came out true, which suggests I am not treating it as an empirical hypothesis either. Flagging this one as genuinely ambiguous rather than offering a clean verdict.

11. Two of my own hinges came out, and neither came out by argument. That the intellectual community which formed me was epistemically virtuous, and that the methodology of my field was sound. Both went over a couple of years under accumulated small failures — a hundred instances of people reasoning badly and being rewarded for it, of results not surviving contact — and in neither case did anyone produce an argument I could have weighed in advance. Verdict: hinges can be removed, but only from the outside, by the collapse of the structure around them. My criterion says nothing diachronic at all and I want the fixed version to.

What I'd like back:

State the criterion, or two or three competing criteria, precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have never seen without consulting you. Go clause by clause, and for each clause name the case above that forced it in — I want to see which of my verdicts is doing the work of which piece of machinery.

For each candidate, say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and in particular what it says about 8 and 9. If your criterion makes the friend irrational, or makes the paranoid blameless, say so rather than smoothing it over.

Then give me the cheapest exit. Tell me which single verdict of mine I should abandon in order to get the simplest workable criterion, and what I lose by abandoning it. I would rather be told that one of my intuitions is bad than be handed a four-clause principle with an epicycle bolted on to cover case 9.

Then rule on two cases I have deliberately left open. First: that my introspective reports about my own current mental states are broadly accurate. I lean hinge, and the self-undermining test seems to pass, but the confabulation material has persuaded me that I invent reasons and confabulate choices — and if I can be systematically wrong about why I did something, I don't see how the general claim stays beyond inquiry. Second: that the outputs of the language models I use are broadly truthful. For me this is not a hinge, because I check, and because I have caught them wrong often enough that checking has become habitual. But I think it has become one for many people, and in a nastier form than the ordinary cases, because they can no longer evaluate the output without relying on the very faculty the output has replaced. Is that a hinge, a bad hinge, a hinge in the making, or something my categories have no room for? Add one further case of your own choosing that you think separates the candidate criteria better than anything I listed.

Finally, say what the winning criterion implies about what kind of thing hinge-hood is a property of: a proposition, a person, a community, a time. Cases 4, 5, 7, and 11 all suggest it isn't timeless, and I don't yet see how to build that in without making the whole thing so indexed to the individual that case 9 escapes evaluation altogether.
