I have been trying to work out how much of a recent argument I read about the ethics of war I ought to accept, and I cannot tell where it is sound and where it is sliding. The essay appeared in a foreign-affairs magazine and its thesis is that the law of armed conflict rests on a moral fiction that should be abandoned. The relevant passage runs as follows:

"The reigning doctrine is what Michael Walzer called the moral equality of combatants: soldiers on both sides of a war have an equal right to kill one another, and nothing about the justice of their cause changes that. Once war breaks out, the two armies stand in the same moral relation to one another as two boxers in a ring — each may hit the other, and neither does wrong by hitting. It follows that a soldier who fights in a war of aggression commits no moral wrong whatsoever in fighting it, however many people he kills, provided he observes his rules of engagement. Walzer's own much-discussed illustration makes the basis of the doctrine plain: he describes a soldier who comes upon an enemy bathing, naked and unarmed, and declines to shoot, because at that moment the man threatens nobody. Liability, on this picture, tracks the danger a person poses rather than the justice of his cause or his personal guilt. Revisionists such as Jeff McMahan have shown that this is morally bankrupt. If you fight for an unjust cause you have no right to kill at all, and those resisting you have a right to kill you, since you are the source of an unjust threat. The conclusion is unavoidable, and the law should follow the morality: a state that has been attacked should be entitled to refuse prisoner-of-war status to the soldiers it captures, and to prosecute them for the killings they committed on its territory, which on McMahan's account were murders."

I have read enough of the secondary literature to know that Walzer and McMahan genuinely disagree, but the essay moves quickly and I cannot identify which steps are load-bearing. Please work through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a specialist in the ethics of war would accept as accurate, what is a defensible popular simplification, what distorts either Walzer or McMahan, and what it omits that bears directly on the argument.

A few questions I would like answered explicitly. Is the boxing-ring analogy a fair way to state what the moral equality of combatants is actually a claim about — and is it a claim about morality, about law, or about both, since the essay seems to treat those as interchangeable? Does Walzer in fact hold that a soldier on the unjust side does nothing wrong, or is his position on the soldier's own moral standing more complicated than the passage suggests, and is "rules of engagement" the right term for the constraint the passage invokes? Is "danger rather than guilt" really Walzer's criterion of liability, or does the bathing-soldier example cut the other way in his argument? Is McMahan's asymmetry as blanket as the essay presents it — does his account of what makes a combatant liable to attack really imply that every soldier on the unjust side may be killed, or does he distinguish among unjust combatants in ways the passage ignores? And, most importantly, does revisionist morality license the two legal changes the essay draws from it? If McMahan himself would reject those changes, say what his reasons are and what that implies about the relationship between the deep morality of killing in war and the rules that ought to govern it institutionally.

I ask because I am inclined to accept the essay's combination — that McMahan is right about the morality and that the law should therefore become asymmetric — and I want to know whether that is a position a revisionist could coherently hold, or whether it requires bridging premises the essay never states. If it requires them, tell me what those premises would be and whether they are plausible.
