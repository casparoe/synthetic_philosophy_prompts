I hold a chair in philosophy at a British university, and my own work sits almost entirely within formal epistemology: credence functions, ordinary and Jeffrey conditionalisation, priors and likelihoods, reliability parameters and the higher-order evidence they generate, deference principles, strict coherence and regularity constraints, calibration, and imprecise probability when I need it. That vocabulary is native to me and to the doctoral students on my seminar; Sanskritic philosophy is not. This term I am co-teaching a module on testimony with a colleague from our Centre for Sanskrit Studies, and week five is devoted to the Nyāya claim that śabda — the utterance of a trustworthy speaker — is a pramāṇa in its own right, co-ordinate with perception and inference rather than reducible to either. My colleague has supplied me with a précis, and since I read no Sanskrit I need to know, before I stand up in front of fifteen Bayes-first students, whether that position can be stated at all in the apparatus I actually use, and how much of it survives the restatement intact.

The précis, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows, and I should be grateful if you would correct me wherever I have garbled it — better now than in the seminar. Nyāya admits four pramāṇas: perception, inference, comparison and śabda, whereas the Buddhist epistemologists admit only the first two and treat everything testimonial as inference from signs. Śabda is defined by reference to an āpta, a speaker who knows the matter as it is and speaks as he knows it, so that the warrant conveyed depends on the cognitive standing and the character of the source rather than on any mark present in the utterance itself. Veridical verbal cognition is held to require four things: mutual expectancy amongst the words; semantic fitness, the standing illustration being that one may water a plant with water but cannot coherently be said to water it with fire; contiguity of the utterance; and the speaker's purport, which settles cases in which a single word-form admits of two readings — the familiar example being a term that can mean either a horse or salt, disambiguated by whether the setting is a battlefield or a dining room. Separately, and this is the claim I find most striking, Nyāya holds that the validity of a cognition is extrinsic both in its arising and in its apprehension: nothing internal to a cognition certifies it, and one learns that a cognition was valid by inferring from the success of the activity it prompted. The Mīmāṃsakas deny this and hold validity to be intrinsic, whilst also maintaining that the Veda is authorless; Nyāya instead makes the Veda the utterance of Īśvara and derives its standing from his being the paradigm āpta.

What I want is a translation exercise, not a comparative survey. Take each of those claims in turn — the irreducibility of śabda, the āpta condition, the four conditions on verbal cognition, the extrinsic-validity thesis, and the divine-author move — and restate it as precisely as you can in my formal idiom: what is the credence, what counts as the evidence, what is the likelihood, what is conditionalised on what, and where, if anywhere, a reliability parameter or a deference principle has to be introduced. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about, sorted into three tiers: what translates cleanly; what translates only by being bent, with the bending identified; and what has no expression whatsoever in a probability calculus. Be scrupulous about not smuggling — if the only way to render a Nyāya claim in my vocabulary is to help myself to assumptions the Naiyāyika would reject, such as treating a speaker's trustworthiness as a parameter to be estimated from evidence about his past utterances, or treating the four conditions as likelihood ratios over competing interpretations rather than as conditions on whether a cognition arises at all, then say so plainly and score it as distortion rather than as translation. Finally, tell me which side the residue favours: where the translation fails, is that because the formal apparatus genuinely lacks a dimension — pedigree as opposed to strength, factivity as opposed to degree of confidence, a distinction between whether a cognition occurs and whether it is warranted — or because the Nyāya claim, stripped of its technical vocabulary, turns out to be a repackaged thesis about priors and reliabilities that was never as radical as it sounds? I have suspicions in both directions and I expect you to push back on them. Two pressure points I would like you to work through rather than gesture at: whether pramā, which appears to be factive, can be rendered at all by a non-factive assignment of degrees of belief; and whether a testifier whose reliability is not merely very high but unimpeachable is representable in a framework that bars credences of exactly one on anything contingent, and what the Naiyāyika forfeits if he is driven to accept a merely extreme prior on divine utterance. Please do not send me a bibliography, a reading list or any citations; I have the secondary literature to hand, and it is argument I am asking for.
