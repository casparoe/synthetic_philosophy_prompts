Background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm retired, spent about 35 years in benthic ecology — tide pools and soft-sediment communities, mostly — and I've only read philosophy seriously in the last few years. Right now I'm chewing on the Hart–Devlin argument about whether the law may enforce morality, the one that grew out of the proposal to decriminalize private homosexual conduct in Britain. I can follow both sides. What I don't have is a good test case, because the usual ones (that business, plus drugs, plus prostitution) come so pre-loaded with real harms and real outrage that you can never tell which principle is producing the verdict. As an experiment that's just bad design: you've confounded your variables.

So I want you to build me one to spec. Think of it as a mesocosm — controlled, and I want to be able to see exactly which mechanism gives the result.

Specs, be strict about them:

1. Setting: realistic and contemporary, one community I'd recognize (small town, hospital, research station, a trade). One paragraph, no more. No trolley-style abstraction, no sci-fi, no courtroom theatrics.

2. The practice at issue must harm nobody. Not "harms them in ways they don't notice" — no identifiable person is made worse off in any respect Hart would count. I want the harm principle to fire cleanly and forbid legal interference, with no wiggle room.

3. And it must not be offensive either. No disgust, no complaints, nobody who could articulate a grievance if you asked them. I'm closing Feinberg's offense-principle escape hatch on purpose, because otherwise you can always relabel legal moralism as harm prevention and the two positions never actually get joined.

4. Devlin must nonetheless have a live case for enforcement — and here's the constraint I care about most. In my case his argument has to run on the constitutive version, not the predictive one. So no disintegration thesis: no once-this-goes-then-that-goes forecasting, no armchair sociology about what the place will look like in a generation. The claim has to be that the society already is, in the present tense, less the society it was — that what's lost is not a future state but a present structure. In my line of work that's the difference between predicting a collapse and simply looking at the community and seeing that the thing holding it in one piece is already gone.

5. To make 4 doable I'll hand you a concept I made up for this, and I want you to use it explicitely and by name: a keystone norm. That's a norm that isn't itself about preventing harm but that supplies the conditions under which a whole set of other, harm-preventing norms get complied with informally — by example, by expectation, by the fact that people can see each other keeping them. Remove it and those others don't fail next year; they're already running on nothing. In your case, name the keystone norm and show the dependency chain: which harm-preventing norms hang off it, and how.

6. Hart's positive-versus-critical morality move must not dissolve the case by fiat. Since I've taken disgust out of the setup, he can't just say that's unexamined popular feeling talking. So tell me what route he does have left, and whether it's enough.

Deliverables, in this order:

- the one-paragraph scenario;
- a spec check, item by item, giving each side's conclusion and the reason for it;
- the single load-bearing feature of the case, and what happens to both verdicts if I delete it — I'd expect it to collapse into a plain private-immorality case where Hart wins easily, so show me whether it does;
- the strongest Hartian attack on your own case, not a strawman: the one that would actually make me give it up;
- what enforcement would look like in practice, and whether Devlin's position here has to be criminal law or would settle for something weaker.

No citations or reading list, please — I can't check any of it and I'm not writing a paper.

Two honest bits at the end. First: which of my constraints fought you hardest, and did you bend any to get the thing to run? Second, and this matters more: say what would change your mind about whether the case works. If you think the constitutive version of Devlin's argument can't be made to fire without a prediction sneaking back in somewhere, say so and point to exactly where it snuck in; if there's some third constraint I could add or drop that would flip the verdict, tell me which one. I'd much rather have it fail on the bench than be handed a tidy result you don't believe.
