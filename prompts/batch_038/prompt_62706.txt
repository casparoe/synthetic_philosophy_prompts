I am in my final year at a small secondary school where I have run an eleven-member philosophy reading group for the past two years. Four of us are applying to summer programmes this spring and one member is sitting an admissions test that includes a philosophy component, so our faculty adviser has let me set a mock paper which the whole group will sit under proper conditions in the library on Saturday: sixty minutes, one essay, closed book, twenty-five marks. The topic I have been assigned is interpersonal comparisons of utility, and I am writing to ask for help designing the question itself, together with everything an examiner would need in order to mark it consistently.

My difficulty is that the obvious question is far too easy for this group. Several of them could produce three fluent pages on the familiar dispute — the mid-century economist's insistence that one person's gain cannot be weighed against another's loss because such weighing is a value judgment rather than an observation, and the standard replies that refusing to compare is itself a substantive normative choice with distributive consequences, so that the sceptic's neutrality is illusory. They have all read that argument twice and would simply reproduce it. I want instead a question built around a concrete measurement procedure that they have never encountered, so that they must actually reason. Here is the procedure I have invented for the occasion, loosely modelled on the way health economists anchor quality-of-life scales between two fixed states. A public agency must divide a fixed budget between two programmes affecting different people. For each affected person it elicits a cardinal utility function in the standard way: given any pair of outcomes the person can rank, she reports the probability at which she is indifferent between receiving the better outcome for certain and a gamble that pays the better outcome with that probability and the worse outcome otherwise. This yields, for each person, a utility function unique up to positive affine transformation, so that ratios of utility differences within a single life are well defined but the zero point and the unit are not. To fix them, the agency uses the same two anchor states for everybody: anchor L is one ordinary uneventful weekday spent at home, and anchor H is one week recovering from a minor operation under general anaesthetic. It stipulates that u(L) = 0 and u(H) = 100 for every subject, declares that all utilities are therefore expressed in a common unit, and then sums across persons to choose the allocation.

What I want the question to turn on is whether that stipulation does the work the agency claims for it. My own suspicion, which I would like the paper to test rather than assume, is that it does not: fixing the same two physical anchors for everyone can establish a common scale only if the anchors are already known to carry equal utility for different subjects, and that is precisely the interpersonal fact the protocol was supposed to deliver — so the normalisation step appears to presuppose what it purports to supply. But the question should be fair to the agency, because there is a defence available: the anchors are chosen as ordinary biological states of a human body, and one might hold that a shared physical basis licenses a shared scale even without any further measurement. I would like a strong candidate to weigh that defence rather than wave it away, and to say what would have to be added to the protocol — some third-person, empathetic, or bargaining-based step, perhaps — for the circularity to be broken, together with a judgment about whether that addition merely relocates the normative assumption to a new place. Please do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations; the candidates will have nothing in front of them and neither do I want to look up references I cannot check.

Kindly organise your reply into the following five sections, in this order. First, "The Examination Question": the question exactly as it would be printed for the candidates, between 200 and 250 words, split into a part (a) worth 15 marks on the protocol as described and a part (b) worth 10 marks on how it might be repaired, with the anchor stipulations stated plainly enough that no candidate can be confused about the setup. Second, "Model Answer": roughly 800 words, written in the voice of a very strong candidate working without notes, with no symbolic mathematics beyond the notion of an affine transformation. Third, "Marking Scheme": a table allocating all 25 marks across named criteria, each row giving the marks available and one sentence stating what a response must do to earn them. Fourth, "What Separates 20 Marks from 14": about 200 words identifying the two or three specific moves that the higher-scoring answer makes and the lower one does not. Fifth, "Common Failures": four or five predictable errors, each with the number of marks it costs and a brief note on why it reflects a misunderstanding of the problem rather than clumsy expression.
