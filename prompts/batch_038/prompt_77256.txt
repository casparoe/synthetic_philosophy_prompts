Thirty-five years ago I joined a national laboratory and spent my working life on detector physics and, in my later years, on the internal governance of large collaborations. I retired three years ago and have since been reading epistemology with more diligence than I ever brought to committee work. This autumn I have been asked to give a forty-minute colloquium talk, with fifteen minutes for questions, in a philosophy department whose strengths are formal epistemology and philosophy of science. The room will contain several people who work on the dispute about whether practical stakes bear on what counts as knowledge; at least one committed evidentialist who holds that nothing but evidence can raise or lower justification; two or three philosophers of science who are sceptical that physics has anything to teach epistemology; and — at my own request — two members of the university's biostatistics group, who will have read none of the philosophical literature and who will care only whether my description of statistical practice is accurate.

The thesis I intend to defend has four parts.

1. The evidential threshold a collaboration must clear before it may announce a discovery is not fixed by the evidence alone but partly by the cost of being wrong. The conventional five-sigma standard — usually glossed as permitting a chance fluctuation of roughly one in three and a half million — is a stakes-relative convention, adopted because a false announcement in this field destroys careers and contaminates a literature, whereas a missed discovery costs only delay. The same calculation, at a lower threshold, governs when we allow an unquantified systematic uncertainty to stand.
2. The dispute over encroachment has therefore been conducted largely without noticing that its central claim is already institutionalised in the discipline least willing to see itself as pragmatic in this way, and that the institution supplies empirical detail the armchair cases do not.
3. The practice also exhibits a feature the literature under-describes: the sensitivity sits at the level of the collaboration, not the individual, and the threshold is fixed in advance by convention rather than computed case by case. That is what makes it usable. A single physicist, alone at two in the morning with an interesting excess, cannot be trusted to recalibrate her own standard to the stakes of the moment; the convention exists to stop her doing exactly that.
4. Relatedly, I want to separate what a collaboration knows from what it is entitled to announce, and claim that it is the announcement norm that is stakes-sensitive. Whether that distinction rescues evidentialism or finishes it off I do not know, and the talk should say so plainly.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight or nine objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by the damage they would do in this room — not the ones I would get at a laboratory seminar, and not the ones a general audience would produce. Phrase each as the person most likely to raise it would phrase it, including the two statisticians, whom I expect to attack my characterisation of five sigma as a matter of stakes rather than of multiple comparisons and trial factors; that objection should appear in their own technical vocabulary, and I need to know in advance whether they are right. For every objection supply: a reply I can deliver in ninety seconds without invoking machinery I have not had time to learn; what I should concede in the first sentence in order to keep this audience's confidence; and where the reply is softest. Mark the one objection that, left standing, takes the whole thesis down with it. Finally, tell me which question I am secretly hoping nobody asks, and what I would say if it comes anyway.

Where the philosophical literature has a better answer than mine, say so rather than coaching me into a defence I cannot sustain; I would rather learn that before the talk than during it.
