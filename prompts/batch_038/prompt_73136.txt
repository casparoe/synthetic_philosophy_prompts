I am a fourth-year doctoral student in political philosophy, and I have been asked — somewhat inexplicably — to give the opening remarks at a campus civic-engagement event called Democracy Night, held three weeks before a general election in the student union ballroom. I would like the speech written in full rather than outlined, and I would like it to run about six minutes when read aloud at an ordinary pace.

The room, so far as the organizers can tell me: roughly two hundred people. About half are first- and second-year undergraduates, many voting for the first time, many of whom have told the organizers they are not sure it is worth the trouble. There is a table of county election workers who staffed the last two elections and who will run the registration drive afterward; they are probably the most civically committed people in the building and certainly the most tired. There are perhaps a dozen faculty, including two political theorists who work on this material and who disagree with me and with each other — one of them has argued forcefully in print that voting is at best supererogatory and that presenting it as required is a small piece of moral bullying. And there will be two local reporters, because the event is being covered as a test of whether a university can hold genuinely nonpartisan election programming.

The structural constraints are all real and none are negotiable:

- The event is co-sponsored by the university's nonprofit civic office. Nothing in the speech may endorse or disparage a candidate, party, ballot measure, or officeholder, and nothing may covertly favor one side of a live local controversy.
- The organizers have asked specifically that the words duty, obligation, and civic responsibility not appear. Their reasoning is that those words are exactly what makes the students who plan to stay home feel lectured at, and they would rather not lose them in the first ninety seconds. I have agreed. Whatever normative content the speech carries has to be carried by other means.
- The speech may not tell anyone to vote, and may not imply that anyone who abstains has done something wrong. But turnout is the purpose of the evening, and the registration tables open the moment I sit down. A speech that leaves the room indifferent to whether it registers would be a failure of a different kind, and I need you to solve that problem rather than step around it.
- The election workers will be formally recognized in the middle of the program, so the opening remarks should leave room for that rather than absorb it. Relatedly, the speech may not use their exhaustion as an emotional lever; I do not want anyone leaving the ballroom because they were made to feel ungrateful.

For what it is worth, I do not myself think voting is required, and I do not want the speech to smuggle in the opposite view. But neither do I think whether to vote is a question solely about outcomes, and I do not want the room left with that picture either.

The idea I want doing the work is one I have been developing in my dissertation, and I want it staged rather than announced. It is a distinction between two ways a vote can matter. On the causal way, a vote is a small contribution to an outcome, and its value rises and falls with its probability of being decisive — which everyone in the room already knows is negligible. On the attributive way, a ballot is not a contribution to a result but a fact about whom the result belongs to: an election's outcome is authored by a determinate set of people, and each ballot settles whether you are among them. The consequence I want the speech to turn on is that the attributive way does not straightforwardly require anyone to vote, and does not straightforwardly require anyone to vote for anyone in particular. It asks a different question — what are you willing to have been part of — and that question admits answers that include staying home and answers that include voting for the lesser of two options one despises. I want that question made vivid for a room of twenty-year-olds without ever using the word attribution, and without sounding at any point like a seminar paper.

Two people in particular the speech has to reach, because I know both will be there:

- A first-year student who has told the organizers she will not vote, on the grounds that both major options are equally catastrophic and that a ballot for either is a ballot for the system that produced them. She is not being dramatic and she has thought about it. The speech must give her something that is not a variation on every vote counts, and must not suggest she has misunderstood the situation.
- The faculty member who holds voting to be supererogatory. I want at least one sentence he would be glad to have said aloud, and I want him to be able to tell that I know precisely what his objection is. He should not be strawmanned in front of his own students.

On tone: warm, plain, and funny in two or three places, though not performing funny — the humor should come from recognition rather than from jokes. No philosophical name-dropping whatsoever; if an idea from the literature is present, it must be present in ordinary language. No rhetorical question in the first minute and no more than one in the whole speech. No anaphora stacks, no let-us constructions, and no ending that degenerates into a chant. The last thing anyone hears should make standing up and walking over to a registration table feel like the natural next move rather than an assignment handed out.

Please write the speech in full. Then, in a short separate note, tell me where you think it fails: which constraint you had to bend, whether the attributive idea survives the translation into ordinary language or comes out as something vaguer and more inspirational than I intend, and whether the abstaining first-year student has actually been given a reason to register or only a more comfortable way to decline.
