I am working out a reading of al-Ghazālī's treatment of causation in the Incoherence of the Philosophers, and before I commit to it in print I want a clear-eyed map of its exposure. What would count against it?

My reading has three parts. First, the discussion devoted to the philosophers' claim that a cause cannot be separated from its effect — the seventeenth of the twenty, the one built around fire and cotton — advances a modal and epistemic thesis rather than an occasionalist ontology. What al-Ghazālī denies, on my account, is that the tie between what is habitually called a cause and what is habitually called its effect can be demonstrated to be necessary; the point about experience is that it registers the effect's occurring at the time of the cause, not its occurring by means of that cause. He is therefore not committed to the claim that God is the only genuine agent in nature, and the doctrine of a divinely maintained habit leaves room for created causal powers exercised within that habit. Second, the chapter is chiefly load-bearing for the first discussion: the real target is the Avicennan thesis that a complete cause entails its effect necessarily, since that is what turns the world into an eternal consequence of the First's essence rather than a voluntary product. Third, the formulations that sound strongly occasionalist — that the fire does not act at all, that the burning is God's doing — are best read as arguing within the opponents' own framework: he is attacking a necessitarianism the philosophers cannot demonstrate, not publishing a natural philosophy of his own.

I would like the following, sorted by how decisive each item would be rather than in the order it occurs to you:

Within the seventeenth discussion itself, which moves commit him to more than the modal point, and what would have to be true of those moves for them to be fatal rather than merely awkward? Consider in particular the appeal to divine power, the treatment of inanimate bodies as non-agents, and the role the miracle cases play: if the argument only showed that the connection is not conceptually necessary, would he have needed it at all to make room for a fire that fails to burn, given that an opponent could accommodate the miracle by divine suspension or by removing a property?

Across the corpus, what pressures the reading: the framing of the work as refutation rather than construction, and whether that framing is doing the work I want or whether it would equally license dismissing his strong statements as exoteric; the three companion works (the exposition of the philosophers' doctrines written as its introduction, the logic appendix, and the positive Ashʿarite theology written to fill the doctrinal space the refutation leaves open) and what each would have to contain to confirm or embarrass me; the classification of the philosophers' errors into innovation versus unbelief and where the causality charge sits in that taxonomy, and what that placement implies about how much he was staking on it; the acquisition account of human agency he inherits, and whether an interpretation that grants real efficacy to created natures in general can sit with a theology that assigns the creation of acts to God; and his unguarded causal talk elsewhere in ethics, jurisprudence, and medicine.

Address squarely the objection I most fear, that my reading renders the chapter philosophically inert: if all he denies is logical or conceptual necessity, then no Aristotelian or Avicennan ever asserted otherwise, since what they claimed was natural necessity, and he would be refuting a position nobody held. How damaging is that? Can the reading be repaired without a retreat that changes what the reading was?

Say what each interpretation predicts and the other does not, and whether there is any place where the two diverge in a way that his texts could settle rather than argument alone. I will tell you what I would personally find most persuasive: a passage in which he affirmatively assigns efficient causal power to a created thing without immediately re-describing it as divine habitual practice, together with an argument showing that the modal thesis by itself cannot do the work he needs it to do.

Weigh the reception evidence: his Andalusian critic read the chapter as an ontological denial of natural efficacy and objected that it destroys science and makes names of things empty. How much weight should a claim about what an author was committed to give a near-contemporary reading that is both competent and hostile?

Then the honest part. Is my reading insulated? A dialectical or audience-relative interpretation can absorb any counter-passage by saying he was arguing on his opponents' premises or writing for readers not qualified for his real view, and I notice I am already relying on something like that move when I dismiss the strong statements as ad hominem. Tell me whether that makes the reading unfalsifiable in practice, whether unfalsifiability is a defect for a claim of this kind — an interpretive claim about an author's commitments rather than an empirical hypothesis about the world — and what, if anything, would make me abandon it outright rather than qualify it further. Finally, tell me which of the considerations you raise a defender of the committed-occasionalist reading would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would wave off as compatible with everything they hold.
