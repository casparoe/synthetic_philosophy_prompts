So I've been messing around with the accuracy-first picture of epistemology — the one where the only thing that makes a set of credences better than another is that its closer to the truth — and I built an argument that seems to demolish it, and now I can't tell whether it's a real argument or a parlor trick. Say accuracy is measured the obvious way: squared distance from the truth-value, summed over the propositions your credance function is defined on.

(1) One credence function is better than another to exactly the extent that it's more accurate; nothing else counts epistemically.
(2) So you ought to have whichever credence function maximizes expected accuracy, where the expectation is worked out using the credences you already have.
(3) Pick any p on which your credence c is comfortably over 1/2 but under 1, and consider the alternative that assigns 1 to p and changes nothing else.
(4) That alternative does strictly better than you do in every world where p is true and strictly worse in every world where p is false; since your own credences say the p-worlds are the likelier ones, it has higher expected accuracy by your own lights.
(5) So (2) tells you to jump to credence 1 in p.
(6) Run (3) through (5) for everything you lean toward and the mirror-image version for everything you lean against, and the accuracy-maximizing move is to become thoroughly dogmatic: 1s and 0s across the board.
(7) No theory that makes total dogmatism rationally required is a theory anyone should accept, so (1) and (2) can't both hold and the framework refutes itself.

My reading group signed off on it, and the one objector's response was basically that maybe the conclusion is just true and my squeamishness is a hangover. For what it's worth I'd much rather keep (1) than (2), since the idea that truth-conduciveness is the only genuinely epistemic virtue is the whole reason I got interested in this. Where does the chain actually break, and what do I pay for the fix? Continuous prose only please, like an email — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
