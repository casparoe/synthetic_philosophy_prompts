I am putting together a seminar exercise on the European Union's democratic deficit, and I want a worked example rather than a survey of positions.

Begin from the distinction, familiar in debates about the boundaries of the demos, between the all-subjected principle — that everyone subject to a given body's rules is entitled to a say in making them — and the all-affected principle — that everyone whose interests are significantly affected by a decision is entitled to a say. Give me one concrete decision taken by or through the Union: identify the policy area, indicate roughly when it happened, and say who decided what and under which authority. The case should be one in which the two principles pick out genuinely different constituencies, so that the decision-making arrangement satisfies one of them while plainly failing the other.

Two further constraints on the example. First, it should not be a case whose moral is legible from the headline: I am not looking for a re-run of the observation that the Commission is not directly elected, or for the Greek bailouts treated once more as a parable of creditor and debtor peoples. Second, and more demanding, I would like a case in which admitting the affected outsiders into the relevant constituency would itself generate a fresh set of persons who are then subject to the resulting rules without any voice in them — a decision whose constituency cannot be closed without leaving someone outside it. If you think no such case exists in European practice, say so and explain why the request is impossible rather than offering a near miss.

Once the example is on the table, explain what the divergence shows. In particular, say which sense of being affected your case relies on — having one's interests set back, or having one's available options foreclosed by a decision one played no part in making — and whether the choice between those senses is doing quiet work in your verdict. Then state whether the difficulty you have identified is a defect in the Union's institutional design, a defect in one of the two principles, or something that neither principle is equipped to register at all.

Please be explicit throughout about the state of the question. Distinguish what is settled or close to settled among people who work on this material from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position — a particular verdict about whether the all-affected principle can even be coherently formulated, for instance, or about whether supranational democracy requires a demos at all — as though it were the consensus view. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I am asking for reasoning that could be handed to students and argued with.
