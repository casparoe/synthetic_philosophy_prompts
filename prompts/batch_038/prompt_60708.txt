I'm two thirds through a novel and the wall I've hit is really a philosophy problem, so I'm asking you instead of my agent.

One of my characters — a retired schoolteacher, call her Agnès — wrote an advance directive at sixty-two, in full possesion of her faculties, spelling out that once she can no longer recognise her daughter or follow a conversation, she wants no antibiotics, no tube, no hospital: keep her comfortable and let her go. She was deliberate about it, the way people are after watching a parent decline, and she made her daughter promise.

Now in the book she's eighty, has dementia, doesn't know who her daughter is, and is content. She hums. She likes the way the light comes in over the sink. If anyone actually asked her, she'd fight off a chest infection with every scrap of appetite she's got left. The daughter has to decide whether to honour the piece of paper, and that kitchen scene is the hinge of the whole thing.

So: what would Montaigne make of this? And I don't want a menu. I want you to land somewhere and defend it. Does he give authority to the sixty-two-year-old who wrote the document, or to the eighty-year-old humming at the table?

There's a wrinkle I keep turning over, and I want you to deal with it rather than smooth it out. I think there are at least two Montaignes. The early one who treated death as a discipline, who spent years rehearsing it so it wouldn't catch him raw. And the later one who got thrown off a horse, came round half-dead, found the whole business strangely sweet and painless, and decided you can't practise for dying and mostly shouldn't try. Agnès's directive is exactly the early man's move — premeditation, self-binding, getting your exit in order while you're still yourself. If the accident undid that move for him, does it also undermine the document? Do the two Montaignes give different verdicts about her, and if so which one is the one I should be writing from?

I also know the essays are soaked in the idea that the self isn't a stable object, that what he's depicting is a thing in transition rather than any settled state — and I'd assume that cuts against a twenty-year-old instruction binding a person who is by now arguably a different creature. But he's also unsentimental to the point of coldness about the dying, and about not letting other people's fuss and ceremony run your last days for you. Make the case whichever way you go, and lean on the parts of him that actually carry weight here, not on general Montaigne-ish warmth.

There's also a live argument in this territory that I'd want you to engage with on the merits, without needing to name anybody: whether what's at stake in these directives is the interests of the demented person herself, who may no longer have any interest in how her story ends, or whether it's really the earlier person's interest in the shape of a whole life, or whether — the version that scares me — it's the family's need for the death to look dignified to them. Where does Montaigne's thinking actually bite on that, and where does he just not have anything to say?

Then two more things. Tell me where your reading is most likely to be wrong: which commitment of his you had to stretch or downplay to get your verdict, and what the strongest counter-case looks like built entirely out of his own material. And since I have to write the scene, give me the bottom line in a sentence or two — if Montaigne were standing in that kitchen with the daughter, what does he tell her to do, and what does he tell her she can stop tormenting herself about?

Commit. I'd much rather be argued with than handed both sides and told to pick.
