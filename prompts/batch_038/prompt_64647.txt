You are a political economist and constitutional theorist working on fiscal federalism and multilevel governance; think carefully before answering, and treat the view below as something to be stress-tested rather than endorsed.

Here is the position I hold. Subsidiarity is not fundamentally a moral principle about who ought to govern, and it is not primarily an information principle about local knowledge. It is a claim about where feedback closes: authority should be placed at the level where the costs of a bad decision fall on those who made it, because that alignment is the only reliable substitute for a supervisor. What matters, on this reading, is not the vertical location of a competence but whether decision-makers are fiscally and politically exposed to the consequences of their own choices. Several consequences follow, and these are what I want pressure-tested: a devolved unit that exports most of its costs — pollution downstream, debt resolved by a central bailout, burdens shifted onto non-residents — is not a victory for subsidiarity but a failure of it; centralization can satisfy the principle when it internalizes costs that lower levels were exporting; the EU's subsidiarity machinery and the German argument over when federal legislation is necessary are aimed at the wrong variable; and identity-based arguments for devolution, that people should be governed by those with whom they identify, are a separate and weaker claim that my view neither entails nor needs.

What would count against this? I want a map of the position's exposure, not a defense of it. Please address:

1. Which empirical findings in comparative federalism would be genuinely damaging — cases where exposure and competence location come apart without any observable loss of performance, or where heavily cost-exporting jurisdictions govern better than tightly exposed ones — as distinct from findings that are merely awkward.
2. Which theoretical results the view stands or falls with: how much weight the Tiebout and Oates apparatus can bear for an exposure-based assignment rule; whether Olson-style fiscal equivalence is already doing all the work, so that my thesis is a relabeling of an existing idea rather than a rival to it; and whether the joint-decision trap critique and the market-preserving federalism literature cut for or against the view.
3. Which of these a committed defender would concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss as beside the point, with reasons.
4. Whether the criterion risks anything at all. Costs-fall-on-deciders can be re-specified after the fact — any centralization explained by a previously unnoticed spillover, any decentralization by local exposure — so give me an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is fatal for a thesis of this kind or tolerable for one.
5. What the view predicts that rival accounts of subsidiarity do not, ideally in the form of one or two discriminating cases.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated and to say whether that insulation is a defect. Begin with a short brainstorm of candidate defeaters before sorting and developing them. You may name arguments, theorems, and concepts, but do not produce a bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus, since I have no way of looking anything up. Write in American English.
