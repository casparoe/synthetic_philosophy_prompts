For my senior humanities seminar we spent the last three weeks on Fear and Trembling, and I am writing the final paper on Kierkegaard's claim that Abraham's situation cannot be made intelligible within ethics at all. I follow the basic architecture: the ethical just is the universal, the set of demands that apply to everyone and can be stated in terms anyone could in principle endorse; the tragic heroes he lines up against Abraham, such as Agamemnon giving up Iphigenia for the fleet, remain inside that universal because their communities can comprehend and even mourn what they did; and Abraham is categorically different because his undertaking has no place in any shared justification, which is also why he cannot speak, and why concealing it from Sarah, from Eliezer, and from Isaac is not a tactical choice for him but a structural one.

What I cannot find is a secular case that actually occupies that position rather than merely resembling it, and my paper turns on whether one exists. So please construct a single, concrete, fully worked-out example of a real human decision that has the full structure of a teleological suspension of the ethical without any of the theology. Set it in an ordinary contemporary situation, name the people and relationships involved, and be specific about what the agent does and what it costs. Then satisfy each of the following conditions, which are the ones that sink every candidate I have tried on my own:

1. Nothing supernatural. The pull on the agent must be describable in naturalistic terms, even if the agent themselves would describe it in other language.
2. What the agent violates must be a serious ethical requirement, something like a duty not to kill, not to deceive someone who trusts them, or not to abandon a person who depends on them, rather than a convention, an institutional rule, or the expectations of their community.
3. It may not be a tragic hero case. If the agent sacrifices one particular person for a public good that the community could weigh and endorse, they have stayed inside the universal and lost the point.
4. It may not be a whistleblower, a conscientious objector, a civil disobedient, a revolutionary, or anyone acting on a moral insight their society has not yet caught up to. All of those appeal to a better or truer universal, which is precisely the move Abraham is denied.
5. The agent's inability to justify themselves to the people they wrong must be essential rather than circumstantial. It cannot be a product of secrecy, missing evidence, classified information, or an audience that simply refuses to listen; those people would have to be unable to accept the reasons even if the agent stated them perfectly and were fully believed.
6. The agent must not come out looking like a monster or a zealot. I want the example to leave the reader genuinely unsettled about how to judge them, not to settle it by making them repulsive.

If you conclude that all six cannot be met at once, say so and identify which condition does the eliminating work, and show me the closest case that survives the other five.

Then answer the question my paper actually needs. Some readers take the Abraham structure and generalize it, arguing that any unconditional commitment to one particular person or project already overrides one's duties to everyone else, so that choosing a friend, a child, or a vocation over the anonymous claims of strangers is the same paradox in a lower register, and Abraham is the rule rather than the exception. Others argue the opposite: strip away the religious frame and the category simply collapses, leaving only fanaticism on one side and ordinary moral courage on the other, with nothing distinctive in between. Take one of these two positions and defend it using your example as the evidence. I am not looking for a survey of both readings or a compromise verdict; I want the claim you think is right, and an account of exactly what in the example licenses it.
