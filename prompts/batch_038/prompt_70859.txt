So — I'm about two-thirds through my third novel and I've got myself into a knot that I'm now pretty sure is a philosophy problem rather than a craft problem, and I want you to engineer me a case instead of arguing with me about it. Context: the book gives a first-person voice to a dead woman. In the book-world she's real but she's the sort of person who'd leave a very thin record — died in the late 1940s, no descendants, nobody who knew her is alive, and all that survives is a couple hundred documents: payroll sheets, three newspaper items, letters other people wrote that mention her in passing. I've made up what the inside of her life felt like for about a hundred pages — smells, grudges, the exact way she talked to her sister, a scene where she decides the most important thing in the book while standing in a stairwell. And the upshot in the book-world is that she goes from a footnote to being beloved: a town renames a bridge after her, teachers assign it, a slander that sat unchallenged for eighty years gets corrected. My friend who teaches bioethics says I'm doing something *to* her. I say I'm doing something *for* her. Neither of us can say what the something is, which is why I want it built to spec rather than debated over dinner.

Here's the spec. Two scenarios, same invented dead woman, same author, same publisher, same year, everything held fixed that can be held fixed. Case A is the above: invented first-person interiority, and the effect on her posthumous standing is unambiguously good. Case B: the same author instead publishes a scrupulous book about her — every sentence verifiable against the documents, nothing invented, no voice at all, just the record laid out in order — and because of what the arrangement lets a reader infer, she comes out looking considerably worse than she actually was: cautious, self-interested, willing to let a colleague take the fall for something. Nothing asserted in B is false. The effect on her standing is unambiguously bad. One paragraph per case, no more. And please resist the cheap outs: no will clause, no destroyed papers, no diary, no interview in which she says do or says don't, no living relatives, no ghosts, no afterlife, nobody positioned to consent or object on her behalf. Whatever verdicts come out have to come out of the views themselves, not out of a stipulation I handed you. Then run three positions across both cases and tell me what each one says and why: (1) the view that a person's interests can outlive them, so the dead can be benefited and set back, and what we owe them is basically not to make their posthumous situation worse; (2) the view that what's owed turns on authorization or standing rather than welfare — roughly the idea that a person's image and inner life aren't material the rest of us get to help ourselves to, whether or not the borrowing turns out well for them; (3) the view that nothing is owed to the dead at all and every duty in this neighborhood actually runs to the living: readers, the community holding the memory, the practice of writing about real people.

The design goal, so you know what counts as cheating: (1) and (2) should come out exactly opposite across A and B — (1) prefers A and condemns B, (2) is uneasy about A and untroubled by B — and (3) should track neither of them, landing somewhere the other two dont. If you find you have to fudge a case to get that, don't fudge it; tell me which leg of the design fails and why. For view (1) specifically I want you to say plainly what the benefit in A actually consists of, given that nobody she knew is alive and she can't experience any of it — that's the soft joint and I don't want it papered over. Then the honest bits: which single feature of A is doing the work for view (2) — I'd of guessed it's the first-person voice, but prove it by giving me a repair, a version of A that keeps the invented interiority in some other grammatical form, and report whether the unease survives the swap; a variant of B in which the (1)-verdict weakens; and the strongest attack the worst-off view can make on the whole setup. Finish practically: what I'd have to change in my own book to satisfy (2) without giving up the voice, if anything — because if the answer is give up the voice, I want to know now and not in draft four. American English please, and no reading list or citations, I won't read them; I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
