I serve on my college's community standards panel, and the case in front of us this term turns out to be less about what happened than about which moral culture each party is operating in. The facts aren't disputed: one student mocked another's accent in seminar and then repeated the bit in a group chat. The respondent holds that a slight you decline to be wounded by is beneath notice, that he would never have escalated it himself, and that an institution willing to adjudicate insults turns students into clients of their own dignity. The complainant holds that the remark degraded him, that the laughter in the chat ratified it, and that anything short of a recorded finding plus a directed apology fails to count as acknowledgment. Our code permits findings only against enumerated standards — one covers "conduct that demeans" — and the chair wants to route the matter to facilitated dialogue as a neutral compromise. Having read the sociology on honor, dignity, and victimhood moral cultures, I don't think that neutrality is on offer: declining to hear a slight is itself a substantive endorsement of the respondent's culture, and in a setting where apologies are status transactions, a coerced one may be worth less than none.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Friday's hearing. Recommend a concrete disposition on the enumerated standard, give reasons both parties could actually answer rather than restate, and state honestly the strongest consideration against your recommendation — including whether any of the three cultures deserves the panel's implicit backing.
