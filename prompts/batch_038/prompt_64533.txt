I've been reading a well-regarded popular book on the philosophy of science, and before I repeat anything from it in my own teaching I'd like a careful accuracy check. The chapter in question concerns Hempel's paradox of the ravens, and the central passage runs as follows:

> "Hempel asked a deceptively simple question: what counts as evidence for the claim that all ravens are black? Nicod's criterion gives the obvious answer — a generalisation is confirmed by each of its instances, so every black raven you observe lends support to the hypothesis. But the claim is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, 'all non-black things are non-ravens', and Hempel laid down a further principle, the equivalence condition, according to which whatever confirms one of two logically equivalent statements must confirm the other. Now a white shoe is neither black nor a raven, so it is an instance of the contrapositive, and by the equivalence condition it confirms the original hypothesis about ravens. Hempel was not offering a joke. The implications are drastic: because confirmation on this account turns only on logical form, a black raven and a white shoe support the hypothesis to precisely the same degree, and indeed almost any observation whatsoever — a thermometer reading, the number of buses passing your window — confirms almost any universal generalisation one cares to write down. Hempel's own eventual way out was to insist that confirmation cannot be a purely formal relation after all, since what matters is what the evidence is *about*: a fact about footwear is not about ravens, so it cannot speak to a claim about them. Contemporary Bayesian treatments have in any case settled the question quantitatively. Because non-black non-ravens outnumber ravens so extravagantly, the probability assigned to 'all ravens are black' is left strictly unchanged by the observation of a white shoe; the correct verdict is therefore that such observations confirm the hypothesis not at all, and the paradox was an artefact of a crude logical theory. What Hempel's puzzle really demonstrates is Hume's old point in a new guise: no accumulation of observations can ever establish a general law, which is why the attempt to define confirmation in purely logical terms was ultimately abandoned."

Three things about this leave me uneasy, though I lack the grounding to say whether my unease is justified. First, a friend who works in statistics assures me the Bayesian calculation delivers a small *positive* increment rather than nothing, and that the interesting cases are ones where the increment is far from negligible. Second, I have a recollection from an undergraduate lecture that Hempel did not retreat from the paradoxical conclusion but embraced it, and that he had an illustration involving a universe containing only a handful of objects in which examining a non-black non-raven genuinely does carry evidential weight. Third, the passage seems to me to slide between a claim about the logic of confirmation and a claim about the justification of induction, and I cannot tell whether those come apart.
