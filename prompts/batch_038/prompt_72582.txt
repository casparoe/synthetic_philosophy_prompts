I would like to request a careful consistency audit of a set of views I hold about choosing between options that cannot be ranked. The occasion is personal rather than academic. In three weeks I must accept one of two research positions and decline the other. The first is a permanent post at a national laboratory in the country where my family lives: well resourced, secure, unlikely to change much over twenty years. The second is a five-year group-leadership position abroad, with considerably more scope, no realistic prospect of renewal, and consequences for my partner's own work that I would describe as damaging rather than merely inconvenient. I have gone over the comparison many times and it has not resolved. Rather than continue circling, I have written down the principles I find myself actually relying on, and I would like to know whether they can all be kept.

1. A choice is rational only if something counts in favor of the option chosen that does not count equally for the option refused. Where no such differential exists, the selection is arbitrary, whatever produces it.

2. It does not follow that I may defer. Letting both offers lapse would be the worst of my available outcomes by a wide margin, and I take choosing before the deadline to be required of me, not merely permitted.

3. I do not think the two positions are equally good. Saying so would imply a single scale on which they score the same, and I do not believe there is such a scale here: what the one offers is continuity, security and proximity to people who need me; what the other offers is scope and a kind of work I will not be offered again. My difficulty is that I cannot rank them, not that I have ranked them level.

4. Preference is not, for me, an independent authority. I prefer things because I judge them better; I do not judge them better because I prefer them. A preference that has not been produced by such a judgment carries no justificatory weight.

5. Because I cannot rank the options, I intend to settle the matter by a procedure fixed in advance, and to treat its outcome as binding. I take that procedure to generate a genuine reason to accept whichever position it selects: a reason I did not have before adopting the procedure, and not merely a psychological device for bringing deliberation to an end.

6. After the choice is made, I expect my preferences to arrange themselves around it. Within a year I anticipate preferring the position I hold to the one I declined, and I regard that as ordinary and healthy rather than as self-deception.

7. Reasons, as I understand them, are grounded in features that make an option worth choosing. I do not think a consideration becomes a reason because I have willed it, resolved upon it, or bound myself by it.

8. I also expect the present stalemate to be temporary in a further sense. Whichever position I take will generate collaborators, projects and obligations that do not now exist, and these will make it evident, later on, that this was the one to take. I expect to look back without regret.

I have deliberately not indicated which of these I would be most willing to abandon, and I would ask you not to read a ranking into the order in which I have listed them or into the length of any item. I am not asking which position to accept, and I am not asking how I ought to feel about my predicament; I want an audit of the list.

Specifically: identify which of these commitments genuinely cannot be held together, and which only appear to conflict until the relevant distinction is drawn. I suspect that at least one pair falls into the second category, but I am not confident that I have correctly identified which. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging. For each, state what would have to be given up or weakened and what the least costly revision would be, including what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the greatest number of conflicts at once, and whether any item is doing substantially more work than its phrasing suggests — in particular, whether anything I have presented as a remark about how I would describe my situation turns out to be a substantive thesis with consequences for the rest of the list.
