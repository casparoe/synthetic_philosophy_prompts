I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I am serving as rapporteur to an interdisciplinary working group — two philosophers, a pain clinician, a behavioural neuroscientist, and a veterinary ethicist — that has spent the last year drafting a consensus statement on when a subject is to be treated as being in pain. The statement is meant to apply uniformly across the populations our institutions deal with: surgical patients, patients with disorders of consciousness, non-human animals, and patients with focal lesions who give atypical verbal reports. Our operative question, which we have never managed to explicate to everyone's satisfaction, is whether a subject's state counts as pain in the sense the statement cares about — roughly, whether there is something it is like for that subject which is bad for them in the way pain is bad.

We have now recorded verdicts on six case files. I give them below in the form they appear in the minutes, including the two dissents, which are part of the record.

1. *A patient of the kind described in the asymbolia literature*, with a left posterior insular and parietal opercular lesion. On pinprick to the affected side she reports, unprompted, a sharp, intense, precisely localized sensation which she calls pain and rates 7/10. She is smiling, continues her conversation, does not withdraw or guard the limb, declines the analgesic offered at the end of the examination on the grounds that there is nothing to take, and over six weeks of testing shows no sign of coming to avoid the testing room. **Verdict: does not count.** One member dissented, holding that her description of the sensation's character was as good a first-person report as we are ever going to get and that we had no standing to overrule it; the majority recorded that what she said about not being bothered carried more weight than what she said about the sensation itself.

2. *A highly hypnotizable volunteer* given a suggestion that the sensation of a noxious thermal stimulus will be dull and of no consequence. He reports the stimulus as intense and clearly localized, says he would call it pain, and says it does not bother him. He nonetheless flinches on every trial, his heart rate rises, his performance on a concurrent tracking task degrades markedly during stimulation, and by the tenth trial he has learned to operate the escape switch quickly and reliably. **Verdict: counts.**

3. *A surgical patient under neuromuscular blockade with inadequate anaesthesia.* No movement, and no departure from the autonomic and derived indices the monitoring system recorded. Weeks later she gives a detailed, spontaneous, distressed account of the intraoperative period — burning at the incision, an overwhelming wish for it to stop, a sense of being trapped — and subsequently develops the intrusive recollection and avoidance one would expect. **Verdict: counts.**

4. *A paid volunteer in a cold-pressor protocol.* He reports 9/10 intensity and says plainly that it is awful and that he wants it to stop; he keeps his hand immersed because the protocol requires it and the payment depends on it, withdraws the moment he is released, and afterwards declines to book the second session even at a higher rate. **Verdict: counts.**

5. *A volunteer who seeks out a noxious stimulus as part of a practice he values.* He rates both its intensity and its unpleasantness as high on the standard scales, and says he wants it to continue; he does not terminate it when he may, and would decline an analgesic offered mid-session. **Verdict: counts**, after by far the longest discussion of the six.

6. *A decerebrate rodent preparation*, the relevant forebrain structures removed, showing robust withdrawal, licking and guarding of the stimulated limb, and normal non-associative sensitization across trials. No report is available and none could be. **Verdict: does not count.** The behavioural neuroscientist dissented, arguing that organized protective behaviour plus sensitization is the same evidence we accepted in file 3 minus the later testimony, and that we had given no reason to think the testimony was doing the decisive work.

Next month we take up a seventh case, which I drew up and on which nobody has yet ruled.

7. A volunteer in a first-in-human trial of a candidate analgesic, tested with noxious thermal stimuli. On every trial he reports, unprompted and consistently, an intense sharp pain, 8/10, correctly localized, and says that it is awful and that he wants it to stop. He does not flinch or guard, and his autonomic indices are flat. His performance on a concurrent demanding task is not merely preserved but slightly better than his own baseline. He does not use the termination switch that sits within easy reach and that he has been shown how to use; when this is put to him mid-session he says he does not feel moved to. Afterwards he has only a vague sense that the hour passed, cannot recall individual trials, describes the session as nothing much, declines the analgesic offered at the end on the grounds that there is no reason to take it, and books another session for next week. So far as we can establish he is not deceptive, confused, or under suggestion, and his concurrent reports replicate across three sessions.

My supervisor has asked me to circulate a memo in advance, predicting how the group will rule and recommending how it should rule if it wants to stay consistent with its own record. I would like your help drafting it. I am not after references or a survey of the literature — we are reasoning from the record, not from the reading list. What I need is the following.

First, which criteria are jointly compatible with all six recorded verdicts? I expect there to be more than one, and I want each stated as a test that could actually be applied to a new case file rather than as a slogan. Second, for each surviving criterion, what does it imply about file 7, and where do they diverge? Third, your best guess at the group's actual verdict, with your reasons for preferring the criterion you think they have been running over its rivals — including whatever you can read off the pattern of the two dissents and the difficulty of file 5, since I have told you where members dissented but not where they wavered. Fourth, a recommended ruling: which of the six is the closest analog, and in which respect; and, if the record genuinely underdetermines file 7, what principle the group would be adopting by ruling one way rather than the other, and whether either adoption sits well with the dissent already on file. Fifth, the further case file that would most sharply separate the surviving criteria, with each criterion's predicted verdict on it.

Please write the memo in prose, and then append a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `surviving_criteria`: array of objects, each with `criterion` (a one-sentence test applicable to an unfamiliar case file), `verdicts_reproduced` (array of file numbers it gets right), `files_it_fails` (array; empty if none), `case_seven_prediction` (`"counts"`, `"does_not_count"`, or `"indeterminate"` — use `indeterminate` only where the criterion genuinely yields no verdict), `confidence` (0–1), and `rationale` (at most 40 words).
- `refuted_criteria`: array of objects with `criterion`, `refuting_file` (number), and `how` (at most 25 words). Include the obvious candidates — protective behaviour as necessary, aversive report as necessary, an occurrent wish to stop as necessary, self-report as authoritative in both directions — so that I can see they have been checked rather than overlooked.
- `best_guess`: object with `predicted_verdict`, `criterion_i_think_they_run`, and `why_preferred` (at most 60 words).
- `recommended_ruling`: object with `verdict`, `closest_analog_file` (number), `respect_of_analogy` (at most 40 words), `principle_if_counts` (at most 40 words), `principle_if_not` (at most 40 words), and `note_on_the_dissents` (at most 40 words).
- `discriminating_case`: object with `description` (at most 70 words), `criteria_separated` (array of criterion labels drawn from `surviving_criteria`), and `predicted_verdict_under_each` (object mapping each of those labels to `"counts"` or `"does_not_count"`).
- `residual_ambiguity`: at most 60 words on what in the group's *question*, as distinct from its record, no case file could settle.
