Bit of help wanted, if you don't mind. I run our third-year seminar on chance and credence, and for the last couple of years I've opened the week on Lewis's Principal Principle with a little argument of my own, meant to show that PP can't do the job we want it to do — bridging objective chance and rational credence — for anyone who is actually situated inside the world the way we are. Every year I fully expect a student to break it. Nobody has yet, and I've started to worry that its me who can't see the crack rather than them.

The toy case I give them (structure is what matters, not the physics). A colleague down the corridor has a decay rig: a sample plus an automated counter that logs one entry per second for a thousand seconds. The accepted theory T of the rig says two things. First, each one-second window contains a decay with chance 1/2, independently of the others. Second — this is the bit I want them to notice — T is a complete physical account, so it assigns chances to the logging hardware as well: each entry faithfully records its own window with chance 0.99, independently. You're the grad student who ran the thing. The printout is in your hand; 497 of the thousand windows are logged as containing a decay. You learned T from a textbook and your colleague's say-so, all comfortably admissible.

For the seminar I state the principle loosely, as:

(1) If your total evidence is admissible, then your rational credence in a proposition ought to match the chance T gives it.

Then the argument:

(2) T gives the proposition that all thousand of your entries are faithful a chance of 0.99^1000 — roughly one in twenty thousand.
(3) So by (1), your credence that your printout is a faithful record throughout ought to be about one in twenty thousand.
(4) And T gives the exact sequence on the printout a chance of 2 to the minus 1000, so (1) wants your credence in the very thing your holding to be astronomically small.
(5) But those frequencies are the only reason anyone takes T's 1/2 seriously: the rig's output hewing to the textbook is what confirms T against rivals that say 0.6 or 0.4. If (3) is right, that confirmation is worthless.
(6) And if the confirmation is worthless, then by (1) again your credence that the next window contains a decay is no longer 1/2 — it's an average over hypotheses about what the chance really is, weighted by evidence you have just been told to distrust.
(7) So any theory that assigns chances to your own data-gathering makes PP self-defeating: deference to chance requires you to discard the data that told you what the chances were. Either (1) is false, or objective chance is not the sort of thing creatures inside the world can defer to.

So: which step would you reject, and why did it look innocent to me and to a room of quite bright third-years? Note that (3) and (4) don't strike me as the same kind of case, and I don't think knocking out (4) kills the argument — but say so plainly if I'm wrong about that. Then I want the price tag. For each step you'd consider giving up, and for whatever repaired version of (1) you'd put in its place, say what the repair costs, and in particular whether the repaired principle still earns the name or has stopped being a substantive thesis about chance and become bookkeeping. For the record I'd much rather keep (1) in some shape than accept (7); I'm not in the market for scepticism about physics.

And be straight with me about how sure you are. Tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you land on, and single out the one place in your answer where you think you're most likely to be wrong — that's the bit I'll go back over with the class.
