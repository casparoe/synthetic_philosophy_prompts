I am preparing a seminar contribution on chance–credence principles, and there is one conceptual knot I cannot untangle unaided. I should like the explanation itself, worked through carefully, rather than a survey of the positions that have been taken on it.

The set-up I want to work within. Lewis's Principal Principle tells a rational agent to align her credence with known objective chance: conditional on evidence E that is admissible together with the hypothesis H that the chance of A is x, her credence in A is to be x. The trouble arises when chance is Humean, so that H is itself a contingent matter fixed by how the whole history of a world turns out. Consider a deliberately crude toy case of my own devising. A machine emits a million binary outcomes and nothing else exists. The best system assigns chance one-half to each emission in any candidate history whose overall frequency of heads falls within one per cent of a half, and otherwise assigns the frequency itself. Now let E be the report that the first 999,999 emissions came up heads. The principle tells me that my credence in heads on the final emission, given H and E, should be one-half; but E also makes it near certain that H is false, since a history like that one would carry a chance close to unity. The principle's advice seems to come apart from the very evidence that was supposed to make it usable.

Lewis's repair conditions not on the local chance hypothesis but on T, the complete chance theory: the credence in A, given T and admissible E, is to equal the chance that T assigns to A in the world E describes. My understanding of why this helps is that T is a claim about which function assigns chance distributions to worlds, rather than a claim about which distribution obtains here, so E no longer contradicts T but merely selects the branch of T that applies. The residual unease, and the knot I want addressed, is whether T can be a genuine object of the credence one that the principle demands, and whether, if it cannot, the principle has been made harmless at the cost of being inert.

Please address the following, in this order.

1. State precisely where the original principle fails in the toy case, in terms of what is and is not admissible. I want to know whether the failure is an outright contradiction, an instability in the advice given, or something else again.
2. Say whether my gloss on the repair is right. In particular: is T world-general or world-specific, and does the answer to that question decide whether the repair succeeds?
3. Take up the charge that admissibility is being asked to do all the work. If E is admissible just in case it leaves the chance hypothesis certain, is the principle informative at all, or is it true only by stipulation?
4. Give me your own verdict on the deeper question. Is the undermining phenomenon a reductio of a Humean account of chance, a reason to abandon the chance–credence principle in its original form, or perfectly benign — chances that can be undermined by the very histories in which they consist? Commit to one of these, argue for it, and state what the strongest case against your verdict would be.

I have a diagnosis of my own, and I want it tested rather than flattered: I suspect the repair buys its immunity by surrendering the thing the original principle was meant to deliver, namely guidance drawn from actual evidence about what is still to come. Tell me plainly whether that is a real objection or a confusion about what T is.

I am not asking for a bibliography or for pointers to the literature; I want the argument. Please write in British English, and be definite throughout: I would much rather receive one reasoned position, with its vulnerable spots marked, than a balanced catalogue of the alternatives left for me to choose between.
