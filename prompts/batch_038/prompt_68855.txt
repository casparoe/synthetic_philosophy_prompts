DRAFT

[1. Two claims, not one]

The best-system account bundles two things that should be pulled apart. First, a supervenience claim: which laws hold depends on nothing over and above the distribution of local matters of particular fact across the whole history of the world. Second, a systematization claim: that dependence runs through a simplicity/strength ranking over candidate deductive systems, and the laws are whatever theorems the winner has. Almost all the heat in the debate is directed at the first claim. I think the more interesting pressure is on the second, and that both sides of the debate need something like it whether they admit it or not. The question I want to organize the paper around is not "do the laws supervene on the mosaic" but "what does the ranking range over."

[2. A rolling window]

The global ranking has a consequence Humeans tend to state and then shrug at: whether a given generalization is a law right now depends on the entire mosaic, including parts of it billions of years in our future. A law can be made true by events nobody will ever witness, and adding enough far-future mess can retroactively demote something that looks like a law from here. That's not just odd, it has a cost. It means the account can't say what the laws are *for* an agent embedded in the mosaic at a time, and it makes the confirmation story look like luck: why should the part of the mosaic I can get at be so systematizable, when systematizability is a property of the whole thing?

My proposal: index the ranking. Fix a window parameter Δ and a time t, and let the best system at t be the system that best trades off simplicity against strength with respect to the mosaic restricted to a Δ-sized neighborhood of t. Two variants, and I'm not sure which I want: a centered one, where the neighborhood extends both before and after t, and a backward-looking one, where it only extends into t's past. The backward-looking variant is fully non-futuristic — lawhood at t depends only on what's already there at t.

Consequences I like. Laws can be born: a generalization enters the best system for a window once enough instances accumulate to make it worth the complexity cost. Laws can die: exceptions accumulate until carrying the generalization costs more than dropping it. What looks like a violation is often just a death rattle, and what looks like a miracle-free world is often a window that hasn't yet earned its generalizations. So the account doesn't need to explain away violations so much as relocate them.

Cost I'll state honestly: Δ is a free parameter, and different Δ deliver different laws at the same t. My defense is that the standard account already has a free parameter — the exchange rate between simplicity and strength — and that adding one more doesn't obviously make things worse. A more ambitious move: set Δ by the characteristic timescale of the processes the system is meant to systematize, which makes Δ relative to a level of description and turns the account into a scale-relative pluralism. I don't know whether that's a solution or a surrender.

[3. Slack, or degrees of nomic strength]

Membership in a best system is binary, but there's a scalar hiding behind it: how much the input would have to change before the generalization falls out. Call this the slack of a law — roughly, how much counter-instance, or how much added simplicity cost elsewhere, the window could absorb before the generalization stops making the cut.

Three things this is supposed to buy. (a) Degrees of necessity without adding anything to the ontology: some laws are more necessary than others because they'd take more of a mess to dislodge. (b) An account of ceteris paribus laws: a cp-law is a low-slack generalization that keeps surviving because the window keeps re-supplying the same idealization conditions, so the exceptions never land inside the window in a way that costs it its place. (c) An account of why the special sciences look lawlike despite their exceptions: their generalizations can have high slack relative to their own window and level of description while having low slack globally, which is exactly what you'd expect if slack is window-relative.

[4. What bears the ranking]

Now the other side. Necessitation views say that what makes it a law that Fs are G is that an Armstrong-style relation of nomic necessitation holds between F-ness and G-ness, and that this relation explains the regularity rather than summarizing it. Fine. But the space of candidate relations isn't sparse, and if the view is going to say which ones are actually instantiated without making the whole arrangement a brute coincidence, it needs a criterion. And any criterion that decides which necessitation structure a whole world has is going to look like a global ranking of candidate structures by something in the neighborhood of economy and fit. So the anti-Humean needs a best-systems-style criterion too; theirs just ranges over necessitation structures instead of axiom systems.

If that's right, then the quidditism objection — that the necessitation relations could be permuted around without changing the mosaic, so they aren't doing any work — and the underdetermination objection — that the mosaic doesn't single out a unique best system — are the same complaint aimed in two directions. In both cases a global ranking is being asked to individuate, and in both cases the natural reply is that it only ever had to order. I want to argue that the rolling-window account is the cleanest Humean way of cashing that reply, because at a window the ranking has a definite job with definite limits, and the leftover underdetermination is precisely what slack measures.

END DRAFT

Some specific things I want your take on:

1. My biggest fear is that [2] isn't metaphysics at all. If lawhood at t depends only on the mosaic in t's backward window, have I just described an epistemology of laws — what's reasonable to believe given the evidence so far — while calling it lawhood? Is there a version of the view that's genuinely about what the laws are and not about what we should say, or should I concede it's an epistemic account and retitle?

2. Does [3] double-count? The best-system ranking already trades off simplicity and strength, and slack is just a fact about that trade-off, so maybe I'm dressing up something the account already contains. If slack is going to be a real addition, what does it have to explain that the binary ranking can't?

3. Is [4] fair to the governing view, or a strawman? I can imagine a necessitation theorist saying the criterion for which relations are instantiated isn't a trade-off at all — it's necessary and knowable a priori, so there's no ranking to compare with mine. Does my argument survive that response, and if not, is [4] worth keeping?

4. Flag anything where I've misdescribed the standard positions, or where I'm using a term in a way that clashes with how it's normally used. I'd rather hear it now than at the talk. Also: does the slack idea smell like something that's already been done, and if so what existing distinction does it collapse into?

5. Which half is stronger, [2] or [3]/[4]? If this has to be a 4,000-word paper I'll have to cut one, and I'd like to know which one a referee would miss less.

6. Is anything here obviously desk-rejectable — a claim I'm making that's just false, or a framing that announces the paper doesn't engage with something it has to?
