I chair the board of a small nonprofit that runs evening drop-in peer-support groups for adults in their twenties and thirties. We are not a clinical service: the groups are led by members rather than therapists, and we employ two part-time staff who handle scheduling, safety, and referrals to outside clinicians. I have no academic training in any of this — I came to the board as a former member of one of the groups and stayed — but over the past two years I have watched something develop that I cannot think my way out of, and I have to put a recommendation to the other six board members next Thursday. The development is this. Around two years ago a term arrived in our rooms that used to belong to the clinical literature on trauma and now circulates freely in online self-diagnosis spaces; call it complex trauma for the purposes of the memo. When it first appeared, one or two members used it. Now close to half of new intakes volunteer it in their opening sentence, and they describe their own histories in the vocabulary of the short checklists they found online, several of them saying some version of "I didn't know it had a name, and once it had one, everything fell into place." Our staff are split, and the split has nothing to do with whether the category is a legitimate one in psychiatry — the board has burned two meetings on that question and gotten nowhere, so I would ask you to set it aside entirely. One staff member thinks this is the best thing that has happened to our members: a name reduces shame, makes a person legible to herself, and speeds her toward help. The other says she can no longer get a member to describe her own experience except through the checklist, that our groups have become a place where people hand each other a script, and — this is the part that has stuck with me, and it is my own observation as much as hers — that because newcomers learn the term from veterans in the room rather than from a clinician, the better we are at mutual support, the faster the process runs. That last point seems to me to defeat the usual reassurance, which is that a good description merely fits what was already there.

I have been turning this over on my own for months. Years ago I read a philosopher of science, Ian Hacking, who wrote about psychiatric classification and about the way a category loops back and alters the very people it was supposed to describe, so that the classification has to be revised to catch up with them, and so on. I would not claim to have understood much of it, but two things I worked out for myself seem worth stating, because I think they are what our arguments keep missing. First, the board collapses two questions into one: whether a category accurately captures what a person already is, and what happens to a person once she possesses the category. One half of the board answers the first question and the other half answers the second, and each believes it has refuted the other. Second, I do not think declining to use a term is a way of standing outside the second question. Our silence would have its own effects on the people in the room, and I do not want a memo that treats non-classification as a neutral default that needs no justification. There is also a practical pressure: our principal funder has begun asking for standardized intake categories in its reporting, so "we simply stop naming things" may not be available to us even in principle.

Please draft the short memo I will circulate to the board before Thursday. The decision in front of us is which of four policies to adopt: (a) use the term openly in intake and in our outreach materials, on the grounds that meeting people in their own language is the whole point of peer support; (b) permit it internally but strip it from anything we publish; (c) decline to use it and train staff to redirect members toward plainer descriptions of their own; or (d) use it, subject to a standing requirement that before any category is entered on an intake form the staff member records at least one formulation of the person's difficulty in the person's own words, and that this formulation, not the category, is what the group leader receives. I am drawn to (d) and would like it argued for seriously rather than waved through, but I want you to recommend whichever option actually survives your analysis, even if it is not that one. The memo should give reasons that both halves of our staff could engage with rather than talking points; should take up the strongest case for the option you reject, including the possibility that my shaping worry is an old panic about contagion dressed in newer vocabulary; should say what we could realistically observe over two or three years that would tell us whether the policy is working, given that we cannot run a controlled study and cannot stand outside the loop to measure it; and should be honest about scope, since whatever we decide has to be carried out by two part-time employees and a rotating set of volunteers. No references or reading list — nobody at this table will have read the sources, and I will bring whatever I can find myself. Put the recommendation on the very first line, ahead of any background, and do not restate my situation back to me; then give the reasoning. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if a board member raised it on Thursday, should make you withdraw the recommendation, and where in your own reasoning you think it is most likely to go wrong.
