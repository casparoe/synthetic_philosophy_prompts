So, day job: I cover neuroscience and consciousness for a general-interest magazine. Once a year the journalism school next door to my office lets me run a small required seminar for their second-years, unofficially called "how not to be taken in by mind-brain stories." These kids are bright and they've all done internships, but they've all soaked up the same pop-sci reflex I spend half the term trying to pry loose: the idea that pain is "just a judgement" or "all in the head," usually deployed in the same paragraph where they use the words sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational without knowing what work those labels are actually doing. The final is a take-home with a 48-hour window and two questions; I want you to write the first one, on pain asymbolia. It has to be a question they cannot pass by reciting the two-pathways story or by paraphrasing the phrase "they feel the pain but it doesn't bother them."

The question should hang on a case I've built for this purpose. Treat it as a constructed composite, not a real patient — I stitched it out of the case-report literature and then deliberately made some of the details ambiguous, and I want the ambiguity to be where the work happens. Call her D.: 47, three years post-stroke, lesion to the left posterior insula and the adjacent parietal operculum. Her discrimination is intact: she localises a noxious stimulus correctly, calls a pinprick a pinprick, rates a burn on her forearm "six out of ten, burning, right here," and her skin conductance to noxious heat is preserved. She doesn't wince, withdraw, guard, or ask for anything; during the exam she giggled when pricked. Asked to describe how the pain feels she says it isn't unpleasant exactly, "more like loud music through a wall — you know it's loud, it doesn't get to you." Now the mixed stuff, and this is the part I want the question to turn on: she sits on a hard chair for two hours without shifting and has developed a pressure sore, and she gave up cooking after the third burn; but she takes her blood pressure tablets religiously, agreed to surgery on a fractured wrist without being pushed, wears a helmet when her daughter tells her to, and will take an analgesic if one is handed to her — she says it helps — though she has never once requested one, and when asked why she took it she says "well, you offered." She also reports that before the stroke she dreaded the dentist, and that now she goes without dread but would "rather not have the drilling, if its all the same to you."

What I want the question to force out of them: whether D. is best read as someone whose pains are still bad for her but who has lost the capacity to be moved by that badness, or as someone whose pains have stopped being bad for her altogether — and they have to say what the difference between those two readings really amounts to, what would discriminate them, and why her own report can't just settle it. There's a live dispute in the background about whether asymbolia is a narrow, pain-specific deficit or a symptom of a broader indifference to one's own body, and my students have seen it in a handout I wrote, so the question can lean on that framing without naming anyone. I also want the practical edge, because these are future reporters and one of them will end up writing about pain clinics: if D.'s pains aren't unpleasant, does she have any claim on analgesia, and does the answer change if she's the sort of person who'll sit on a pressure sore for an afternoon? Please give me the question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that says specifically what separates an A from a B by naming the moves each has to contain, plus four or five mistakes that should cost marks and roughly how many. No bibliography or citation list anywhere — I don't want names standing in for argument — and please don't let the model answer fizzle into "it depends which theory of unpleasantness you prefer." It should actually pick a reading and argue that D. is better evidence for it than for the rival.
