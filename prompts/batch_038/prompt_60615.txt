I work part time on the patient-communications side of a hospital genetics clinic — basically I write the plain-language text that goes out with test results — and there's a fight brewing that I'm suposed to have settled by Friday. We're rolling out one new template that has to cover three very different kinds of result: a pharmacogenomics variant that changes how someone metabolizes a common blood thinner, a variant in a breast cancer gene, and a polygenic risk score for type 2 diabetes. The medical director wants a single consistent voice across all three. Our resident philosopher-in-training — a nurse with a masters in bioethics who reads way more than her job requires — won't sign off on any of it. Her objection is to the words carries information about, instructions for, and codes for. Her position is that DNA doesn't carry or code anything, it's a molecule that a cell gets used by, and that talking about a code quietly assumes the trait was sitting there the whole time waiting to be read out, which she thinks is precisely the picture that makes patients react to results so badly. The director thinks the opposite: that the informational talk isn't a metaphor at all but a plain description of what a sequence actually does, since the sequence makes a specific difference to what gets built, and calling that a code is no more figurative than calling a dial on a thermostat a setting. He also says if we strip the word information out of everything we'll look like we're hiding the science.

What I can't put my finger on is that neither of them seems to notice the three results aren't the same sort of thing, and I have a feeling that's where the whole argument actually lives, but every time I try to say it out loud I get steamrolled by one of the two speeches above. For the blood thinner result the chain from sequence to how a drug gets handled is pretty short and pretty reliable. For the breast cancer one we're reporting a lifetime percentage, and I honestly don't know what it would mean to say a stretch of sequence codes for a number like that. And the polygenic score is something we compute from thousands of spots in the genome plus a bunch of assumptions about which reference population we're comparing you to, which feels nothing like receiving a message and everything like being placed on a curve.

So: which of my two colleagues is closer to right, or is this actually three separate arguments wearing a trenchcoat and pretending to be one? Tell me what the word information is doing in each of the three cases and whether it's doing the same work in all of them, or whether one of them survives the nurse's objection and the others don't. Then, because I do have to ship something, give me the strongest phrasing I can honestly defend for each of the three results — plain English, no hedging mush that reads like a legal disclaimer — plus one or two sentences I can hand the director explaining why a maximally consistent template would have to be wrong about at least one of them. No reading list, I just want the reasoning and the wording.
