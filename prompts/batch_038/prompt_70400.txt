My roommate's writing a dissertation on when pain is bad for the person having it, and she keeps using me as a guinea pig for her cases. Her verdicts so far:

- Woman with an insular lesion, textbook pain asymbolia: says the pinprick is sharp and definitely in her left hand, says it doesn't bother her, and leaves her hand right where it is. Her pain isn't bad for her.
- Woman in labor with no epidural: the contractions are agony, she's begging for them to stop, and afterwards says she'll never do it that way again. Bad for her.
- Masochist who pays good money for it and enjoys every minute: not bad for him.
- Chronic pain patient put on high-dose opioids: says the pain is just as intense as it was, she just doesnt mind it anymore. Not bad for her.
- Patient under general anesthetic with withdrawal reflexes and a stress-hormone spike in his labs: not in pain at all, so the question doesn't come up.
- Her grandfather, late-stage dementia, can't tell anyone anything, but winces, guards his hip, and won't put weight on it: bad for him.

The case she's presenting next week is a patient who during a procedure reports sharp, exactly localized pain, says it isn't unpleasant and doesn't want it stopped — but afterwards calls it horrible, refuses to have the procedure again, and says she knew at the time that it was awful even though it didn't feel awful.

What does she say? I want the machinery more than the yes or no: which rule she's actually running, which other rules fit all six of her verdicts just as well, what each of those gives you on the new case, and which one you'd bet on and why. And if two of the rules never come apart anywhere above, name one extra case that would split them. Please answer in British English.
