I am in the third year of a philosophy degree and currently taking a module on the philosophy of scientific reasoning; my assessed essay is on the underdetermination of theory by evidence. Before I start writing it I would like to establish where my own view actually sits, because I have noticed that I answer questions about underdetermination differently depending on how they are framed. Could you therefore design a diagnostic instrument for this debate — a decision tree together with the questions that feed it — which sorts a person into a position on the basis of what they say about cases rather than on the basis of any label they claim for themselves?

Please construct the logical space first and populate it with names afterwards. The distinctions I want represented as branch points are these. First, whether the rivals are separated only by the evidence we happen to have, or by even all possible evidence — the division Laudan pressed between transient and permanent underdetermination. Second, whether the problem arises holistically, from the impossibility of testing a hypothesis without auxiliary assumptions, or contrastively, from the existence of rivals equally well confirmed by one and the same total evidence. Third, whether the rivals in question are ones we can presently write down and compare, or ones nobody at the time thought of. Fourth, whether anything over and above evidence can break the tie — theoretical virtues, pragmatic considerations, the norms of a scientific community — and, if so, whether those resources are taken to be truth-conducive or merely expedient. Fifth, whether the lesson is confined to empirical science, or is allowed to constrain how much metaphysical weight a successful theory may carry.

The instrument should contain no more than ten decision nodes and between eight and twelve leaves, and it should be usable by a second-year student who has not read the literature: phrase each node as a scenario or as a claim to accept or reject, never as a question about which ism one belongs to. Order the nodes as you think best for discrimination, and say why you chose that order. The leaves must include Quine's globalised holism; Duhem's restricted treatment of the problem in physics; Laudan's deflationary response; a Bayesian response resting on the convergence of posteriors under further evidence; epistemic and ontic structural realism, if your tree can genuinely tell them apart, and if it cannot, say so rather than merging them silently; constructive empiricism; instrumentalism; the pessimistic induction and Stanford's argument from unconceived alternatives, with an explicit verdict on whether these are two distinct leaves or one; a view on which warrant attaches to communities of inquirers rather than to theories; and Feyerabend's permissiveness, if you think it earns a place.

Four kinds of case must appear among the questions. At least one must involve formulations that are intertranslatable rather than genuinely rival — matrix mechanics and wave mechanics is the obvious candidate — since the tree needs a node that prevents a mere reformulation from being scored as underdetermination. At least one must be a case in which the theory eventually accepted was not among the alternatives anyone had considered. At least one must be a case of transient underdetermination later settled by new evidence. And at least one must be purely local, such as the infinite number of curves compatible with finitely many points.

The feature I most want, and which I do not think is standard in treatments of this debate, is a scope-mismatch analysis. For each leaf, state whether that position predicts a person will answer the global and the local questions the same way: a Quinean ought to be uniform, whereas someone who thinks transient underdetermination is routinely resolved by extra-evidential considerations may be pluralist about the global case while treating curve-fitting as settled. Design at least two questions whose only job is to expose such a mismatch, and treat a mismatch as a diagnostic result in its own right rather than as an inconsistency to be tidied away.

Alongside the tree itself, please supply: a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves; a list of the pairs of leaves your tree cannot really separate, with a note on what further question might separate them; and the unoccupied cells of the space — combinations of answers that are coherent but that nobody defends — each with a diagnosis of whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has explored it. Write the whole thing in British English. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or suggestions for further reading; I want the instrument. Where you attribute a view to a named philosopher, keep to the level of generality you can state with confidence, and flag anything you are unsure of rather than asserting it.
