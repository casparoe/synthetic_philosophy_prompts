**The question, as it appeared on the exam:**

> Question 3 (of 4). Short answer, 400–500 words, forty minutes, closed book. Explain the distinction between ψ-ontic and ψ-epistemic treatments of the quantum state, and state precisely what would have to be true for a view to count as ψ-epistemic rather than merely informal or anti-realist about ψ. Then say what the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem does and does not establish about the reality of the wavefunction, and classify two interpretations you have read about this term. Close with two or three sentences explaining, to a reader with no physics, what is and what is not at stake in the dispute.

**The student's answer:**

> The distinction is drawn inside the ontological-models framework, which assumes that a system has some underlying physical state λ, whether or not quantum theory tells us what it is, and that λ fixes the probabilities of measurement outcomes. A quantum state ψ is then treated either as a state of knowledge about λ or as part of the description of λ itself.
>
> A view is ψ-ontic when distinct pure states correspond to disjoint distributions over λ: given λ, there is at most one ψ the system could have. A view is ψ-epistemic when at least two distinct pure states give distributions over λ that overlap, so that some λ is compatible with more than one ψ, and the quantum state therefore carries information not already contained in the physical state.
>
> The Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem was widely reported as proving that the wavefunction is real. What it shows is narrower: if systems prepared independently have independent physical states, then no ψ-epistemic model can reproduce the predictions of quantum theory. The result depends on that preparation-independence assumption, and it depends on there being an underlying physical state at all. QBism and textbook instrumentalism offer no such state, so they are not ψ-epistemic in the technical sense and are not refuted by the theorem; they simply decline to play.
>
> Among the interpretations that do play, the Everett interpretation is ψ-ontic: the universal wavefunction is the whole ontology, so there is nothing left over for it to be knowledge about. Bohmian mechanics looks superficially ψ-ontic — the wavefunction is a real object, evolving by the Schrödinger equation and pushing the particles around — but it is a hidden-variable theory, and in a hidden-variable theory the quantum state can only be a statistical summary of one's ignorance of the hidden variables: the particle positions are what there is, and ψ is the nomological ingredient telling them how to move. On the taxonomy above, then, Bohm is ψ-epistemic.
>
> What would rescue a ψ-epistemic view from PBR is a denial of preparation independence: the physical state of one system would have to be correlated with that of another, however far apart and however separately prepared they were. That is a kind of action at a distance, or else a conspiracy about how laboratories happen to be arranged. So the theorem leaves the epistemic camp where Bell's theorem left the local hidden-variable camp — not refuted, but owing an explanation.
>
> For a general reader, one analogy: ψ is like a chart produced by a weather centre. The chart is not the atmosphere, but neither is it arbitrary; two centres can issue different charts of the same air mass, and both can be perfectly serviceable. A ψ-ontic view would be a chart so complete that no other chart of the same air mass could be equally correct. The two camps make the same predictions for any experiment we can currently imagine running, so what divides them is a question about what a theory is for, not about what happens next.
