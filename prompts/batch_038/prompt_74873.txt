Last term I helped run a small workshop on measuring wellbeing and it went badly in an instructive way: two philosophers, a couple of affective-neuroscience people, and a health economist who lives inside survey instruments and QALY-style numbers, and for fourty minutes everybody said "pleasure" while meaning three different things. One meant a felt tone, one meant whatever the subject would choose more of, one meant a valence dimension in some core-affect model, and nobody noticed until we were deep into a fight about experience machines. So for the next edition I want to open with a sorting instrument that shows people which account of pleasure they're already committed to, before anybody starts arguing.

What I want is a decision tree — a flowchart is fine — with maybe seven or eight branch questions and eight to ten leaves. Every branch has to be a concrete situation or a first-person question that a neuroscientist or an economist could answer without having read any philosophy, and none of them can hint at where the answer sends you: no "do you think pleasure is a sensation?", that's just asking for the label. Cases I definitely want worked in somewhere: the identical glass of cold water that is glorious on a July afternoon and faintly disgusting the same day in January; the person who says the pain is still there but it doesn't bother them anymore, and what your tree does with them; someone who sincerely wants an experience to keep going while denying they enjoy it, plus the mirror case of enjoying something they'd sooner end; being pleased about a friend getting a job, where there seems to be no sensation at all; a drug-induced high with no object whatsoever; the person who takes pleasure in the fact that they're taking pleasure; and one about two pleasures — a good pun and a deep-tissue massage — where the subject reports no introspectible common ingredient but insists both were pleasant.

The leaves should be the real positions, named where they have names and marked "unnamed" where nobody actually holds them: the single-common-feel view; family resemblance or disjunctivism about hedonic qualities; desire- and preference-based reductions; higher-order-attitude accounts; the imperative or "more of this" content account; attitudinal/propositional hedonism à la Feldman, where pleasure isn't a feeling at all; the natural-kind or property-cluster view on which pleasures are one biological kind without one shared phenomenology; and full deflationism, where "pleasure" gets dropped from wellbeing theory altogether. Build it out of the logical space rather than out of the roster of names — I want to see which combinations of answers are available and unoccupied, each with a line on whether that empty cell is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just nobody's thought of it yet. Be honest about which pairs of leaves your tree can't really separate; my suspicion is the imperative account and the higher-order account will give identical answers to everything I can think of, and I'd rather you say so then invent a fake discriminating case. No bibliography or reference list, I'll add those myself. Finally, two things about your own confidence: tell me how sure you are that these leaves are genuinely distinct positions rather than one view wearing different hats, and single out the branch question you're least certain actually splits anything — the one where you suspect you've manufactured a distinction no real respondent will feel.
