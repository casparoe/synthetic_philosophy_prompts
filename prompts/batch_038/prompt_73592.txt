Getting dragged onto a committee at my sister's city government has turned into a philosophy problem, and I want the spec in front of me before I read one more definition of corruption. Context: they're writing an integrity code covering procurement and permits, and three of us were asked to say what should actually count as a violation. Sounds trivial right up until you write the cases down, because half of them run through customs nobody around here thinks of as corrupt at all, and the retired judge on the panel kept insisting that if it's legal it can't be corrupt — which I'm pretty sure is wrong but I can't articulate why. And there are two things supposed to come out of this that I think pull against each other: a code an auditor can apply to a named person and get a determinate answer out of, and something general enough that a research group could use it to compare districts and countries without just exporting our public/private distinction onto places that never had one.

So what does an account of corruption actually have to deliver to be adequate for either of those jobs? Give me the desiderata — the cases it has to come out right on, the distinctions it's not allowed to collapse, the things it has to stay consistent with — with the motivating case or consideration behind each, and be blunt about which of them are real requirements and which are just the house style of one camp. Here's the pile I can't sort:

- a department head who awards a paving contract to her brother-in-law after a genuinely open tender at market price, and the roads get built well and on time;
- a village headman who steers state fertilizer toward his kin and toward the households that backed him, where everyone locally would call it dereliction, not favoritism, if he didn't;
- a public hospital where patients hand the porter an envelope to get seen sooner, and the envelopes are a real fraction of what makes the staff show up;
- a mining firm's "community development" grant to a foundation whose board includes the minister's wife, disclosed in the annual report, nothing illegal about it;
- a legislator who takes industry money, votes the industry's way, files all the paperwork, and sincerely believes the policy is right;
- a mayor who never touches a cent himself but has let the entire permits office run on unofficial expediting fees for a decade, because a crackdown would have frozen construction and lost him the next election.

Some of the things I want addressed explicitly, though I'm sure I'm missing others: it can't just be illegality, since that gets the judge's view backwards in both directions; it has to say something about the clean-hands mayor who personally benefits from nothing; it has to tell me whether a purchasing agent taking kickbacks at a private firm is the same phenomenon or a different one that happens to share a word; it has to make room for degrees, since the patient's envelope and the mining grant don't feel equally bad and I want to know if that's an intuition worth honoring; and it can't classify every reciprocated gift as corrupt, or else basically all premodern political life is corrupt and the concept isn't doing any work. Also whether it's allowed to be revisionary — telling people that their whole system is corrupt — or has to stay close to ordinary usage.

Flag any pair of these you think can't be jointly satisfied. My guess is cross-cultural generality plus determinate verdicts plus not smuggling in our norms is where it snaps, but tell me if that's the wrong triple. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and whether any of the intuitions in my pile should be thrown away rather than respected — I have a suspicion about the headman case and I want to know if the suspicion is justified or just squeamishness. Then, briefly, a couple sentences each and no monument-building: how the public-office / abuse-of-entrusted-power account, the public-interest account, and the corruption-as-rotting-a-practice's-own-standards account do against your list.
