My mum went on HRT in about 1989 and stayed on it for something like fifteen years, because her GP told her it would protect her heart and pretty much every woman she knew was doing the same. Then in 2002 that huge American trial — the NIH one, the women's health initiative — got stopped early because the hormone arm was showing more heart attacks, strokes and breast cancer, not less, and overnight the thing she'd been told was preventative turned into the thing that had been quietly harming her. Use collapsed. And then, slowly, the second reversal: it turned out the trial women were way older than the typical new starter, average age around 63 and many years past menopause, so a bunch of people started arguing the results never applied to a 50 year old beginning at the menopause, and now the guidance carves things up by age and by how long since your last period.

I'm not a academic and nobody in my family wants to hear about this anymore, but I went down a rabbit hole and ended up in philosophy of probability, on the reference class problem — the idea that to attach a probability to one single case you first have to say which class of similar cases you're counting it in, and then Reichenbach's rule about using the narrowest class you've got decent statistics for, Hempel's maximal specificity requirement, and the worry that you can always gerrymander a class so it spits out whatever answer you wanted. That's the lens I want. Walk the whole HRT story through it, in both directions.

First the mapping, and be careful about it — say who's assigning the probability, to what, over what class. When the observational work (the big nurses cohort and the rest) said hormone users had much less heart disease, what exactly was the reference class, and what made it look like the right one? The standard diagnosis afterwards was that women who chose HRT were richer, thinner, less likely to smoke, and more likely to see a doctor anyway — so tell me what kind of failure that is in reference class terms. Is that just the gerrymandering problem, or a different animal, given that the trait defining the class was caused by the same things that drive the outcome? And where's the line between a legitimate narrowing and a self-serving one — you can obviously keep adding conditions (takes HRT, non-smoker, sees a doctor regularly, etc.) until the class tells you exactly what you already believed.

Then: what does randomising actually do to the problem? Solve it, or just move it somewhere else? Then the trial's own population as a class — when someone says those results don't apply to a 50 year old, is that a claim the trial class was too wide, too narrow, or just the wrong shape? And when people now single out women within ten years of the menopause, is that Reichenbach's rule working properly or is it motivated rescuing of the old observational number, and how would you tell the difference from the inside? Then my mum specifically, in 1996, one woman being handed a number: is there any fact of the matter about her probability, and does the relative-versus-absolute risk business belong inside this problem or beside it?

Finally, flip it round. Does the episode show the rule is unusable, because you can't know you picked the right class until you already have the answer you were hunting? Or does it show the rule survives with a causal-relevance patch? Or that the frequentist framing was never the right tool for a one-off patient? I want you to be as happy saying where this lens goes quiet as where it bites — don't force it to speak on the bits it can't handle.
