I am a high school student working on an independent research project about the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials, and I keep finding that two objections get blurred together under the single label of victors' justice, even though they seem to be doing different work. The first concerns retroactivity: that crimes against peace were invented for the occasion, so the defendants were convicted of conduct that no law made criminal at the time they performed it. The second concerns the structure of the proceeding: that judges and prosecutors were drawn exclusively from the victorious powers, that only nationals of defeated states ever appeared in the dock, and that comparable conduct by Allied forces was simply never charged.

Could you explain whether these are genuinely independent objections, or whether the second collapses into the first? I especially want to know what the standard reply to the retroactivity charge commits one to. As I understand it, that reply holds that the London Charter did not create new substantive law but declared international law that already existed, and that the real novelty was jurisdictional, supplying a tribunal where no body had previously been competent to try an individual for such acts. If that reply succeeds, does it answer the asymmetry objection at all, or does that objection survive intact because it is about who may prosecute rather than about whether the conduct was criminal? And does it make any difference that the Tokyo Charter was promulgated by the occupying Supreme Commander instead of being annexed to a multilateral agreement among states?

One case seems to sit right between the two objections, and I cannot tell which side of the line it falls on. At Nuremberg, as I understand it, the defense for Admiral Dönitz put into the record written answers from Admiral Nimitz establishing that the United States Navy had waged unrestricted submarine warfare in the Pacific from the opening of the war, and the Tribunal, though it still convicted Dönitz, declined to assess any punishment on account of the submarine-warfare conduct. Was that the Tribunal effectively conceding the asymmetry objection, or an ordinary exercise of sentencing discretion with no bearing on it?

When you answer, please mark where the settled ground ends. I would like to see which parts of this picture historians and international lawyers treat as uncontroversial and which parts remain genuinely disputed, and I would rather you flag a contested reading as contested than present one side of a live argument as though it were the professional consensus.
