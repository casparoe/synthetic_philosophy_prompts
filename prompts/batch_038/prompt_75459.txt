I am drafting a seminar paper on whether there is anything to the idea that history runs in cycles, and before I commit myself to defending or attacking any particular scheme, I want to be clear about what such a scheme would have to deliver in order to count as a theory rather than a suggestive metaphor. The candidates I have in mind span a wide range: Polybius's rotating sequence of constitutions and its degenerative steps; Ibn Khaldun's account of how group solidarity carries a lineage into power and then thins out over roughly three generations; the twentieth-century morphological schemes that treat a culture as an organism-like whole passing through seasons over something like a millennium, and those that treat the civilization as the unit which encounters challenges and either responds or fails to; and, more recently, the quantitative literature on secular cycles and elite overproduction, which tries to make the pattern measurable and even forecastable. I should add that the standard I have in mind is a demanding one: I do not want a scheme that merely makes the past intelligible after the fact, but one that a planning body could act on in advance, since that is the only respect in which a cyclical theory would have an advantage over the linear or ratcheting picture of history, on which accumulated technical knowledge simply does not decay.

Here are some of the things people have demanded of such theories, and I suspect strongly that some of these are genuine requirements while others are just the aesthetic preferences of one camp: predictive precision about the timing of phase transitions; an internal mechanism rather than a correlation across cases; units of analysis that can be identified independently of the periodization the theory itself proposes; compatibility with the fact that the agents in the story are people who could have acted otherwise; immunity to the charge of being true only in hindsight; scope conditions that explain why some societies show no cycle at all; and consistency with the observation that material capabilities do not appear to reset between one cycle and the next. Write the desiderata: exactly five requirements that any adequate cyclical theory of history must meet, drawn from this list or replacing parts of it where you judge that what is being demanded is not a requirement at all but a prejudice.

Present the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one sentence or two each, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph. Each item should name the requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — an actual historical episode, a feature of the schemes above, or a methodological point, as appropriate — and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference particular to one camp, saying which camp where relevant. If one of the five does more work than the others in excluding the schemes I listed, say so within that item; if any two of the five cannot be jointly satisfied, which I expect is the interesting possibility given that the mechanism is supposed to run through the beliefs and loyalties of agents who might come to hold the theory itself, flag the conflict inside the later of the two items rather than in a summary.
