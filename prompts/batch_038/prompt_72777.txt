So here's something I've been chewing on since I moved. Two years ago I relocated from a small country town where basically none of this stuff existed to a big city where all of it does, and I've accumulated a pile of gut reactions about which local customs are actually morally binding and which are just customs, and they will not line up. My roommate, who is doing a poli-sci degree and is insufferable about it, says I'm being arbitrary. I might be. Here's the raw list, roughly in the order I noticed them:

- Not tipping at a sit-down restaurant here: wrong, obviously wrong. Even though I think the whole tipping system is a scam that lets owners pay people less than a real wage, and I've said so out loud at dinner parties. I'd never stiff a server.
- Not tipping the person at the coffee counter where you order on a kiosk and they hand you the cup: totally fine. The little screen swivels around offering 20, 25, or 30 percent and I feel like I'm being shaken down, but pressing no tip costs me nothing morally.
- Tipping the delivery guy who brought my food cold and half-spilled: fine, arguably right. But tipping the one who did a great job feels like a different kind of thing entirely, not just more of the same, and I can't say why.
- Jaywalking at 3am on an empty street, no cars for a block in either direction: fine.
- Jaywalking at 3am on that exact same empty street, but there's a guy standing on the corner waiting to cross: not fine. Same street, same zero risk, and now its off limits. I have no idea what the extra ingredient is tho.
- Sitting down right next to a stranger on an otherwise empty bus instead of a few rows over: I think this is a real violation of something. There's no rule, nobody's harmed, nobody's even clearly insulted. But I feel like I watched someone do it once and my reaction was moral, not aesthetic.
- Back home there's no queue for the bus, it's a scrum and you fight your way on. When I visit I scrum like everyone else and I feel like a mark the whole time. When my friend from home visited me here last month and squeezed in front of two people at a food truck, I was furious at him, way out of proportion to the stakes.
- The pair I genuinely cannot reconcile: in a country where you're expected to slip the border guard a small bill to get your passport stamped, I refused, felt completely clean about it, and would tell anyone to refuse. In that same country I tipped 20 percent at restaurants for two weeks, resenting every cent, and it never once occurred to me to stop. Both are practices I think are bad, both are expected of visitors, both just cost me money, and in neither case does my choice change the practice by a hair. One I felt required to go along with and one I felt required to resist. My roommate says the difference is that one is illegal, but that can't be it, because I'd jaywalk right in front of that guard.

My working theory, which I keep having to abandon, is that a convention only picks up moral force when somebody has formed an expectation that I'll follow it and would be worse off if I don't. That handles the restaurant, the food truck, the scrum, and it explains why the counter screen is different, since nobody expects anything there. But it fails on the bus seat and on the guy waiting at the corner at 3am, where nobody is worse off and nobody even knows, and it seems to tell me I should pay the border guard, since he is definitely expecting it. So I think it's wrong, I just don't know what goes in its place.

What I want: a principle, or a small set of them with an explicit priority ordering, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a stranger and they'd reproduce my verdicts. Tell me which of the cases above is doing the load-bearing work for each clause, so I can see what each clause is buying. Then run it on two I haven't decided yet: (1) a group chat where everyone acknowledges messages with an emoji reaction instead of replying, and I just never react to anything, though I do respond substantively whenever it actually matters; and (2) my building's unwritten rule that you hold the lobby door for someone carrying stuff, which I always follow, but where I think the guy on the third floor who never holds it for anybody isn't doing anything wrong, even though I'd think he was doing something wrong if he took the door from my hand and let it slam on me. And be honest about the tradeoff I'm expecting here: if there's a simple principle that gets most of my list but has to overturn one or two verdicts, I'd rather hear that named as the better deal than a baroque one that saves everything. Which of my verdicts does your principle actually make me give up?
