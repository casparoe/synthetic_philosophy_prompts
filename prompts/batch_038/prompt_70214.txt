**The question has to be self-contained** — candidates may not have read the literature, and the take-home instructions say they mustn't appeal to it, so anything they need gets stated inside the question. Here's the material to build it out of (restate it your own way, tighten it up, but don't change the mathematical content — I've checked the proof sketch and it goes through):

*Setup.* Worlds are assignments of a utility in [0,1] to each of countably many locations, indexed by ℕ = {0,1,2,…}. So a world w is a sequence ⟨w₀, w₁, w₂, …⟩ with every term in [0,1]. A decision theory here is a weak order ≽ on these worlds, represented by a real-valued V.

- (A1) **Weak order & bounded utility.** ≽ is complete and transitive, and every V(w) ∈ [0,1].
- (A2) **Permutation invariance.** For any bijection π of ℕ, ⟨w₀,w₁,…⟩ ~ ⟨w_{π0}, w_{π1}, …⟩. (Locations are labelled by numbers, but nothing hangs on the labels.)
- (A3) **Countable additivity.** There are weights pₙ ≥ 0 with Σₙ pₙ = 1, and for any world and any partition of ℕ into blocks B₁,B₂,…, V(w) = Σₖ p(Bₖ)·V(w|Bₖ) whenever the series converges, where V(w|B) is the value of the world that agrees with w on B and is 0 elsewhere.
- (A4) **Real-valuedness.** V(w) is a real number for every world.

*Toy theorem.* A1–A4 are jointly inconsistent. (Proof sketch, for your own use: for each n let Gₙ be the world that is 1 at location n and 0 elsewhere; let G be the world with G(n) = n. Then G = Σₙ n·2⁻ⁿ·Gₙ is a countable convex combination, A3 forces V(G) = Σₙ n·2⁻ⁿ = ∞, which contradicts A4 and A1. There is a well-known impossibility result in this general vicinity — bounded utility against countable additivity against uniform treatment of the locations — but say nothing in the question itself that requires a candidate to know who proved what.)

*The injected proposal.* Now suppose a theorist offers this escape, which I want you to give a neutral name in the question (something like **Discounting**): keep the worlds, keep utilities in [0,1], keep countable additivity, and define

V_D(w) = Σₙ 2^{-(n+1)} · wₙ

with w ≽ w′ iff V_D(w) ≥ V_D(w′).

The question itself should have three parts:

(a) [8 marks] A technical warm-up. Show V_D(w) ∈ [0,1] for every world, show ≽ is a weak order, and identify *exactly* which assumption of the toy theorem Discounting gives up — naming the hypothesis, not the vibe.

(b) [13 marks] The real crux. A colleague says Discounting is a cheat: the theorem only bites because of the uniform-treatment assumption, so dropping it buys an escape at the price of the very thing that made the problem hard, and besides, no self-respecting ethics discounts *locations* rather than times. Have the candidate assess that, and steer them onto the question that decides it — whether the weighting 2^{-(n+1)} could ever be non-arbitrary, given that the index n is supposed to carry no normative meaning at all. Tell candidates that an answer resting on intuitions about how infinite sums ought to behave gets no credit.

(c) [4 marks] A short, definite-answer coda: can Discounting be adjusted — any weighting scheme you like — so that permutation invariance comes back while real-valuedness, bounded utility and countable additivity stay? Prove your answer.
