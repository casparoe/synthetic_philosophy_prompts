I spent forty years in a geochemistry lab measuring things, retired three years back, and have since fallen down a rabbit hole of old philosophy and economics texts with a Tuesday night group at the public library. This term we did Berkeley's Querist — yes, the bishop, the immaterialist — which is a strange beast: hundreds of "whether..." questions and not one answer anywhere in it. Two bits have stuck with me. He asks "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?", and a bit earlier, whether wealth is not proportioned to industry "and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?" To my ear that's a man saying money has no worth of its own and is just an accounting device whose whole job is to keep people working.

The group has been wedged on this for two months now and I want an outside opinion. Dave, who ran a bank's research department before he retired, holds that Berkeley is a real monetary theorist, a century ahead of the curve: tokens not metal, speed of circulation not the pile of it, and the entire book is an argument that Irish paper credit could cure Irish idleness. Janet, who spent her career on medieval charters, says that's pure projection — Berkeley isn't theorising anything at all, he's writing a pamphlet for 1730s Ireland where coin was scarce and the rents were flowing off to England, and "ticket" is a loose metaphor at most. She has ammunition too, because Berkeley also says things that sound like the crudest quantity theory: more money moving means more industry, and if circulation is reprocally proportional to the quantity of coin then the nation can't be a loser. Every week the two of them trade passages and nobody moves an inch. My own hunch — and it's only a hunch, I want it stress-tested — is that the genre is doing the damage: a "whether" question aimed at persuading a reader isn't a claim anybody is asserting, so they're using the same sentences as evidence for two different things, Dave reading the text for doctrine and Janet reading it for polemic, and neither one can see the other's move.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A verbal one about what counts as having a "theory of money"? A substantive one about what Berkeley believed? Or two questions hiding under a single heading — one about an eighteenth-century text, one about whether credit money really works — with Dave answering the second and Janet the first while both insist they're arguing about the same thing? And tell me straight what would count as progress. In my old life, when two groups fought about what an instrument was measuring, you built a better measurement and the argument died; here the man is dead, he never answered his own queries, and there's no experiment anybody can run. So is there something — a distinction, a reframing, a different sort of evidence entirely — that would actually break the deadlock, or should we admit the question is ill-posed and get on with our tea? Please diagnose it from what the two sides actually say rather than giving me a guided tour of Berkeley scholarship; I'm not going to chase down the secondary literature anyway, so no reading lists or citations, just your reasoning.
