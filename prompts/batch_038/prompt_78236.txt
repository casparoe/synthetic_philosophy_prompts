I am drafting a normative framework for adjudicating whether a given work stoppage is legitimate, intended to be applied in cases where the law of the relevant jurisdiction is silent, ambiguous, or actively hostile to the stoppage — so it cannot simply track legal categories, and it must be usable by a third party (an arbitrator, a solidarity committee, a funder deciding whether to support a strike fund) who has access only to public facts about the dispute. Before I commit to any existing account of the right to strike or the right to organize, I want the specification: the list of requirements any adequate normative theory in this area has to meet in order to serve that purpose. Please write that list.

For orientation, here are the cases my framework will actually have to deliver verdicts on. Treat them as stipulated; do not correct their facts or ask which jurisdiction they are in.

1. A three-year contract contains a no-strike clause. In month twenty a crew walks out over a machine that has maimed two workers; the union leadership, mid-arbitration on the same issue, refuses to authorize the stoppage.
2. Workers at a parts supplier, who have no grievance of their own against their own employer, refuse to ship to a struck plant down the road.
3. The struck plant is the only producer of a treatment without which a class of patients dies within weeks. The workers' leverage comes precisely from that fact.
4. Workers classified in that jurisdiction as independent contractors jointly set their rates. The legal system treats this as price-fixing rather than as protected collective action, although their economic dependence on a single buyer is identical to that of the employees next door.
5. A union holds exclusive bargaining rights over a unit and must, at its own expense and on pain of liability, defend a member who paid nothing, cannot opt out of being represented, and campaigned publicly against the union.
6. The employer's response to a strike is to hire permanent replacements; the union's response is to stoppage the replacements' supply chain.
7. A stoppage is called by union leadership over the objection of a majority of the members; and, in a separate variant, by a minority of members over the objection of leadership.
8. Workers do not withhold labor at all: they work strictly to rule, decline overtime they have always worked, and slow the line. Output falls by a third.
9. The stoppage's stated aim is a change in national immigration policy, not a term of employment; its leverage comes from disrupting production anyway.
10. The employer has broken the law, and the available legal remedy would arrive in four years, by which time the workforce has been dispersed.

For each requirement in your list, tell me which of these cases (or which further case you construct) motivates it, and why it is a requirement rather than a taste. I also want you to say explicitly which of the following distinctions a theory may not collapse, and which of them you think are illusory: an individual worker's liberty to quit from a group's power to withhold labor; withdrawing one's own labor from preventing others from supplying theirs; a right held against the employer from a right held against the state or against the general public; a claim-right that cannot be bargained away from a liberty that can be traded for wages in a contract; having the right from being able to exercise it without ruin; and the worker, the majority, and the union-as-institution as rival holders of the right.

Include requirements of consistency with background facts the theory cannot argue around: that no one in a liberal order may be compelled to labor, so the bare liberty to walk out is uncontroversial and all the interesting work concerns where collective force comes from; that strike leverage is, in general, proportional to harm imposed on people who are not parties to the dispute; that the right to strike is routinely waived by contract in exchange for wages and stability; that unions simultaneously coerce their members (dues, discipline, exclusive representation) and constitute the only counterweight to unilateral employer power; and that a right whose exercise costs the exerciser their livelihood is worth more to workers with good outside options than to those with none.

Most of all, tell me whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that grounding the right in the individual's freedom to dispose of her own labor, demanding that it protect genuinely collective power including action against third parties, and demanding that it not impose serious costs on uninvolved persons cannot all hold at once, because the collective power is generated by exactly those costs — but I want that argued or refuted rather than assumed. Also identify the single requirement that does the most to rule out candidate theories, and say which requirements you think are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of particular camps.

Format: give the requirements as a table with these columns — `id`; `desideratum` (stated so that it could be applied to a stoppage no statute recognizes); `motivating case`; `why this is a requirement`; `status` (non-negotiable / camp-specific / contested); `exclusion power` (what sorts of accounts it rules out and how). After the table, in prose: the joint-satisfiability result, with the argument; then a brief scorecard of three or four leading families of account against the list, no more than a paragraph each — the table is where I want the work done.
