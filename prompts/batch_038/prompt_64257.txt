A friend and I spent most of a Sunday on Wittgenstein's On Certainty and finished the day further apart than we started, because he assembled an argument I could not answer and cannot accept. It is meant to show that hinge epistemology, instead of blocking skepticism, delivers a stronger version of it than the skeptic ever managed — and does so while granting Moore every ordinary thing he claimed to know.

I have reconstructed it as follows; he insisted on keeping the steps separate.

(1) Justification runs through a background: I can have evidence for p only against a body of propositions I neither doubt nor test, because testing any claim requires that others be held fixed.
(2) The propositions held fixed in this way are the hinges, and being a hinge consists in not being evidentially supported within the system one belongs to.
(3) Whatever is not evidentially supported is not known.
(4) So no hinge is known.
(5) If a subject does not know p, then not-p is epistemically open for that subject: nothing she has rules it out.
(6) So for every hinge h, not-h is open to the subject whose hinge it is. That the world came into being five minutes ago, complete with misleading traces, is open to me; so is my being a handless brain in a vat.
(7) Nothing here depends on which system one is in. An astrologer who holds that the stars govern character holds it fixed in the same way, tests nothing against it, and interprets everything by means of it. By (2) through (6), her hinge is not known and its negation is open to her.
(8) If not-h is open to each of us, then neither of us has grounds for preferring our hinges to the other's.
(9) But by (1) the justification of every ordinary belief runs through hinges. If hinges cannot be compared, no body of belief can be compared with any other.
(10) So a theory introduced to explain how certainty is possible, and to expose radical doubt as unintelligible, ends up making every framework equally good and every belief equally unsupported. It proves too much: it is a license for the flat-earther and the conspiracy theorist, issued in the name of the philosophy that was meant to deny them standing.

Two things trouble me, and I cannot tell whether they are two problems or one. I find (10) incredible — not merely unwelcome, but such that I cannot see how anyone who has ever corrected a child's arithmetic could sincerely hold it. And the argument looks self-consuming: (1) is itself a philosophical thesis about justification, which my friend advances as something he has grounds for. If he has grounds, then (1) is evidentially supported and is no hinge, which puts pressure on the account of what being held fixed amounts to in (2). If he has none, then by his own (5) its negation is open to me, and he has given me no reason to follow him.

What I want is not a survey of hinge epistemology but a diagnosis. Name the step you would remove — one step, if one suffices, and the one whose removal costs least. Then take each of (3), (5), and the classification performed in (7), all three of which he considers beyond reproach and none of which I could see my way past, and explain why it looked unobjectionable, what it equivocates on or quietly assumes, and what the position forfeits if it is given up. Three questions follow from that. If you reject (5), do you retain a principled account of when ignorance leaves a possibility genuinely open, or has the notion of an open possibility been retired without replacement? If you reject (3), how do you keep the resulting view from becoming a rival thesis about what hinges are, rather than a refusal to treat them as candidates for knowledge at all — and if it is such a refusal, does that not make the book itself unreadable as argument? And if you reject the move in (7), what distinguishes a hinge from a prejudice that has simply never been challenged, given that the distinction cannot itself be an evidential one?

If I had to choose, I would keep (1) and (2) and pay elsewhere, since without them I can see no account of why evidence ever runs out. But I should like to know the price, and whether I can afford it.
