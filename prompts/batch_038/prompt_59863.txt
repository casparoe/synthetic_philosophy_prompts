I have been revising a paper for a graduate workshop on memory and political obligation, and I would be grateful for comments on the enclosed outline and drafted sections rather than line edits. The workshop is made up mostly of political theorists with one historian of Central Europe, and I am trying to pitch the piece so that it survives both. The thesis I am defending is this: the standing debate over whether a polity owes its past remembrance or forgetting has been conducted almost entirely on the axis of quantity — how much memory, how long, at what intensity — when in fact it belongs on the axis of allocation. Once you ask not how much a state should remember but at which level of a layered polity the various tasks of remembrance should be performed, the familiar opposition between a duty to remember and a case for forgetting largely dissolves, and it dissolves in a way that neither of the two authors I build on ever quite states. I want comments on whether that is a real thesis or a repackaged platitude, and on whether the machinery I borrow to articulate it is sound.

Part I reconstructs the position I mean to complicate. Margalit, as I read him, distinguishes two registers of obligation: an ethical one, thick and particular, owed to those with whom one shares a concrete history, and a moral one, thin and universal, owed to persons as such. Remembering, on his account, is paradigmatically an ethical matter, because it presupposes a shared past and a community for whom that past is constitutive; the demand that we remember certain evils rests on the fact that we were there, or that those who were there are ours. From this he draws conclusions about the political enforcement of memory that I find strikingly modest — that law should as a rule secure the conditions of remembrance rather than settle its content, since coerced memory is not memory at all. Part II turns to Rieff. Where Margalit asks what we owe the past, Rieff asks what regimes of memory look like, and he sorts them into roughly four families according to the disposition a society takes toward its own history: one that has decided the past is finished and no longer instructive, one that commands a single official remembrance and treats rival accounts as treason, one that tolerates a plurality of competing accounts without adjudicating among them, and one that treats memory as a symptom to be managed therapeutically, prescribing closure in the language of health. The claim I lean on hardest is his observation that the regimes most intent on erasing a people begin by erasing its archives: the destruction of the record precedes or accompanies the destruction of the group, because once the documentary trace is gone, denial becomes available. Part III is where I try to earn my keep. Both authors, I argue, assume that the duty to remember must be assigned to a single agent, and then disagree about how heavy a load that agent can bear. Subsidiarity suggests a different move. The principle holds that matters ought to be handled by the lowest, most local competent authority, and that a higher body may intervene only where the lower one genuinely cannot achieve the end; what matters is that its test of competence is about capacity to discharge a function, not about the importance or the subject-matter of the function. Applied to remembrance, this requires disaggregating the duty into at least five distinguishable tasks: gathering testimony from those who can still give it; maintaining the physical and documentary record; marking occasions; interpreting what the record means; and transmitting a settled meaning to the young. These tasks do not have to sit with the same agent, and I think the entire quarrel between remembering and forgetting is an artifact of treating them as one bundle.

Part IV draws the consequences. The Margalit-style argument succeeds, on my account, at the level at which a shared past actually constitutes a we — a village, a denomination, a family, an association of survivors — and it fails at the level of the polity taken as a whole, where there is no single such we. Federalists have long insisted that the union is not a larger municipality but a different kind of thing, with different capacities and different disabilities; the same goes for memory, and the distinctive competence of the national level in this domain is not to fix an interpretation but to hold interpretations open. That yields a two-clause doctrine. Custodial: the national level owes the maintenance and protection of the infrastructure of memory — archives, registers, access to records, the physical sites themselves — because these are precisely the goods that cannot be adequately supplied locally, being vulnerable to local capture and local indifference, and because their destruction is the only form of forgetting that silences memory without ever having to command it. Interpretive abstention: the national level owes no authoritative reading of what the record means, and may not displace the readings generated below, because an official national interpretation that crowds out local ones is a subsidiarity violation in the epistemic register. I think this also lets me split Margalit's ethical/moral distinction rather than accept it whole: the polity does have thick relations, but they run through the layered structure rather than through the center, so the thick duties attach to the constituent communities while the thin duty of the whole is to protect the possibility of thickness anywhere within it. And it explains what is right about the therapeutic case for forgetting — that a community may legitimately close its own archive and decline to reopen it — while showing why that option cannot be generalized into policy: the state may not declare the past settled, because it is not the party who was wounded.

Here is what I specifically want from you. First, accuracy: flag every place where I look like a flat-footed or unfair reader, especially whether I have flattened Rieff's families of memory regimes into something tidier than what he offers, whether I have collapsed distinctions in Margalit that he insists on, and whether the framing claim that both thinkers address themselves primarily to the nation-state and so miss the layered case is fair or a strawman built for my convenience. Second, and more importantly, the three cases I have parked in Part V, on which the whole thing may founder. (1) A diffuse historical injustice — dispossession across an entire territory over generations, with no single site, no bounded archive, and survivors dispersed through every level of the federation. My custodial clause localizes badly here, since the wrong outruns every local unit, yet my abstention clause forbids the national reading that seems called for. I suspect the case forces an exception and I cannot find a non-ad-hoc way to write it in. (2) A national school curriculum that teaches a canonical account of a civil war, including its causes and its moral valence. I want to say this violates interpretive abstention, but I cannot explain how it differs from national curriculum in mathematics or road-safety law, which nobody thinks subsidiarity forbids, and I suspect the difference has to do with whether a domain admits of a settled answer rather than with who is doing the teaching — which may be a different principle from the one I claimed. (3) A federal court's findings of fact in a landmark decision, which become, willy-nilly, the most durable official interpretation of events that the polity ever produces. My doctrine implies the judiciary has been doing memory work all along under a label nobody uses, and I do not know whether to welcome or resist that implication. Third, tell me honestly whether the custodial/interpretive distinction is load-bearing or whether it collapses: one reader of an earlier draft said that maintaining an archive is already a way of interpreting it, since selection, cataloguing, and access rules encode a view of what mattered, in which case my abstention clause either demands nothing or demands the impossible. Fourth, suggest what I should cut. The piece is at roughly 4,600 words and the workshop wants 6,000 at the outside, and I suspect Parts I and II are doing more reconstruction than an audience that knows both authors needs, while Part III's account of subsidiarity is too thin to carry the weight I put on it. If you think the federal apparatus is decoration — that the same argument could be run with any vocabulary of divided authority and the specificity buys me nothing — please say so bluntly, since that is the objection I most expect from the historian in the room.
