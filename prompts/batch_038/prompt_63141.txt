We are roughly eighteen months from a referendum on independence in a post-conflict territory, and our drafting exercise has stalled on the question nobody expected to be hard: who is entitled to be on the register. I am a senior legal adviser to the electoral commission. My team is five lawyers and two statisticians, none of us trained in political theory, and we have been asked to produce a single written procedure that our seven commissioners can defend line by line in public hearings and that an international observer mission could audit against the roll afterwards. The agreement also commits us to running ordinary elections every four years for at least a generation on the basis of the same register, so this is not a one-off founding act that we can bracket as extraordinary.

The advice we have managed to read is largely diagnostic, and the diagnosis is unhelpful in practice. We are told the boundary problem is circular: a people cannot democratically decide who the people are, because any vote about the boundary already presupposes one. Our own drafting reproduces the circle concretely. If we adopt residence, we enfranchise families who arrived under the previous regime's settlement programme and exclude those it expelled; if we adopt descent, we enfranchise a diaspora that has never set foot here and exclude long-settled permanent residents who have; if we adopt the principle that everyone whose interests are affected should have a say, we cannot apply it at all, because whether a given group's interests are affected depends on where the boundary falls, which is precisely what the referendum is supposed to settle. Meanwhile the marginal cases keep arriving on my desk weekly, and each of them is a small, unaudited re-founding of the demos: a stateless household of three generations, a pastoralist community whose grazing lands straddle the proposed line, an emigrant who left at the age of four and has now come back.

So I want the translation rather than the diagnosis. Write a procedure we could actually run: an ordered set of questions to put to each category of claimant, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, which facts or documents settle a question as opposed to merely raising it, and at which step the procedure stops producing an answer and hands the decision to the commissioners because what remains is a question of values rather than of fact. I want it explicit about how the procedure escapes the circularity, or fails to: whether by fixing some prior, non-democratically authorized decision as a starting point, by making the boundary revisable on a stated schedule, by separating the question of who votes from the question of who counts as a member, or by some other move — and what it costs us to escape that way rather than another. Flag every step where a judgment call is dressed up as a rule, including the ones where I would not notice that it was. Then run the procedure on three cases: families brought in under a state-sponsored settlement programme forty years ago, who speak only the metropolitan language and can show no ties elsewhere; residents who fled across the border during the war, have lived abroad for nine years, and now want to return; and a pastoralist community whose recognized grazing lands straddle the proposed boundary and which is formally registered as a national of neither side. Include at least one case where the answer is one I personally will resist — my sympathies run toward including everyone with a life staked in the territory — and explain plainly why it comes out against me rather than accommodating my preference.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. Which of these decisions would remain exposed to the charge of illegitimacy no matter how carefully the register were drawn and how transparently the criteria were published; which objections from the losing side deserve an answer rather than a procedural shrug; and what an honest public defence of our work would have to concede. Where you rely on a distinction that comes from the literature, give it to me in the form the procedure uses rather than as a name and a date — I am not collecting references, I am collecting something my team can run on Monday.
