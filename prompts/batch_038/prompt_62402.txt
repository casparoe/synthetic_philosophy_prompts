You are a philosopher of law who also reads parliamentary manuals for pleasure, and who has thought hard about how a body's rules relate to that body's power to change those rules. Please reason carefully before answering: work the analysis out privately, and give me only the finished list.

I serve as volunteer parliamentarian for a professional society of roughly twelve thousand members, and I have run into features of our governing documents that I know exactly how to operate but cannot make sense of in any deeper way.

The Bylaws. Article XII provides that the Bylaws may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members voting in a referendum, on a proposal from the Board or from a petition signed by five percent of the membership. Its final sentence reads: the provisions of Article I and of this Article XII may not be amended, suspended, or repealed. Article I is a short list of the society's fundamental principles — its name, its purpose, one member one vote, and a nondiscrimination clause. So the amendment rule protects itself, alongside those principles, against every verb anyone in our debates has managed to think of.

Two things happen in practice. At the annual meeting the Assembly routinely suspends a rule of order for the duration of a single meeting on a two-thirds voice vote, and nobody calls that an amendment; last year a member moved to suspend the entrenching sentence "for the sole purpose of taking up" an amendment to Article I, and the presiding officer ruled the motion out of order because the sentence names suspension explicitly — adding, in answer to a point of inquiry, that had it named only amendment, the ruling might have gone the other way. Separately, the Board issues annual interpretations of the Bylaws, the most recent holding that emeritus members who pay no dues count as members in good standing for purposes of the referendum roll; everyone agrees these are not amendments, even though they change who votes, and they lapse unless renewed.

Behind all of this sits the federation we belong to, whose own constitution says the federation may annul any provision of a member society's bylaws that conflicts with it. It has never annulled anything of ours, and Article XII was added in 1998 without objection from them.

Deliverable: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item states one philosophical commitment this arrangement presupposes — a view about where the society's authority to make and unmake its rules comes from, or about what makes one act an amendment and another a suspension or an interpretation — names a rival view that the commitment excludes, and says whether the practice genuinely forces that commitment or merely sits comfortably with it. At least one item must address whether the self-protecting sentence can do the work it appears to do, and at least one must say what would have to change if we stopped treating the assembly that wrote Article XII and the assembly sitting today as the same lawmaker.
