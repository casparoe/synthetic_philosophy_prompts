I'm about three quarters through a novel whose narrator does catastrophe modelling for a reinsurer — someone who spends her working life turning vague dread into decimals. The scene I'm stuck on: she's asked, on the record, for one number, and she gives a range instead, and the book is meant to make that read as integrity rather than evasion. Before I commit to writing it that way I want to know whether it actually is integrity.

So — give me your credence, as a number, that rational belief is sometimes *required* to be imprecise. The claim I mean is the strong one: that there are propositions where carrying a single sharp probability is itself a mistake and not merely a useful fiction, and where a set or interval is the only honest representation of the state of mind. Not the weaker claim that imprecise credences are coherent and sometimes convenient, which I take to be pretty much settled — if you think even that isn't settled, tell me, because it changes what I can put in her mouth.

I want the decomposition, not just the headline figure. Sub-terms with a number and a sentence of justification each, roughly along these lines, though feel free to add or cut: whether any decision rule for a set of priors can both get the agent to act and avoid quietly collapsing back into a single number; how much damage the dilation worry really does — the result that you can be guaranteed to lose precision about some claim whichever cell of a partition you learn you're in — and whether that bites against required imprecision specifically or is absorbable; whether the Ellsberg-style intuitions actually license intervals or only something weaker, like non-additive measures or a claim about how much evidence you have; how much of the whole dispute is verbal, two descriptions of one psychology rather than rival accounts; and what you'd put on the field landing on one side over the next twenty years. Then show me how you're combining them and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to — what single thing, if it went the other way, would flip my character from principled to weaselly.

There's a third position I keep circling and I don't know if it belongs in the decomposition or is a red herring: that width and resilience are separate properties, so a narrow interval resting on thin evidence and a wide one resting on a lot of evidence are different states that neither a sharp number nor a bare interval distinguishes. If she's really occupying that position rather than the imprecise-credences one, say so and tell me what it costs her.

Last thing, and it matters to me: keep the settled apart from the contested. People who work on this disagree about a lot of it, and I don't want to hand a minority view to a character as though it were the received position. Wherever your probability is your own judgement rather then something the literature has more or less agreed on, flag it as such.
