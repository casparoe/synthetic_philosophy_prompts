In three weeks I give a talk at a workshop on the foundations of probability and decision theory, defending a claim about material most of the room will regard as out of place there. The audience is largely formal epistemologists, with two economists who work on Savage-style representation results and two historians of early analytic philosophy who have spent real time in the Ramsey papers. None of them works on Ramsey's late, non-technical writings, and at least one holds that talk of life's meaning has no business inside a theory of rational belief.

My thesis is that the reflections on the meaning of life Ramsey left from his final year — written between the completion of his work on probability and his death at twenty-six — are not the diary of a dying prodigy but a position with recoverable argumentative structure: a melioristic naturalism on which a life's value is constituted by the pursuit of ends we know we will not attain. More contentiously, I argue that this position is continuous with the pragmatist strain in his account of belief as a disposition individuated by its role in action, and that it supplies the normative component his representation theorem deliberately omits.

The argument runs: on Ramsey's view degrees of belief are fixed by their bearing on choice, so the question of which systems of preference are worth having cannot be settled within the formalism; his late reflections answer that question in terms of ongoing activity rather than attainment; hence the life writings and the probability paper address adjacent halves of a single problem.

Prepare a Q&A sheet rather than a defence. I want the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ranked by how much damage each would do, and phrased as a formal epistemologist or an archival historian would phrase them — including the methodological ones about elevating a young man's last notes into doctrine, and about what his Vienna psychoanalysis has to do with any of it. For each: a reply deliverable in two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is soft, and a short list of what I should concede in my first five minutes. Finish by identifying the one reply that has to land for the talk to be worth giving, and the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
