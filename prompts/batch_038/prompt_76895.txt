I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, but I've been reading decision theory on my own because my team is trying to redesign how we settle disputes about architecture and incident response, and that reading led me to Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result: two rational agents with a common prior who come to have common knowledge of each other's posterior beliefs about an event must assign it the same probability. Behind it sits the common prior assumption itself — the idea, roughly, that all rational disagreement is disagreement about information rather than about starting points, so that any apparent difference in priors should be pushed back into differences in what each agent has already absorbed over a lifetime.

I want to test that apparatus against Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men, and I'd like you to tell me whether the film is a dramatization of it or a counterexample to it. I can't work out which, and the standard things said about the movie — reason against prejudice, one man against eleven — don't help me decide.

The puzzle I want pressed: all twelve men hear the same evidence at trial, so if they share a common prior and that evidence is genuinely common knowledge among them, they should walk into the jury room already in agreement. Eleven vote guilty. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the assumption seriously, and that answers these specifically:

1. Which jurors' positions are best read as differences in priors, and which are actually differences in likelihoods — how much weight eyewitness testimony carries, how much a boy from the slums is credited? The old man's theory about the downstairs witness wanting to be noticed, and the bigot's theory about slum children in general, look like neither. Does the film mark these three things apart, or does it collapse them into a single notion of what a man brings into the room?

2. Juror 8's case is built almost entirely on facts that were already public: the supposed uniqueness of the knife, the noise of the elevated train, the downstairs witness's leg and his account of how long it took him to reach the door, the marks a pair of glasses leaves on a nose. He is not disclosing private information; he is making common knowledge salient. Aumann's mechanism, by contrast, is iterated announcement of posteriors. Is the film showing a process the theorem covers, or one it conspicuously fails to cover — agreement driven by argument over evidence nobody had bothered to examine?

3. Read the secret ballot as mechanism design. Juror 8 proposes a private vote after his arguments fail, declines to participate himself, and says he will go along if the other eleven are unanimous; exactly one other man then comes out not guilty. A public vote would have announced an unbroken eleven and left the twelfth little room to be the visible dissenter. Does the film understand that it is protecting the revelation of private information, and does that make the deliberation a case of rational herding being switched off, or something else?

4. The last holdout converts by tearing up a photograph of himself and his son and breaking down, not by an argument. One juror who mostly wants to reach a ballgame switches earlier and is angrily rebuked by another for treating the vote as weightless. Only one late conversion — the most analytically self-possessed juror's, over the eyeglass marks — is clearly evidence-driven. Given that distribution, is unanimity here truth-tracking convergence, or convergence that has come apart from truth-tracking? And does the ending vindicate the common prior assumption (priors were the whole problem, and once one man's collapsed the room agreed) or refute it?

Please commit. Argue for one verdict about the film — what it claims about disagreement, and whether the common prior assumption functions in it as a resource or as a target — rather than laying out both readings and leaving me to choose. If you think my framing is mistaken, say so and argue it: if the prior-versus-information distinction does no real work on this material, or if common knowledge in the technical sense is so demanding that the theorem was never a candidate for describing a jury room, I'd rather hear that defended than accommodated. Then tell me where your own reading overreaches — which claims rest on a contestable interpretation of the theorem, and which scenes you are bending to fit. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations; I want your judgment, not references.
