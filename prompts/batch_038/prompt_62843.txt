I am a third-year PhD student in political philosophy and a member of the bargaining unit of my university's graduate employees' union. Our contract expires in six weeks; the committee has called a mass meeting for tomorrow evening at which we vote on whether to authorize a strike, and if the vote passes we expect to be out for something like three weeks. The meeting will close with short remarks making the case for authorizing and for staying out, and the committee has asked me to give them. The difficulty is that I have been writing a seminar paper all term on collective action and social ontology, and I cannot stop hearing the free-rider problem in the middle of what is supposed to be a straightforward speech. About forty of the people in that room took a seminar in which that problem was the central case, and they will not tolerate being told that their individual body on the picket line is what forces the administration to move. With roughly 1,100 of us in the unit and what the committee believes is a threshold effect on teaching operations, the chance that any one member is pivotal is very small, and I am not willing to build five minutes of a speech on a claim I take to be false.

So the remarks have to supply a reason to authorize and to stay out that survives the concession that no single person's participation is causally decisive. I do not want solidarity invoked as a warm feeling, and above all I do not want the speech to shame anyone. One member has said publicly that she may not be able to walk out at all; she is a single parent whose stipend is already spoken for, the room knows who she is, and the speech must be able to address her without turning her into an illustration. It should concede that the costs of this strike fall very unevenly across the unit and that this is a substantive objection to the committee's strategy rather than a failure of nerve on anyone's part. At the same time it has to land for the people who have never read a page of anything about this: partners and children who came along, undergraduates who helped with the petition drive, two emeriti who will be standing at the back. Whatever distinction carries the weight — between contributing to an outcome and being part of the group that produces it, say, or between what my presence causes and what it tells the other thousand people who are deciding whether to show up — should be stated once in ordinary language and then never named again.

Please write the closing remarks. Before them, give me two sentences saying what you take the reason for staying out to be and why that reason is not defeated by the fact that no individual member is pivotal; I want to be able to check that the causal claim has not simply been smuggled back in under a vaguer word. The speech itself should be no more than 350 words, written to be read aloud by a tired person into a gymnasium with poor acoustics, and it should not end on a chant or a slogan.
