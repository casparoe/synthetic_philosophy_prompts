I am a novelist, and for the past three years I have been living inside the head of a woman who spent her working life building mortality tables for a nineteenth-century life office — a mind formed entirely by ratios over classes, and then broken by one death that the classes did not predict. To get her inner life right I have been reading C. S. Peirce's series "Illustrations of the Logic of Science" in Popular Science Monthly, and I have stalled on the opening sections of the fourth paper, subtitled "The Probability of Induction" (vol. 12, April 1878). I am quoting from an online edition of the magazine in which the typographic fractions have been badly mangled, so I have deliberately excerpted only the prose and one rule, and left out his worked numerical examples. I have not yet reached the later sections of the paper, and I would rather not have you summarise what you believe he goes on to say: I want your close reading of what is actually in front of us, and your judgment about what these moves commit him to.

> WE have found that every argument derives its force from the general truth of the class of inferences to which it belongs; and that probability is the proportion of arguments carrying truth with them among those of any genus. This is most conveniently expressed in the nomenclature of the mediæval logicians. They called the fact expressed by a premise an antecedent, and that which follows from it its consequent; while the leading principle, that every (or almost every) such antecedent is followed by such a consequent, they termed the consequence. Using this language, we may say that probability belongs exclusively to consequences, and the probability of any consequence is the number of times in which antecedent and consequent both occur divided by the number of all the times in which the antecedent occurs. From this definition are deduced the following rules for the addition and multiplication of probabilities:
>
> Rule for the Addition of Probabilities.—Given the separate probabilities of two consequences having the same antecedent and incompatible consequents. Then the sum of these two numbers is the probability of the consequence, that from the same antecedent one or other of those consequents follows.

and then, at the start of the next section:

> The conception of probability as a matter of fact, i. e., as the proportion of times in which an occurrence of one kind is accompanied by an occurrence of another kind, is termed by Mr. Venn the materialistic view of the subject. But probability has often been regarded as being simply the degree of belief which ought to attach to a proposition; and this mode of explaining the idea is termed by Venn the conceptualistic view. Most writers have mixed the two conceptions together. They, first, define the probability of an event as the reason we have to believe that it has taken place, which is conceptualistic; but shortly after they state that it is the ratio of the number of cases favorable to the event to the total number of cases favorable or contrary, and all equally possible. Except that this introduces the thoroughly unclear idea of cases equally possible in place of cases equally frequent, this is a tolerable statement of the materialistic view. […] The great difference between the two analyses is, that the conceptualists refer probability to an event, while the materialists make it the ratio of frequency of events of a species to those of a genus over that species, thus giving it two terms instead of one.

What I need help with:

1. The "two terms instead of one" charge, precisely. When Peirce says the conceptualist refers probability to an event while the materialist refers it to a species-within-a-genus, is he claiming the conceptualist is addressing a different subject matter, committing a category mistake, or merely speaking loosely? What is the second term doing for him — is it a relatum, a reference class, or a rule of inference in disguise?

2. The claim that probability "belongs exclusively to consequences." How does that square with the definition given in the very next clause, which is a ratio of co-occurrences of antecedent and consequent? If probabilities attach to leading principles rather than to happenings in the world, why is the resulting number still computed from frequencies of happenings? Tell me whether there is a real tension here or only an unfamiliar vocabulary.

3. Whether the calculus is neutral. He says the rules are "deduced ... from this definition." Would a degree-of-belief theorist accept the addition rule unchanged? If she would, what is left of the dispute, and does that locate the disagreement in the objects probability is ascribed to rather than in the arithmetic?

4. The remark about "cases equally possible" being substituted for "cases equally frequent." Is that a complaint about vagueness, about circularity — equipossibility already smuggling in a probability — or about something else? How much of the materialistic/conceptualistic contrast rests on it?

5. What my narrator may say about one case. On the strength of these passages alone, is there any proposition about a single, non-repeatable death that this framework licenses? I need to know whether Peirce's account makes the single-case probability false, meaningless, or merely derivative, because those three give my character three entirely different kinds of crisis.

6. The classification worry. If probability is a ratio of a species to a genus, everything turns on which genus one picks, and the frequency facts alone do not seem to settle it. Does anything in these excerpts constrain the choice of class, or is it left to the inquirer's interests — and if the latter, has he let the conceptualist back in through the rear door? Give me the strongest form of that objection and the best reply available from within the quoted text.

Finally, a sentence-by-sentence rendering of the second extract in plain English, and a short list of the two or three places where an attentive but untrained reader is most likely to go wrong. I am not after scholarship: no references, no secondary literature, no attributions beyond what the passage itself names. I want your reading and your reasoning, and I am content to be told where the excerpt simply does not decide the question.
