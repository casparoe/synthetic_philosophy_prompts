I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of two years on a book whose antagonist sells out her brother's safehouse; whenever an editor or an early reader asks me why she does it, my honest answer has been some version of the same sentence — because of the way she was made, and because the scenes I gave her earlier made her that way. It struck me only recently that this is very nearly the shape of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility: that you do what you do because of the way you are, so that being genuinely responsible for your actions would require being genuinely responsible for the way you are, which no one can manage. (I gather there is a P.F. Strawson somewhere in the vicinity writing about resentment and the attitudes; please set him aside, as my interest is the regress rather than the reactions.) I am fluent in the vocabulary of my trade — motive, backstory, characterisation, the insistence that an action be earned, the well-worn business of characters who begin to surprise the person writing them — and largely unlettered in the analytic literature, so I would ask you to keep to my terms rather than mine.

What I want is a translation, followed by an accounting of what the translation could not carry. Render the load-bearing moves of the Basic Argument in the vocabulary of craft, as faithfully as you can, without quietly importing my framework's assumptions and handing them back to me as Strawson's; where a move fits only by being bent, mark it as bent rather than smoothing it out. Then tell me what, if anything, in the argument has no counterpart at all in how novelists talk about the people they invent. My own suspicion is that authorship does illicit work here: I stand outside my characters' causal histories in a way that nothing stands outside mine, so the analogy may be flattering the argument rather than exposing it — and the character who revises herself across drafts, who is supposedly capable of second-order change, may be the point at which my vocabulary simply runs out. I would like to know whether that asymmetry is a genuine residue or a confusion on my part.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing paragraph: the first three carrying the argument's central moves in my vocabulary, each with a brief note on whether the rendering is clean or forced; the fourth naming what the craft vocabulary cannot express; and the fifth delivering your verdict on whether the failure of translation reflects a poverty in the novelist's language, a poverty in the argument, or neither.
