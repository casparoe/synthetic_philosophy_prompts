In a philosophy-of-mind seminar I am auditing, one participant argued that the Stalinesque option for explaining the colour-phi illusion is not merely untestable but incoherent, and he got there by leaning hard on the political provenance of the term. His reasoning: a Stalinist show trial is a public spectacle, so the falsified verdict has to be delivered before an audience that also saw the original events, whereas Orwellian revision happens quietly in the archives afterwards, when nobody is watching. Transferring that structure, he claimed, a Stalinesque account of colour phi must posit some inner audience present at the confabulation — an audience that would therefore have to have been conscious of the two flashes in their real order first — which either collapses the Stalinesque option into the Orwellian one or reinstates the Cartesian theatre by the back door. The stakes he attached to this are the usual ones: whether reports of a spot changing colour midway along a path it was never on can be taken at face value.

Please audit the analogy. Set out explicitly what in the political case maps onto what in the perceptual case — the record, the archivist, the audience, the trial itself, the verdict, the moment of publication — and then identify where the mapping fails. Distinguish the disanalogies that actually bear on his inference from those that are merely decorative, and say whether the conclusion (Stalinesque is incoherent, or reduces to Orwellian) survives once they are on the table. Finally, if you think the political pairing is the wrong vehicle for this distinction, propose a better one and say what it does and does not license. Describe the experimental phenomena in your own terms; I am not after references.
