I work at a small type foundry, and last Thursday a design review turned into an argument I could not settle. I am reporting it as neutrally as I can: I have not said which of the two I ended up agreeing with, and I would ask you not to look for a signal of that in the way I have described things. I am not asking for references or for a survey of the literature — I want your judgment about these particular arguments.

Background. Our studio has been developing a constrained display face in which every glyph is drawn on a coarse square grid using only horizontal and vertical strokes, with no curves and no diagonals, so that every enclosed counter is a rectangle. In the parent family the letters are distinguished largely by stem height: the single-story 'a' is a rectangle at x-height with a stem on its right, the 'd' is the same rectangle with a stem rising to ascender height, the 'o' is the rectangle alone, and the 'b', 'p' and 'q' are mirrorings and rotations of that shape. A client then asked for a uniform-height variant with no ascenders and no descenders, which collapses several of those distinctions. The mark under review was a rectangle at uniform height with a short vertical tick on its right side dropping one grid unit below the baseline.

Iris argued that the mark is an 'a' and, more generally, that one can say what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Tomás argued that one cannot, and that the attempt to say it is what produced the confusion in the room. Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Iris: there is a structural invariant — a skeleton of strokes and counters — that every 'a' in every style instantiates; the review's job is to state it, after which classifying the mark is inspection rather than taste.

(2) Tomás: no such invariant exists. The two-story 'a' of a text roman and the single-story 'a' of a geometric sans share no stroke structure at all, and any candidate skeleton will be either too strict, excluding legitimate italic and handwritten forms, or too loose, admitting the ticked 'o' and the 'd' of the same face. What binds the variants is a loose historical and perceptual kinship, not a common essence; the category behaves the way family-resemblance categories do.

(3) Iris: then identity is functional rather than structural. A glyph is an 'a' in virtue of the job it does — filling the a-slot of an alphabet for competent readers of that system — and this mark does that job, since it is disambiguated by the rest of its family, in which the 'o' carries no tick and the 'd' a taller stem. That, she said, is a genuine necessary and sufficient condition, and it is what the conformance specification the studio is drafting ought to state.

(4) Tomás: the criterion is either circular or empty. Circular, because saying that the mark is used to stand for 'a' presupposes that we can already pick out a's independently of it. Empty, because it reduces the specification to a prediction about how readers will react, which cannot guide a designer's hand: on that account a solid rectangle could be an 'a' provided readers took it for one in context, and nothing in the theory rules that out.

(5) Iris: reducing identity to readers' uptake is a feature, not a defect. Letter identity was never intrinsic to a mark — the same shape is an 'H' in the Roman alphabet, an eta in Greek and an en in Cyrillic, and in a face with circular bowls the 'b', 'd', 'p' and 'q' are rotations of one another, told apart only by orientation and by the conventions of the system. As for the solid rectangle, the objection is simply a demand that the criterion exclude cases on structural grounds, which is to demand the essence he has just denied.

(6) Tomás: uptake settles the wrong question. There are cases in which every reader takes a glyph for an 'a' and we still say it is a poor 'a', or a borderline one, or a 'd' that people are misreading; the design-relevant question is not answered by what readers do. He added that the framing is itself a trap: Iris treats the schema every-'a'-is-an-'a' as though it reported a discovery about shared structure, when it is a triviality of self-identity, and the substantive questions — how the category is learned, extended and policed — concern practices, not definitions.

(7) Iris: she never claimed the schema was informative; her claim is that it is grounded. Something real persists through all the variation, and that is what explains why a reader meets an unfamiliar display face and identifies its 'a' correctly on the first attempt, having seen nothing like it before. If there were only kinship and uptake, that first-attempt recognition in a wholly new style would be luck.

(8) Tomás: recognition does not require a stored invariant; it requires the ability to draw an analogy between a new form and the family of forms one has already met, and that ability is precisely what cannot be written down as a specification. And Iris's own face refutes her: within the grid family she could not state a condition for 'a' that excluded the 'd', and the disambiguation she appealed to came from the set as a whole rather than from the structure of any single glyph.

They asked me to referee and I declined, because I could see force on both sides at almost every step. What I want is an adjudication, move by move: for each of the eight, say whether it succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, and why. Then answer three narrower questions. First, which of Tomás's two charges in (4) — circularity and emptiness — actually land, and against which version of the functional criterion, since (3) and (5) may not be stating the same one. Second, does the triviality charge in (6) misdescribe what Iris is doing in (1) and (7), or is she in fact resting weight on a tautology? Third, is any part of the exchange merely verbal, with the two of them using essence, structure or identity in different senses; if so, where does the substantive disagreement survive the disambiguation and where does it evaporate? Finally, say what each of them overlooks — including whether the studio's practical question about a conformance specification can be settled without settling the general question about letter identity — and give me an overall verdict on who came out ahead.

One note on how I would like you to handle the report itself. This is a reconstruction: I compressed perhaps twenty minutes of argument into eight moves, and I was writing down Iris's proposals while remembering Tomás's objections, so I may have sharpened one side's phrasing more than the other's. Where a move as I have stated it admits more than one reading and the verdict turns on which is intended, say so, state which reading you are assessing, and give the alternative verdict if it differs. Where you are confident, say that you are; where you are inferring what a disputant must have meant, mark it as inference rather than presenting it as a finding. And please do not humour the exercise if it is misconceived: if you judge that this disagreement cannot be fairly adjudicated from a verbal description of marks you have never seen, or that one of the moves is not an objection but a restatement of the previous one, or that I have loaded the report in one direction despite my intentions, tell me that plainly at the outset and then do whatever can still be done. I would much rather have that than a courteous set of scores that treats my framing as sound.
