I'm a backend engineer at a company that makes industrial food-processing equipment, and we localize our safety warnings, install guides and service docs into eleven languages. It used to be human translators plus a bilingual engineer eyeballing the safety-critical parts. Six months ago we swapped in an LLM pipeline with machine post-editing, the cost dropped about 70%, and now our PM has started telling customers the system understands the source documents. I've been reading around this and the closest thing I found is Hofstadter — the Gödel, Escher, Bach guy also wrote a whole book about translation, Le Ton beau de Marot, and his complaint as I understand it (could be wrong, I haven't got a philosophy background) is that a system that just maps surface strings onto surface strings isn't translating meaning at all, it's doing something shallower that looks right up until the context shifts. That's exactly the thing I need to be able to test, because our whole saving depends on dropping the human read for some document classes and keeping it for others.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run on a candidate model plus a document class, that decides whether the model's output is being controlled by the meaning of the source or by its surface form, and therefore whether we can cut human review for that class. I want ordered steps, what counts as a pass at each step, how many test items you need before a result means anything, and what the aggregate rule is for going from item-level results to a ship/no-ship call on the class.

Anchor it on failure modes I actually have. The same English sentence about a "guard" has to come out differently in Spanish depending on whether the guard is a person or a sheet-metal part, and our system gets that right maybe four times out of five based on the neighboring sentences. When a service tech writes a garbled or physically impossible source sentence, the model cheerfully emits fluent target text instead of flagging it. And two source paragraphs that say the same thing in very different words sometimes come out inconsistent with each other, while two paragraphs with near-identical wording but different referents come out identical. Build the procedure around tests like those, and tell me how a technical writer with zero linguistics is supposed to author them, plus how we keep the test items from leaking into the training data of whatever model we evaluate next quarter.

Also be explicit about: what to do at a step where I can't construct a clean test pair; which distinctions from the philosophy you're keeping and which you're throwing out to make it usable; where the procedure stops and hands the call to a human because its a risk-appetite question rather than a fact question; and what it can never establish — I assume passing every behavioral test still doesn't prove there's a model of the world in there, and I'd like to be told plainly whether that gap should worry me.

Constraints: about two days of work per document class, output gets reviewed by our QA lead and then signed off by a regulatory affairs person who has never read a philosophy anything, and it has to be re-runnable every time we swap models.

Then run it on three cases and don't soften the results: (1) lockout/tagout safety warnings for a hydraulic press brake, (2) a 240-page maintenance manual that's mostly part numbers, torque specs and short imperatives, (3) a product brochure. I expect 3 to pass and I half hope 1 fails, because if 1 passes we're going to stop having humans read it and I'm not sure I want that. Last thing: tell me where the whole meaning-vs-surface-form framing is fooling me — if a model could be made to pass every one of these tests without anything worth calling understanding, say how.
