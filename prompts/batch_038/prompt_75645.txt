So I got into a two-hour argument with a friend last night about a grant application and I'm still not sure wether he's right, so I'm outsourcing it to you. I sit on the grants committee of a family foundation — about $35M a year, mostly global health, plus a smaller "existential risk" sleeve my uncle added a decade ago. We vote again in nine days and I've been asked to circulate a short memo on one application before the meeting.

The application: a two-person group wants $180,000 over 18 months to build a screening protocol they say would detect, in frontier models before deployment, a failure mode that could plausibly be catastrophic at scale. Fine so far. The problem is their write-up does the expected-value math out loud: they estimate a 3-in-10,000 chance the protocol works as advertised, and claim that if it does it touches something like 10^15 future people. So the "expected" benefit dwarfs our entire annual budget by a lot. They also volunteered, unprompted, that they'd accept a clawback on their own management fee if an independent panel rates the protocol a dud at the end of year one.

Our board has a rule, adopted after we funded something embarrassing in 2013, that a grant can't rest on a claimed probability of success below one in a million. Three-in-ten-thousand clears that easily. But my friend's whole argument is that the rule is a joke — it polices the number people write down, not the number they actually believe, and any applicant who bothers to read the rule will just write 2-in-10,000. He also pointed out there are something like 40 more applications of this exact shape in the pipeline, so whatever we do here we'll be doing forty times over the next two cycles.

He wants decline. I think I want approve-with-conditions but I couldnt say what the conditions should be without them being arbitrary.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate. It needs to land on a clear recommendation — fund, fund with specific conditions and a dollar figure, or decline — plus a short rationale the other trustees can actually vote on. And it has to deal with the two things he keeps hammering: whose probability we're supposed to use when the applicant's own number is both above our threshold and, on my honest read, too high; and what our answer here commits us to across the next 40 of these. Give the strongest version of the decline case in your own voice, then say whether it wins and why — he'll be reading the memo too and he's sharper than me. Two pages max, plain English, no jargon dump; if you need one term of art, define it in a clause. I'd much rather the conditions be the kind a five-person foundation can actually monitor than the kind that sound rigorous and turn out to be unfalsifiable.
