ok so i was at my brother's thing on saturday and got dragged into a two hour argument with his mate Rob about asylum, and every time I made a point he landed back on the same line: you claim in the first safe country you reach, full stop. So by his reckoning anyone who passes through two safe countries and then claims somewhere else has basically forfeited it — they're shopping for the best deal. I pushed back and got nowhere, and I've been stewing on it since. I don't want you to settle the politics or give me a reading list, I want the sentence itself taken apart.

The case we were actually fighting over: someone flees a place where they'd be jailed, gets into a neighbouring country on a student visa, spends about a year there without claiming anything, then flies to a third country where their sister lives and claims on arrival. Rob says they should have claimed in year one and the third country is entitled to put them back on a plane.

First, give me a menu of the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, with a verdict on each. Like — is it a procedural rule about which state is allowed to examine a claim, is it a principle about how the burdens of protection get shared fairly between states, is it a claim about what the individual deserves or loses by waiting, or is it just a description of the point at which the danger stopped? Are those actually separable, and does his version only work by sliding between them? For each reading, say whether it's true or at least defensible, and then — this is the part I want — whether it would actually get him to "the third country can turn them away," because I suspect several of them are true and none of them get him there.

Related: Rob also said the refugee convention has something in it about arriving directly from the territory where your life was threatened, and I said I was pretty sure that bit was about not prosecuting people for crossing illegally, and neither of us actually knew. Sort out what that kind of provision can and can't be made to do here. And I know Europe has some arrangement where the first country of entry is responsible for a claim, but isn't that a deal between a specific group of states rather than a rule the whole world signed up to — does that matter for whether the slogan is true or just locally enforceable?

Then the word safe. I said a country can be safe in the sense that it won't hand you over and still be no place to actually have a life, and he said I was smuggling in a right to be happy. Work out what safe has to contain for the notion to do any work at all: non-return only? a legal status? the right to work, to be with family, to not sit in limbo for a decade? Tell me which of those are load-bearing for the slogan and which are me overreaching. Also two things I couldn't answer on the night: whether a country can be safe in general but not safe for this particular person, and whether it can have been safe when they passed through and not be safe now — does the slogan even have the resources to handle either, or does it just assume both away?

Last, tell me which reading Rob was most likely running when he said forfeited, because I think that word was carrying something none of the other readings carry, and give me a sentence or two I can use next time that doesn't make me sound like I'm lawyering at him.
