**Working title: accuracy-first epistemology is really a claim about convex sets**

Thesis: the standard accuracy arguments for probabilism arent about accuracy in any substantive sense. They're about the geometry of the target set, and once you see that, the framework turns out to be a lot weaker than advertised — it can't on its own get you from "be coherent" to "be precise."

Sketch. Finite partition, credence function b assigning a number to each cell, scoring rule measuring distance from the true v_w (1 in the actual cell, 0 elsewhere), Brier as the running example.

1. The probability functions over that partition are exactly the convex hull of the v_w's.
2. Any b that isn't a probability function lies outside the hull, and by the projection lemma for strictly convex divergences there's some p in the hull closer to *every* v_w than b is. So b is strongly dominated.
3. That's the entire argument for probabilism.

So the load-bearing parts are (i) the target set is convex and (ii) the divergence has the Pythagorean/projection property — not the word "accuracy," and not the particular scoring rule. And since any proper scoring rule on a finite partition is a Bregman divergence up to a forecast-independent term (Savage's representation), (ii) comes free once you insist on propriety. Accuracy-first epistemology then amounts to: your credences should be convex combinations of candidate doxastic states, where the candidates are the worlds.

Now the punch, and the part I'm least sure of. An imprecise state — a set of probability functions rather than one — is also a convex set, at least when it's closed under mixing. So run the same machinery with a different target set and you get a dominance argument for the imprecise state: anything outside its convex closure is dominated, anything inside isn't. The geometry that made probabilism look inevitable is equally happy with fat sets. If that's right, the precisificationist needs a premise from somewhere else — that mixing isn't an available operation for the agent, that the algebra is fine-grained enough to collapse the set, something about which alternatives count as admissible — and accuracy considerations were never going to supply it.

Where I'd like your comments:

- Is (2) licensed as I've stated it? I think the dominating point beats b against every world, but I'm fuzzy on whether you need the divergence to be generated by a strictly convex function of the whole vector rather than cellwise, and whether closedness of the hull matters in the finite case at all.
- Does the imprecise paragraph work, or does it slide between "convex" and "the convex hull of the v_w's"? I can hear a defender saying a fat set isn't the hull of anything meaningful, and that the real content of probabilism is that the extreme points are exactly the worlds. If that's the right reply, is my thesis just false or only overstated?
- Bluntly: is any of this interesting, or is it the received reading that everyone in the literature already grants? I want to know if I'm rediscovering the wheel.
