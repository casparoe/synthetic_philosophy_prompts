I'm an epistemologist, so I've read the Genealogy but never taught it, and I'm out of my depth in a small way here. A general-audience magazine asked our department to sanity-check a companion essay for a public lecture series on resentment, and the thing got dumped on me. The author isn't a crank — she's clearly read the book — but a bunch of the sentences feel like they're sliding sideways and I can't say where or why. Here's the load-bearing paragraph, more or less as she has it:

Nietzsche's project is to show that our moral concepts have a history, and that the history is disgraceful. 'Good' originally just meant noble, strong and beautiful: the aristocrats named themselves good, and the idea of the 'bad' came along afterwards, as an afterthought. Then the powerless — first the Jews, then the Christians, soaked in ressentiment — pulled off a revaluation: they declared their masters 'evil', and defined 'good' as the opposite of evil, that is, as meekness, poverty and humility. So slave morality is master morality flipped over, and because it was born out of hatred instead of self-affirmation it isn't merely explained by its origin, it's condemned by it. Bad conscience, on this picture, is punishment turned inward: once the state walled us in, the aggression we could no longer discharge became fear directed at ourselves, and that fear is what we call guilt. And the ascetic ideal — the priest's claim that this life is worth saying no to — was a confidence trick that science has now seen through, so once we know where morality came from we're simply free of it.

Two things from you. First, walk me through it claim by claim and tell me what a Nietzsche scholar would sign off on, what's a fair compression for a magazine, and what's actually wrong or actively misleading — and I want reasons out of the book's own argumentative machinery, not vibes about tone. I've got two hunches of my own I'd like checked. One: I don't think 'flipped over' is right, becuase the two schemes aren't symmetrical — the noble pair is good/bad and the priestly pair is good/evil, and I have an intuition that those two oppositions do structurally different work, with one term in each pair doing something primary and the other being derivative, but the derivatives aren't the same kind of thing and I can't articulate it. Two: the move from morality's growing out of ressentiment to morality's being unjustified looks to me like the genetic fallacy, and I have a foggy memory of a bit in the second treatise about how knowing what something is *for* tells you nothing about where it came from — which seems to cut against her inference. But then what is the genealogy doing, if not refuting? If the honest answer is that it's diagnostic rather than probative, spell out what it's diagnosing and who it's supposed to convince.

Second, and practically: for each claim you call wrong, is there a one-line repair that keeps her sentence but makes it defensible? I'd much rather hand the editor 'yes, with these five edits' than 'no'. Skip the bibliography and the secondary literature entirely — I don't need names, I need to know wether this piece survives being read by somebody who has actually read Nietzsche.
