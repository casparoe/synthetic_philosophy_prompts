I'm on the model validation team at a mid-size bank — basically the person who has to say whether a model the business wants to use is fit for purpose. Around 15 reviews a year, three working days each, and my write-up goes to a committee where half the people aren't quants, then occasionally to the auditor. The problem I keep hitting is what to do with assumptions that are flatly false.

Last quarter I objected to a mortgage prepayment model and the desk head waved me off with the line that you don't judge a model by how realistic its assumptions are, only by whether it predicts — plus something about expert pool players making shots as if they'd solved the equations. And honestly I couldnt answer him, because the fit is excellent. But I dont buy that it settles anything, and the whole just-check-the-predictions move isn't something I can actually run when there's one historical dataset, no counterfactuals, and the model is about to be used in a rate environment that isn't in the sample.

So: write me a procedure I can run on any model in those three days. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers force me to stop and write "not fit for purpose," and which steps aren't really about facts at all and have to be handed to a human — flag those explicitly, because otherwise I'll dress up my judgment as method and the committee will treat it as objective. I vaguely remember people trying to sort assumptions into kinds — ones that only buy tractability, ones that fix the range the model is supposed to work in, ones that are load-bearing for the prediction itself — but I don't have the details, so build whatever sorting you need into the procedure and tell me what each kind licenses and what it doesn't.

Then run it on three:

1. the prepayment model above — assumes every borrower refinances the instant it's optimal, no frictions, no inertia, perfect foresight of where rates go; backtest error is tiny, but the entire backtest window is one rate regime;
2. an economic capital model that aggregates loss streams across business lines as independent — everyone knows they aren't, the correlation shows up exactly in the bad tail, and relaxing it makes the thing unsolvable;
3. a deposit pricing model I actually like and want to keep, which assumes a single representative customer who notices every rate change immediately.

If it kills number 3, fine, say so. But also tell me which of the three your procedure is least reliable on, what it structurally can't detect — I suspect there's a class of false assumptions it'll wave through no matter how carefully I follow it — and which steps fall apart when nobody can give me the number the step asks for.
