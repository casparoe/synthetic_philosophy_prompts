I referee a monthly reading group in my department, and two of my colleages got into it over the Principal Principle last Thursday and I never managed to settle it, so I'm outsourcing the refereeing to you.

Setup so you know what they were fighting about: there's a coin machine that dispenses coins whose chance of heads on the next toss is exactly 1/2 — that's what the manufacturer says, and nothing in our physics suggests otherwise. Dana is a philosopher of physics, Eli is an epistemologist. Here's roughly the exchange; I'm compressing, but I don't think I'm putting words in anyone's mouth.

1. Dana: Chance is the thing you defer to. If you know the chance of A is x, and the rest of what you have is admissible evidence — nothing about the future, nothing self-referential — your credence in A should be x. Observed frequencies matter only insofar as they're evidence about what the chances are.
2. Eli: Run the case, then. You know the chance is 1/2, and you also know the machine has come up heads on each of the last thousand tosses. That's a fact about the past, so it's admissible on your own criterion. Conditionalizing on it plainly pushes your credence above 1/2. So your two norms hand down different verdicts in one and the same case, which means the Principle can't be the whole story about chance-guided credence. It has to sit on top of a prior over chance hypotheses — and once you have that prior, it isn't adding anything.
3. Dana: That's not a conflict, it's an update. If chances are whatever the best systematization of the total history says they are, then a thousand straight heads is evidence that the chance was never 1/2 in the first place. Conditionalizing on the streak lowers your credence in the chance hypothesis; then the Principle tells you to match the numbers you're left with. No contradiction anywhere.
4. Eli: That's the disaster, not the rescue. On your view the chance hypothesis itself has to assign positive probability to futures which, had they been realized, would have shown the hypothesis false. So the theory instructs you to conditionalize on evidence that would destroy the theory. And the Principle was supposed to be a bridge rule — a priori, connecting chance to credence — but if chance hypotheses can be undermined by ordinary history, its authority turns out to be hostage to which way the world happened to go.
5. Dana: Lewis saw this and signed off on an amendment. Don't match your credence to the unconditional chance; match it to the chance conditional on the rest of your evidence. Undermining disappears, because you never defer to what chance says simpliciter.
6. Eli: It disappears because you've deleted the Principle. What's left says your credence in A should equal your conditional credence in A given the chance hypothesis and your other evidence — which is a theorem of the probability calculus, true of any hypothesis you care to substitute. There's no bridge from chance to credence anymore, and you still owe us an account of why your credences in the chance hypotheses are what they are. That's where all the content lived, and now the Principle has nothing to say about it.
7. Dana: That charge proves too much. Conditionalization itself is "just a theorem" once you fix a prior, and you don't think conditionalization is empty. What the Principle does is tell you that among all the hypotheses in your prior, the chance ones have this special claim on your credences — that's a substantive constraint, not a tautology.

Nobody budged and the room got booked out from under us. Then give me an overall verdict: is there a version of chance-deference that survives Eli's moves 4 and 6, or is he right that every repair either leaves a live conflict or bleeds the principle of content?
