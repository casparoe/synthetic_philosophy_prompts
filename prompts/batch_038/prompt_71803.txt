I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I've got a piece commissioned on the "we're probably in a simulation" argument — specifically the version people reach for when they want to talk down the whole far-future, astronomical-stakes thing (the crowd that says a billion years of flourishing outweighs basically everything happening right now). My editor wants numbers in the piece, not vibes, so here's what I need.

Rate each of these five considerations on a scale from -5 to +5 for how much it should push someone *toward* betting on the far future, with two or three sentences each on why:

1. If there are going to be vast numbers of simulated people, then most people-like-me are sims, and a sim's life is cheap, short, and gets switched off whenever the run ends — so those astronomical stakes aren't mine to chase.
2. But the sims that would exist get run by civilizations that survived their own dangerous century. Doesn't the prospect of all those sims being out there constitute evidence that getting through is possible — which cuts the other way?
3. Whatever happens inside a simulation seems causally sealed off from the level running it, so nothing I do here reaches the "real" future, and the real future is supposedly where all the value is.
4. A universe with cheap, abundant computing is a universe that can spin up trillions of worthwhile lives later at almost no cost, which makes the "act now or it's lost forever" panic look overblown.
5. If a huge fraction of minds are sims and sims are conscious, the total number of minds that ever exist is way bigger than the biology-only estimate — which should make the stakes larger, not smaller.

Then rank them by how well they'd actually surive a determined reply from the other side, and flag any that are secretly the same argument wearing a different hat.
