I'm in a medieval Jewish philosophy seminar and we just spent two weeks on the Rambam's reasons for the commandments, and honestly the thing i cant get over is that the same guy seems to be pulled in two directions. In the Guide he explains the sacrificial system as a kind of accommodation — the whole world sacrificed, Israel had just come out of Egypt, and God did not ask them to drop a deeply rooted custom cold, the way you don't ask a person to abandon a habit all at once. But then in the Mishneh Torah, in the laws of kings, he writes that the messianic king will rebuild the sanctuary and the whole service will go back to how it was, sacrifices included, and a few lines later that the Torah's law is everlasting, nothing added and nothing taken away. And a later critic of his presses the point that Abel and Noah brought offerings long before there were any Egyptians or Chaldeans around to be weaned off anything, so the idolatry story can't be the whole reason.

Here's my opening, and I've tried to make it a fair one rather than a gotcha. Suppose I tell you that in some later century the world has been converted by your own reasoning: almost nobody thinks slaughter is spiritually elevating anymore, most people find it repellant, and there is now a way to grow flesh in a vat — no animal is ever born, none is ever killed, and what comes out is hard to tell from the meat of a lamb. Say the sanctuary is standing again and the messianic king wants to restore the service. Two questions. First: is that vat-flesh a fit offering? Second, the one I actually want you to wrestle with: if the reason a commandment was given has expired, does the commandment expire with it?
