For nineteen years I have worked in software quality assurance, and for the last seven I have run the test group at a company that writes firmware for hospital equipment, which means my entire professional vocabulary is about what a person is entitled to put in writing. A fault goes into the tracking system as a defect only if it is reproducible against a named test case on a named build; a complaint from a single unit in the field is logged as an observation and explicitly marked suspected until somebody can repeat it; every entry carries a severity, a priority, and an owner; and the two failure modes we are actually graded on are escapes — real faults that shipped because nobody reported them — and noise, the volume of reports that engineering kicks back because they cannot reproduce them and therefore cannot act on them. I mention all of this because over the past year I have been reading, slowly and without any of the jargon, about a philosophical argument I cannot get out of my head: the dispute over what it takes for someone to be entitled to flat-out assert something. As I understand it there are at least four candidate standards on offer — that you may assert it only if you know it, only if it is in fact true, only if you believe it, or only if you would be reasonable in believing it — and the arguments turn on cases that seem to me to have obvious counterparts in my day job: the ticket that has almost certainly already lost, the clinician who relays a diagnosis supported by overwhelming test results that she does not herself accept, the person who says the flight is delayed because the board says so and nothing else, and the pair of people who hold exactly the same evidence while facing wildly different consequences if they are wrong. I should say plainly that I never studied any of this; this came up at a book group that mostly reads history and had no patience for it, and I have been turning it over by myself since.

What I want is a translation into the only language I really speak, carried out as faithfully as you can manage, followed by an honest account of what the translation could not carry. Take each of the four standards in turn and render it as a reporting policy for my team: what would the rule look like posted on the lab wall, and what kind of bug tracker would it produce after a year of use? Then take the four cases above and say, for each, whether it lands on something I already do, whether it lands only if I bend my vocabulary, or whether there is nothing in my world that answers to it at all. I would particularly like your treatment of two practices I use constantly and cannot place. The first is the suspected-versus-confirmed label, which seems to be a way of lowering the commitment attached to a report without being penalized for having made it; I want to know whether that corresponds to anything the philosophers say about hedging versus flat-out saying, or whether it is a distinction my profession invented for bookkeeping reasons that does no philosophical work. The second is the kickback, the moment when engineering refuses to accept a report until it is accompanied by a reproducible case; there is an argument in what I have been reading to the effect that only the strictest of the four standards makes sense of the propriety of demanding to be shown how you know, and I cannot tell whether our kickback procedure supports that argument or quietly subverts it.

Then the residue, which is the part I actually care about. Where something refuses to translate, I want your judgment about which side the defect sits on, and I want you to choose rather than balance. Two readings keep presenting themselves and I cannot decide between them. One is that my framework is genuinely impoverished: triage by severity and reproducibility is a method for rationing other people's labor, and so has no room for what the disputants are actually arguing about, which may be something about what a speaker owes to the truth as such, or about flat-out saying being different in kind from guessing aloud. The other is that the disagreement is thinner than it appears — that knowledge, reasonable belief, and even truth are simply different settings on a dial I already turn every week, namely how much escape risk versus how much noise the organization is willing to absorb — so that what looks like a debate about the essence of a kind of act is a debate about where to set a tolerance. Tell me which of those you think is nearer the mark, and tell me where I should distrust the analogy rather than enjoy it. One caution before you begin: it would be very easy to fudge this, so please do not import my assumptions and label the result a translation. In particular, do not treat our reporting policy as though it settles what assertion is for, and do not assume without argument that a defect report and an assertion are the same sort of thing. If the correct conclusion is that a report is a move inside an institutional procedure while an assertion is supposed to be something else entirely, say so directly, and say what follows from that for the four standards. I am not looking for references or for names to chase down; I want the reasoning laid out where I can check it.
