I write a weekly-ish column on AI and ideas and my editor has greenlit a piece about the robotaxi industry that is not allowed to be another "is the tech ready for prime time" hot take. The case I keep going back to is the Cruise incident in San Francisco in October 2023: a pedestrian was hit first by a human-driven car, thrown into the path of the AV, the AV braked and struck her, and then, as part of a pullover maneuver meant to get the vehicle out of the travel lane and into what the industry calls a minimal risk condition, it dragged her roughly twenty feet with her underneath. Then there's the aftermath, where the company walked regulators through the initial impact and did not proactively bring up the dragging part until it was pressed. I've got the reports and the regulator correspondence in a folder and can hand you any detail you want, so don't spend effort reconstructing the timeline — I'm not after a fact-check piece.

What I actually want to know is whether the frame problem is the right lens for this, and if so, which one. My half-formed hunch, which you should feel free to demolish: the narrow technical problem from the McCarthy and Hayes tradition — writing frame axioms so you don't have to state all the obvious non-effects of an action — can't literally be what went wrong, because there is no symbolic action-description layer in a production autonomy stack that could have had frame axioms in the first place. And yet something structurally adjacent plainly did go wrong: the system had a representation of a scene, took an action, and did not carry forward the fact that the pedestrian's position relative to the vehicle had changed. So is that the frame problem wearing a costume, or a genuine instance of it at a different level of description? Relatedly, the qualification problem and the ramification problem are supposed to be distinct from the frame problem proper, and I can't tell which of the three, if any, this case instantiates — and there are at least three rival diagnoses in the industry's own idiom that I want you to weigh against it: an ontology or vocabulary failure (nothing in the representation corresponds to person-under-the-vehicle), an objective or reward failure (the pullover policy just was never trained against this outcome), and plain old out-of-distribution weirdness. Are those rivals, or the same failure described at different levels?

So: write me about 1200 words as a working analysis I can lift passages out of, not a blog post. Start by sketching the mapping explicitly before you commit to anything — who or what counts as the system, what counts as the action, what the state is, what "relevant" could mean here, what the evidence base the system is reasoning over actually is — and only then argue. Be disciplined about where the lens goes silent; if the frame problem simply has nothing to say about the corporate half of the story, say that. Though I do want one short section on a riskier idea I had: whether the disclosure failure is the same structural shape one level up, an organization acting without carrying forward what its own earlier action changed, and whether that analogy earns its keep or is just wordplay. I want you to write as a philosopher of mind and AI who has actually read enough autonomy engineering to not embarrass yourself, and I want you to think it through carefully first, then come down somewhere definite: pick the diagnosis you think is right, say which of the readings of the frame problem the case vindicates or refutes, and give me your reasons. Don't hedge by listing the options and leaving me to choose — if you think my hunch in paragraph two is wrong, tell me so in the first line. And don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'll put names and dates in myself; where you're leaning on a contested reading of one of these philosophers, flag it in a sentence so I know not to hang the piece on it.
