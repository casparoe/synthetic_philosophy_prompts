I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I am about to file a column on embryo selection. My editor wants the central argument compressed into a single passage near the top, and this is what I have drafted:

"Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence says that prospective parents who are able to choose among the children they might have should choose the one expected to carry the fewest genetic risks — in practice, that they ought to screen embryos and discard those most likely to produce a diseased child, and that a couple who declines to do so has failed morally rather than merely expressed a preference. The argument is impersonal in the sense that it is indifferent to the particular child: because an embryo that is never implanted never becomes anyone, nobody is wronged by passing it over, and this is where Parfit's non-identity problem does the work — it puts the choice beyond the reach of ordinary person-affecting morality, and hence beyond reproach. Which is why Habermas's objection to liberal eugenics bites here with full force: selecting an embryo for its expected traits is designing a child, and a designed child can no longer regard himself as the undivided author of his own life."

Before I file, I need an accuracy audit. Go through the passage claim by claim and sort each into three piles: what a defender of the principle would accept as written, what is a fair popular simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation. Pay particular attention to any term doing double duty — words like impersonal, best, and eugenics have technical senses in this literature that are not their everyday ones, and I suspect at least one of them has slipped. Then tell me what role the non-identity problem actually plays in the argument, and whether the closing move from Parfit to Habermas is licensed at all, or whether Habermas's objection is aimed at something other than selection among embryos. One more thing: my own hunch is that the disability-rights objection, which the passage never mentions, is the serious one, and that Habermas is doing decorative work in the final sentence. Is that hunch right? If it is not, where does the real pressure on the principle come from? Close by rewriting the worst sentences so that a philosopher reading the column has no grounds to write in.
