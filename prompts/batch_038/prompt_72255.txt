I head the scientific-basis group at a regulatory agency. Our documents underwrite rules and engineering standards with design lives of twenty-five to fifty years — a disposal standard, an occupational exposure limit, a materials qualification — and once a number or a mechanism is baked into a standard it becomes extremely hard to change, because the standard is then cited in contracts and picked up by other jurisdictions. Lately our own reviewers have been pressing a worry with an old name in the philosophy of science: the science our standards rest on may be superseded well inside the standard's own service life, the way successful theories of heat, combustion, and the propagation of light were superseded after doing excellent predictive work. So we may be committing an institution for four decades to a claim that will not survive fifteen years.

Please write a screening procedure my analysts can run on any theory-dependent claim before that claim is allowed to carry load in a long-lived standard. It has to be usable by people who have never read the realism literature: a sequence of questions to put to a claim, what a satisfactory answer to each looks like, and a small set of possible verdicts — for instance, safe to rely on outright; rely on it only with an explicit margin or a triggered re-review; do not build on it. I want it to draw on whatever that debate actually makes usable: the observation that when a successful theory is discarded some of its content is retained and some is thrown out, and the question of what separates the two; the difference between what a theory posits in order to do explanatory work and what it merely presupposes as an instrument for getting predictions out; the asymmetry between a mature, quantitatively confirmed claim embedded in a wide web of independent evidence and a young one; and the distinction between a claim our standard needs to be *true* and a claim it only needs to be *robust under replacement* — that is, a claim whose falsification would leave the derived number standing. Say explicitly which of these distinctions you have kept, which you have dropped as unusable in practice, and why.

Then run the procedure on three claims: (a) that a corrosion product layer forms on our canister material, passivates it, and thereafter self-limits its own growth rate, which is how we justify a very long release delay; (b) a linear no-threshold dose–response default for a certain intake pathway, which functions less as a confirmed mechanism than as a conservative convention; and (c) a phenomenon characterized in the literature only in the last decade, well reproduced in the laboratory but with at least one competing explanation still live. One of these three currently carries load in our flagship standard, and a verdict against it would cost us a redrafting cycle we have no budget for — do not soften the result on that account.

Constraints on the deliverable: an analyst should be able to run the screening in about half a day per claim; the verdict is signed off by me, and I am not a philosopher, so the write-up has to be legible to a non-specialist without losing the argument; the procedure must state what to do when the history of the relevant field offers no usable precedent rather than defaulting to assuming the claim will survive; and every step where the analyst is in fact making a judgment call should be labeled as one instead of being dressed up as a mechanical test. Close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it can license any estimate of the probability that a given claim survives, or identify in advance which component of a theory is the one that will be discarded.

One request about how you handle this: if you think the premise is confused — if no ex ante screening can tell which parts of a theory will survive, or if the realism debate yields nothing an analyst could actually apply without the answer already being fixed by their intuitions — say so plainly at the top rather than producing something that looks rigorous and is not. I would much rather be told the task cannot be done well than handed a checklist that launders what we already wanted to conclude.
