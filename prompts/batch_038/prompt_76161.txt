I'm a backend engineer and somehow ended up as one of four people on our company's partnerships panel — we sign off on deals with outside labs before legal drafts anything. Thursday I have to circulate a written recommendation and I'd rather it not just be "trust your gut, keep the relationship warm." So here's the deal, and once I laid it out on a whiteboard I'm pretty sure it's a centipede game, which I looked up after realizing what was happening (some early-80s paper by a guy named Rosenthal, I think).

Two years ago we signed a framework agreement with a university spin-out over a runtime/compiler thing, and it has an option ladder in it. Every quarter, on the first business day, whichever side holds the turn can either call the deal at that quarter's fixed price or pass. Calling splits the pot on a schedule that favors whoever calls; passing grows the pot (pegged to a formula involving our joint benchmark numbers) and flips the turn. Eight quarters total, so eight nodes, and ours was node 1 — we passed. They passed at 2 and 4, we passed at 3, and now node 5 is ours; the window closes ten business days after it opens. Three wrinkles that I don't think are in the textbook version. First, the pot isn't smooth: it jumps at node 5 because their key patent issues then, and at node 6 their share of the split steps up under a clause their lawyers call a "loyalty kicker," which makes node 5 the last turn anyone gets under the old split. If nobody ever calls, the framework expires at node 8 and the joint IP reverts to us as a non-exclusive license only, which our product team says is worth a lot less than any buyout. Second, their lead negotiator is a professor who publishes on bargaining, and our analyst swears calling was strictly better for them at both nodes 2 and 4 no matter what we did afterward — they passed anyway, and I genuiney can't tell whether that's him being soft, him signaling something to us, or him not being allowed to call by his own university committee, which is a whole other principle-agent mess. Third, the cash isn't fungible on our side: our CFO has ring-fenced this budget against another acquisition he wants in Q3, so passing carries an internal cost that appears nowhere in the ladder.

One panel member wants to go further and announce in writing that we will never call — i.e. burn our own option — on the theory that it forces them to either call early or let the thing run to term where they get much less. Another member says the game-theory framing is itself the trap and the only reason this has been amicable for two years is that neither side has acted "rationally" once. Draft the memo I circulate: pick a course of action (call at node 5, pass and eat the kicker, go for the commitment move, or try to reopen the schedule) with a concrete deadline attached, and give reasoning the other three can actually argue with, including a real treatment of the strongest point on the losing side rather than a strawman. I do want it to engage the theory honestly instead of waving at it — backward induction says we should have called at node 1 and that this whole thing should have collapsed on day one, and it obviously didn't, so what does that actually license me to conclude about them, about us, and about whether the model is even the right instrument here? Tell me under what conditions passing is genuinely dominated for us and under what conditions it isn't. A friend of mine who does quant stuff told me "irrationality is just a payoff you haven't found yet" and I have not been able to unsee that.
