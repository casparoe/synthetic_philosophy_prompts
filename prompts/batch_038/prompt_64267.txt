next week I've got a 50-minute section, 22 first-years, whiteboard only — no slides, and the room's too small to move furniture around much. We're two weeks into personal identity: they've done Locke's prince and the cobbler, a chunk of Parfit, and most of them have settled into some version of "psychological continuity is what matters" and are feeling pretty pleased about it. Now I have to teach Dennett's "Where Am I?" from Brainstorms — the one where his brain gets lifted out, lives in a vat in Houston, and drives his body around by radio while the body goes off to Tulsa.

Don't explain the story to me, I know it fine. Plan how I explain it.

- what has to be in place before the question even bites for them. Do I open with the intuition that obviously you're wherever your brain is, or open cold with the surgery? Opening with the intuition seems to make them smug and then the story just confirms what they already said; opening cold seems to waste twenty minutes before anyone knows what's at stake. Tell me which, and what exactly goes in the first five minutes.
- the one moment in the story to park the whole section on. My instinct is the backup — the second brain and vat getting switched on — but argue me out of it if that's the wrong hinge. And tell me how far to go: do they get the ending, or do I stop before it and make them supply it?
- what I put on the board. I want some diagram or physical bit of business — maybe getting a student to point at where they are — that makes the question start to look like it's demanding the wrong kind of answer, instead of me just announcing that "where" is doing something odd. Describe what gets drawn, in what order, and what the students should notice without being told.
- the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, and the point in the sequence where I head each one off. The ones I'm braced for: they'll treat it as a straight brain-vs-body tug of war and want to vote; a couple will conflate it with the envatted-brain thing from epistemology and think Dennett is running a skepticism argument; somebody will say the duplicate doesn't count because copies never count. What's the one I'm not seeing that'll cost me the most time?
- a single closing question that separates students who got the deflation from ones who just picked a team.
- what I should flatly refuse to get into at this level, and how to say so without sounding like I'm dodging.

Also: a good third of them are going to find the setup ridiculous — a brain with a radio in it — and I need the room to not turn into a comedy hour. Give me something to say or do in the first ten minutes that buys seriousness for the case without asking them to suspend anything they'd call common sense.
