You are a professional epistemologist working on higher-order evidence, the norms of rational belief, and the formal modelling of both. Please think carefully and work through this in stages rather than answering immediately.

Last night I argued until two in the morning with a close friend — herself a philosopher of broadly Bayesian persuasion — about a view I hold, and I want a sober map of its exposure before I commit to defending it in print. She thinks I have insulated the view with qualifications; I think her objections trade on bad descriptions of the cases. I would rather you tell me I am wrong.

The view, which I will call the Transparency Constraint, has three clauses. First, it is never rationally permissible to believe that p while believing that your belief that p is irrational — a ban on akrasia at the level of outright belief, not merely a coherence ideal. Second, higher-order evidence is genuine evidence: information about your own reliability, your conditions of assessment, or the provenance of your reasoning can defeat a first-order belief that the first-order evidence alone would support. Third, it follows that in every case that looks like stable, rational level-splitting, one of the two levels must in fact have shifted — either the first-order belief is revised or attenuated, or the higher-order assessment is. There is no residue. I add a further commitment that my friend attacked hardest: this is not merely a claim about how attitudes must hang together, but a substantive constraint on descriptions of evidence. Any putative case of rational level-splitting is a case in which the specification of the agent's total evidence has gone wrong somewhere.

Her three objections, as best I can reconstruct them. (i) Misleading higher-order evidence is pervasive, not exotic: the exhausted diagnostician, the sleep-deprived analyst, the philosopher who knows she has been given a drug that impairs her reasoning about a certain class of problems while her reasoning proceeds flawlessly. In such cases, she says, the agent has everything rationality could ask for at both levels. (ii) The ban makes which level errs a matter without a principled answer: the thesis says someone is necessarily going wrong but cannot say who, which she claims is a diagnosis, not a theory. (iii) Iteration: if higher-order evidence is genuine evidence, so is higher-higher-order evidence, and either the constraint is trivially satisfied at every level or it generates a regress with no stopping point.

She also pressed a case I have not been able to answer, and I want you to try. Call it the Undercut Auditor. A proof-reader at a mathematics journal is told by a colleague she has overwhelming reason to trust that she has been assigned a paper in a subfield whose notation she systematically misreads. Independently, she has strong reason to think the colleague's information came from a scheduling database that mislabels the subfield, and that she is in fact competent here. So: her first-order evidence supports taking the proof to be valid; her well-sourced higher-order evidence says she is unreliable on exactly this material; her higher-higher-order evidence says that higher-order evidence is bad. Does the Transparency Constraint yield determinate advice about what she ought to believe? Which level wins, and by what principle that is not simply an assertion that the highest level always does?
