For thirty-one years I directed an analytical chemistry group at a federal laboratory, and I retired this past spring. To keep myself honest about whether my mind still works, I enrolled in a continuing-studies sequence in moral philosophy, which has led, as these things apparently do, to an unforeseen second occupation: I now sit as the lay member on the ethics consultation service of our regional hospital, and I have additionally been asked to design and teach a short training for the volunteers in a community program who act as substitute decision-makers for people who have lost capacity and either named no agent or named one who cannot be reached. These volunteers are retired teachers, a postal worker, two nurses, a former county clerk. They get perhaps four cases a year, they meet the patient perhaps twice, and they are asked within a day or so to decide about ventilation, about feeding tubes, about antibiotics. They are conscientious people with no philosophical training and no time, and what they currently have to work with is a laminated card that tells them to ask themselves what the patient would have wanted.

I want to give them something better, and I suspect the philosophical literature on idealized deliberation is the place to get it, though it was certainly not written for the purpose. As I understand it, on a family of views running from Brandt's talk of desires that survive repeated exposure to criticism and relevant information through Railton's account of what one would want oneself to want if fully informed and vividly acquainted with what one's situation would actually feel like, a person's reasons are fixed not by their occurrent preferences but by the preferences they would arrive at under specified better conditions; and in the machine-intelligence literature there is a related strategy, which I believe goes by the name of indirect normativity, of specifying the procedure that would yield the answer rather than the answer itself, precisely because the answer cannot be written down in advance. Several of my colleagues on the committee find this whole line of thought vacuous in a clinical setting: they say the idealized patient is unobservable, that no surrogate can run the procedure without smuggling in their own preferences, and that the result will simply be the surrogate's values wearing the patient's name. I think they are roughly half right, and I am inclined to build the projection risk into the instrument rather than argue that it has been designed away. If I have garbled any of the above, correct me plainly; I am new at this and would rather be told.

So please take the role of a philosopher who works across metaethics, the philosophy of action, and clinical ethics consultation, and who has also written decision aids for people with no training in the field. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming in the open: before producing anything usable, work through which features of the idealized-deliberation picture can survive being turned into a protocol, which must be dropped, and which can be preserved only as explicitly flagged judgment calls. Then give me the procedure itself, written in American English, on the following understanding.

- It must be runnable by a non-expert in about an hour, at a kitchen table or a bedside, with a pencil. Give the steps in the order they should be asked, say for each what counts as a sufficient answer and what merely looks like one, state where the procedure stops at "no" or at "defer," and mark every point where it hands the decision to a human being — the attending physician, the committee, the family as a body, occasionally a court — because what is left is a question of value rather than a question of fact.
- Build in a device of my own, which you are welcome to reject or revise if it is a bad idea: treat idealization as three separately adjustable knobs — information, critical rationality, and vivid acquaintance with what the outcome would feel like from the inside — and require the surrogate to record, for the decision in front of them, how far each knob is being turned, what the default setting is, and what would justify a departure.
- Give me a stopping rule on idealization itself, which I take to be the thing the theory most needs and least supplies: a test for the point at which further idealizing stops reconstructing the patient and starts inventing a stranger. If the identity objection to these views has anything practically usable in it, I want it here, in operational form.
- Because the idealized agent's verdict cannot be audited, require a short written record at each step: what the judgment rests on, what evidence would have changed it, and what a second surrogate who reached a different conclusion would have to dispute. I would rather make the projection problem visible than pretend to solve it.
- Flag honestly every step that is a judgment call dressed in procedural language, and close with a statement of what the instrument cannot do and where it will misfire in practice.

Apply the finished procedure to three cases. First: a seventy-four-year-old former machinist with advanced ALS who was tracheostomized and ventilated after a crisis, who signed a directive nine years ago refusing life support where there is no reasonable prospect of a life he would value, who now communicates by eye-gaze and reports that he is glad to be alive and looks forward to his granddaughter's visits, and whose daughter — his agent, exhausted, working two jobs — insists that continuing is a betrayal of the man she knew. Second: an eighty-six-year-old woman with advanced dementia who does not recognize her children, whose directive written at sixty-nine asked that pneumonia not be treated if she ever became someone who no longer knows anyone, and who is now by the staff's account cheerful, hums along to the radio, tolerates feeding well, and shows no sign of distress; her son wants the directive honored. Third: a twenty-seven-year-old man recovering from a traumatic brain injury with no directive and no named agent, for whom a court-appointed guardian from our program has met him twice, the only informants are an estranged sister and a former roommate, and the guardian's own documented history shows a man who prizes independence above everything and distrusts institutions.

Let me tell you my priors so you can tell me when the procedure disappoints them, and please do so without softening it: I lean toward treating a written directive as close to dispositive, and I am suspicious of present contentment in dementia as evidence of anything. At least one of the three cases should come out against me if the procedure is doing its work rather than mine, and I would like you to say so explicitly at that point. Finally, name thinkers and positions where naming them earns its place, but do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — I cannot look anything up, so anything you invoke has to be explained in the text itself, in plain language, at the moment it is used.
