Here's my problem. Next Tuesday I've got seventy-five minutes with about a dozen people at my library's evening discussion group — two nurses, a guy who dispatches trucks, a couple of retirees, nobody's had any philosophy since high school — and the topic I picked is whether your individual choices matter when the harm is real but no single act makes a detectable difference. Driving, meat, skipping the vote, that family. Whiteboard and markers, that's it. I want them to leave able to argue about the thing rather than just feeling vaguely guilty, which is what happened last time I tried anything in this neighborhood.

My instinct was to hang the whole session on voting, because it's the one case they all already have opinions about. Now I suspect that's a trap: they arrive pre-convinced their vote doesn't count, so it's a fight they've already had with themselves, and whatever I put on the board gets sorted into that old argument instead of doing any work. So first tell me whether voting is salvageable as the carrier or whether I need something else entirely — and if something else, what the example has to have and what it has to not have. The part I can't resolve is whether I want a sharp threshold in it. A case where a million acts pile up smoothly with no single one crossing a line, versus a case where the millionth act tips it over, seem to teach completely different lessons and I honestly can't tell which one is the more honest picture of the situations they actually care about. If the answer is "you need both, in a specific order," say that and tell me the order.

Then the sequencing: what has to be up before the "your act makes no difference" argument can even be stated properly? Half of them will hear it as a claim about measurement — too small to detect — rather than as a claim about whether anybody was made worse off, and I don't know what has to be in place first to stop that slide. Relatedly, where does the expected-value response go, because I'm fairly sure one of the retirees will independently reinvent something like it inside the first ten minutes and I'd rather build on him than contradict him, but I also don't want the night to collapse into him being right. And give me the wrong ideas to expect, each one tagged to the moment in the sequence where it should get shut down. The one I'm bracing for: they'll accept something like "you're one-ten-millionth responsible" and think that's a satisfying place to land, or worse, hear it as permission. I'm assuming the whole question of whether causing harm is even necessary for an act to be wrong is off the table at this level — but tell me what else goes in that "deliberately not touching it" pile, and how I should say out loud that I'm leaving it out rather than letting them think I've covered everything. Last thing: some way to tell by the end of the night whether the distinction actually landed, that isn't just them nodding at me politely.
