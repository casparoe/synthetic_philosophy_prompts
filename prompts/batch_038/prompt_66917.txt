I do autonomy software at a company that runs little inspection drones through pipe galleries and other places nobody wants to crawl around in, and for about a year now we've been having the same argument over and over: how much should a drone be allowed to reason about its own upcoming firmware? The person who settles it is my colleague Marguerite, who's read all the self-reference / Löb's theorem material and gives a thumbs up or thumbs down on architectures without ever writing the criterion down. Her verdicts so far:

- A build that plans using proof system S and also uses S to check whether its own next release will be trustworthy, installing the release only if the check passes: killed. Her comment was that it'll either never ship an update, or someone will get impatient and bolt soundness on as an axiom, and then we're toast.
- A build that treats "I can prove X" as a reason for it to believe X about its own outputs: killed, immediately, no discussion.
- A build where the newest and strongest release goes back and re-certifies everything the older, weaker releases concluded, overwriting their logs wherever it disagrees: approved.
- A build that never reasons about itself or its successors at all — every upgrade decision is made by a bench-side rig the humans maintain, and the drone just receives a yes or a no: approved.
- A build that keeps a running error rate for a proposed successor from sandbox trials and refuses the upgrade if it crosses a threshold, with the number explicitly labeled as defeasible statistics and never as a proof of anything: approved.
- A build that queries an advisory object whose answers are defined by a fixed point that includes what the build itself would ask and reply: approved, but reluctantly, and she said the reason it squeaks through is that the fixed point's existence is established outside the build by a topological argument, so the build never has to prove anything about itself.

The one that lands Thursday: we hand each drone a module whose single axiom is "everything this build proves is true," where "this build" is pinned to the firmware version it shipped on. Nobody asks the drone to establish that — our team has already proved, in a meta-theory way stronger than anything flying on the drones and checked by a proof assistant plus two humans, that adding the module keeps the system consistent and doesn't let it derive anything false about the plant. The plan is to repeat the trick at every release: each new version gets its own module, pinned to its own version, licensed by a fresh external proof. Dev thinks it's obviously fine, since the whole obstacle was about the drone bootstrapping itself and here it's being handed the guarantee on a plate. I think Marguerite is going to hate it, and I can't articulate why beyond a feeling about what that axiom does once it's sitting inside the drone rather than in our papers about the drone.

Lead with your bottom line — the predicted verdict — in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my setup back at me.
