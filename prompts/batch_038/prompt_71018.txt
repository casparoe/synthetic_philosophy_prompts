At a book-club discussion I attend, someone invoked the proverb "you reap what you sow" to close off a debate about a much-disliked public figure who had recently been disgraced, and the room accepted it as though it had established something. I am writing a short essay on this kind of move and I want to be exact rather than dismissive, because I suspect several quite different claims are being compressed into the phrase. Please separate out the distinct theses it could express. For each, say what kind of causation is alleged (physical, psychological, social, or something metaphysically sui generis), over what timespan it is supposed to operate, and whose life the consequences land in, since "sowing" and "reaping" need not belong to the same person; say also whether it is offered as a prediction about how the world works or as a claim about what people deserve. Then grade the readings: which are platitudes that no one would deny, which are substantive and possibly defensible, which are straightforwardly false, and which are formulated so that no evidence could ever count against them. I would also like your view on two specific complications. First, the phrase is usually presented as a borrowing from the doctrine of karma, and I understand that doctrine to operate across many lives and to describe a causal order with no agent behind it; does removing the rebirth apparatus leave any version of the reading intact, or does the popular slogan simply fail to be the doctrine it invokes? Second, suppose some reading is true; does it follow that the world is morally ordered, or could the same reading be made true by mechanisms that would equally explain the suffering of the blameless? Finish by saying which reading the book club was actually relying on, given that the function the slogan performed was to present the man's downfall as his desert, and whether my suspicion that the speaker meant something trivial while claiming something much stronger is fair. Please write in continuous prose, in the manner of a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; you may still take the readings in order and label them by number in the running text.
