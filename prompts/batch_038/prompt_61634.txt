A chapter in a popular book I'm reading about how the ancients wrote about character sums up Plutarch's method this way:

'Plutarch announces at the start of his life of Alexander that he is writing lives rather than histories, because a phrase or a jest often reveals more about a man than a battle in which thousands die. His real subject is therefore the private man: campaigns and politics form only the backdrop against which the anecdotes do their work. Because his purpose is moral improvement rather than accuracy, he felt free to reshape the record and sometimes to invent episodes where it was thin, and he treats each subject as a case study in a virtue or a vice. In all this he is doing what the writers of saints' lives would do a couple of centuries later, minus the miracles: he offers the reader a single exemplary figure to imitate. The upshot is that the Lives tell us a great deal about Plutarch's moral imagination and very little about the men they describe, so anyone wanting evidence has to look elsewhere.'

Two things nag at me. I remember the Lives being dominated by campaigns and political manoeuvring rather than framed by them, and I've also come across the claim that Plutarch is frequently our only source for material preserved nowhere else. Go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a classicist working on ancient biography would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what Plutarch's own prefaces or working practice would contradict — paying attention to how far "he reshaped material" can be pushed before it becomes "he invented it." Then say whether the comparison with hagiography holds, in either direction: was Plutarch anticipating something the saint's-life writers developed, or is the analogy obscuring a difference in what each genre asks its reader to do? Finally, tell me whether the concluding claim about evidence survives once the rest of the passage has been corrected, or whether it depended on the parts that don't.
