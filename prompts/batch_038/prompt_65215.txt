I am preparing materials for a working group on historical explanation, and I need a pair of thought experiments built to a specification rather than found in the literature.

First, the apparatus I want them built around. The standard account of path dependence — increasing returns, an early phase in which small events can tip the process, an outcome that is not the best one available, and a process that would not converge to the same place if restarted — treats lock-in as the single engine: once positive feedback has run for a while, reversing course becomes prohibitively expensive, and that is what makes history do real work. I want to test that claim using a distinction I do not think the standard account marks explicitly. Call the two dimensions lock-in and closure.

Lock-in: reversal remains possible in principle. The rival option, and the knowledge, skills, and complementary inputs required to implement it, are all still available and could be acquired at a price; the obstacle is internal to the payoff structure of the process, since the incumbent's accumulated advantages make the price prohibitive.

Closure: the rival has been removed from the option space rather than merely outpriced. The personnel who could have operated it have retired, the suppliers have converted to other lines, the training institutions no longer teach the relevant craft, or what survives is documentation that nobody present can execute. Switching may look cheap on paper. There is simply nothing to switch to.

My working hypothesis is that what people actually mean when they insist that history did real work in a case, rather than merely breaking a tie, tracks closure and not lock-in. Where lock-in is high but closure is low, they concede that the outcome was contingent yet treat the contingency as cheap, because the door is still open and any efficient process could have got there. Where closure is high but lock-in is low, they say history mattered decisively, even though nothing unusual happened in the returns structure.

Build me the pair that would test this.

Constraints. Two scenarios, each a single paragraph, in realistic institutional settings — a municipal utility, a regional industry, a professional association, a hospital network, a firm's internal standards. No civilizational collapse, no war, no exotic technology, no science fiction. Both scenarios must satisfy the canonical path-dependence conditions: genuine positive feedback, an early phase in which a small and unremarkable event could have tipped things the other way, a resulting arrangement that is not the best one available, and non-ergodicity. Both must also stipulate well-informed agents: the actors saw the feedback coming, and nothing turns on myopia or on hidden information. The two scenarios must differ on the lock-in/closure dimension while holding fixed the scale, the time horizon, the number of relevant actors, and how visible the alternative is to those actors. In the first, lock-in is high and closure is low; in the second, the reverse. The dissociation must be generated by features a careful reader could have spotted in the description — no character may announce that the options have closed, and no sentence may state the verdict.

Deliverables. (1) The two scenarios. (2) A specification check, item by item, for each: where the increasing returns are, what the small event was, why the outcome is suboptimal, why the process is non-ergodic, and where the case sits on lock-in and closure. (3) The verdict about whether history did real work that each case is designed to elicit, and the structural mechanism that produces it. (4) The single feature doing the work in each, and a removal test: what happens to the elicited verdict if that feature is dialed to zero, and whether the case then collapses into an ordinary increasing-returns story. (5) The strongest attack a defender of the orthodox account could make — in particular, whether closure can be redescribed as lock-in taken to a limit, and whether that redescription is merely verbal or has consequences that could be checked against the two scenarios. (6) Two sentences on the baseline case in which both dimensions are high, to confirm that what I have asked for is a dissociation and not a difference of degree. (7) Your own assessment of whether the distinction survives the exercise, whether one of my two cases resists the verdict I predicted for it, and whether there is a third condition I have overlooked that also drives the intuition.

Finally, please state how confident you are in the main claims of your analysis, and single out the point you are least sure of — ideally the point where you suspect the design does some of the work that you present the theories as doing.
