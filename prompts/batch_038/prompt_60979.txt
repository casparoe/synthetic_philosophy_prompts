THE QUESTION (two hours, closed book):

"Pain asymbolia is often said to show that the badness of pain does not consist in how pain feels. Set out that argument as clearly as you can, making explicit what it assumes about the relation between pain's sensory features and its affective ones. Then explain and evaluate two responses available to someone who holds that pain is intrinsically bad. For each response, state explicitly which of the following it accepts and which it rejects: (i) the asymbolic patient feels a pain; (ii) that pain is unpleasant; (iii) pain's unpleasantness is its badness."

THE RUBRIC (20 points): 6 for an accurate statement of the syndrome and of the argument built on it; 8 for two responses that are correctly characterized and genuinely evaluated rather than merely described; 4 for explicit and correct handling of (i), (ii) and (iii) under each response; 2 for organization and precision of expression.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

"Pain is the standing example of something bad in itself: bad not because of what it causes but because of what it is like to undergo. Pain asymbolia is the case most often wheeled out against that picture.

The syndrome is associated with lesions of the insula and retroinsular region, more often on the left, and a comparable dissociation can be produced by cingulotomy and, in a milder and reversible form, by high doses of morphine. What makes it philosophically interesting is a split inside a single modality. The sensory side is intact: patients localize the stimulus, grade its intensity, distinguish sharp from burning, and use the word pain readily. The affective side is gone: they do not withdraw or guard the limb, they may smile or laugh while being pricked, they will let a hand rest on something hot, and they report that the pain is there but does not bother them. The standard two-dimensional taxonomy of pain — sensory-discriminative on one side, affective-motivational on the other — supplies the description: asymbolia looks like what happens when the second dimension is destroyed and the first is left standing.

The argument built on this runs as follows. If pain's badness were a matter of how pain feels — if unpleasantness were just one more feature of the sensory phenomenology, sitting alongside location and intensity — then it should be impossible to keep that phenomenology and lose only the badness. Asymbolia shows that it is possible. So unpleasantness is something other than the felt quality of pain, and since the felt quality is all the asymbolic keeps, the badness of pain cannot be intrinsic to pain. It must be instrumental: pain is bad because it signals tissue damage, and unpleasantness is merely the device that gets the signal acted on. The point is not academic. Asymbolic patients, like children born unable to feel pain, accumulate burns, fractures and untreated infections and die young, which suggests that what has been knocked out is not the aversiveness of pain but pain itself, and that the intrinsic-badness story has been trading on a confusion between the two.

Two responses are open to someone who wants to keep intrinsic badness.

The first denies (i). On this view — Grahek is the usual name for it — the affective dimension is not an extra attached to a sensory core but part of what makes a state a pain; a state with the sensory character and no affect is not a pain at all, but at most a representation of bodily disturbance. The response therefore rejects (i), and so (ii) and (iii) never get a chance to apply. Its cost is plain: it renders a sincere, competent first-person report systematically false, which is an unattractive commitment for a theory of pain, and it leaves unanswered rather than resolved the question of why the state that remains is not bad.

The second response accepts (i) but relocates unpleasantness. On the motivational account associated with David Bain, unpleasantness is not a raw feel but something like an experienced desire, or an imperative, that the pain stop: a property of experience, but a motivational property rather than a qualitative one. On this view the asymbolic's pain still carries that imperative — the content that it is to be stopped is intact — but the patient is simply not moved by it, which is why the pain seems not to bother them. So (ii) is true even in asymbolia, and (iii) is preserved in the sense that the badness of pain just is the experienced demand to stop it, a genuine feature of the experience rather than a downstream effect of it. This response is more conservative than the first, since it leaves the patient's report intact; its weakness is that it owes an account of why a demand that is present but unfelt should count for anything to the patient who has it.

Between them, the two responses show that the asymbolia argument depends on the assumption that unpleasantness, if it were phenomenal at all, could not come apart from sensory phenomenology — an assumption neither side grants."
