I'm in year three of a philosophy of biology PhD and I'm stuck on a chapter that has to engage with Dennett's crane and skyhook vocabulary, partly because my supervisor is fond of it and partly because the ML researcher I share an office with keeps insisting that skyhook is just a philosopher's word for the fact that we assumed the training data. I've definately read the relevant stretch of Darwin's Dangerous Idea more than once and I still can't say what the distinction is doing that isn't already done by older, duller complaints: that an explanation begs the question, that it explains the explanandum by presupposing it, that it leaves out a level of organisation, that it's empirically unsupported. So the first thing I want is an explanation, as precise as you can make it, of what content the crane/skyhook pair adds over and above those familiar charges — and separately, what greedy reductionism adds over and above "this reduction hasn't been earned" or "you skipped a level". If the honest answer is that it adds nothing but rhetoric, I want that said, with the reasoning, rather than a charitable reconstruction I can't use.

Second thing, and this is the bit I actually care about. Someone in my reading group claimed that crane-talk works as an immunising strategy: if a reduction fails you say the reducer was greedy, if it succeeds you say the crane was there all along, so the vocabulary never sticks its neck out. I've been trying to build a criterion that would resist that, and I keep landing on something like this — call it the design-debt test: an appeal to X is a skyhook relative to a question Q iff accepting X as the answer to Q leaves you owing a design-historical explanation of X of the same sort you were offering for Q's subject matter, whereas X is a crane iff accepting it discharges that debt rather than adding to it. My worry is that this makes the label hostage to the question, so the same mechanism flips: natural selection looks like a crane for the vertebrate eye and more like a debt-generator for the origin of the first replicators, and human engineering looks like a skyhook when you ask why the bridge stands but like a crane when you ask how the lineage of bridge-designers got so good. So please work through whether the test does the job or whether I've just rebranded the old begging-the-question objection, and then run it (or a better one you propose) on three cases where I can't tell which side they land on: the origin of self-replication from prebiotic chemistry; an innate, unexplained language faculty of the Chomskyan sort, which is posited with no design history behind it but is supposed to speed up the acquisition of competence; and a human-designed evolutionary search, e.g. a genetic algorithm used to produce a circuit layout, where the crane is itself a designed artefact. For each case say which label you'd give it and what your reason ultimately turns on.

Structure it as: Bottom line (four or five sentences, up front, no throat-clearing); What the vocabulary adds (~300 words); The immunising-strategy worry (~350 words, and please steelman it properly before responding to it); Three hard cases (~400 words, a paragraph each); What survives (~200 words on whether there's a version of the distinction worth using in a thesis chapter or whether I should quietly drop it). British English, and I don't need a reading list — I've got the book and my supervisor has got the rest. Last thing: if you think I've muddled two distinct objections into one, or that my design-debt test misreads what Dennett is up to, or that the whole framing is a straw man that no serious reductionist would recognise, then say so plainly at the very top instead of politely answering the question I asked.
