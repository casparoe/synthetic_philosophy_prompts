So — I write novels, not philosophy, which is presumably why I said yes to this and am only now, three days out, properly scared of it.

Here's the setup. My last book is set in an invented country about fifteen years after a stretch of communal killing (it isn't about anywhere in particular, though every reviewer insists it is). The peace settlement in the book contains what I call the quiet clause: the truth commission's own archive is sealed for fifty years, it becomes a criminal offence to publish the names of informants from that period, the school curriculum skips the decade entirely, and the new national day of remembrance is written so that it commemorates nobody in particular. My narrator is the archivist whose job is the shredding. She never blows the whistle. I've been getting grief about that ending for two years.

On Thursday I give the opening talk at a two-day workshop at a law school's human rights center, theme is archives and transitional justice. I'm the only non-academic on the plenary. The room will be maybe sixty people: mostly law profs and legal historians who work on truth commissions and right-to-truth doctrine, a handful of psychologists who study how trauma gets passed down generations, one moral philosopher whose whole subject is collective memory and who will be far better read than me, and a lot of grad students who've been told that novels are a soft way into hard questions and want to check whether that's true. Twenty-five minutes, then forty-five of questions.

What I'm going to argue, roughly, so you know what they'll be shooting at:

- People lump together *not commemorating* something and *actively destroying the record of it*. My book is about the second, harder case, and my claim is that even that one can be right. I'd rather hold that line than retreat to the easy version where a country just doesn't build a museum.
- The duty to remember always gets stated as a duty of "we," but the we keeps sliding. The survivors, their grandchildren, and me in a safe country reading about it are not the same duty-bearer. I want to say the force of the duty falls off steeply with distance from the harm — which has the awkward consequence that the people who most need permission to forget are the ones closest to it, and the ones best placed to insist on remembering are the ones who pay nothing for it.
- I don't want to lean on never-again instrumentalism at all. Partly because I suspect it's weak, partly because even if remembering did prevent the next round, that wouldn't settle what you're allowed to demand of the generation that has to live inside the remembering.
- Records aren't neutral storage. Once an archive is public and citable it becomes ammunition — names, addresses, who testified against whom. The book's claim and mine is that a society running on rumor and family story might be doing something functionally better than one running on a litigable public record. I know how that sounds out loud.
- There's an asymmetry in the harms. Telling has costs that are concentrated and immediate: the coalition collapses, the neighbors resume, somebody gets killed. Forgetting has costs that are diffuse and deferred: nobody can prosecute in thirty years, a grandchild can't grieve properly. We're terrible at weighting the second against the first, but that isn't a reason to pretend it's not on the scale.

I suspect four and two are where I'm softest, but you may see worse.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones a general festival audience would give me — ranked by how much damage they'd do to the talk. Phrase each the way that audience would phrase it, in their vocabulary: the legal people will reach for doctrine and for the difference between not prosecuting and not recording, the psychologists will reach for transmission and silence, the philosopher will want to know whose memory and whether a group can owe anything at all. For each one: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and a note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me what I should concede in the first three minutes to take the sting out of the two worst ones, and what the single reply is that has to land or the whole talk collapses.

Two constraints. No reading lists or citations — I'm not going to read anything between now and Thursday, and I can't cite case law from a stage, so every reply has to work with no footnotes attached. And finish with the question I'm hoping doesn't come up. I have a guess (something along the lines of: would you be so keen on forgetting if it were your family in the sealed boxes), but you might see a worse one, and I'd rather hear it from you than from the third row.
