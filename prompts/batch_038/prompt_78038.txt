I run a lit-and-philosophy reading group (grad students, half of them over from English) and next month we're on Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go alongside some epistemic injustice reading — mostly Fricker, plus Dotson on testimonial quieting and smothering. I want to stress-test a hunch before I write up the discussion sheet, and I'd rather you tell me it's overreading than be polite about it.

The hunch: Fricker's two species both assume the harm is a gap. Either the hearer docks your credibility, or the shared stock of concepts is missing the one you'd need to make sense of your own life. Hailsham isn't a gap, though. The students are up to their necks in concepts: carer, donor, complete, possible, the Gallery, deferrals, guardians. All of it is supplied by the apparatus that's farming them, all of it is more or less accurate, and none of it lets anyone say the thing that actually needs saying. Kathy narrates the whole book inside that vocabulary and never once reaches for a word like killed or harvested — she'd have to, if the concept were there, but it isn't, and the novel is writen in the absence of it. So: is that a distinct kind of wrong, not a hermeneutical lacuna but something like a hermeneutical overdose — resources plentiful, functional, and calibrated so the underlying fact stays unsayable? In my notes I've been calling it hermeneutical capture, which I know is a grandiose label. Does it earn its keep, or is it Dotson's contributory injustice and Medina's active ignorance wearing a new hat? Straight answer please, including whether something already in the literature does the job better.

Second, and this is where I suspect the book is ahead of my framework. Miss Lucy and Miss Emily disagree about exactly this. Lucy cracks — she tells the students plainly what they are and what's coming, and she's out of the school for it; Miss Emily, at the end, defends the gradual half-hidden method as the only reason they got anything resembling a childhood. Fricker has no slot for that dispute. For her the closing of a hermeneutical gap is an unmixed good and the injustice is structural, nobody's fault. Ishiguro gives us two people who both think they're serving the students' interests, one of whom is right that full disclosure would have wrecked them. And the kicker: when Lucy does tell them, almost nothing happens. The students absorb it and go back to their lessons, because they already knew. I want the reading to sit in that rather than tidy it away — is Miss Emily's position actually defensible on epistemic-injustice grounds, or is it just the oppression with better manners? And what do we make of the fact that the truth-teller gets sacked and the euphemism-manager gets a quiet retirement?

Third, maybe the strongest bit: Tommy's tantrums as a kid. He knows something he can't put into words, so he screams and thrashes, and the response is for the other children to pin him down until he's calm. That reads to me like the sharpest picture of testimonial quieting in any novel — the utterance is there, it's just not in a form the audience will take up, and the audience's fix is suppression rather than uptake. Tell me whether the book licenses that or whether I'm doing all the work myself.

What I want back is criticism, not a framework summary: where the vocabulary genuinely lights the novel up, where it's inert, and where the book beats the theory — what does Ishiguro show about the politics of euphemism that a journal article can't? Point me at the specific scenes you'd have the group reread, and be blunt about the label. I dont need sources or a reading list, just the argument and the scenes.
