I am in the third year of a novel that follows one family across three generations, and I have arrived at a conceptual difficulty that further research into the period will not resolve for me.

The first part is set in a mountain village in the early twentieth century, where a slight offered to a family must be answered in public or the family ceases to exist as a social fact. The second part is set in the nineteen-seventies, where a descendant of that family argues, in a courtroom and in a faculty meeting, that every person carries a worth no insult can reach. The third part is set in the present and turns on a grievance aired online, in which the standing of the wronged party seems to be built up by the wrong itself, and in which the wrong only appears to count once it has been witnessed.

My problem is phenomenological rather than historical. When I write the first part, my honor-bound characters keep acquiring the second part's psychology: they suffer inwardly, they are troubled by their own estimation of themselves, they want to be respected as persons. I suspect this is a category error, but I cannot say what precisely the difference in kind consists in, and until I can, the village scenes read as modern people in period costume.

So I would like you to compare honor and dignity as I have sketched them — not as successive eras, but as two rival accountings of where a person's standing comes from and what is capable of damaging it. I want the comparison organized around the following questions, and I want you to commit to a position on each rather than report what each side of the debate has said.

1. Audience. Honor is supposed to reside in the eyes of others, dignity in the conscience; yet a dignity that no one acknowledges appears to do no social work whatsoever. Is the difference one of kind — a different source of standing — or one of scope, so that dignity is simply honor whose audience has been expanded to everyone and, ultimately, to no one in particular? Take the stranger, the exile, the person whom nobody knows and nobody is obliged to notice: what exactly does dignity confer on him that honor withholds?

2. Loss and repair. Honor can be lost and only imperfectly recovered; dignity, we are assured, is inalienable. But if dignity genuinely cannot be diminished, what is it that gets violated when someone violates it — and what does the violated person actually have left to complain of? There is also a consequence for my plotting that I want you to confront directly. Honor is negotiable: a feud can be closed by compensation, by a public apology, by a marriage, by first blood, by a price agreed between families. Dignity admits no price and therefore, perhaps, no settlement. Is the dignitarian order, precisely because it cannot be bought off, in some respects less forgiving and less capable of closure than the honor order it replaced? Defend or reject that inversion.

3. Scarcity. The standard claim is that honor is a limited good, so that one man's ascent is another's descent, while dignity is non-rivalrous and can be extended to all at no cost to anyone. Test both halves. Can honor be gained without someone losing it? And is dignity non-competitive in practice, given that recognition, standing before a court, and the authority to be believed have all had to be won and remain unevenly distributed? If the answer is that dignity is competitive in fact while professing not to be, say what follows from the gap between profession and practice.

4. The structure of the response to insult. In honor, the insult must be answered because the insulter counts: his opinion is a real force in the world, and ignoring him is what does the damage. In dignity, the insult is ideally beneath answer, because the insulter lacks any standing to diminish anyone — the correct posture is to remain unmoved and to route the matter to an institution. Compare these two responses as forms of social violence. Which is the more annihilating of the two, the blow or the refusal to be moved, and what must each party believe about the other for the response to work?

Then, drawing on all four: which of the first two is the closer relative of the moral register of my third part, the culture of publicly aired grievance? There is an argument I keep encountering, that this register is honor's competitive structure — scarce status, dependence on an audience, escalation in response to slight, standing won and lost in front of witnesses — dressed in dignity's universalist vocabulary, so that the injury is invoked as evidence of moral standing rather than as a debt to be repaid. Argue the case for or against that genealogy, and then give me something operational: what would a single scene have to show for me to know which lineage it belongs to?

Finally, and briefly: tell me where the honor–dignity contrast, as it is usually stated, is a caricature, and what a novelist most reliably gets wrong when she attempts to write from inside honor.

I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of thinkers, or a survey of positions; I have no way to check sources at the moment and would much rather have your reasoning. What I do want is a verdict wherever I have asked for one.
