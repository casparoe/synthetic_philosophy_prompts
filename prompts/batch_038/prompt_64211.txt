I am putting together a session for a graduate seminar in ancient political philosophy in which we read the first two books of the *Laws* alongside a contemporary policy case, and I want the pairing to hinge on a real question rather than on a cute juxtaposition. The *Laws* is striking about wine: the Athenian Stranger observes that anger, lust, insolence, and covetousness — and also wealth, beauty, and strength — intoxicate a person and turn his head, and that wine therefore offers "a cheap and comparatively harmless test" of such conditions together with a means "of affording practice in them" (649d); better to probe a morose and savage man's disposition with the help of Dionysus, he suggests, than to risk finding out about it through money transactions (649e). The drinking is meant to be organized rather than free: there must be "sober and sedate men to act as commanders over the un-sober" (671d), since the drinker's soul grows soft and heated and ductile, as it was when young (671b), and wine is elsewhere treated as a gift bestowed "as a medicine potent against the crabbedness of old age" (666b). I am aware that these are the words of a character and not automatically of Plato, and I would rather the seminar not collapse that distinction in either direction.

The modern case I want to set beside this is the spread of state-licensed psilocybin services — a screened client, a trained facilitator who sits with them for several hours in a comfortable room, no prescription and no diagnosis required — along with the regulatory momentum behind psilocybin-assisted treatment for depression that has not responded to ordinary care, and the quieter fashion of sub-perceptual microdosing among people who work long hours at desks. A colleague of mine insists the mapping is obvious and the verdict therefore predictable: the facilitator is the sober commander, the session is the regulated symposium, and Plato would welcome the new wine. I doubt it is that simple. The Stranger's regimen is a public test of civic temperament conducted in company, whereas the modern practice is a private remedy for one person's suffering; and a substance said to disclose truths and to dissolve the sense of self is not evidently the same instrument as one whose entire rationale is to render the soul pliable for training.

Suppose, then, that Plato were shown these practices in detail — the licensing regimes, the facilitator training, the clinical trials, the microdosing — and asked what a well-ordered city should make of them. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: each item should state one judgment he would make, ordered from the one he would most confidently endorse to the one he would most firmly reject, and at least one item should address the gap between Plato the author and the Athenian Stranger as his mouthpiece, while at least one should identify a feature of the modern practice that has no counterpart at all in the wine-regimen.
