I am assembling the opening reading for a workshop on rationality and cooperation, and I would like a poem that sets the problem rather than a lecture that states it. The problem is the centipede game.

Write a pantoum of six quatrains. Set it at exactly six decision points: two players alternating, a pot that grows by a coin each time it is passed along, and either player able on their turn to take the pot and end things, keeping the larger share. Keep the scene concrete — a table, coins, two people — and keep the vocabulary of the discipline out of it. No line may contain the words equilibrium, dominant, rational, or induction. Rhyme or do not, as you prefer, but keep the lines short enough to be carried whole through repetition.

Let the form do the reasoning. Move backward: the first quatrain stands at the last decision point, where taking is simply better and there is nothing left to think about, and each quatrain thereafter steps one node closer to the beginning, inheriting its predecessor's second and fourth lines as its own first and third. I want that inheritance to be the contagion of the argument — the same sentence arriving at each new node and finding it already decided. Someone who knows the game should be able to say which decision point each quatrain is standing at. Close the circle as the form requires, so that the final quatrain's second line is the poem's opening line and its fourth line is the third line of the opening quatrain: the walk ends at the first node with the last node's sentence still in its mouth.

Two further constraints, and these are the ones that matter most:

- A pantoum's repeated lines are supposed to change meaning on the return. Make at least two inherited lines do that work — on first appearance a comparison of what each player would get, on reappearance a claim about what the other person must be like, given that they are still sitting there.
- One quatrain must register the moment the argument is handed an observation it cannot accommodate: a player asked to move after the other has done what the reasoning has already ruled out. Do not resolve this inside the poem. Let the inherited lines keep coming anyway.

After the poem, add a note of no more than half a page in which you commit. Say plainly whether you think the backward-induction conclusion is right for this game, and if it is not, name the specific step that fails — whether it is the counterfactual reasoning at nodes the reasoning itself has ruled out, the assumption that both players' rationality stays common knowledge after one of them has acted against it, or something about how the payoffs are being described. Pick one and argue for it; I do not want a survey of the positions with the choice left to me. Say also where the poem takes liberties with the game or leans on a reading of the puzzle that a defender of the standard argument would reject. And answer this question directly: would the argument survive if the players were told only that the game ends somewhere, not where — if the final node existed but its location were not common knowledge?
