I am presenting a paper at a criminal law and legal theory workshop in three weeks, on the so-called responsibility gap for autonomous weapon systems. My thesis is deliberately narrow, and I want to defend it as narrowly as possible:

First, when a lethal autonomous system kills unlawfully in virtue of behavior that emerged from its training and optimization rather than from anything a human specified, tested for, or could reasonably have foreseen, none of the ordinary routes to criminal responsibility reaches a person. The operator lacked the relevant mental state; the commander neither knew nor could have known; the engineer's causal contribution is real but not culpable; the manufacturer's exposure is civil and regulatory rather than criminal. Each proposed repair — stretching command responsibility to a constructive-knowledge standard, imposing strict criminal liability on those who deploy, treating the firm or the system itself as a bearer of responsibility — either fails to reach the case or reaches it only by punishing someone who does not deserve punishment, which on my view is a greater moral cost than the gap it closes.

Second, the conclusion is therefore not that we require a new doctrine, but that a certain class of wartime harms will be nobody's fault in the criminal sense, and that this residual belongs on the ledger a state consults when weighing deployment, alongside expected civilian casualties — not in a footnote as a paradox to be dissolved.

Third, I separate this from the broader talk of an accountability gap and claim only the criminal one: duties to investigate, compensate, and constrain design survive intact, falling on institutions rather than on blameworthy individuals.

The audience is about fifteen legal philosophers and criminal law scholars. Two are committed retributivists who regard deserved punishment as a positive good and will resist any conclusion that leaves a grave harm unblamed. One works on the internal architecture of the mental-state requirements and is hostile to moralized glosses on technical legal concepts. One served as a military prosecutor and suspects the gap is illusory, because in courts-martial practice negligence and command responsibility are far more elastic than my paper concedes. One specializes in corporate criminal liability and believes the analogy to firms accomplishes everything my argument says nothing can. There is also a scholar of victims' standing who will want to know what political work the phrase nobody's fault is doing. Nobody in the room works on machine learning, so I do not expect technical objections.

Prepare my question period. Give me the objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument, phrased in the idiom its proponent would use, each with a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Mark which objections share a common root and can be answered together; state what I should concede at the outset to purchase credibility, what I must not concede at any price; and identify the single question I am hoping nobody asks.
