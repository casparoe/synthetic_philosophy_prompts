I'm TA-ing a small reading group on uncertainty this term — mixed bag of philosophy PhDs, two stats people, and one economist who keeps insisting that all you need for the Ellsberg-type cases is a Beta(0,0)-style prior — and for session three I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts people into a position on imprecise credences, rather than a list of positions for them to nod at. The trouble is that every summary I can find organizes the debate around one axis (precise vs. imprecise) and then quietly slides over to a completely different axis (how do you decide once your credences are a set), and my students end up thinking that accepting the Ellsberg intuition commits them to maximin, which it plainly doesn't. So here's the ask: build me a tree, maybe seven to nine branch nodes and six to nine leaves, where every node is a concrete case or a claim a first-year student could react to without knowing which leaf they're walking toward. No node is allowed to be a label question — nothing like 'do you think your credences should be a set of probabilities?' — it has to be something like: here's a coin whose bias was chosen by a mechanism you don't understand, and here's the same coin where you're just told it's fair but you've never seen it flipped; do your attitudes toward bets on the two differ, and if so, is the difference in your evidence or in your temperament?

Some of the distinctions I want forced onto the tree, because they're exactly the ones people conflate: whether imprecision is a claim about one's own representational state or a claim about the world being genuinely indeterminate; whether the credal set has to be convex, and what breaks if you let it be a disjunction of two sharp priors instead; whether countable additivity and conglomerability survive (I want a leaf for 'sharp but non-conglomerable,' because people act like it's impossible and it isn't); whether updating must be conditioning-only or whether some rules legitimately let you re-decide how wide to be after seeing data, i.e. dilation as bug versus dilation as feature; whether imprecision is ever rationally *required* or merely permitted when you're imprecise; and the reflection question, whether your present range commits you to your future self landing inside it. Then treat the decision rule as genuinely orthogonal: make the tree sort on whether the agent ends up choosing by maximin, by Gamma-maximin, by interval dominance, by Levi-style E-admissibility, or by picking one member of the set after the fact, and say explicitly which combinations of 'imprecise credences' and 'single-option rule' are behaviorally indistinguishable from a sharp Bayesian with a pessimism parameter. If two leaves can't actually be separated by any answer pattern my students could give, flag that pair rather than pretending the tree is finer than it is.

For each leaf give a name if one exists in the literature, and if the cell of the space is empty give it a descriptive name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment; also tell me, for every unoccupied combination you notice, whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because nobody wants it, or because nobody's bothered. Finally, run me through your own tree: on the unknown-bias coin I really can't produce a single number and I don't think thats a confession of laziness; I'm unmoved by dilation, I'd rather my interval widen than fake precision; but I do insist that conditioning is the only legitimate update rule, no rule that lets me renegotiate how imprecise to be after the fact; and when a decision rule leaves me unable to choose between two options I find that intolerable, so I'd rather risk being money-pumped than be left with no choice. End the whole thing with a last line of the exact form `Verdict: <leaf name>` telling me where I land, and if my answers turn out to sit in a cell your tree can't separate, say so in that line instead.
