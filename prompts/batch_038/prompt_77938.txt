I serve on the animal-welfare committee of a company that farms crickets and black soldier fly larvae for feed, and for two years I have been applying a rule of thumb I have never managed to justify. I would like to know what it commits me to, and which of my verdicts it cannot keep.

The rule of thumb, which I will call the one-ledger rule: an organism's experience carries moral weight in proportion to the degree to which its states are pooled into a single running account — one place where a present harm can be weighed against a remembered or anticipated good, and where behaviour is steered by that pooling rather than locally, event by event. More pooling, more weight; no pooling, no weight.

My verdicts:

1. Dog — full weight, uncontroversially.
2. Laying hen confined for a laying cycle — full weight; and what I object to is not the crampedness as such but the frustration of behaviour that presupposes memory and planning: nest-site selection, recognition of individuals, the ordering of a day.
3. Adult octopus — full weight, though uneasily. Most of its neurons lie in the arms, and the arms solve problems locally. If pooling is the criterion, this may be several partial ledgers rather than one large one; I give it full weight regardless.
4. Honeybee — small but nonzero weight. I would accept a modest cost to avoid large-scale bee suffering.
5. Adult cricket — less than a bee, on the grounds that the bee's model of its world is more elaborate. I suspect this is just me ranking intelligence, and I would like to be told so if it is.
6. Black soldier fly larva — no weight. A gut with a skin; nothing is pooled.
7. A nematode — no weight, despite learning, avoidance, and the integration of odour with feeding state across some three hundred neurons. This is the verdict I most want to be argued out of, if it can be.
8. A decapitated cockroach whose body walks for hours — no weight.
9. Ascidian: the free-swimming larva gets a hair of weight; the adult, which settles, attaches, and gives up most of the neural apparatus the larva had, gets none.
10. A person under general anaesthesia for four hours — full weight, undiminished. Likewise a dreamless sleeper, and a hibernating marmot in January.
11. A split-brain patient whose hemispheres issue conflicting commands — one person, one ledger, full weight. I refuse to count them twice.
12. A small reef fish reported to pass the mark test, a result other researchers have disputed — I give it more weight than the dog, on the strength of that test alone, which I know is a strange thing to do.
13. A pigeon drilled by operant conditioning until it uses a mirror to peck at a mark on its own body — no weight at all.
14. A robot insect with an onboard world-model that plans routes, avoids obstacles, and returns to charge when depleted — no weight.
15. A lentil-sized cortical organoid in a dish, showing sporadic coordinated electrical activity — no verdict, and I will not invent one.

Two tensions I can see and cannot resolve.

(a) 10 against 7. If weight tracks how much is being pooled right now, the anaesthetised person is a zero for four hours and the nematode a small positive throughout, so my ranking is backwards. Every repair I have tried turns on capacity rather than exercise; but dispositional pooling is precisely what I want to deny the robot in 14.

(b) 12 against 13. Same behavioural evidence, opposite verdicts. The only difference I can name is that the fish's model was built by its own history in a real sea, whereas the pigeon's was installed by an experimenter for the purpose of passing the test. If that difference carries the weight, it must be doing work elsewhere as well, and I do not know whether I want it to.

What I want from you:

- State precisely the principle, or the short list of candidate principles, that best fits this pattern of verdicts. Where a clause is needed, say which numbered case the clause exists to accommodate.
- For each candidate, state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then take a side: is the complexity required to save every verdict worth paying, or should I give one or two up? Name the single verdict you think I must reverse, and give the argument that forces it.
- Rule on the three cases where I have no settled view: 15; an ant colony, in which the individuals look like near-automata but the colony allocates labour, navigates, and makes war — is the colony a ledger, or is it zero summed with zero?; and a person in advanced dementia whose today no longer coheres with yesterday, where I want to say full weight but my rule appears to require me to say the ledger has shrunk.
- Propose one further case, real or contrived, that would discriminate between your two leading candidates.

Please close with a single line in exactly this form, so that I can tabulate the answer:

Verdict: <short name of the principle you recommend, or REVISE> | reverse: <case number(s), or NONE>
