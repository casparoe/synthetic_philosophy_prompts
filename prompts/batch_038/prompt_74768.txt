For nearly two decades I have read the rationalist blogs and forums — the LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten lineage and its various offshoots — and I now help run an online reading group drawn from that community. My members are quantitatively fluent. They have done calibration training, they quote point probabilities for everything from the weather to geopolitical events, and a fair number of them hold that a rational agent must always be able to say a single number, since a range is either a hedge, a motte-and-bailey in waiting, or an admission of laziness. I should like to test that disposition rather than argue with it, and the instrument that suits my purpose is a diagnostic one.

Please approach this task as a formal epistemologist who has taught graduate seminars on the foundations of probability and who also has a genuine knack for writing quizzes that sort people by what they believe rather than by what they claim. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a brainstorming section, clearly marked and kept separate from the deliverable, in which you map the logical space: the dimensions along which theories of partial belief differ, the options available on each dimension, and which combinations are populated by positions someone actually defends and which are empty. Only then construct the tree, so that it is derived from the space rather than reverse-engineered from a list of labels.

The deliverable is a decision tree titled Which theory of partial belief do you actually hold?, pitched at the reader described above. Ten to twelve branch questions; nine to fourteen terminal leaves. The following constraints matter to me.

1. Every branch question must be a concrete case, or a specific claim about one, phrased in plain English and answerable in a sentence by someone who has never encountered the technical vocabulary. No question may contain the words imprecise, credal, dilation, second-order, or any -ism. The tree must sort readers by their responses to cases, never by their self-description.

2. The distinctions the tree must turn on include, at minimum: whether every question admits of a sharp probability at all; whether an unsharp state, if one is admitted, records something about the character of the evidence or merely the agent's ignorance; how such a state is represented — an interval, a set of probability functions, a distribution over distributions, or a sharp number accompanied by a separate measure of evidential weight; whether it is updated by conditioning every member, and what one makes of the possibility that the state thereby becomes less definite, or fails to become more definite even over a long run of observations; whether one accepts a requirement that one's beliefs be as sharp as one's evidence permits; and which rule governs choice when the state is not sharp.

3. Cases that must appear somewhere in the tree: a coin of unknown manufacture, described to the reader only as a coin from a factory about which they have been told nothing, with its chance of heads requested before any flip; an urn holding one hundred balls, each either red or black in an unknown proportion, with one draw to come; an instrument known to be reliable but whose error distribution has been only loosely characterised; and a case in which the reader is told in advance that, whichever of two observations they receive, their estimate of some hypothesis will afterwards be less definite than it is now.

4. Leaves that must appear: the precise Bayesian who reaches one half for the unknown coin by symmetry; the precise Bayesian who denies that any symmetry licenses a number and adopts a sharp prior regardless; the maximum-entropy position; more than one position that allows unsharp states but separates over the choice rule; the position on which the world is itself probabilistically indefinite rather than merely opaque to us; the position on which probabilities are sharp but carry a separate dimension of evidential weight; and at least one deflationary position on which unsharp states are an expressive convenience with no role in a theory of rational belief.

5. Each leaf carries the standard name of the position if it has one, or unnamed if nobody defends it, together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Any choice rule you name should get a one-line gloss, as half my group will not know them.

6. State which combinations your map treats as unoccupied, and diagnose each: is the cell empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply tried it?

7. Flag candidly any pair of leaves your tree cannot in practice separate, and say what further question might separate them — or whether the difference is merely verbal.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; my group has no library access and I have no interest in invented references. Attach a name to a position only where the label is genuinely standard and you are confident of it, and otherwise coin a descriptive one.

Present the tree as a numbered, indented Markdown outline, each node with a short identifier of the form Q3a so that answers can be recorded as a string. After the tree, append three worked examples: a reader's answers described in ordinary prose, and where they land. One of the three should be a reader whose answers fit no leaf at all, and you should say what the instrument ought to report in that case. Aim for roughly two thousand five hundred words in total.
