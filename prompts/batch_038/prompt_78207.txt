I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of psychiatry and I'm writing the final. I need one essay question worth 25 marks, answered in 90 minutes in class, and it has to hang off a case I've invented so that nobody can answer it from memory.

The case — feel free to tighten the prose but don't sand off the interesting bits:

A national insurer rolls out an algorithm that scores every member for "sustained distress risk" using claims history, pharmacy fills, appointment no-shows, and sleep data from anyone who's synced a wearable. Above a threshold you get a flag on your chart, visible to every clinician in the network, plus a nurse outreach call offering a short CBT program. Members are never told they've been flagged; from the inside, the whole intervention is just a phone call. Separately — and this is the part I actually care about — the term leaks. It turns up in a wellness marketing campaign, then on social media, and within a couple of years people are using it to describe themselves in online communities that have nothing to do with the insurer, and organizing around workplace accommodations under it.

So there are two feedback paths that a lazy answer will collapse into one: people getting classified without ever taking up the description, and a description getting taken up by people the classifier never picked out. The question should ask whether this is a looping effect in Hacking's sense, and it should be built so that "yes, obviously" and "no, not really" are both live answers, with the marks coming from tracking the two paths and saying what would settle the matter.

Please give me: the question exactly as I'd print it on the paper; a model answer of around 800 words; and a marking scheme broken into the specific moves that earn points, what each is worth, and a line each on how students typically botch it. I want the scheme to say out loud what separates an A from a B, where the B answer recites interactive kinds and indifferent kinds perfectly and still misses the crux. Also tell me which objections a student might raise that I should be happy to award marks for, and which ones sound clever but shouldnt.

No bibliography, obviously — I just want the argument.
