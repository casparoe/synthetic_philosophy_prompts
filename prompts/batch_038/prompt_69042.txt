So a bit of context, because this is a weird one. I've been the science-and-ideas columnist at a weekly magazine for about nine years now, but before that I did an MA in history and philosophy of science, and my old dissertation supervisor still runs the senior seminar in phil sci — twenty-ish students, mostly philosophy majors with a couple from history of science. She's out on leave this term and, apparently unable to let a past student escape, has asked me to set the take-home final, on the grounds that I'm the one who still remembers what an epicycle is. The term was on explanation and evidence: inference to the best explanation, the realism/anti-realism fight, the Bayesian reaction to all of it, and the whole prediction-versus-accommodation business. What I absolutely don't want is a question that can be answered by reciting the schema (surprising fact; hypothesis would explain it; so the hypothesis is probably true) and then listing explanatory virtues like a shopping list — scope, simplicity, unification, fecundity, done, A please. Half the class will have that memorised from the handout and will reproduce it whether or not it does any work. I want a question where the virtues actively pull against each other and where the interesting move is noticing that the thing that looks like the decisive consideration isn't.

Here's the case I've made up to hang it on — every number and every name is invented, I scribbled it on a train, so please don't go looking for it and don't cite any real study anywhere in what you produce. A colonial seabird, call it the cragbird, nests on eleven cliff colonies on a remote island. Between 2010 and 2019 the counted population fell about 70%. Three explanations were live by 2019. H1 (the vector story): an undescribed nest parasite arrived with timber shipments and is killing chicks. It was never properly observed — a few specimens recovered from one colony in 2017 were "consistent with" an unknown mite, barcoding inconclusive — but in a 2014 funding application, written before the collapse data existed, the group behind it predicted that colonies nearest the island's freight port would crash first and hardest, and that is exactly what happened: the port-adjacent colony was down 90% by 2016 while the far-north colonies were still stable. H2 (the food-web story): the collapse tracks the industrial removal of a small pelagic fish from the surrounding bank from 2009. It explains the crash, a doubling in eggshell thinning, a simultaneous decline in a second and unrelated seabird on the same island, and a similar decline on the mainland 400km south where the same fishery operates. Nothing new is posited; on unification and scope it is by far the lovelier story. But it was formulated in 2018, after the pattern was public, with its parameters tuned to fit, and when pressed on why the port colony crashed earliest despite having the lowest measured fishing pressure of any colony, its authors added the auxiliary that those birds forage close in and so felt local depletion first — a 2019 tracking study then showed they forage furthest of all. H3 (the artifact story): the census protocol changed in 2014, fewer counters and boat surveys replacing cliff-top counts in bad weather, so the real decline is nearer 25%. Cheapest ontology of the three, explains some of the abruptness, but explains neither the eggshells nor the second species, and predicts the drop should look uniform across colonies when the south-to-north gradient is the strongest signal in the data. Then, for the second part of the question, give them this epilogue: in 2021 a group uninvolved in any of that found the toxin. A dinoflagellate bloom, toxin detected in dead chicks at the port colony and two others, and the colony-by-colony timing tracks water temperature rather than shipping or fishing. Nobody had proposed it before 2020.

What I need from you: the exam question itself, formatted so I can paste it straight into a paper, in two parts. Part (a) asks them, as of the 2019 decision point, what a scientist should have concluded and why — and it has to be built so that the tempting answer (H2 is loveliest, so H2) and the other tempting answer (H1 predicted, so H1) both need defending against a specific pressure: the auxiliary in H2 didn't just look ad hoc, it was independently testable and it failed, which is evidence rather than taste; and the fact that H1's prediction was written down in 2014 seems to matter, even though once you've conditionalised on the datum the date the hypothesis was formulated is epistemically inert. I want them to have to say what, if anything, really does the work in the prediction/accommodation asymmetry — the tuning and the possibility that H2 would have been bent to fit whatever turned up — rather than just asserting that predicting is better than accommodating. Part (b) hands them the 2021 epilogue and asks what it shows and doesn't show about the 2019 reasoning: whether a rule that picked the wrong answer from a set that omitted the right one is thereby discredited, and whether that objection generalises to methods that also can only ever weigh hypotheses somebody thought of. Give me a model answer of about 850 words, then a marking scheme out of 30 broken into five or six must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a one-liner under each on how students typically botch it, and a short paragraph saying exactly what separates an A from a B. And please, no bibliography, no reading list, no quoted passages and no page numbers in any of it — they sit this with their notes only and I'll be marking from the arguments, not from who-said-what. Names can crop up, van Fraassen's bad-lot point and Lipton's stuff on loveliness versus likeliness are fair game since the class has had both, but nothing should hinge on remembering a publication year or a phrase verbatim. I've got a column due Thursday and my editor already thinks this is a strange use of my week, so the sooner the better.
