So I just spent three hours arguing with my friend Marcus and I still have to give the eulogy on Saturday, which is why I'm writing this to you instead of sleeping.

Quick version. My thesis advisor — call him Alan — died last week at 87. He was a moral philosopher by job, but his actual life's work, fourty-five years of evenings and summers and borrowed church halls, was investigating claims about survival of death: mediums, sittings, deathbed visions, people reporting a specific dead relative at the foot of the bed. His hero was Henry Sidgwick, the Cambridge ethicist who helped found the Society for Psychical Research in the 1880s, and Alan genuinely thought of himself as carrying on that job — not debunking, not believing, just gathering and weighing testimony the way Sidgwick wanted it gathered. He weighed it, and it never added up to proof, and he said so every single time anyone asked, cheerfully, for four decades.

Marcus's position, which he made at some length, is that an honest eulogy for Alan is impossible: either you comfort the family and thereby imply something Alan explicitly forbade, or you tell the truth about the empty result and it lands in the room as "he wasted his life." He said pick one and stop pretending. I don't think he's right but I can't do it myself yet, and I'd like you to try to prove him wrong.

Alan left a note with his executor. The rules in it, as best I can reconstruct them:

- Nothing that says or implies he found what he was looking for. He hadn't, and he knew he hadn't.
- Nothing about his watching over us, or being at peace now, or being with his wife again. He'd have marked those as unearned.
- But also nothing that lets the room conclude the other way. No "and in the end reason won," no smugness, no letting the sceptics have the last word over him. He thought premature dismissal was a worse epistemic sin than premature belief, and he'd hate being used as a prop for it.
- Something has to be said about why he kept going. Here's the version he repeated at me constantly, and I want this in there in plain words: Sidgwick's problem was that being rational and being moral pull apart unless something guarantees they line up. Duty points one way, your own good points another, and there's no proof they ever meet, so practical reason is left split down the middle. Alan thought that if you could show people survive death, that's the missing piece — the two finally converge and ethics stops resting on an unargued hope. So the sittings were never a hobby or a spooky phase. They were an attempted proof of a theorem he couldn't get. That's the thing I most want the room to understand.

The room: two of his children, who are practising Christians and will hear any talk of evidence and empty results as an insult said over their father's body. His sister, who is a working spiritualist and does readings, and who will hear the whole speech as a verdict on whether her life is real. About a dozen philosophy grad students who know the Sidgwick material cold. And one table of retired colleagues who privately thought the entire project was an embarassment to the department, and who are waiting to see whether I mention it.

What I need: roughly five minutes, so 650–750 words, written to be said aloud by a non-performer, with room to breathe. Honest in Alan's terms — not one overclaim he'd have flagged — and not a lecture, not a conversion pitch in either direction, and it has to actually comfort his kids without lying to them.

A couple of things about how I want you to work. You're a moral philosopher who also happens to have written a lot of eulogies for academics, so you know both the arguments and the genre's traps. Think carefully before drafting: first, as a short warm-up, list the eight or ten sentences that will be most tempting to reach for in this speech and mark each one sayable or unsayable, with a phrase of reasoning tied to his note and to who's sitting where — that's the brainstorming I actually need, and it's fine if some of them turn out to be unsayable for interesting reasons. Then write the eulogy. Then, separately, give me three alternate versions of the single hardest sentence in it, the one about what to say about a search that came up empty, and say which you'd pick and why.

Two more requests. Don't put anything in quotation marks as a Sidgwick line — I can't check it, and Alan would have despised an unverifiable quotation, so paraphrase him or leave him out. And if you decide Marcus is right and one of these constraints genuinely can't be met alongside the others, say so plainly and give me the closest achievable speech, rather than quietly dropping a rule and hoping I don't notice.
