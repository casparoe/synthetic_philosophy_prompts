I'm running a one-off joint session between our philosophy of action seminar and a decision-science reading group, and I need a sorting instrument for the handout. About twenty people will be in the room: half have read the weakness-of-will literature and are comfortable talking about intentional action, better judgment, and the conditions under which acting against one's own ranking counts as a real puzzle; the other half have read almost nothing outside the present-bias and discounting literature, and talk fluently about naive and sophisticated agents, time-inconsistent preferences, and commitment devices. Both groups will claim to be describing the same episodes — the person who resolves on Sunday night to decline the invitation and accepts it anyway on Monday at noon, the saver who systematically undersaves for retirement, the dieter at the cake. What I want is a decision tree, nine to eleven nodes, that a participant can walk through in about ten minutes and arrive at a named position on what self-control failure actually consists in, where every node is a concrete case or a plain-language question about a case, with two or three answer options phrased so that someone who has never heard the debates cannot tell which leaf each option leads to. No node may ask anything as direct as "do you think akratic action is intentional?" or "do you accept time-inconsistent preferences?" — the sorting has to happen through what people say about the situations, not through the labels they would choose for themselves.

Please build the tree out of the logical space first and out of the roster of positions second: identify the questions on which the accounts genuinely come apart — whether the action is done intentionally at all, whether the agent still holds the better judgment at the moment of acting, whether that judgment is a standing disposition or something occurrent, whether the explanation runs through the agent's own preferences or through a contest between parts of her, whether the episode is a failure of rationality or simply a change of mind — and let the positions be located by their coordinates, with the combinations nobody occupies noted rather than deleted. The leaves should include, at minimum: the deflationary view that there is no coherent phenomenon to explain; an intentionalist view on which the action is done for a reason and the conflict is explained by the relative strengths of desire and belief; the family of views that relocate the failure by holding that the agent's judgment itself shifts at the moment of action; identification-style views on which what matters is whether the agent endorses the desire that moves her; the view that dissolves the puzzle into character and virtue rather than into any single episode; the behavioral-economics leaves, including the naive/sophisticated/splits distinction and the commitment-device account of self-control; and at least two positions you expect to be unnamed because nobody or almost nobody defends them — for those, say whether the coordinate is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is stable but unattractive, or because it has simply not been tried. Somewhere near the top, please put a node that forces a commitment on the structural question I care most about: whether the preference reversals that generate hyperbolic discount curves and the episodes philosophers call akrasia are one phenomenon described in two vocabularies, one a special case of the other, or two phenomena that merely overlap.

A few further requirements. At least one pair of leaves should be separated only by the agent's own report about her state of mind — for instance, an agent who acts against what she says she still judges best but sincerely denies ever having felt any pull toward the alternative — and the tree should make explicit whether that report is being trusted, because distrust of self-ascription is one of the live fault lines in the room. Also include a case that discriminates the inverse question, not just failure but its absence: someone whose judgment genuinely swings with the temporal proximity of the options, and on whom we feel no pressure to apply the language of weakness at all. The cases should not all be the classroom classics; alongside the Ulysses-style precommitment case and the procrastinator, I would like at least one small-animal case (the dog trained away from the food on the table), one case in which the failure is distributed across a long sequence of individually trivial postponements so that there is no moment at which the agent does anything dramatic, and one case in which a sophisticated agent deliberately exploits her own predicted future softness. For every leaf, give one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the case it handles worst, or the claim it must make that sounds indefensible when stated baldly. Then flag, rather than paper over, which pairs of leaves your instrument cannot actually separate, and say what further question would separate them if one exists. Finally, tell me where you would expect the two people in my seminar who disagree most vehemently about this material to nevertheless land on the same leaf.
