ok, context first: I TA a huge intro micro section and every single semester the price-controls week turns into a food fight. Last Thursday a guy who works night shift at the hospital said the supply-and-demand diagram in the textbook was "violent" toward tenants, and an econ major shot back that rent control is about the one proposition economists actually agree on, and I just stood there unable to say anything useful because I think both of them were half right and I don't have a way of showing the class how both halves can be true at once. I want something I can hand out at the start of section that makes them hold the two things simultaneously instead of picking a team, and I figure satire is the only format that can do it without sounding like a lecture.

So here's what I want: a corporate-style annual performance review, the full bureaucratic apparatus — the rating boxes, the competencies grid, the employee-comments section at the bottom where you're supposed to initial — except the employee being reviewed is a rent stabilization ordinance, and this is its 41st annual review at a midsized American city. Build it out of these pieces: an employee info block (job title, ordinance number, department of housing, date of hire, and a "reports to" line naming a city council that has never once issued it a direct instruction but has quietly expanded its job description every few years); a self-assessment in the ordinance's own voice, proud and defensive, bragging about tenure and retention rates while being completely, un-self-consciously silent on output; a manager's evaluation grading it on the standard rubric of output, growth, customer satisfaction and cost efficiency, with the running joke that it scores at the very top on retention and flat zero on growth and the manager genuinely cannot tell whether that's a failing grade or the entire point of the position; a 360-degree peer feedback section with four or five short quoted blurbs — a tenant who's been in the same two-bedroom for twenty-two years, a nurse who's been number 340 on a waiting list for three years, a guy who owns two duplexes and does his own roofs, a developer, and a housing advocate who objects to the review instrument itself on the grounds that its baseline assumes the pre-control market was fair, which is exactly what's in dispute; an "areas for improvement" list that names real mechanisms instead of vague HR fluff — maintenance decay and quality slippage, non-price rationing in the form of waiting lists and key money and side deals over appliances and parking, misallocation between the retired couple in the four-bedroom and the family of five in the one-bedroom, the vacancy-decontrol loophole and what it does to both sides' incentives at the margins, the lock-in effect on people who can't afford to move for a better job, and the awkward admission that the whole thing would be a total no-op if the ceiling were set above the clearing rent; then a development plan with goals for next year that are quietly impossible, and end on the threat of a performance improvement plan. Keep it deadpan, no winking, no authorial aside — the humor should come entirely from a bureaucracy taking itself completely seriously. And please be fair: don't invent mechanisms that don't actually apply to price ceilings, don't make the tenants cartoonish, and don't let it collapse into a "government bad" bit. I want the ordinance to land some real hits too — the short-run inelasticity of supply, the fact that a voucher or direct transfer is means-tested and stigmatizing and administratively expensive and that cities reach for the ceiling precisely because it moves money without requiring visible taxation, the second-best point that when other distortions are already in place the "efficient" benchmark might not be the relevant comparison, and the plain fact that efficiency isn't the only value in the room. Roughly 900 to 1100 words, American English. Use a made-up city, made-up numbers, and made-up people, and don't name any real ordinance, real study, or real economist — I do not want my students googling it and catching me getting a citation wrong.

After the piece, tack on a short note to me, maybe 150 words, about where you took liberties. I want to know which of the mechanisms you put in the "areas for improvement" list you're confident genuinely follow from a binding ceiling and which ones are contested or depend on parameters you had to assume — how elastic is supply, over what time horizon, in what kind of market — plus anywhere you leaned on a reading of the ethics that someone could reasonably argue against, and whether the fair-to-the-ordinance bits I asked for are actually defensible or just generous. Flag the single point you're least sure about, and tell me how confident you are in the piece overall on whatever scale makes sense to you.
