I am putting together the handout for one week of a graduate seminar on the semantics of conditionals, and I have decided it should be a decision tree rather than a reading list: something a student can walk down after granting Lewis's triviality proof, and arrive at a named position without having read the responses to it. Two features of the class shape the exercise. First, about half the students have done measure-theoretic probability and half have not, so every branch question must be phrased in terms of what an agent accepts, asserts, or would stake money on; the formal machinery may appear in the description of a leaf, but never in a question. Second, the room has no projector and I want the whole thing to fit on a single page, which means six to eight branch questions and eight to twelve leaves, each leaf carrying a name for the position (or the word *unnamed*, if you judge that nobody actually holds it) plus one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

So that the tree is anchored to the argument rather than to the history, here are the premises I want it to start from, in my own formulation. (i) An indicative conditional expresses a proposition, so there is such a thing as the credence an agent has in it. (ii) Credences in sentences containing conditionals obey the ordinary probability calculus, including additivity over incompatible disjuncts and the usual rule for updating on new evidence. (iii) The Probability Thesis, which Lewis labels PC: whenever the antecedent has positive credence, the credence in the conditional equals the conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent. (iv) PC holds for every credence function in the class under consideration, or at least for a class that is closed under conditionalizing on any sentence containing no conditional. (v) Non-degeneracy: there is a pair A, C such that neither is certain, neither is ruled out, and learning A does not settle C. Both the 1976 paper *Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities* and the 1986 sequel *Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities II* are in play, and the sequel matters for the design because it shows that merely restricting the class of credence functions does not save PC unless the restricted class also fails to be closed under conditionalization on conditional-free sentences — so closure under conditionalization should be a node of its own rather than folded into the node about the scope of PC. The root of the tree is therefore the commitment that (i) through (v) cannot all be true, and for concreteness the questions should run on a single worked pair throughout, something like A = the lab ran the assay twice and C = the sample was contaminated.

Beyond that, four requirements. Every branch must be a yes/no or three-way question that a student could answer without any idea which leaf it leads to; if a question can be answered correctly just by knowing which camp the student fancies herself in, it is a bad question and should be replaced. The following leaves must appear somewhere, though you decide where: keep truth conditions but reject PC and accept that assertibility tracks conditional credence for a reason that is not truth-conditional; treat the indicative conditional as a truth-functional compound and explain the divergence between its probability and its acceptability by a separate convention or implicature; deny that indicative conditionals have truth conditions at all, so that PC is a norm on degrees of belief in suppositions rather than a semantic identity; the expressivist or hyperplan variant of that denial; the contextualist or variable-domain variant, which preserves PC only relative to a limited set of credence functions that is not closed under arbitrary conditionalization; and any view that keeps PC by weakening the calculus or enriching the semantics so that the probability of a conditional comes out marginally above the corresponding conditional probability. Then, for every combination of answers your branch questions permit that has no occupant, say which kind of empty it is: ruled out by the theorem itself, unoccupied because the view would be unattractive or unstable, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate and state what further question would separate them, if one exists — and bear in mind that my supervisor's standing hypothesis is that this whole debate is verbal, a quarrel about what *probability* and *assertibility* are being used to name, so I would like the tree to expose the node at which that hypothesis becomes either clearly right or clearly wrong rather than to settle the matter in a footnote.
