I'm in the final year of a moral responsibility module, and I've been asked to circulate my own position on standing to blame ahead of Thursday's seminar so that the rest of the group can attack it. Here is the package, as plainly as I can state it:

(1) Blame is at bottom a protest against a wrong. It need not be expressed, addressed, or even known about by anyone in order to count as blame; entirely silent blame is still blame.

(2) A blamer's own record can deprive her of the standing to blame. Hypocritical blame is not merely tactless or imprudent: something is wrong with the blamer's making of it, even where the judgement it embodies is entirely correct.

(3) The best explanation of (2) is unfairness. The hypocrite implicitly claims an exemption from a standard she holds others to, and so denies the equality of persons. This is roughly the line taken by Wallace and by Fritz and Miller, and I find it more convincing than the standing-scepticism Sher presses.

(4) Aptness and standing come apart. Blame is apt whenever its target is blameworthy, and nothing about the blamer bears on aptness; standing is a further question about the blamer's right to make the protest.

(5) Standing is a matter of degree, and the relevant degree is comparative badness: the graver my own undisclosed violation of the very norm I invoke, the less standing I have — even where I apply the norm to myself and would accept the same sanction were it discovered.

(6) Self-blame is the cure. A hypocrite who privately and sincerely blames herself for her own identical fault retains her standing, however assiduously she keeps both faults secret.

Three cases I've been using to test these:

*The ledger.* A tutor keeps a private notebook in which she records, each week, that her colleagues miss deadlines and that she alone is reliable. She misses them just as often, knows it, and never records it against herself. She never voices a word of blame to anyone.

*The convert.* A panel chair plagiarised heavily as an undergraduate, has since wholly reformed, and now regards her former self as, practically speaking, a different person. She censures a student for plagiarism without hesitation and without any sense of self-implication.

*The heavier hand.* A panel chair whose own undisclosed misconduct is far worse than the matter before her, and who is perfectly consistent: she applies the standard to herself and has said she would accept the same penalty.

Please audit this rather than lecture me. For each case, say what verdict (1)–(6) jointly deliver, and flag any case where they deliver none. Then tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do once the questions are properly separated. Rank the real tensions by severity; for each, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the package; and identify the single revision that would clean up the most. My own sense is that everything fits, and I would rather be told firmly that it does not than be flattered. Close by saying how confident you are in the audit overall, and by singling out the claim in it that you are least sure of. Please write in British English.
