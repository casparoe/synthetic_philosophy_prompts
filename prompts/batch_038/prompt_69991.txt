I am a senior program officer at a private family foundation with roughly $40 million in annual grantmaking across three areas: global health, criminal-justice reform, and scientific research. Eighteen months ago the board adopted a giving policy committing us to maximizing the expected value of our grants, and that language has produced a recurring stalemate in our review meetings. The near-term effects of the proposals in front of us are modest and reasonably well characterized — infections averted, arrests avoided, trained researchers retained — while the indirect and long-run effects are potentially orders of magnitude larger and essentially beyond our ability to estimate: eleven years of cash transfers reshape a regional labor market and the politics of a district; a reform that lowers admissions to one county's jail shifts enforcement pressure to a neighboring county; a fellowship program changes which questions a field asks for a generation. Two factions have formed among the trustees. One says that since we cannot know what we are doing, ranking grants by expected value is a pretense, and that we should fund only what is locally verifiable and stop claiming more than that. The other says that if the long-run effects dominate, then aiming at them is the only serious option, and it is the locally verifiable grants that are the pretense. I have been asked to settle this at the staff level with something we can apply, not with a position paper.

Deliverable: a decision procedure for a grant file, runnable by a program officer with no formal training in decision theory, in about two hours, producing a recommendation memo that our executive director must countersign. She is a former state auditor and requires that every ranking in the memo be traceable to a stated basis; she will not sign "the officer's overall judgment" as a reason, though she will sign "the officer exercised judgment at step four, where the procedure specifies that judgment is required, on the following grounds."

Before the steps, take a position on what our problem actually is, because the procedure differs depending on the answer and I do not want a hedge. Either the difficulty is specifically a moral one — a reason to doubt that the right thing to do is fixed by the consequences of particular acts, and hence a reason to revise or abandon the board's policy — or it is an ordinary case of decision-making under deep uncertainty that any framework we substituted would face equally, in which case the policy can stand and what we need is a better decision rule. Pick one. Say what follows for us from picking it, and say what the strongest case for the other answer is and why it does not move you.

Then the procedure itself. At minimum:

1. The steps in the order they should be executed, with a statement for each of what a sufficient answer looks like and what merely looks like an answer. Specify in particular what the officer writes down when the true response to a step is that we do not know and could not find out — a response I expect to be common, and one that must not function as an automatic yes or an automatic no.

2. An explicit taxonomy of the kinds of ignorance we face, with a different treatment for each: magnitude unknown but direction known, versus direction itself unknown; ignorance that a modest piece of funded research could reduce, versus ignorance no feasible study would touch; effects concentrated on identifiable present people, versus diffuse effects on people who do not yet exist. If you think some of these distinctions will not survive contact with real grant files, say so and tell me which to drop.

3. A rule governing when remote or speculative consequences may override a well-measured near-term case and when they must be set aside as inadmissible. Argue for the rule rather than asserting it. Separately: our evaluators have applied a three percent annual discount rate to costs and benefits for years, inherited from a cost-benefit contractor and never defended by anyone here. The procedure has to tell us what to do about it, since as far as I can determine that single number has been quietly deciding whether the long run counts at all.

4. The points at which the procedure terminates in a recommendation — fund, decline, fund conditionally, or refer — and the points at which it stops short and hands the choice upward because what remains is a question about values rather than fact. Be specific about which body decides: me, the executive director, or the board.

5. An honest accounting of which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume, and of how the procedure would be worked by an applicant or a colleague who has read it. Assume it leaks. I would rather design for that than discover it.

Apply the procedure to three files now in front of me, showing your work at each step:

A. $1.2 million to scale an unconditional cash transfer program in a region where we have been the largest funder for eleven years. The direct effects are supported by multiple randomized evaluations. The internal objection, raised by a colleague, is that eleven years of transfers has altered local wage structures and land prices and made local officials more answerable to donors than to constituents, and that none of this has been measured and none of it will be.

B. $300,000 to an advocacy organization campaigning to abolish a schedule of court fees and fines in our state. The direct effect on the roughly 40,000 people who pay them annually is real but small in dollars. The organization's principal claim on us is that a successful campaign would set a precedent other states would follow and would shift what counts as a legitimate way for a court to fund itself. Neither claim is testable within our grant period.

C. $500,000 to establish a doctoral fellowship program aimed at reducing a low-probability, high-magnitude risk — the applicant's example is misuse of engineered pathogens, and they are equally willing to argue from the risk of misaligned artificial intelligence. Their case is that expected value is dominated by the tail. Our executive director's marginal note on the file reads that the tail is invented.

Exactly one of these three, tell me which, should come out of your procedure with a recommendation the officer who brought it forward will not want. I would prefer to learn that from a procedure than in the boardroom, where two of the three are pet projects of trustees.

Close by stating what the procedure cannot do. If your view is that no procedure of this kind can be simultaneously faithful to expected-value maximization and usable by people with our resources, I want that said outright, along with the least bad version you would have us adopt anyway and precisely what we surrender by adopting it.
