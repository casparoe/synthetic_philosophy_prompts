ok so i run a small writing group for people who work in and around security studies, and I want to hand them a model of what I'm after: satire that turns out to be completely fair to the thing it's satirising.

Write me a job advertisement — full recruiting-page format, about 700 words, 800 max — for a government post that sounds entirely plausible: something like Deputy Director for Strategic Credibility in the ministry of defence of an unnamed nuclear-armed state. Flat, bloodless civil-service vacancy register throughout. Sections: about the role, key responsibilities (6-8 numbered bullets), essential criteria, desirable criteria, what we offer, the application process, and a short FAQ sidebar.

The conceit: every line is boilerplate HR prose, but the actual content of each one is a real paradox or standing problem from deterrence theory, described accurately. A strategist reading it should keep recognising their own field in the fine print and never be able to say the ad has exaggerated anything. So work in things like: having to make a threat that would be catastrophic to carry out and yet somehow be believed; the fact that appearing to have slipped your own leash is a professional asset and something you can cultivate on purpose; delegating authority far enough down the chain that the adversary knows you could actually launch, which makes accidents likelier but the deterrent stronger; a system that deters perfectly precisely because nobody can switch it off, and the hiring problem that follows from building one; the way a rock-solid strategic balance seems to make smaller proxy wars safer to have; the ally who needs to be shown the guarantee really extends to them and can never be shown it; the fact that a first-strike incentive shows up mainly as a deadline during a crisis rather than as a plan; and the fact that the entire performance record of everyone who has ever held this job is indistinguishable from pure luck.

Rules: deadpan, no winking at the reader, and don't label the paradoxes — no naming doctrines or dropping the technical jargon as a signpost. The reader should be able to name each one themselves from the job duty. No real people, countries, or administrations; invented ministries are fine, and it's actively encouraged to have the ad note that the post holder will be liasing closely with their opposite number in the rival state, since both sides need the other one to be competent at this.

The desirable-criteria section should contain at least one item that reads as a normal competency on first pass and is quietly awful on the second. The "what we offer" section should make the compensation and performance-review package a joke about success being unobservable in this role, without ever saying that in so many words. And the application process should end with a written exercise that is paradoxical in itself — something about asking candidates to evidence a catastrophe they averted, where supplying the evidence would prove they hadn't averted one.

Aim for funny to someone who's read the strategic studies canon and legible to someone who's never thought about nuclear weapons at all: the HR voice should do the translating for the lay reader. Keep the seperate sections clearly headed so it looks like a real posting.
