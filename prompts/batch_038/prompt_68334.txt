I work as the resident philosopher on a small evaluation team inside a government spending-review unit. We've been asked to publish a set of distributional weights — roughly, how much extra weight a pound of benefit gets when it lands on someone at the 20th income percentile versus the 80th. The senior economist on our external review panel has been telling ministers the whole exercise is intellectually fraudulent, on the grounds that interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing aren't a thing, that the ordinalists buried them in the 1930s (he name-checks Robbins every single meeting), and that if we publish weights we're smuggling in a comparability metaphysics we can neither state nor defend.

I've got twelve months of protected time and one research assistant who's decent at formal modelling but has never read welfare economics. Output has to be a working paper the panel can't just wave away. Here's the menu of projects I've been circling, and I want your view on which one actually deserves the year, with the rest ranked as fallbacks:

- The zero-point question: do distributional weights need comparisons of levels of wellbeing, or only of changes? If everything we do compares gains and losses, maybe a common unit with floating zeros is all that's required and the economist's objection misses entirely. I can't tell whether this distinction does any real work or is just tidiness.
- Extended sympathy / impartial observer routes: whether "what would I prefer if I were her, with her tastes" can be made non-arbitrary rather than a smuggled identity assumption, and whether it quietly presupposes a preference-satisfaction theory of wellbeing.
- Adaptive preferences: whether the reported and revealed preferences of people who've been deprived for a long time are systematically deflated, which would mean our weights are calibrated on corrupted inputs — and whether there's any non-question-begging way to detect that deflation rather than just assuming it.
- Conversion heterogeneity: same income, very different ability to turn it into a decent life (disability, chronic illness, caring responsibilities, cold homes). Does that push the weighting problem off income and onto functionings, and does that make comparisons harder or easier?
- The deflationary option: maybe comparability is a political question, not a metaphysical one — we pick weights, justify them publicly, and stop pretending the metaphysics settles anything. Possibly the right answer, possibly a surrender dressed up as sophistication.
- A conceptual/historical project on whether the impossibility doctrine was ever actually argued for or just stipulated as a methodological rule that later hardened into a result.

Tell me which of these is upstream of which — my hunch is that the zero-point one and the adaptive-preferences one are load-bearing and the rest hangs off them, but I might have that backwards. Also tell me bluntly whether "answer the economist" is even the right target, or whether that's a vanity project that would produce a paper nobody reads.

No bibliography and no reading list, I'm not going to chase references and I want your reasoning rather than your citations. Please use these sections in this order:

- Bottom line — four sentences max: your pick, then the rest in order, a phrase of reason each.
- What I'm scoring on — about 150 words on your criteria (how much else depends on it, whether two people can finish it in a year with no data, whether it would change what we actually publish) and how you trade them off against each other.
- The year — about 300 words on what we'd concretely do with the twelve months and the RA, what a successful paper looks like, and what the honest failure mode looks like.
- Ranked runners-up — about 100 words each for the rest, in order, saying specifically why each one loses to the top pick.
- What I'd cut — about 150 words, what comes off the menu and why.
- What would reshuffle this — about 150 words on concrete triggers: what argument or result showing up in the next couple of years would push the deflationary option to the top, or kill the zero-point project outright.
