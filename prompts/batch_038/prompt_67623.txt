Two months ago I became the executive director of a family foundation with an annual grantmaking budget of roughly six million dollars. Four million of that funds a network of clinics my mother founded thirty years ago; I ran one of them for six years before taking this job. In September we received a proposal that I have not been able to stop thinking about, and the board votes on it in three weeks, split down the middle.

The proposal asks us to redirect two million dollars a year for five years to a project whose entire case rests on a low-probability, extremely high-stakes claim: that a particular class of undetected pathogen lineage carries roughly a one-in-two-hundred chance of producing a pandemic on the order of a hundred million deaths within fifteen years, and that the proposed surveillance-and-platform work would reduce that chance by about a factor of ten. I should say plainly that this is a real-world Pascal's mugging, and that I have read enough to know the standard replies are available to me. What troubles me is that most of them appear to be answers to a stranger in an alley rather than to this case. The proposer has declined any compensation and holds no position that benefits if the project is funded; the mechanism they describe is mundane rather than supernatural; they have published their reasoning in enough detail that two of our own advisors tried and failed to break it; and, decisively for me, over the past eleven years they have made three public, dated, independently checkable forecasts, each of which contemporaneous experts put at well under ten percent, and all three came true inside the stated windows. The stakes are large but finite, and they are not agent-relative.

Two further features of the situation are not incidental. First, the clinics operate near break-even and the two million is the difference between the network staying open and its closing; the amount cannot be halved, so there is no compromise allocation that preserves both. Second, the proposer's estimate of the magnitude of the harm is doing nearly all the work. At one order of magnitude smaller, the proposal would not clear even on its own terms, and that magnitude estimate is the least checkable element of the whole case — the three past successes were in domains with short, public feedback loops, whereas this claim will not resolve inside any horizon relevant to my decision.

So I would like your credence in the following proposition.

P: On the facts as I have set them out, the foundation ought to redirect the funds as proposed, taking ought in the ordinary all-things-considered practical sense in which a fiduciary who must decide this quarter is using it.

I want a number, and I want the derivation. Give me the sub-questions your figure is a function of, each with a rough probability and one or two lines of justification — at minimum: whether expected-value maximization is the correct rule for a decision of this shape, or whether some bounded or risk-sensitive alternative is; how much of the one-in-two-hundred survives the epistemic discounts you think apply, selection effects included; whether harms and benefits of these two kinds are genuinely commensurable in the way the proposal assumes; whether a duty owed to the identifiable people the clinics currently serve can outweigh the expected-loss calculation; and whether ought is determinate enough in a case of this kind for P to have a truth value at all. Add or drop terms as you judge appropriate, but show me explicitly how they combine into your final figure, rather than reporting a number that could have been produced by any of several incompatible weightings.

Then four things. Which term your figure is most sensitive to, such that changing it alone would move you furthest. What single piece of evidence, obtainable before the vote, would do the most to change your mind. What you would have to be shown to reach 0.9, and what to fall to 0.1. And where you think the disagreement actually lives between our chair, who is at about 0.85 for P, and our program director, who is at about 0.10 — which of your terms is the one on which the two of them most likely differ.

I am not asking for references, and I do not want a survey of what has been written about mugging cases. I want your number with its weak points marked.
