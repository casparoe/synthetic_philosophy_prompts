Setting: I got roped into a working group writing the eligibility rules for a public long-term care benefit. Fixed pot, claims decided case by case by caseworkers who only ever have what's on the form, and the enabling statute actually bars us from asking claimants certain questions about their past conduct. Half the group keeps reaching for the idea that you shouldn't be compensated for bad luck you brought on yourself, and nobody can say what would have to be true for that to become a workable rule. So before I read another paper on luck egalitarianism I want the spec.

Write the desiderata: what any view that wants distributions to track responsibility but not luck has to deliver. For each one give me the case that motivates it, whether it's non-negotiable or just a camp preference, and whether it constrains the criterion of justice or the decision procedure an agency actually runs — and flag where you think those two come apart, because my hunch is that a lot of the standard objections (the motorcyclist who rides without a helmet, the person with a taste they never chose, the two identical lottery tickets) are really attacks on a procedure dressed up as attacks on a criterion.

Cover at minimum: where the brute-luck/option-luck line can be drawn non-arbitrarily when the choices themselves are shaped by circumstances, and whether that regress is fatal or just needs a stopping point somewhere; ex ante versus ex post versions; equality of what (resources, welfare, access to advantage) and why that choice isn't seperate from the luck/choice question; and what the theory owes to the person it turns down — some publicity constraint on how the verdict can be stated to them without it being an insult.

Then: which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and is some subset jointly unsatisfiable once you add the information constraint? My suspicion is that genuinely tracking choice, not interrogating people about their tastes and risk beliefs, and never leaving anyone under a floor can't all be had at once — if that's right, say which one gives. Also tell me which things people in this literature demand that you think are bogus requirements, prejudices of one camp wearing a constraint costume, and whether scope questions (non-residents, future claimants, animals) belong on the list at all or are some other theory's business.

End with two or three lines each on how Dworkin-style resource accounts, Cohen/Arneson-style access-to-advantage accounts, and the wholesale rejection of the project do against the list. Briefly tho — the criteria are the point.
