In my applied ethics reading group one of the grad students keeps pushing an analogy about manipulation that everybody finds compelling, and im not sure I buy it. Her version: persuasion is like handing someone a map; manipulation is like quietly rotating the map in their hands while they're reading it. Same information, different orientation — they still feel like they're navigating freely.

She uses it for two conclusions. First, that manipulation isn't really about the manipulator's intent, or even about deception — it's about the frame. So a recruiter who's being completely straight with you, or a newsroom that just picks which story leads the page, can be manipulating without lying or hiding anything. Second, that disclosure-based fixes are hopeless: telling people "this is sponsored content" hands them the map back but doesn't un-rotate it, because the rotation happened at the level of how the options are laid out, not at the level of any particular fact you could correct them about.

Can you audit it? Lay out the correspondence table properly — map, hands, rotation, the reader's sense of orientation, whatever else is doing work — then split the disanalogies that actually undercut her two conclusions from the ones that are just interesting. I especially want to know whether "same information, different orientation" is even available for beliefs and desires the way it is for a physical map, since a map has an independently correct way to be held and I'm not convinced preferences do. And if the analogy fails, is there a better one that would carry (1) without carrying (2), or the other way round?
