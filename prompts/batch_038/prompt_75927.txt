I have been asked to contribute a critical notice of the concluding chapter of a recent edited volume on practical rationality, a chapter written for a mixed audience of philosophers, economists, and clinicians and much praised for its accessibility. Before I put anything in print I would like an independent accuracy audit of its central passage, which I reproduce here in full:

"Hard choices are cases in which two options are neither better than one another, nor worse, nor equally good. The standard argument for the possibility of such cases, due to Ruth Chang, is the small-improvement argument. Suppose a career as a clarinetist and a career as an accountant are neither better nor worse than one another. Now improve the first by a small amount — a slightly better conservatory, a marginally more secure contract. If the two careers had been exactly equally good, that improvement would have made the clarinetist's career better than the accountant's. But it plainly does not: the small improvement does not tip the scales. So the two careers were never equally good; and since they are not better or worse either, they are incomparable. What the argument refutes is the trichotomy thesis — that any two items comparable under a single value must stand in exactly one of the three traditional relations — and with it the completeness assumption built into standard decision theory.

The upshot is more radical than it first appears. Where options are on a par, reason is simply exhausted: there is nothing further to be said about which is better, and the agent can do no more than will one. Such a choice is arbitrary in the strict sense, since nothing about the options makes it the choice to make. This is also why parity is so often confused with vagueness. People notice that we cannot say whether the clarinetist's career is better than the accountant's and conclude that 'better than' is vague in such cases, when what is really happening is that the concept ceases to apply. Recognizing the difference has consequences well beyond individual deliberation. If health and economic output are genuinely incommensurable, then cost-benefit analysis cannot be what it claims to be — a procedure for discovering the right trade-off — and is instead a technical-looking way of announcing a decision reached on other grounds."

Please work through this claim by claim rather than in summary. For each substantive claim, tell me whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification for a general audience, or a misrepresentation, and in the last case what the view actually holds. I am especially interested in three things. First, whether the small-improvement argument is correctly stated, and in particular whether the step from 'they were never equally good, and neither is better' to 'they are incomparable' is licensed by the argument as its proponent deploys it — the passage later speaks of the options being 'on a par,' and I cannot tell whether the author is treating these as two names for one relation or has slid between them. Second, whether the characterization of the choice in hard cases as arbitrary, and of reason as exhausted, is the view's own or the author's editorial addition, and what turns on the difference. Third, whether the concluding inference about cost-benefit analysis survives once the earlier claims are corrected; note in particular that the passage's final use of 'incommensurable' may not mean what its earlier talk of comparability meant, and assess whether the policy conclusion depends on the stronger or the weaker sense.

I would also like the two principal parties to the underlying dispute represented on their own terms: what a defender of parity should object to in this passage, and what a sceptic who explains hard cases by imprecision or vagueness in the comparative relation — rather than by a fourth relation — should object to, including whether that sceptic is entitled to the objections or is helped by parts of the passage.

Finally, a matter that arose in correspondence. I put to the author the following worry: if a small improvement never changes the verdict, then a long enough sequence of small improvements ought eventually to yield an option plainly better than the other, even though no step in the sequence tipped the scales; and if she instead concedes that parity is not preserved across such sequences, parity becomes intransitive and one can build triples in which A is on a par with B, B is on a par with C, and C is better than A. She replied that she is content to accept cycles in the betterness relation. Assess that reply: whether it is consistent with the rest of the passage, whether it is consistent with her claim that the concept ceases to apply rather than being vague (I take cycles to be something vagueness-based accounts are designed to avoid, though I may have that backwards), and whether cycles in 'better than' constitute a genuine cost for a theory of choice, an embarrassment only for the deployment of that theory in decision-theoretic modelling, or neither.

Two notes on what I want back. I have my own suspicions — that the passage conflates parity with incomparability, and that it overstates a modest thesis about the trichotomy into a refutation of a formal framework — but I may be wrong about both, and I would rather be told so plainly than have my hypotheses confirmed by courtesy. And I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading; what I need is the analysis itself, with the reasoning exposed at each step so that I can check it against the text.
