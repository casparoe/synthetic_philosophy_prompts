I'm a third-year political theory PhD student — dissertation is on sortition and lottocracy, so this is squarely my lane — and this term I'm also teaching a first-year writing seminar themed "juries and justice." Next week we're screening Lumet's 12 Angry Men, and I'm torn about it. Half the class will walk in already believing the movie is a paean to ordinary citizens getting it right, and the other half will have seen some youtube essay about how it's really about one man bullying eleven into submission. I want to use it as a genuine test case for sortition as an institution, not as a feel-good illustration, and I also don't want to be contrarian just for the fun of it. So: read the film the way a lottocrat and an anti-lottocrat would each read it, and tell me which of them the film actually supports, if either.

Concretely, treat the twelve as a lotted body and ask what the movie shows about the epistemic case for sortition. Things I want you to actually engage with: the opening 11–1 vote and how close it is to staying 11–1, and what it would have taken for that verdict to hold — nothing in the procedure guarantees a Juror #8, and I think the honest reading has to concede that the film is equally a picture of how easily a random sample converges on a wrong answer. Then the three moves that actually flip people: the knife he produces after buying a lookalike in a pawn shop near the kid's home, the juror who grew up in the same slums demonstrating that a switchblade gets used underhand rather than stabbed downward overhand, and the older juror's reading of the downstairs witness's need to matter for once plus the eyeglass-marks point about the woman across the street. Are those a vindication of the cognitive-diversity claim — that a descriptively mixed random sample holds knowledge no panel of experts would have — or are they just lucky perturbations that happen to break a correlated error? My own working phrase, which you're welcome to tear apart, is that the knife scene is a "correlation break": the value of a random body isn't in the averaging, it's in the near-certainty that somebody in it holds a belief uncorrelated with the group's mistake. Does that idea survive contact with the film, or does the film show the correlation break being supplied by luck rather than by design? Related: the vote changes by persuasion, not aggregation, which means the independence assumption that the jury theorem needs is not just violated but arguably shouldn't be wanted — is the film making that point, or am I forcing it? And the size thing: the film never shows or mentions how the twelve were chosen, and n=12 is tiny; Athens, if I remember right, ran its people's courts with juries in the hundreds partly because small panels are cheaper to bribe and easier to bully — correct me if I'm mangling that. So is the movie an argument for sortition at scale and against sortition at twelve, given that we watch two jurors (the one with the ballgame tickets, the one whose whole position is rage at his own son) do exactly the capture work a bigger panel would have diluted? Also, the defendant is a poor kid from the same slum as the one juror who resembles him, and nobody else on the panel is anything like him — what does that do to the descriptive representation story? And finally the verdict is unreviewable and the jurors walk away anonymous: sortition's fans love the absence of re-election incentives, but the scene where the bigot keeps talking and everyone just turns their backs and drifts to the window seems to me to show the price of unaccountability rather than its absence.

Two things I don't want. First, don't let the film become an illustration — I want you to say where the institutional lens distorts it, since the movie is also doing a lot of work on conscience, on postwar masculinity, on class resentment, and on what it costs a person to sit in judgment, and a purely procedural reading flattens all of that. Where does the work resist my reading, and what would an honest critic have to concede? Second, no reading lists, no citations, and please don't quote the dialogue — I don't have the script to hand and I care about the shape of the scenes, not exact wording. Point me at specific moments I should screen for the class, and end by saying what you think the film adds to the sortition debate that argument alone can't: is there something you can only learn from watching a random body decide, as opposed to reading about one?
