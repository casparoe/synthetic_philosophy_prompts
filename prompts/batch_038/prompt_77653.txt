I'm putting together a short write-up of my own views on diachronic rationality for a reading group, and I want someone to stress-test the package before I stand up and present it. Please write in American English — I'm British and half the group isn't, and I don't want the spelling to be the thing everyone remembers.

Here's roughly where I've landed. I'll number them so you can point at them.

1. My preferences over some real things are genuinely intransitive, and I don't think that's a confusion on my part. The standing example: I'm weighing three job offers. J1 pays the most, is in a city I dislike, and the work is dull. J2 pays moderately, is in a fine city, and the work is decent. J3 pays badly, is in a great city, and the work is the most interesting thing I've been offered. I rank J1 over J2, J2 over J3, and J3 over J1. I've re-run this in my head with the descriptions shuffled, with the numbers changed, and with a friend forcing me to defend each pairwise judgment separately, and the cycle holds. It's not a framing artifact and it isn't me being vague about a near-tie.

2. I'm not myopic. I can foresee my own future choices pretty well, and any theory of rational choice that gets its results by assuming the agent can't anticipate what she'll do three steps from now is describing something other than ordinary adult deliberation.

3. So I don't think the money-pump argument shows that (1) is irrational. Run it on my case: I currently hold J3. You offer to move me to J2 for a small cost, then from J2 to J1 for a small cost, then from J1 back to J3 for a small cost, and I'm where I started minus three payments. I grant the mechanics. I deny that it indicts me. The pump needs either an agent who can't see the sequence coming, which (2) rules out for me, or an arrangement of offers that doesn't correspond to anything anyone actually faces.

4. I accept separability in the usual sense: whether one option beats another shouldn't turn on what else is on the menu, or on states of the world that are the same either way.

5. I accept what I'd call diachronic non-akrasia: it's irrational to knowingly take a course of action that, at the time of acting, you expect your later self to regret and to pay to undo. If I can see the loop coming and step into it anyway, something has gone wrong with my reasoning, whatever my preferences look like.

6. But I deny that avoiding a loss across a whole sequence is a requirement of rationality in its own right. Rational assessment happens choice by choice, in the situation the agent is actually in at that moment. A "net loss over the sequence" is never itself an object anyone chooses, and I think a lot of the diachronic literature quietly promotes it into one.

7. Precommitment and constraint are rational and central, not a workaround — forming intentions now that narrow what I'll allow myself later is a real part of being an agent, and I don't think it's evidence of a defect in my preferences that I sometimes want to bind myself.

8. Being pumpable, if it ever happened to me, would be a practical badness — I'd be poorer — but not thereby a rational failing. Outcomes aren't the currency of rational assessment.

What I want from you:

- Go pair by pair and tell me which of these actually pull against each other and which only look like they do. I'm specifically interested in the ones that look harmless separately. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than dumping them all in one heap.
- For the worst ones, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision is, plus what the revision costs me — I'd rather lose something small and know it than keep everything and be incoherent.
- Then tell me which single change would clean up the most, and whether the package as it stands reads as one recognizable position or as two incompatible camps bolted together. If it's the latter, name the camps.
- Work through my J1/J2/J3 sequence concretely, node by node, when you make your case. I want to see what each commitment licenses me to do at the first offer, at the second, and at the third, not a summary of the debate.
- If any tension you find depends on a detail of how I've set the case up — the size of the payments, whether the offers come from one agent or three, whether they arrive all at once or spaced out — say which detail and how the verdict would shift if I changed it.

Two ground rules. Don't give me a reading list or cite anyone; I want the argument, not the scholarship. And don't spend words explaining the standard positions to me, I know them — I'm asking about *my* set. I've deliberately not told you which of the eight I'd be most willing to drop, so don't try to guess my attachment; just tell me what the logic says has to go.
