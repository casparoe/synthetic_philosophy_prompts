**Coordinates and Quotients: On the Ontology of Features**

**§1.** Mechanistic interpretability has adopted a realist vocabulary without, so far as I can see, having earned it. Practitioners speak of features as things a model *has*, of directions in activation space as the features themselves, of circuits as mechanisms a model implements, and of the aim of the enterprise as enumerating what is inside. Skeptics have generally answered with a familiar instrumentalism: these posits are like the beliefs and desires of the intentional stance, useful for prediction but not candidates for existence. I want to argue that both positions are aimed at the wrong target, and that the reason they cannot be separated by evidence is structural rather than contingent.

**§2.** Three claims organize current practice. First, individual units are polysemantic: a single neuron may respond to cat faces, to car fronts, and to something else entirely unrelated. Second, the standard diagnosis of polysemanticity is superposition: the model represents more features than it has dimensions, along directions that are not axis-aligned, paying for this with interference between them. Third, the standard remedy is to learn an overcomplete sparse dictionary over the activations at a given site, on the expectation that the atoms of the dictionary are closer to monosemantic and are the right units on which to perform causal interventions. Validation throughout is interventionist: an attribution earns credit when patching, ablating, or steering at the attributed site changes behaviour in the predicted direction.

**§3.** Here is the difficulty. For a network with rectifying nonlinearities, the group of transformations of the hidden activations that leaves the computed function exactly invariant is not the full linear group but the group of permutations and positive rescalings of units; more general changes of basis are broken by the nonlinearity. That is a smaller symmetry than one sometimes hears claimed, but it is already enough for my purpose, and at the level that matters most — the dictionary — the redundancy is larger still. An overcomplete dictionary is not uniquely determined by the activations it reconstructs. Many dictionaries, related by rescalings, by near-duplicate splitting of atoms, and by redistribution of shared variance, reproduce the activations equally well and satisfy the sparsity constraint equally well. Which one obtains depends on hyperparameters chosen for engineering reasons.

Suppose we adopt, as gauge theory does, the principle that a claim carries empirical content only insofar as it is invariant under the transformations that leave everything observable fixed. Then the sentence "this direction is the golden-retriever feature" is not, as stated, a claim with content. It is a claim relative to a choice of dictionary, and the choice is not fixed by the observations. What is invariant lies elsewhere: the network's input–output profile, and the way that profile shifts when activations at a site are altered.

**§4.** This yields an unorthodox reading of the field's central discovery. Polysemanticity is not a fact about models; it is a symptom of working in a gauge that has not been fixed. Neurons are polysemantic because the neuron basis is arbitrary with respect to the function computed, and arbitrary coordinates can be expected to cut across whatever structure there is. Superposition, likewise, is not the model's cramming more into less space. It is the observation that a convenient coordinate system fails to diagonalize the structure of interest.

If that is right, one would expect the remedy — sparse dictionaries — to fix the gauge. It does not. It replaces one arbitrary coordinate system with a family of them, indexed by hyperparameters and initialization, each of which is equally good by the only criteria the method itself supplies. The non-uniqueness is not a defect to be engineered away with scale; it is what one should expect from a procedure whose objective underdetermines its solution. Hence no improvement in tooling can settle the ontological question, because the tooling is not sensitive to the distinction in dispute.

**§5.** I propose, then, *quotient realism*. What is real in a network is not any dictionary of features but the equivalence classes of dictionaries under the redundancy relation, individuated by their interventionally detectable consequences. A feature is real just in case it corresponds to a stable element of the quotient: something that makes a predictable difference to behaviour across a range of interventions, and that is recoverable, in one guise or another, from any adequate coordinate system. On this view the field is not wrong to talk about features; it is wrong to suppose that features are items inside the model rather than invariants of the model's causal profile. This also explains a standing embarrassment — that practitioners cannot agree how many features a model contains, or which dictionary is the right one, without anyone being able to locate an error.

**§6.** Two consequences. First, a prediction: better objectives and greater scale will not produce canonical dictionaries. Disagreement between dictionaries will persist and will be unresolvable in principle, while agreement about interventionally characterized behaviours will improve. If that prediction fails — if some procedure converges on a unique dictionary across hyperparameters, architectures, and training seeds — quotient realism is false and features are items in the model after all. Second, a caution: safety arguments that presuppose the completeness of a particular dictionary inherit that dictionary's redundancy. A claim of the form "the model contains no representation of X" is gauge-dependent until it is relativized to a specified class of interventions, and until then it is not the sort of thing that could be true.

Is §5 a substantive thesis, or a relabelling of the familiar point that representation ascriptions are relative to the modeller's interests and to the level of description chosen? 5. Give me two or three concrete revisions. Locate them by section, paraphrase the offending passage back to me so that I can see you have read it, and say what should replace it. Do not rewrite the paper.
