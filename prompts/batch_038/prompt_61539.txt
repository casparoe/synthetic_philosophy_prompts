I teach a required 100-minute session in a second-year legal theory course — twenty-six students, a flat room with movable tables, no budget and no technology beyond a whiteboard. For three years I sat on my university's student conduct panel, the last of them as its chair, and the panel wrote the guidance it then applied; I have the uncomfortable memory of my own reasoning becoming steadily more deferential to the office from the moment I occupied it, and more impatient of the students who pointed out that our rules were partly arbitrary. Reading Pascal's fragments on justice and force this summer — the bundle usually headed justice, together with the prudential remark that turns up in the misery material rather than there, warning that it is dangerous to tell the people the laws are unjust because they obey only insofar as they believe them just — felt less like seventeenth-century apologetics than like minutes of a meeting I had actually chaired. I want to run an activity in which the students meet that structure in what they do rather than in what I say, and in which Pascal's name and the fragments themselves stay out of the room until the debrief.

The claim I want them to run into has four parts, and I would like the game's machinery built around them rather than around a familiar bargaining or public-goods setup. First, that a rule everyone concedes to be fair is powerless without enforcement, and that its defenders get contradicted rather than answered. Second, that force without justice is not merely tyrannical but is accused — it reliably draws the vocabulary of illegitimacy out of the people subject to it. Third, the asymmetry that drives Pascal's conclusion: justice is disputable, whereas force is highly recognisable and not up for argument, which is why the practical recipe — make just what is strong, or make strong what is just — almost always gets executed in the second direction, and a group ends up calling the strong just. Fourth, the prudential point about disclosure: obedience rests on a belief in the justice of the law, so revealing the arbitrariness of the arrangement may destroy a compliance that the arrangement itself was sustaining. To stage these, I have in mind a sealed-rule device: in phase one the tables draft allocation rules which are then sealed and handed to an enforcer drawn by lot, so that in phase two only the office-holder knows the rule and everyone else must reconstruct it from observed decisions — justice literally opaque, force literally visible. Alongside it, a grievance ledger in which any challenge is adjudicated by the enforcer alone, is unreviewable, and costs the complainant something if it fails, so that the accusation phase two provokes is priced and then suppressed. Then a disclosure phase in which I reveal that the office was filled by coin toss and that the sealed rule was one of three I had written in advance, of which only one had any input from the table. I would also like two control tables running degraded versions — one with an enforcer who has no sanctioning capacity at all, one with sanctioning capacity and no stated rule — so the comparison is available in the room rather than asserted by me. The payoff scheme has to work without money and without grading coercion: convert it into something the students actually care about but that costs me nothing, and tell me what that can be and where the line is.

So please give me the whole runnable thing: full rules and role descriptions, the materials list and what needs to be pre-written before class, a minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes, and a debrief that maps each phase and each observed behaviour onto the specific Pascalian claim it instantiates, including how I should handle the tables whose results contradict the lesson. Tell me frankly what will go wrong the first time I run this with law students — my guesses are that they will reverse-engineer my pedagogical intent within five minutes and then perform either compliance or rebellion for me, that the grievance cost will chill the room so thoroughly that phase two generates no accusation at all, that the disclosure will land as betrayal by the facilitator rather than as insight, and that whoever ends up holding the office will play it as a caricature — and give me a mitigation for each. Finally, be honest about the limits: Pascal's claim is not an empirical prediction about compliance rates, and an activity like this one can easily deliver a realist moral about legitimacy being nothing but power, which is precisely what he refuses, since the point of the diagnosis sits inside a larger argument about the misery of the human condition. Say which feature of his view the game cannot reproduce and what I have to supply verbally to stop the session ending as an edifying lesson in cynicism, and note where his remark that an order founded on opinion is sweet and voluntary but temporary, while an order founded on force always reigns, could be worked into the design or should be left out. I am not asking for readings or for a bibliography. What I do want is calibration: mark how confident you are in each claim you make about Pascal's argument, flag in particular whether you are sure of the direction of that recipe and of whether the prudential fragment belongs with the justice material or with the misery material, and single out the one point in the whole design you are least sure of — the place where the mechanics are most likely to teach the students something other than what I have described.
