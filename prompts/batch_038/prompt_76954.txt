Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about Bell's theorem and came out of it with her insisting that my position is incoherent and me unable to say why it isn't, so I would like a dispassionate audit rather than another conversation. For context: I am a third-year physics student who has read enough philosophy of physics to be dangerous; she is a philosophy graduate student whose specialty is precisely this literature. We are agreed on the physics. She concedes that the experiments since 2015 closed the locality and detection loopholes and that later runs using astronomically generated setting choices did not weaken the result; I concede that these are not merely technical achievements to be discounted. What we could not settle is whether the interpretive package I hold can be held all at once. Her only diagnosis is that I have "misunderstood what Bell's theorem actually assumes," which does not tell me which clause to strike, and her one concrete charge is that items (b) and (c) below are flatly incompatible — that a world which violates local causality cannot also be a relativistic world. That strikes me as the shallowest version of her objection, and possibly not an objection at all, but I am not confident.

Here is the package. Please treat each item as something I sincerely hold, not as a strawman I have set up to be knocked over, and do not infer my priorities from the order in which I list them, because I genuinely do not yet know which of these I care about most. (a) Bell's theorem is not a theorem about deterministic hidden variables in some narrow sense: the condition he later formulated as local causality — that a full specification of the beables in a region's past light cone screens off correlations between events in spacelike-separated regions — implies the factorization condition, and it binds stochastic theories exactly as it binds deterministic ones. The familiar complaint that Bell only ruled out local hidden variables, and that genuine chance provides an escape, is a confusion. (b) Given (a) and the experiments, nature is not locally causal: no theory that reproduces the observed statistics satisfies Bell's factorization condition. (c) I nevertheless want to be a relativist in the full sense — not merely that superluminal signalling is impossible, but that there is no preferred foliation of spacetime and nothing in my metaphysics privileges a frame. (d) I accept the decomposition of factorizability that Jarrett proposed and Shimony renamed parameter independence and outcome independence, and I hold the strong version of the peaceful-coexistence line: what quantum mechanics violates is outcome independence only, while parameter independence holds at the level of the theory itself and not merely in the observed statistics. Nothing at my detector can depend, even in principle and even before any averaging over hidden variables, on which measurement my colleague chooses at the distant one. (e) Measurement outcomes are single and definite: when the apparatus registers, one thing happened rather than a branching. (f) I will not buy any of this with conspiracy — the settings are statistically independent of the state prepared at the source, and I regard both the superdeterministic and the retrocausal routes as non-starters. (g) The wave function is a real physical object, and the nonseparability of the entangled pair state, the joint system having properties not fixed by the properties of its parts, is what does the explanatory work; on my view the Bell violation shows that spacetime is not the fundamental arena, not that there are superluminal signals. (h) At the same time I share Bell's own preference for theories of local beables: the primitives of a physical theory should be things located in spacetime regions, and a theory whose sole fundamental object is a field on configuration space has not answered the question of what the world is made of.

Now the task. Go through the list and tell me which items genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do, keeping that distinction explicit throughout, since I suspect a good part of her charge rests on treating an apparent tension as a real one. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting eight complaints of equal weight, and for each state the minimum revision that removes it, what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package, and whether the repair cascades — that is, whether weakening one item to save another destabilizes a third. Where more than one repair is available for a given tension, set the alternatives side by side with their respective prices instead of quietly recommending one. Then tell me which single change would buy back the most, and whether any of the tensions are merely verbal, in which case say what the parties mean by the words that is doing the damage. I would rather have a hard verdict than encouragement: please do not restate the theorem to me and do not survey the interpretive landscape, both of which I know well enough. What I want to know is whether these eight commitments can be held together by one person, and if not, exactly which of them that person should stop claiming.
