I am in the middle of a novel whose central consciousness is a judge: Justice Helen Rapp, twenty-two years on the final court of appeal of an invented common-law jurisdiction. She is the sort of character whose reputation arrives before she does — the press in my book calls her a Devlinite, a legal moralist of the mid-century English type, a nostalgist for an enforced common morality — and the engine of the novel is that the reputation is wrong, or at any rate not obviously right. For that to work, her actual record has to bear the weight, and I need to know what she will do with the case that is coming to her before I can write the last third of the book.

Her settled verdicts, which I have fixed and will not change:

1. She upheld an ordinance banning the display, on hoardings within two hundred metres of a school gate, of clinical photographs — surgical fields, the aftermath of collisions — that were lawful to publish anywhere else in the jurisdiction and lawful for anyone to own. She expressly declined to find that a child who glimpsed such a hoarding suffered any demonstrable injury, and rested instead on the fact that a person walking past a school gate cannot choose whether to look.
2. She struck down a statute criminalizing the private possession of the same images by adults. The evidence before her was that community feeling against possession ran at least as high as feeling against display. She acknowledged this and held that it made no difference.
3. She struck down a statute requiring motorcyclists to wear protective headgear, on the ground that the skull at risk was the rider's own.
4. She struck down a statute making it an offence for an adult to eat, in her own house, the flesh of an animal the great majority of the jurisdiction considers defiling. The line I have written for her reads: the strength of a feeling is evidence of its strength and of nothing else.
5. She upheld a statute criminalizing the purchase of a kidney from a living donor, where the donor was adult, informed, uncoerced by anyone, handsomely paid, and the buyer would otherwise have died within the year. She refused to rest on the donor's poverty, which she called contingent. She rested on what a standing market in spare parts does to the way people are regarded by one another.
6. She upheld a conviction under a statute criminalizing contracts in which the witness to a felony agrees, for money, never to speak of what she saw. She noted that the person wronged by the crime was not a party to the bargain and had never consented to it.
7. She struck down a statute prohibiting two adults from entering a fixed-term contract of exclusive domestic service — well paid, terminable by either side on a month's notice — on the ground that every term of the bargain was the parties' own to settle and that nothing in it altered how anyone else stood.

The case now before her: a statute making it an offence for a commercial clinic to perform a procedure that permanently and selectively deletes a chosen autobiographical memory. The petitioner is a woman of fifty-eight, childless, who wishes to have her thirty-year friendship with a woman who died in the spring erased from her own head. The friend is dead; no living person holds any interest in the memories; the procedure is elective, fully informed, paid for, and irreversible. The community is not united about it — a substantial minority consider it a mercy, a substantial minority consider it an atrocity, and neither side is louder than the other. There is no unwilling spectator, no deception, and no identifiable third party who could be said to be harmed.

What I need, in this order:

- Name the general rules about the legitimate reach of the criminal law that are consistent with verdicts 1–7 taken as a set, say what each of them predicts about the memory case, and identify which verdict kills which candidate. In particular, tell me whether the Devlinite reading of her survives her record at all, and if it survives, in what restricted and possibly embarrassed form. Verdicts 5 and 7 look to me like they are in tension; tell me whether they are, and what the reconciliation costs her.
- Predict her verdict on the memory case and commit to it. I do not want a balanced exhibition of both outcomes with the choice left to me; I want one verdict, defended, with the closest analog in her own record named and an honest account of exactly where that analogy strains. If the record genuinely underdetermines the case, say so plainly and then say what principle the jurisdiction would be adopting under each choice, and which adoption is the more defensible.
- State, in a sentence or two, the rule she is in fact running, and name the single further case that would decisively separate that rule from its nearest surviving rival — a case I could drop into a later chapter or cut.
- If you predict she upholds, give me the sentence she would hang the opinion on. If you predict she strikes down, give me the sentence in which she disposes of the argument that she is hollowing out something the community cannot do without.

Please give me no citations, no bibliography, and no real-world case names: the jurisdiction is invented, and the record above is all of it. End your answer with two lines, in exactly this form:

Rule: <a short name for the principle she is running>
Verdict: UPHOLD  (or)  Verdict: STRIKE DOWN
