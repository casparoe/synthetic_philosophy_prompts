My department is retiring a colleague at the end of the year — she came here as a one-year visiting instructor in the fall of 1994 and never left, thirty-one years, same basement office — and there's a party in three weeks where I've foolishly volunteered to read something. She's a metaphysician who works on persistence, so I want to write her a sestina that's secretly an argument about whether she's literally the same person who walked onto campus that September. So: 39 lines, six stanzas of six plus the three-line envoi, unrhymed, roughly ten syllables a line, loose enough to be read aloud by a nervous person. I'm not in her area at all (early modern, mostly Hume's religion), I've been reading around this stuff for about two weeks, so if anything I say below is wrong or muddled please tell me before you write it rather than quietly fixing it.

The form has to carry the theory instead of decorating it. Pick six end-words that each sustain two readings at once — an ordinary word about a working life (the office, the students, the years, "again") and a term from the debate about how things persist (now, then, part, whole, same, change are the obvious candidates, but I'd rather you found better ones). Then lean on two features of the sestina. First, every stanza contains all six end-words, each exactly once, and what varies from stanza to stanza is only their order — read a single stanza as a moment and you have everything present at once, wholly, which is what the endurantist insists on; read the whole poem as one long thing made of six parts held together by the rotation rule and you have the four-dimensionalist, for whom the person is the pattern across the stanzas and no stanza is her. I want the poem to keep both readings alive and not tell me which one it's picked until very late. Second, the rotation is a closed cycle, so the opening order of the six words would only come round again in a seventh stanza — and there is no seventh stanza. The return is promised by the rule and never delivered inside the poem, which is exactly the sentence I plan to say out loud at the party, that she's the same person who taught intro in '94.

Two things I want the poem to actually know, because this is where the retirement stings. One: there's a real difference between the worm theorist and the stage theorist, even though they're both four-dimensionalists about temporal parts. For the worm theorist my sentence is literally true — she is that whole spread-out thing, 1994 stage and podium stage alike. For the stage theorist the woman standing up to say thank you is not strictly identical to the visiting instructor at all, only counterpart-related to her, and "same person" is a courtesy we extend across the gap. I'd love the poem to feel that difference without ever naming it, and to notice the consolation hiding in the worm view: the 1994 part isn't gone or destroyed, it's still perfectly real, just not reachable from this end. Two: the old objection that if she's a spread-out worm then nothing ever really changes, you just get one part having a property and a different part lacking it, and the endurantist answer that real change needs one and the same thing to be young and then old — plus what the endurantist owes in return, namely an account of how a single object can hold incompatible properties, where the honest reply is that she has them at times, or by relation to times, rather than flat out. And the envoi is the payoff: three lines, all six words, two per line, everything present simultaneously and squeezed into a third of the room it had. That's the endurantist's dream of complete presence and it should cost something — whatever the words gain by being all in one place, they lose the ordering, the sense of one following another, the fact that some of them used to be young.

No jargon in the poem itself: no "exdurantism," no "temporary intrinsics," no names, and absolutely no ship that gets its planks replaced and no river anyone steps into twice, she's heard both a thousand times and it would ruin the room. It has to land for the two-thirds of the audience who've never taken a metaphysics course, so on the surface it should just be a good poem about a person and a building over thirty-one years, with the philosophy as the reason it hurts. If you think the envoi can't do what I'm describing, say so and propose another form before drafting — but I'd much rather it stayed a sestina.
