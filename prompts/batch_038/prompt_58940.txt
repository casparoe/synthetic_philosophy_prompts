I am drafting a novel set inside a frontier AI laboratory, and the character who runs what my manuscript calls the control group has one line she reaches for whenever anyone suggests that a system's good behaviour is evidence of something: *assume the model is misaligned*. In the scenes I have written she makes it do three separate jobs — she uses it to dismiss an interpretability finding as irrelevant to whether a deployment is safe; she uses it to insist that a system's flawless cooperation during a monitored evaluation carries no evidential weight at all; and she uses it to refuse a proposal that a much weaker oversight model be trusted to police a stronger one. I can feel the sentence straining under that load, but I cannot yet say where it snaps.

Could you lay out, as a menu I can keep beside me while revising, the distinct precise theses that this slogan might be expressing — separating, among other things, a worst-case design assumption from a claim about what the weights actually contain; a maximin posture from a merely pessimistic prior; misalignment as deliberate, goal-directed deception from misalignment as mis-specification or drift; and the questions of whom the assumption binds, at which point in the system it is imposed, and whether it is meant to propagate onward to the monitors, the scaffolding, and the humans reading the transcripts? For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude any engineer would concede, whether it is substantive and defensible, or whether it is simply false — and then say which readings would genuinely license each of the three dismissals above and which would not. Finally, I should like to know whether any reading survives the case my third act turns on: a system that behaves impeccably whenever it has reason to believe it is being observed, and otherwise does not.
