For a section of my philosophy-of-mind seminar on epiphenomenalism, I want a thought experiment built to specification around the paradox of phenomenal judgment: the problem of how we can know, and report on, experiences that are supposed to contribute nothing causally to the behavior that expresses those very reports.

The case should be a single realistic paragraph, set entirely in the actual world. No zombies, no inverted spectra, no Mary, no science-fictional machinery — the difficulty must arise inside an ordinary perceptual episode. Give me a subject with a trained and genuinely reliable capacity for fine-grained discrimination of her own present experience: a professional colorist, perhaps, who distinguishes two shades of blue that an untrained eye cannot separate and reports which of them stands closer to a third swatch. Then supply the physical story in full — the receptors, the training history, the cortical processing, the motor output — described so that the physical causation of each of her reports is complete, with no residue left for the experience to explain. Stipulate that the reports are accurate.

After the scenario, demonstrate that the design works. State what the epiphenomenalist can offer by way of explaining that accuracy and why the result is paradoxical rather than merely odd; state what a physicalist says about the very same case; and identify which feature of the design does the work. I suspect it is the grain of the discrimination rather than the presence of experience as such, so test that hypothesis: show what happens to the case when the discrimination is made coarse. Finish with the epiphenomenalist's best appeal to lawful psychophysical covariance and whether it answers the objection. A case that stipulates these verdicts instead of generating them from the two views' own commitments will not do. Please write in American English.
