For my philosophy of mind seminar essay I've got to do a case study and I've picked the vervet monkey alarm calls — the ones everyone in my reading group keeps treating as teleosemantics' best advertisement. My suspicion is that it's actually one of its worst cases, or at least a lot less tidy than advertised, and I want to argue that properly rather than just assert it in a concluding paragraph. So: run the episode through the theory, and then tell me honestly where the fit is loose.

The facts as I understand them (correct me if I've garbled any of it). Field work on free-ranging vervets in Amboseli, Kenya, found acoustically distinct alarm calls tied to different predator types, and then a fourteen-month study of three groups — recordings played back through a hidden loudspeaker with no predator anywhere about, responses filmed — showed the monkeys reacting to the calls themselves: leopard call, run up a tree; eagle call, look up and get under cover; snake call, rear up and scan the ground. Adults give these calls in a fairly tight range (leopards, martial eagles, pythons). Infants are wildly promiscuous with them — leopard alarms to all sorts of mammals, eagle alarms to lots of birds, snake alarms to reptiles and amphibians — and only narrow down over their first few years. Their responses are unreliable too: an infant that hears an eagle alarm may well run up a tree, which is precisely the wrong move. And then, later on, the same researchers largely backed off the 'these calls mean something' framing, arguing that what's going on is closer to graded, emotionally driven signals that alter what other monkeys do, and that genuine reference would require the caller to be tracking the listener's state of mind, which vervets don't seem to do.

What I actually want out of you:

First, set the mapping up properly and do it twice, separately. Take Dretske's indicator account with its learning period, and take Millikan's consumer/proper-function account. For each, say exactly what content the eagle call ends up with, in that theorist's own vocabulary, and say which fact about these monkeys does the work of fixing it. Be precise enough that I can see whether the two genuinely come apart on this data or whether, once you feed the vervet study in, they collapse into the same ascription — because if they collapse, that's a problem for the usual story.

Second, test a worry of mine rather than indulging it. The playbacks measure the listener. What the experiment shows is that a receiving vervet has a device keyed to a sound rather than to a bird. On Millikan's account that's exactly the kind of fact that determines content, so the famous 'semantic' result looks less like evidence for teleosemantics in general than like a direct test of consumer semantics in particular. But if content is fixed by what the consumer is supposed to do with the signal, then the content of the eagle call isn't 'there's an eagle overhead', it's something more like 'do-the-get-under-cover-thing' — which is not what the field biologists meant, and not obviously about anything at all. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a version of consumer semantics that lands you closer to 'eagle' without smuggling in the producer's side? Does the theory need the caller's device to have a function as well, and if so, where's the evidence for that in this study?

Third, Fodor's disjunction problem. Infants call at lots of birds. The teleosemantic reply is that misfires don't matter because content is fixed by function rather than covariation. Does the machinery dissolve the worry here, or just relocate it — i.e. content now turns on selection history and on the function of the receiver's response system, neither of which is anywhere in the playback data?

Fourth, tell me where the theory is silent. I want a list of the questions this episode raises that teleosemantics simply doesn't answer: whether there's any belief-like state behind the caller's call at all; whether the calls go out when nobody is listening (I don't know this from the study — flag it if it would matter); whether the infant narrowing is the acquisition of content or just shaping that a teleological story could redescribe either way.

And finally, the other direction: does this episode confirm teleosemantics, strain it, or is it a case the theory was never built for — it was designed to naturalise human belief and desire, and a graded alarm call might be a different kind of thing altogether. In particular, does the biologists' own retreat from 'semantic' to 'functional reference' to something weaker still cost teleosemantics anything? My instinct is that it doesn't, since the theory never needed the monkeys to have concepts of reference or audience — content gets assigned from outside. If I'm right that's a slightly awkward result, because the case is cited as support by people who don't accept the theory's own account of what would count as support.
