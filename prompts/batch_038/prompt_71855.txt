I am a senior in a philosophy-of-science seminar at my high school, and for my extended essay I have invented a toy cosmological model so that I can argue about something concrete instead of about real proposals. The model is made up. I know the general shape of the problem I am circling is a real one in cosmology, and I have deliberately changed the details, so please treat it as my construction rather than as something to be looked up.

Model S says this. Inflationary expansion produces a very large number of causally separated pocket universes, most of which end up empty and cold. In such an empty pocket, a thermal fluctuation will occasionally assemble a complete observer out of the vacuum: a body with a brain, together with apparent memories of a childhood, of years of training in physics, and of having just read a particular number off an instrument. I will call these flicker observers. They exist for a fraction of a second before dissipating, which is why nothing in their (apparent) memories actually happened. Model S says that for every ordinary observer — one whose memories were genuinely caused by a real past — there are about 10^40 flicker observers. It also says that the fluctuation reproduces exactly the same physical brain state an ordinary observer would be in, so that from the inside there is no experiment that could distinguish the two. Model S is not otherwise empirically empty: it predicts the observed pattern of temperature fluctuations in the cosmic background radiation to within current error bars, and no competing model has been able to reproduce that.

Now the reasoning I cannot get straight in my head. Model S implies that nearly every observer having my experiences is a flicker observer. I am an observer having these experiences. So if Model S is true, I am almost certainly a flicker observer, and my memory of having just checked Model S against the background data was never caused by any data. My advisor says this refutes Model S. I would like to understand what sort of refutation that is, and I have four specific questions.

1. Normally, when a theory implies something we do not find, we can protect the theory by giving up an auxiliary assumption — we postulate an unseen planet, or we concede that our sample was not representative. Here the auxiliary assumption seems to be that I am typical rather than a flicker observer. Why is abandoning that assumption not an acceptable rescue? What distinguishes sacrificing an auxiliary hypothesis from destroying the practice of using evidence at all — and if I have destroyed that practice, in what sense is my own argument above still an argument?

2. Cosmologists routinely assume that we do not occupy a privileged place. Why does that assumption seem harmless when we use it to estimate how common planets are, and explosive here? Is the relevant difference that in the ordinary case the properties of the sample are independent of my observing them, while here the sample includes the reliability of the observer doing the sampling? If so, please explain that difference more carefully than I have.

3. People often dismiss multiverse models as untestable, but Model S looks over-tested rather than untested: it makes a prediction about my own situation, and the prediction is that I am mistaken. Please explain the difference between a theory that makes no predictions about our observations, a theory that makes predictions we cannot physically reach, and a theory whose predictions about our observations can only be generated by first adopting a premise about where we sit among observers. Which of the three is Model S?

4. Does Model S's agreement with the background-radiation data still count in its favor, given that if Model S is true my possession of those data is very probably an accident of the vacuum? I would like to know whether the answer differs from the case of a theory that predicts my measuring instrument is broken. And does the absence of any rival model that matches those data help at all here, since my difficulty is not with Model S's uniqueness but with my own trustworthiness as a reporter?
