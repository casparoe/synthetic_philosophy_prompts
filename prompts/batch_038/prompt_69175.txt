I want to nail down what a theory of higher-order evidence actually has to deliver before I start adjudicating between the ones on offer, so: write me the specification, the list of requirements any adequate account of "what should I believe when I get evidence that my believing was compromised" has to meet, with the case or consideration standing behind each one.

By cases I mean things like: you find out you were reasoning while badly sleep-deprived; you're told a pill you took an hour ago reliably degrades exactly the kind of inference you just made; a friend you rate as your epistemic equal on the question tells you flat out that you got it wrong; you learn the person who told you that is a paid contrarian whose job is to shake your confidence in whatever you conclude; you discover you were moved to a different table in a restaurant and can no longer recall which bill you actually checked; you learn that a long stretch of your past reasoning on this topic was done while infatuated. I want to know which of those a theory must get right and which it's allowed to bracket, and why.

Same for the distinctions it can't collapse — evidence about your performance on this token judgment vs evidence about your general competence at this type, evidence bearing on how you gathered your evidence vs evidence bearing on what the world is like, evidence that's self-locating vs evidence that's straightforwardly propositional, "you're unreliable here" vs "you went wrong" vs "you're being overconfident", a genuine undercutting defeater vs a mere reason to lower confidence, and having a reason to doubt p vs having a reason to doubt your believing p. If you think a distinction on that list is fake, say so and say which, that's fine, but then explain what a theory should do with the cases people use it for.

And the background results it can't just contradict: whatever the preface paradox and the lottery cases show about consistency norms, the fact that a belief plus "my belief-forming was shot" sounds Moore-paradoxical but isn't strictly a contradiction, ordinary conditionalization and what it does and doesn't let you do with self-doubt, the enkratic requirement and the standing objections to it, and the self-undermining worry that pops up when a theory's own verdicts are fed back in as evidence.

Two more things I want from you. First, there's a requirement I think is floating around that nobody has stated cleanly, and I want your version of it plus a verdict on whether it's real: something about a theory needing to be usable in the moment, i.e. it has to tell the agent what to do from inside the very reasoning capacity whose reliability is in question, without either silently trusting that capacity or collapsing into "suspend everything forever." Call it in-the-moment actionability or whatever you like, define it precisely, give the case that forces it, and say whether it's a genuine desideratum or an excuse for dogmatism dressed up as a constraint. Second, mark every requirement as non-negotiable or as a prejudice of a particular camp, and if some subset turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — say, responsiveness to real higher-order evidence plus non-gullibility in the face of manufactured doubt plus non-question-begging assessment — say so plainly and point at which one has to give, because that's the part I most want to see. Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates.

End with a couple of sentences, no more, on how the three or four big families of answers do against the list, and don't bother with a full scorecard.

Finally: give me your confidence for the main claims you're making, and single out the one item on the list you're least sure belongs there at all and why.
