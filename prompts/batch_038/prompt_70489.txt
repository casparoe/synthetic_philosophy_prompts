Hi — I'm drafting the second section of our MPhil philosophy of mathematics paper and I could do with a hand on one of the essay questions. It's on Benacerraf's dilemma, and I'm sick to death of questions that just invite a summary of "What Numbers Could Not Be" (1965) and "Mathematical Truth" (1973), which is all most candidates manage anyway.

Here's what I want the question to turn on. The standard deflationary line is that the 1973 argument leans on a causal theory of knowledge, so we can now wave it away: reliabilism and safety accounts don't need causation, so the dilemma evaporates. I want candidates forced to see why that isn't a free lunch. There's a pincer I've been toying with. Either the reliability requirement is demanding — in which case you still owe an account of what the link between our arithmetical beliefs and the numbers actually consists in, and you've merely renamed the causal problem — or, once you accept the structuralist picture from the 1965 paper, it's trivially cheap: any old progression that preserves the structure will do equally well, so "our beliefs are reliable" is satisfied by construction and does no epistemological work whatsoever.

To make it concrete I want a stipulated case embedded in the question, something along these lines (please improve on it, it's a first sketch): two wholly isolated communities, the Alphans and the Bethans, each of whom develop a full arithmetic out of a different starting progression, with no shared vocabulary for their objects and no means of cross-checking each other, who nevertheless agree on the truth value of every arithmetical sentence either can state. The candidate has to say whether the structuralist's readiness to embrace this case vindicates her epistemology or demonstrates that she hasn't got one.

Level is MPhil — they've read both papers closely but they aren't specialists, and they seperate reliably into people who can argue and people who can only label. Constraints: one question, answerable in roughly 45 minutes of a three-hour paper, worth 25 marks. State explicitly on the paper that they needn't cite anything and shouldn't attempt to quote; I want argument, not bibliography.

Could you give me:

(a) the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the stipulated case spelled out properly rather than in my shorthand above;

(b) a model answer of about 700 words that actually takes a position — I don't want a neutral literature tour that ends by saying reasonable people differ, I'd much rather it committed to something defensible that a strong candidate could push back on;

(c) a marking scheme of five must-hit points, 5 marks each, with a one-line note under each on how candidates typically botch it.

For (c), the failure mode I most want flagged is the candidate who writes "structuralism solves the identification problem, therefore the epistemological problem is all that's left over", as though that were progress rather than a restatement. Definately worth its own line. Also flag anyone who treats the causal theory as a mere historical relic of 1973 rather than as doing substantive work in the argument.
