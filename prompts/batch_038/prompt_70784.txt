I teach philosophy of economics and one of my PhD students has this paragraph in her prospectus, where it's supposed to motivate an instrumentalist defense of agent-based models:

> Milton Friedman's 1953 essay settled the question of what assumptions are for. The realism of a theory's assumptions is beside the point; the only thing that matters is whether the theory predicts well. Friedman's evidence for this is that our most successful theories are wildly unrealistic: the formula for a falling body assumes the body is falling in a vacuum, and the expert billiard player is described as though he solved the relevant differential equations for each shot, which obviously he doesn't. From this Friedman draws the striking conclusion that the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions, which means that an objection consisting in pointing out how far a model's assumptions are from the facts just misses the point. The assumptions are instruments for generating predictions, and they get confirmed by the same thing that confirms the theory: its track record. On this view, arguing about whether firms really set marginal cost equal to marginal revenue isn't science at all.

Before I send it back, how much of that is actually wrong as opposed to just loose? Go at it claim by claim: what would a careful reader of the essay sign off on, what's a defensible simplification, what's a distortion. Two things i specifically want nailed down — whether the significance/unrealism line licenses the use she's making of it, namely that we can stop litgating the truth of assumptions altogether, and whether predictive success confirms the assumptions themselves or only the theory that carries them. I suspect the billiards example is doing much narrower work than she allows; tell me if I'm wrong about that too. Then close with the single strongest objection to her reading that she'll have to answer in the paper. Under 500 words, please.
