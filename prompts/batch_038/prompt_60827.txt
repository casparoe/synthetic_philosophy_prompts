I'm the unlucky one who has to type this up. Our philosophy-of-mind reading group has been going in circles on cognitive penetration of perception for a semester now — roughly one half sits in the Fodor/Pylyshyn line (early vision is encapsulated, and every alleged counterexample dissolves into attention, memory, a shifted response criterion, or subjects working out what the experimenter wanted), and the other half thinks that line is being defended by definitional fiat, that "perception proper" keeps shrinking to whatever hasn't been penetrated yet, and that two decades of results have hollowed the position out. We're now writing a small interdisciplinary grant bid with a vision scientist, and the reviewers are obviously going to ask whether we even agree on what's being tested. So before anyone drafts the methods section, I want you to write the joint statement an actual adverserial collaboration between these two camps would produce — the document both sides could put their names to. Not a dialogue, not a referee's verdict on who's right, and not an essay on why the debate is stuck: a constructive text with the residual disagreement made exact.

Here's what it needs. First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — including what each thinks is actually established by the old wine study where an odorless red dye was added to a white wine and even experienced tasters came out with red-wine vocabulary for it (they currently can't agree on what that shows, so I want it pinned down in wording both accept), and what each makes of the demonstrations where you're told and fully believe two patches on a screen have the same reflectance and they still look different. Second, a disagreements table: for each live disagreement, the claim stated neutrally, then the penetration side's own preferred wording of its position and the impenetrability side's own preferred wording of theirs, plus a column marking whether each side thinks that particular disagreement is substantive or just about words. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody — and for each, a concrete paradigm both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, ideally something checkable rather than "the effect will be there / won't be there."

To stop this from being generic, seed it with two designs I sketched and have them argue over these specifically. Design A: a color-adaptation study. Everyone adapts for forty seconds to the same greenish field and is then tested on a neutral gray patch; the pixels are identical across conditions, and the only difference is a belief installed beforehand by a cover story plus a photo — in one condition the adapting field is a white tile under green light, in the other it's a genuinely green tile under white light. Outcome measure is the aftereffect nulling point. Design B: a masked same/different color discrimination, where a brief narrative establishes a category for the target (say, a ripe fruit in shade vs. a chip of dyed plastic), the display is identical across conditions, the subject is never asked anything about the category or the narrative, there's a debrief check that the belief was actually held, a concurrent verbal-load condition, and a nonverbal adjustment response alongside the button press so you can see whether the shift lives in the percept or in the willingness to say so. For each design I want: whether each camp agrees it bears on the dispute at all, the one or two controls each would refuse to run the study without, and their preregistered prediction including direction and roughly how big. Then let each camp add one further design of its own choosing.

Also in there: a short section where both sides recast the disagreement in signal-detection terms and say whether reporting sensitivity and criterion separately would dissolve any of it, and if so which part survives that move; a definitions box with a shared vocabulary both would sign (including an explicit ruling on whether attention-mediated effects count as penetration, because I'm pretty sure half the argument lives there); for each camp, what it would accept as evidence against its own view, stated so the other camp agrees it really would count; and one line each on where they think the literature has caricatured the other side. Please finish by saying plainly which parts of the dispute the collaboration has revealed to be merely terminological and why. American English throughout. And don't give me a bibliography or any named papers with years — if you need to gesture at an existing result, describe the paradigm in a sentence or two instead, since half our group will be reading this offline and fake citations would be worse than none.
