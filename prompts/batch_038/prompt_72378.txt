I'm a third-year cognitive science student double-majoring in philosophy, and this term I'm taking a seminar on concepts and categorization while also sitting in on a typography studio as an elective. Halfway through the term I realized that the running example I care about most is one our readings barely touch: the letters of the Roman alphabet. I got pulled in through Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project, the attempt to get a program to design "gridfonts" — complete alphabets drawn from a very small grid, so that the glyphs remain mutually recognizable as a, b, c and so on while sharing a consistent visual style. What struck me is that the project seems to presuppose something philosophically loaded: that letterness survives enormous distortion, that two shapes with almost no contour in common can be tokens of the same letter, and yet that a shape can stop being a letter at all through changes smaller than the ones it survives. My reading group has four members — a would-be palaeographer, a working type designer, a perceptual psychologist, and one person who thinks the entire question is confused — and last week we argued for two hours without noticing that we were answering four different questions.

What I want is the instrument that would have sorted us in ten minutes. Please write a "what actually makes every *a* an *a*?" sorting quiz for that group: a handout of nine case-based questions, each presenting one concrete situation and offering two or three responses, followed by a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto named positions. Before the questions, lay out the logical space you are working in — four or five axes of commitment that generate the positions, for instance whether letter-identity is fixed by the token's own shape, by its relations to the rest of the alphabet, by its causal ancestry, by the intentions of whoever produced it, by the classificatory dispositions of readers, or by an external standard such as a character encoding or a typographic tradition — and say which combinations of answers along those axes are jointly available. Only then should you locate actual views in that space. Each leaf of the resulting taxonomy needs a name (use the standard one where there is one, coin a descriptive one where there isn't) plus a single sentence on the commitment that leaf finds most embarrassing. The audience is third- and fourth-year students who have read the categorization literature but nothing on the metaphysics of artifacts or types; keep the handout to roughly two pages and the key to one.

A few constraints. No question may name a view or ask directly whether letters are abstract objects, types, or social kinds; someone who has never heard of any of the positions must be sorted purely by their reactions to cases. Please make sure these situations are among the nine, or tell me why one of them makes a bad question: a random ink blot that happens to be contour-for-contour identical to a printed *a*; a copying chain running three hundred years in which every scribe faithfully reproduces what is in front of them, and whose final form shares no visible feature with the first; a series of twenty shapes morphing continuously from a textbook double-storey *a* to an *o*; a blackletter *a*, a Times *a*, and an italic single-storey *a* set side by side; a glyph its designer insists is a new kind of *d* but which every reader takes for an *a*; and a damaged stone inscription where a palaeographer reads *a* with complete confidence despite half the bowl having chipped away. At least one question should be a trap — one where the intuitively obvious answer is the answer that misclassifies you — and the key should identify which question that is and which position it catches. Finally, tell me which cells of the space come out empty and why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored); flag every pair of leaves your nine questions cannot in fact separate, and say what additional case would separate them; and close with a short paragraph on whether the instrument is script-bound — whether someone raised on Devanagari or Hangul would answer it differently for reasons that have nothing to do with which view they hold.
