I am writing a novel whose central device is an anachronism handled seriously rather than comically: Ibn Khaldun is restored to life for one working season in a university library, and instead of marvelling at the present he does what he did all his life — reads other people's histories, badly at first and then correctly. Several chapters turn on what he makes of the modern quarrel about whether societies collapse at all, or whether that word merely names a change of shape that the losers of it experience as catastrophe. I need that quarrel worked out from inside his own apparatus — asabiyyah, the opposition of badawa and hadara, mulk, the natural lifespan he assigns to a ruling house, and the science of umran he claimed to have invented in the Muqaddimah of 1377 — and not in the generic sage-of-history voice that historical novelists usually put in such a mouth. He was a Maliki judge, a plague survivor, and a man who made his living between courts; I want the temperament that goes with that, including his contempt for historians who report wonders without asking whether the social conditions for them existed.

My chapters intercut three cases, and I have already committed to the following on the page, so your answers must engage these specifics rather than the question in the abstract.

The eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE: within a few decades the Mycenaean palaces are destroyed or deserted, the Hittite capital is abandoned, Ugarit burns and is never reoccupied; Egypt survives the Sea Peoples under Ramesses III but loses its Levantine possessions; cuneiform administration gives way over the following centuries to alphabetic writing and to Levantine and Cypriot commerce that eventually rebuilds the networks; sediment and isotope records in the region point to a sustained arid interval across the transition. My characters argue over whether this is a systems collapse — the failure of an interdependent complexity that had become expensive to maintain — or the fall of a ruling layer beneath which peasant and pastoral life continued largely unchanged.

The Classic Maya southern lowlands: over the eighth and ninth centuries the great capitals stop raising dated monuments, dynastic authority loses its credibility, and settlement density falls sharply, while the northern Yucatán, Chichén Itzá foremost, is rising as the south empties. Drought appears in the lake and cave records, but so does endemic warfare and a political theology in which the ruler's rituals guaranteed rain and harvest. Millions of people speak Mayan languages today.

Rapa Nui: the large endemic palm becomes extinct and the island is deforested; the standard account makes this a self-inflicted ecocide that produced famine, war and the toppling of the moai, which continued into the 1830s. Competing work stresses the Polynesian rat's effect on palm regeneration, and reads the islanders' stone-mulch gardens as intensification rather than desperation. Hereditary chiefly power gives way to warrior leaders and then to the birdman competition at Orongo, in which the clan that retrieves the season's first tern egg from the offshore islet controls the distribution of resources for a year. The real demographic catastrophe is external: some two to three thousand people at first European contact in 1722, Peruvian slaving from 1862, epidemic disease, and a low of 111 inhabitants by 1877, before Chilean annexation in 1888.

Give me his verdict, case by case, on whether the word collapse applies — and if he rejects it, what term from his own vocabulary he would use instead, since I do not want him merely echoing a modern scepticism. Then take up the difficulty that interests me most: his cycle runs on a periphery, on a fresh group with unspent group feeling arriving from the margin to replace a softened one. Rapa Nui has no margin. What does his theory predict for a closed system in which asabiyyah can be consumed and never replenished from outside? Does it yield a monotone decline, a segmentary equilibrium of competing lineages, or does it simply stop applying, and would he say so? Relatedly, he dated a dynasty at roughly three generations; the Rapa Nui chiefly line claimed descent from a founding ancestor and outlasted that by centuries in ritual prestige while losing real command to the matato'a. How does he absorb that? And would the birdman cult strike him as a collapse of authority or as an ingenious successor mechanism — a way of allocating mulk by annual contest when hereditary succession can no longer command assent?

Finally, and this matters to me more than elegance: keep the settled apart from the contested. For each case, tell me which elements of the sketch above are common ground among specialists and which are live disputes where competent people disagree — the rat hypothesis on Rapa Nui is one I suspect, and the chronology of Chichén Itzá another — and mark clearly anywhere my framing quietly assumes a minority position. Do not let a contested reading into my manuscript wearing the clothes of a consensus, and where you are yourself unsure, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over. Close with a short note on where your reconstruction takes liberties: which of the things you have put in his mouth he could plausibly have argued from what he actually wrote, which are inventions my conceit requires, and which depend on a disputed reading of his own work — his remarks on depopulation after the Black Death, which killed his parents and most of his teachers, come immediately to mind. Argue rather than survey; I want no bibliography and no citations, only the reasoning.
