You are a senior philosopher of physics who has spent a decade handling manuscripts for a journal in the foundations of gravity, and you are now drafting an internal handbook chapter for newly appointed associate editors. Think carefully and work through the conceptual terrain in draft before you produce the deliverable; the chapter will be used by people who have a working knowledge of differential geometry but who will not redo an author's calculations, and who must nonetheless reach the same triage verdict on the same manuscript.

**The problem.** I am the handling editor at such a journal. Something like one submission in six invokes the hole argument — the argument, reconstructed by Earman and Norton in 1987 out of a construction Einstein had already considered in 1913, on which a generally covariant theory appears to admit two models that coincide everywhere outside some region and differ inside it, the two being related by a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside that region. What reaches my desk takes several recurring forms: papers claiming to have located a new indeterminism in some generally covariant theory; papers claiming that the hole argument now settles, or now fails to settle, the dispute between manifold substantivalism and relationalism; papers claiming that the argument transfers to electromagnetism, Yang–Mills, Newton–Cartan theory, or the canonical formulation of quantum gravity; and papers claiming that the usual dissolution — treating diffeomorphism-related models as representing one and the same physical situation, what Earman and Norton called Leibniz equivalence — is unavailable in some special case. My two associate editors and I disagree about which of these are triage decisions and which must go out to a specialist referee, and none of us can state the criterion we are actually using.

**Deliverable.** A reusable triage procedure for this class of manuscript.

Begin with roughly 400–600 words of analysis before the procedure itself: what the argument actually establishes as a matter of mathematics; what the live interpretive options are, including the option that individuates manifold points by means of the metric field rather than by bare haecceity, and the algebraic option that dispenses with the manifold altogether; and the four or five failure modes you see most often in such submissions. Then convert that analysis into the procedure — do not simply restate it.

The procedure should consist of six to ten ordered checks. For each check state: (a) the question it puts to the manuscript; (b) what a satisfactory answer looks like as it would appear in the text of a paper; (c) what failure looks like; and (d) the consequence of failure — desk rejection, rejection with reasons, revise-and-resubmit at the triage stage, or escalation to a specialist referee. The checks must collectively let a non-specialist editor adjudicate at least the following discriminations: whether the second structure the author produces is a model of the theory at all, given the field equations in the region where the drag is nontrivial, and whether the author varies the matter fields along with the metric or holds them fixed; whether the fields occupying that region are dynamical or whether some non-dynamical background structure is being recruited to individuate points, and what the presence of such structure does to the symmetry group of the theory; whether the two structures are isomorphic as models, or merely related by a map that preserves some of the structure and not all of it; whether the claimed physical difference is expressible in quantities that both parties to the metaphysical dispute accept as meaningful — coincidences, relational observables, invariants — or only by appeal to the identity of bare manifold points; whether the diffeomorphism in play is trivial at infinity or acts nontrivially there and so alters conserved charges; whether the announced indeterminism concerns initial data failing to determine a unique isomorphism class of models, or something else entirely; and whether the metaphysical conclusion is being asked to do work that the mathematics does not supply.

**Constraints.** I have forty-five minutes per manuscript at triage. Two associate editors and I must be able to run the procedure independently and land in the same place. The managing editor signs off on every desk rejection, so each rejection must be defensible in writing to someone with no expertise in the area. Mark explicitly which checks are mechanical — runnable from the text by a non-specialist — and which are interpretive, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle: in particular whether Leibniz equivalence is a semantic stipulation about a theory or a consequence of its formalism, and whether a journal is competent to adjudicate that at all. Where a check can be gamed by a determined author, say so and say what the giveaway is.

**Worked cases.** Run the procedure, in order, through three submissions, showing each check firing and the resulting disposition, and for each write the two to four sentences I would actually put in the desk letter.

1. A technically careful paper that performs the drag in a matter-free region of a globally hyperbolic vacuum solution, observes that the two metrics take different component values at the same manifold points, and concludes that general relativity is indeterministic and that manifold substantivalism is therefore false. Assume no mathematical errors.
2. A paper that fills the region with a prescribed, non-dynamical scalar field, uses its level surfaces to individuate points, drags the metric alone, and argues that the two resulting models are genuinely physically distinct rather than gauge copies, so the indeterminism is real — and further argues that relational individuation has been shown to fail. Assume the author handles the field equations correctly as far as they go.
3. A paper on asymptotically flat spacetimes arguing that diffeomorphisms which do not fall off to the identity at infinity relate models differing in their conserved charges, that the identification rule must therefore be restricted to a proper subgroup, and that the standard dissolution consequently fails in the very sector where it was supposed to hold. The prose is poor and the notation idiosyncratic.

At least one of the three verdicts should be one I would rather not have to deliver, and you should say which.

**Form and limits.** Numbered checks; 1,800–2,600 words in total. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations beyond those already in this prompt — my editors will not be able to look anything up, and neither will I. Do not put anything in quotation marks that purports to quote a real text; paraphrase instead. Define each technical term the first time it appears, and where you are genuinely unsure of a claim, flag the uncertainty rather than smoothing it over.
