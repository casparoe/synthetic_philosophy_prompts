Thirty-five years I ran a small optics lab, and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy the way other men fall into golf. I've got a reading group — mostly other retired scientists and engineers, plus two grad students who put up with us — and next month we're doing the argument that we might be living in a computer simulation, together with the argument that the very far future is where nearly all the value is and that we should therefore be, in the jargon, fanatical about it. Half the group's reaction to putting the two next to each other is "if we're in a sim then it's a video game, nothing we do matters, relax." The other half says "a sim is still minds having experiences, so nothing changes at all." I'm fairly sure both of those are far too coarse and that there are maybe a dozen distinct places to land, but I can't lay them out cleanly, and I want to hand the group something they can walk down by themselves without me standing at the front of the room.

So: build me a decision tree, or a sorting quiz with a scoring key, that starts from a commitment everyone in the room already shares — say, you give the simulation possibility more than token credence, and you think the far future could contain vastly more well-being than the whole past has — and ends at a named position on how much, and in what way, the simulation possibility changes what you ought to do about the long run.

Rules, please:

- Nine to twelve questions. Every one a concrete situation or a choice among stated options, never a request for a label. Nobody in my group should need to have read anything to answer them, so if a question uses a technical term, gloss it inside the question.
- Cut the top of the tree along the distinction I care most about, which is *how* the sim possibility is supposed to bite: does it change what you believe about the size and length of the future, does it change what that future would be worth, or does it change what you can actually cause? If you think that's a bad way to cut it, say so and show me a better cut — but do cut it, dont just list the views alphabetically.
- Use these three cases of mine somewhere in the tree. Polish the wording if you like, but keep what makes each one bite.
  - Case A, the caretaker: you learn for certain that whoever runs our simulation deletes a sim once its inhabitants reach a particular technological level, and otherwise lets it run for aeons. One friend says the astronomical future was therefore never on the table and we should stop agitating about it; another says we now have a lever on how long we get, so we had better be extremely careful which technologies we push. Which of them has it right — or are both right, or is this news not the sort of thing that could settle either?
  - Case B, the trade: a stranger offers to make the next three centuries for our own descendants about ten percent worse, in exchange for a trillion lives inside some simulation we will never see, never influence, and that has no causal connection to us whatsoever going from miserable to wonderful forever. Do you take the deal?
  - Case C, the long shot: an oracle you trust completely tells you the odds are a million to one that we're in a simulation which gets switched off within a century, and a million to one the other way that we're in base reality with an astronomically long future ahead of us. One of my group says that settles it, since a million-to-one shot at a googol good lives beats everything else on offer; another says the oracle has just told us to stop caring about the long run. Which one is making a mistake, and where exactly?
- Make sure at least one leaf is a position on which the simulation possibility makes you *more* ambitious about the long run, not less. If you think nothing coherent sits there, keep the branch in, mark it empty, and tell me why.
- Each leaf gets a short name (coin one — most of these have no standard name and I'd rather you invented a good one than borrowed a bad one), one sentence on the most uncomfortable thing it commits you to, and one sentence on what a person at that leaf would do differently on an ordinary Tuesday. That last part matters, because I suspect several of your leaves are the same position in different hats and I want to catch it.
- Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say what further question would separate them, or admit that none would.
- Wherever an answer combination is logically available but nobody holds it, keep the branch and diagnose the emptiness: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I care most about the merely unexplored ones, since two of my group members may be standing in one.
- End by telling me which single question does the most sorting in a room like mine — the one where we'll split, and where the split matters most.

Plain language throughout, indentation and arrows rather than pictures, and no reading list, no citations, no "for more on this see" — I can find things myself and I'd rather have the whole instrument in front of me in one go. If one of the questions I've asked for turns out to be a bad question, keep it in but tell me its bad, and why.
