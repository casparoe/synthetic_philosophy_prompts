My friend Desmond writes the moral-questions column for our diocesan paper and he's an Augustinian about lying — absolute prohibition, no exceptions, but he keeps insisting that hiding the truth isn't the same as lying. His verdicts so far:

- A woman sheltering her sister from a violent ex tells him the sister isn't in the house; she's in the detached garage. Desmond says its fine, the sentence is true, and better still would be to say nothing.
- A man asked by a mugger where he keeps his cash says nothing and eats a beating. Also fine — silence is never a lie.
- A nurse tells a frightened kid the shot won't sting, knowing it will. Wrong, and Desmond is blunt that kindness doesn't help.
- A priest asked by a reporter whether the parish is broke says they have no money to give him, meaning the benevolence fund is empty; the building fund isn't. Fine — the sense he actually holds is true and the words can carry it.
- A man tells his mother her cooking is the best in town when he thinks it's mediocre. Wrong, even though she'd be crushed by the truth.

New one he's got in his inbox: a husband asked point-blank by his wife whether he still loves her says of course he does, while intending only that he wishes her well.

What'll he rule? Which rules fit all of the above, which fits best and why, and would he change his mind if the same words came from a witness under oath instead of at a kitchen table? Finally, give me the case that would seperate the live rules for good.
