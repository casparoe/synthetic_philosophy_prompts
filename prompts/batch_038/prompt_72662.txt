I am convening a seventy-five-minute seminar in a second-year course on early modern philosophy, with a whiteboard as my only medium. The twenty-two students have read the Monadology and the Discourse on Metaphysics but nothing of Leibniz on law or justice; they have also read Hobbes, and one of them is a politics major who is determined to reduce any normative claim to power or to enlightened self-interest. The session has a single target: to get them to hold Leibniz's account of justice as a substantive ethical position in its own right — justice as the benevolence of the wise, taking pleasure in the happiness and perfection of others, admitting of ascending grades of strict right, equity, and piety, with the highest grade governed by the identification of perfection with harmony, that is, with agreement in variety — rather than as a corollary of the monadology.

I want the plan of the explanation, not the explanation.

First, the dependency structure. Give me the sequence of components with an explicit statement, for each, of what it presupposes and what becomes available only once it is in place. I need to know which pieces are load-bearing for the ethical conclusion and which are merely background, because I have seventy-five minutes and not two hours.

Second, an ordering constraint I have imposed on myself: the metaphysics is not to be front-loaded. My students already assume that Leibnizian ethics is downstream of the monads, and I want to enter through the juridical material instead. Tell me the first point at which harmony genuinely becomes load-bearing, why it cannot be introduced earlier than that without becoming decoration, and how to signal at that point that something previously stated is now being cashed out.

Third, the carrying case. I want one concrete dispute, capable of being re-described at each of the three grades in turn, so that the ascent from strict right to equity to piety is demonstrated on the same material rather than announced across three different examples. Tell me what the case must exhibit, what it must lack — my own suspicion is that it must not concern punishment and must not be a case in which the parties' interests happen to coincide, but I may be wrong about both — and the bare description I should write on the board before any interpretation is offered.

Fourth, the misconception map, separated into the errors they will bring with them and the errors my own sequence will manufacture. From the first group I expect at least: that the best-of-all-possible-worlds thesis licenses quietism; that pre-established harmony is itself doing the ethical work; that windowless monads make genuine moral relations between persons impossible; that a God who chooses the best makes Leibniz a voluntarist about justice; that harmony is an aesthetic ideal indifferent to anyone's suffering; and that the highest grade simply amounts to maximizing total happiness. For each, identify the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off and the shortest intervention that will do it, and tell me if any of them is better allowed to surface and then turned to my advantage.

Fifth, the so-what juncture. Name the single place in the sequence at which a competent student can concede every premise I have given and still ask why any of it obliges them, and give me the move that closes that gap at that place rather than at the end.

Sixth, one closing question, together with the answer that would show the idea has landed and the superficially similar answer that would show it has not.

Seventh, what I should deliberately omit — the technical distinctions among kinds of necessity, the full argument of the theodicy, his quarrel with Pufendorf — and what I should say aloud in the room to mark each omission as an omission rather than an oversight.
