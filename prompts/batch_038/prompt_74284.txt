The assignment (take-home, 800 words, week three):

> 'A government that delivers growth, safety, and competent administration has earned the right to rule, whatever the ballot box says.' Critically assess this claim. Your answer should explain what performance legitimacy is supposed to be, and say whether the delivery of goods can supply whatever it is that elections are supposed to supply.

The student's answer:

---

The claim in the prompt is a version of what political scientists call performance legitimacy: the idea that a regime's right to rule can rest on what it delivers rather than on how it was chosen. It's worth saying at the start that legitimacy here is not the same thing as stability. A regime can be stable because it is feared and because its opponents are in prison. That is coercion, not legitimacy. Legitimacy, in the sociological sense Weber gave the word, is a matter of belief: a regime is legitimate to the extent that its subjects believe it has the right to rule, and obey for that reason rather than from fear or calculation. Weber sorted legitimate domination into three types — traditional, charismatic and legal-rational — and performance is not one of them, which is already a hint that the prompt is asking for something unusual.

Singapore is the standard case. The People's Action Party has governed continuously since 1959; elections are held regularly, though the field is not level; and yet trust in government runs among the highest in the world and the material results are extraordinary — public housing, savings, safety, a state that visibly works. If we ask whether Singaporeans think their government has the right to rule, the answer appears to be yes, and on the Weberian account that settles the question. The government is legitimate because it is believed to be legitimate, and it is believed to be legitimate because it delivers. This is also why performance legitimacy is more fragile than democratic legitimacy. A government whose title rests on results has to keep producing them. A democracy can survive four bad years, because the losers of an election still accept the authority of the winner; a performance regime cannot survive a bad decade, and when it stops delivering it loses its legitimacy. That is why governments of this kind are always one recession away from a crisis, and it is also why the collapse of a regime that had promised growth is proof that it never really had legitimacy in the first place: what looked like a title was only a run of good luck.

The obvious objection is that performance legitimacy lacks accountability. Without elections there is no mechanism for removing a government that has stopped performing, and no way for citizens to register dissent before the crisis arrives. This is a real difficulty, but I think it is less damaging than it looks, because the objection assumes that consent and performance are rival sources of legitimacy, and on Locke's view they are not. In the Second Treatise consent need not be express: anyone who holds possessions or enjoys any part of the dominions of a government thereby gives his tacit consent and is bound to obey its laws for as long as he enjoys them. A Singaporean who owns a flat, uses the roads and takes the benefits of the state has, on that account, consented. So performance is not an alternative to consent; it is the ordinary way that tacit consent gets generated, and the two are really the same fact seen from opposite sides. The prompt's "whatever the ballot box says" is an overstatement only if you assume consent has to be given at a ballot box, and Locke denies that.

Where I think the prompt does go too far is the word "earned." Earning implies that delivering goods creates an entitlement that cannot be revoked, and that can't be right, since it leaves no room for the possibility that a highly competent government is also an unjust one. Order and growth are goods, but they are goods delivered to some people and withheld from others; a regime can be extremely effective at producing aggregate prosperity while imprisoning the minority that objects to it. The performance account has nothing to say at that point, because it measures outputs and not their distribution, and the belief of the majority cannot do the work of justifying what is done to the rest. So performance legitimacy can explain why a government is accepted, and even why it is accepted for good reasons, but it cannot by itself show that the government has earned the right to rule.
