You are a philosopher of physics who works on quantum foundations and has taught the ontological-models framework to graduate students. Please think the matter through carefully before answering, and since I suspect that half of our dispute was a failure to fix the question rather than a disagreement about the answer, begin by spelling out in your own words what the two camps in this debate are actually committed to before you adjudicate between my friend and me.

Last night we argued for three hours about the theorem of Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph, and by the end I could no longer tell which of us was talking nonsense. My reconstruction ran as follows. Harrigan and Spekkens divide the ways of modelling quantum theory into two camps: psi-ontic models, in which the quantum state is part of the physical state of the system itself, and psi-epistemic models, in which it is a compendium of what is known about an underlying physical state that would equally have been compatible with some other quantum state. Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph then proved that psi-epistemic models are impossible, provided only that systems prepared independently have independent physical states. It follows, I said, that the wavefunction has been shown to be a physical thing rather than information about a physical thing, that the result closes off the programme of treating the quantum state on the model of a probability distribution in classical statistical mechanics, and that anyone still calling the quantum state a state of knowledge is simply refuted.

My friend made three replies. First, that the theorem shows no such thing: it establishes only that distinct quantum states cannot overlap in the underlying state space, which is a claim about a mathematical framework, not a demonstration that the wavefunction is an object or a field, since even a Bohmian who counts as psi-ontic may regard the wavefunction as something closer to a law than to a material thing. Second, that QBism is entirely untouched, and indeed that the theorem is silent about the reality of the wavefunction altogether, because it concerns a framework that no one is obliged to enter. Third, that PBR is in the end a Bell theorem in disguise, the locality premise having simply been traded for an independence premise, so that no interpretive news has been delivered; he added, in support, that the authors themselves describe their result as being in the same spirit as Bell's theorem. I accused him of moving the goalposts on the second point and of not knowing what the theorem says on the third.

Please tell me where I went wrong and where he did. Specifically: does psi-ontic, as the framework defines it, amount to the claim that the wavefunction is a physical object, or is that a further step, and if it is a further step, what are the options I said I could not see? What exactly does the argument assume, and is my word "only" defensible? How much work is the independence assumption doing, and which views survive precisely because they deny it? Is his second reply a legitimate point or a retreat into immunity, given that the theorem plainly seems to bear on someone who wants to keep an observer-independent underlying state? And is the comparison with Bell defensible in the weak sense while still being wrong in the sense he intended? I remain inclined to think that if the quantum state is not information it must be a physical thing, so please be blunt with me if that instinct is what led me astray. Finally, state in a sentence or two what the theorem does license me to say about the reality of the wavefunction, and a sentence or two about what it does not.
