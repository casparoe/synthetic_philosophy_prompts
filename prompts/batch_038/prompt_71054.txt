So I'm in a seminar on the ethics of international criminal law this term (we did Lemkin in week three, which is why any of this is on my mind), and separately I sit on the "designations committee" of my uni's human rights society. Six of us, and our job is deciding what words the society's campaigns and annual report use — genocide, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity, atrocity — and becuase we're an annoying bunch of law and philosophy students we write a short rationale for every call and then treat our own past rationales as precedent. I've been asked to draft the rationale for Thursday's case, and I'd rather write the one that's consistent with us than the one that's consistent with my politics, so I want to know what our own track record actually commits us to.

The record so far, roughly in order: Armenia 1915 — genocide, unanimous. Rwanda 1994 — genocide, no debate. Srebrenica — genocide, following the tribunals, and we were explicit in the rationale that killing the men and boys of the enclave counted as destroying a substantial part of the group even though everyone else was expelled rather than killed. The Holodomor — declined, 4–2, on the grounds that the evidence we had pointed at class and political targeting across the USSR rather than intent to destroy Ukrainians as such, and political groups aren't on the Convention's list. Rohingya — first year we said crimes against humanity and possible genocide, because the UN mission's own wording was hedged; two years later, once the documentation had piled up, we moved to a flat genocide. Uyghurs — designated last spring on the strength of the birth-prevention and seperated-children material, with a rationale stressing that there was no mass killing and that the Convention's five acts aren't a killing-only list. And one refusal nobody remembers: a partner org asked us to designate for a small indigenous group whose language is down to a few hundred elderly speakers and whose ceremonies had basically lapsed, and we said no, 5–1, because the loss wasn't anybody's plan — migration, the labour market, television in the majority language. Someone argued at the time that this flatly contradicts the Uyghur call and it's still an open sore; the chair's answer was that Lemkin's own definition is about a coordinated plan aimed at the essential foundations of a group's life while the Convention narrows that to five acts, so we're Lemkinian about the plan requirement and legalistic about the act requirement. I don't think anyone's checked whether that's stable, and I suspect it isn't.

The new case is from an embargoed dossier our partner org gave us, so I'll leave names out. A state is fighting a separatist insurgency in a highland region whose population is a distinct religious and linguistic minority. As far as the dossier shows there's no mass killing, no sterilisation programme, and no systematic removal of children into another group — the schools stay open, they're just not allowed to teach in the minority language any more. What there is: shrines and monasteries demolished or converted to warehouses, clergy banned from instructing anyone under eighteen, the minority language banned in the courts and in all public administration, the highland population resettled as dispersed households across majority towns, regional history rewritten out of the national curriculum, and one leaked, unauthenticated internal memo saying the objective is for the community to stop existing as a distinct community within a generation. Two wrinkles: the state's position is that it's targeting an armed movement, not a group, and honestly the minority and the movement are hard to pull apart; and the diaspora organisation is publicly begging us for the word because a designation from us would get picked up by two national papers, which half the committee thinks is exactly the wrong reason to give it. So — what verdict best coheres with our precedents? I can see at least four candidate rules we might be running: strict Convention acts plus documented specific intent; the Lemkinian coordinated-plan rule; a don't-name-it-until-it's-adjudicated-or-overwhelming evidentiary rule; and possibly an unacknowledged the-word-is-a-lever rule, given the early Rohingya hesitation. Lay out which of those fit all seven precedents, what each predicts for Thursday, which precedent is the closest analog and why, and what we'd be adopting with either vote — my hunch is that "yes" quietly signs us up to a culturicide reading the Convention's drafters voted down 25 to 16, while "no" quietly signs us up to a killing-centred reading we already rejected in our own Uyghur rationale, but tell me if that's wrong. Then give me the hypothetical case we should put on next term's agenda that would force the candidate rules apart rather than leaving them all agreeing again. Please actually commit to a recommendation with a confidence level rather than handing me a menu — and if you think the exercise itself is misframed, say so up front instead of humoring me: if a term with a legal definition shouldn't be run like case law by six undergraduates, or if the way I've described our precedents already stacks the deck toward one answer, or if the dossier details I've given you are too thin to decide anything on, I'd rather hear that plainly than get a polite write-up.
