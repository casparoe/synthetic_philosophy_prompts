**Question as printed:**

Throughout, (S, d) denotes a two-person bargaining problem with d = (0, 0), S ⊆ ℝ²₊ nonempty, compact, convex, and comprehensive (if x ∈ S and 0 ≤ y ≤ x componentwise, then y ∈ S), with some x ∈ S strictly greater than d; conv denotes convex hull; a problem is *symmetric* if (x₁, x₂) ∈ S exactly when (x₂, x₁) ∈ S.

(a) [4 points] Define the Nash bargaining solution N(S, d) and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution K(S, d). In each case say what the ideal point a(S) is, and whether a(S) must belong to S.

(b) [6 points] Let S = conv{(0, 0), (4, 0), (3, 1), (0, 1)}. Compute N(S, d) and K(S, d).

(c) [6 points] Nash's characterization of his solution uses independence of irrelevant alternatives. Kalai and Smorodinsky drop that axiom and substitute a different one. State the axiom they substitute, and give an example — a pair of bargaining problems, with the relevant computations — showing that the Nash solution fails it.

(d) [4 points] True or false, with justification: N(S, d) = K(S, d) if and only if S is symmetric.

**Candidate 14's answer:**

(a) The Nash solution is the point of S maximizing the product x₁x₂ of the two players' utilities; under the assumptions stated it is the unique maximizer, and it is Pareto optimal. The ideal point is a(S) = (max x₁ over S, max x₂ over S), i.e. the best each player could get if the other were left at nothing; it need not itself be feasible. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the point where the Pareto frontier of S meets the segment joining d to a(S), equivalently the largest t ≥ 0 such that d + t(a(S) − d) ∈ S. It is also Pareto optimal.

(b) S = {x : x₂ ≤ 1, x₁ + x₂ ≤ 4, x₁, x₂ ≥ 0}, with vertices (0, 0), (4, 0), (3, 1), (0, 1). The Pareto frontier is the segment from (3, 1) to (4, 0), i.e. x₁ + x₂ = 4 with 0 ≤ x₂ ≤ 1; the flat part from (0, 1) to (3, 1) is not Pareto optimal, since x₁ can be raised there at no cost to player 2. The ideal point is a(S) = (4, 1) — player 1 can reach 4 at (4, 0), player 2 can reach 1 — and (4, 1) ∉ S since 4 + 1 > 4. For K, points on the ray from d are (4t, t), and feasibility requires 4t + t ≤ 4, so t ≤ 4/5 and K(S, d) = (16/5, 4/5) = (3.2, 0.8). For N, on the frontier x₁x₂ = x₁(4 − x₁) with x₁ ∈ [3, 4]; the unconstrained maximum of x₁(4 − x₁) is at x₁ = 2, which lies outside the interval, and the function is decreasing on [3, 4], so the maximum is at x₁ = 3: N(S, d) = (3, 1), with Nash product 3. Consistently, the KS point has product (3.2)(0.8) = 2.56 < 3.

(c) The substituted axiom is monotonicity: if S ⊆ S′ with the same disagreement point, then no player can be worse off in the larger problem, i.e. φ(S) ≤ φ(S′) componentwise. Intuitively, adding feasible agreements cannot hurt anyone. KS satisfies this, because it is defined by the ray to the ideal point and the ideal point can only move outward when S expands, so the intersection with the frontier moves outward too. Nash does not satisfy it. Example: let S be the unit square conv{(0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1)} and let S′ = conv{(0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1), (0.5, 4)}, so S ⊂ S′. Then N(S) = (1, 1). For S′, the upper boundary is x₂ = 6x₁ + 1 on x₁ ≤ 1/2 and x₂ = 7 − 6x₁ on x₁ ≥ 1/2; the Nash product on the second piece is x₁(7 − 6x₁), maximized at x₁ = 7/12, so N(S′) = (7/12, 7/2). Player 1 has fallen from 1 to 7/12 even though the feasible set grew, so the Nash solution violates monotonicity.

(d) True. If S is symmetric, then both solutions satisfy the symmetry axiom, so both lie on the line x₁ = x₂, and since both are Pareto optimal they must be the same point, namely the symmetric point of the frontier. Conversely, if S is not symmetric the two solutions come apart: in part (b) S has no symmetry, and indeed N = (3, 1) ≠ (3.2, 0.8) = K. The reason is that Nash looks only at the product of the two payoffs, while KS is anchored to the ideal point, and when the problem is asymmetric those two anchors pick different points of the frontier. So N = K exactly on the symmetric problems.
