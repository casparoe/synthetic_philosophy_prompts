I'm a third-year PhD student in HPS, nominally working on Einstein's philosophical correspondence but actually spending all my time in a joint reading group with the phil-of-religion people, and last week a single sentence derailed the whole session. Someone waved around 'God does not play dice' — the line everybody assumes is Einstein's signature objection to quantum theory — and used it to make two claims within about five minutes of each other. First, that Einstein's quarrel with quantum mechanics was ultimately theological rather than physical, so anyone who doesn't share his religion can just discount it and get on with the physics. Then, immediately after, that the very same sentence shows Einstein was a strict determinist who thought every event is fixed by law, which the same person deployed to argue that Einstein had no room left for moral responsibility at all. I couldn't say what was wrong with either move at the time, and it's been bugging me ever since, mostly because the sentence seems to be doing an enormous amount of argumentative work while saying almost nothing on its face.

So what I want is a careful unpacking of what that sentence could actually be asserting. Give me the distinct precise claims hiding in it, roughly from the trivial to the radical — a claim that there is no objective chance in nature, a claim about whether a statistical theory can be a complete description rather than merely a useful one, a claim about predictability in principle as opposed to in practice, a claim that the world is uniform and lawful at all, a claim that a theory of this kind is aesthetically or methodologically unacceptable to the person saying it, and whatever else you think belongs on the menu. For each of them say whether it's a platitude that nobody disputes, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's actually false, and whether Einstein plausibly meant it. Bear in mind the tension I keep hitting: the same man, in a slightly different register, said "I believe in Spinoza's God who reveals himself in the orderly harmony of what exists, not in a God who concerns himself with fates and actions of human beings", and elsewhere leaned on Schopenhauer's point to the effect that a person can do what he wills but cannot will what he wills. Which makes me wonder whether 'God' in the dice sentence is doing any agential work at all, or whether it's really just a stand-in for nature, in which case the whole thing stops being theology and becomes a claim about chance and completeness that an atheist could hold just as happily.

And then, the payoff I actually need for the seminar: which of those readings, if true, would genuinely license the 'it's just his religion, so it's not physics' dismissal, and which ones leave his objection standing as a substantive physical or metaphysical complaint about whether quantum mechanics is a final theory? Same question for the second move — which reading would have to be true for the determinism-to-no-moral-responsibility inference to go through, and does the slogan supply enough for that, or does it need premises Einstein never states? I suspect at least one of my colleague's arguments needs a lot more than the sentence gives him, but I'd rather have it laid out than asserted.
