I'm the secretary of a nine-person board that runs a community garden and hands out a few small grants. Next month I'm pitching my proposal at a regional meetup of board officers — mostly town clerks and rec-department staff, two parliamentarians who are strict about Robert's Rules, one city attorney, and a guy who teaches public finance at the community college. The proposal: when a grant decision comes down to several conditions, we vote on each condition seperately and let the yes-or-no on the grant follow automatically from those votes, instead of voting the motion up or down directly. I like it because you can actually see why we decided what we decided. But I ran it on paper against last year's votes and it sometimes flips the result — the board ends up rejecting a grant that a majority of us would have approved if we'd just voted on the motion.

Two wrinkles I already know about: I want each member's vote on each condition recorded in the minutes, and I can see us approving something in the spring that flat contradicts a condition we insisted on in the fall.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense. The objections this crowd specifically will raise, in the words they'd use, ordered by how much damage each does — with a reply I can say out loud in about a minute, and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which ones to just concede up front. And end with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
