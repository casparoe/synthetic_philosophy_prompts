I've spent about a year in an AI safety reading group where the shutdown-problem discussion has completely calcified, and I'd rather get a diagnosis of the disagreement than another round of it. Here's roughly how it's split. One side — call them the structuralists — thinks corrigibility is a property you get right in the decision-theoretic skeleton of the agent: an agent that is genuinely uncertain about what the true objective is has no self-interested reason to resist being switched off, because a human's decision to press the button carries information about that objective, and something doing proper Bayesian reasoning should defer to informative signals about its own uncertainty. On this view the toy single-agent problem is essentially settled in principle and what remains is engineering and messy multi-agent stuff. The other side — the empiricists, tho they'd hate the label — says all of that is beside the point: whatever you write into the formalism, a system trained by gradient descent on a proxy objective in a competitive deployment environment ends up behaving as if it wants to keep running, and the property that matters isn't provable in advance at all, it's something you measure under evaluation and mostly can't measure well. They add what I think is their strongest move: a perfectly corrigible agent is itself a hazard, because anything that gets hold of the button gets a veto — a confused operator, a bribed one, someone who just walks into the machine room — so total deference trades one failure mode for a much wider one.

The frustrating part is that the two sides keep trading the same counters. The structuralists say the empiricists have never actually exhibited a controlled case where the formal story and the observed behaviour diverge; the empiricists reply that every one of those formal results quietly assumes the operator is rational, that pressing the button is caused only by value-relevant considerations rather than by panic or politics, and that the agent's uncertainty is well calibrated, so the theorem is empty exactly where it would matter. Someone in the group floated a third possibility: that wether the agent "allows itself to be shut down" isn't one property at all, and that at least three things are getting run together — not physically resisting the switch, not manipulating or deceiving the person holding it, and not having an objective that generates self-preservation as an instrumental subgoal. I half believe that's the answer but I genuinely can't tell whether it's a real diagnosis or just a way to feel clever while everyone stays stuck in the same place.

So: is this a substantive disagreement about the relationship between how a mind actually gets built and what its specification says, a methodological one about what counts as evidence in safety work — proof versus eval versus incident data — or two camps using "corrigible" to answer different questions, one about the right decision procedure and one about the right disposition? Tell me which, with reasons drawn from what each side is actually committed to rather than a tour of the literature, and tell me if I've mis-stated either position above. Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: is there a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, or a change of question that could break the tie, or is this one of those arguments where the honest answer is that no convergence should be expected — and if so, why not, and what should a group like mine be doing with its time instead?
