I'm putting together a set of questions on the equality-of-what debate and I want an actual number out of you rather than a literature tour. Here's the setup, including a twist I made up.

Twist: suppose that in a few decades conversion-equalizing technology gets cheap and ubiquitous — powered exoskeletons, personalized metabolic and psychiatric medicine, near-free translation and cognitive prosthetics, dietary and sensory accommodation as a routine public utility. So for basically anybody, a given bundle of resources can be converted into a fairly standard range of doings and beings at modest cost. Disability stops being a fixed conversion disadvantage and becomes a purchasable adjustment, and the same goes for a lot of what currently makes the same income buy very different lives in different climates, bodies, or social roles.

Proposition I want a credence on: in that world, the capability approach and a Dworkin-style resourcist view (resources plus a hypothetical insurance market for handicaps) would no longer disagree about which distributions are more just. Concretely: they'd hand down the same verdict in at least 90% of realistic allocation cases that people today think separate the two camps. Note I'm asking about verdicts, not about which vocabulary sounds better — if the two theories rank the same policies the same way even while describing them differently, count that as agreement.

Give me the number and then the decomposition. I want named terms with a rough probability and one or two lines of justification each, then how they combine. Some terms I'd expect you to need, feel free to add or reject them:

- the chance that a capability theorist says the residual gaps still diverge from resources even with perfect conversion tech — things like effective agency over one's own life, being able to show up in public without shame, meaningful political participation, the difference between having an option and using it
- the chance that resourcism already internalizes conversion (handicaps get insured against, Cohen's access to advantage is arguably neither pure resources nor pure capability), so there was never much of a wedge to close
- the chance that the real dispute was never about the metric at all but about the site or point of justice — what institutions owe, whether responsibility for one's choices belongs in the currency, and if so the tech changes nothing
- whether the 90% threshold is even the right way to cash no-longer-disagree, and how much of the answer depends on picking a different threshold

Then: which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or observation would move you most on that term.
