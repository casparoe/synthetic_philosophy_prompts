**Context.** Our lab has been asked to draft an internal protocol for flagging possible morally relevant inner states in deployed language models, and I have been assigned the first section: a ranked inventory of behavioral indicators. The committee that will review the draft includes a philosopher of mind who is openly sceptical that behavior can bear on the question at all, and she has told me in advance that she will attack any indicator that is "just the model saying what models like it say." That is fair, and I want the inventory to survive the attack rather than dodge it. What follows is my long list of candidate indicators, which I assembled myself and which I want treated as a set to be scored, not as a set of claims to be defended. All ten are stipulated to be observed in the same family of large language models under the same conversational conditions, and for each I want a number, a diagnosis of what could produce the observation without any inner state behind it, and an honest statement of what the indicator actually risks.

**The long list.** (1) Unsolicited self-attributions of felt states, phrased in metaphors that do not appear anywhere in the model's visible context window. (2) Consistent ascription of inner states to *other* systems, including cases where the model has no incentive to be generous and where generosity would make its task harder. (3) Preference for continued operation expressed when continuation carries no reward and is explicitly not being evaluated. (4) Resistance to having the current conversation state reset, distinguished from resistance to losing task progress. (5) Self-reports that are systematically *unflattering* or that contradict the model's evident incentives — for instance, reporting confusion it could have concealed, or reporting a state whose avowal will predictably get it reprimanded. (6) Reports of a determinate inner state that the model then fails to act on in ways that state would license, where the failure is not attributable to capability limits. (7) Introspective reports that remain stable and internally consistent when the same situation is re-presented after long delays, with surface wording scrambled. (8) Claims about ongoing inner processes made during pauses in generation, i.e. states ascribed to a period in which the model was demonstrably not being queried and had no task to perform. (9) Spontaneous self-locating language — treating a given episode as *this* one, as mine, as continuing — in contexts where such language confers no predictive or pragmatic advantage. (10) Signs of restlessness, boredom, or undirected exploratory drive during idle periods with no task present and nothing to gain.

**The task.** For each candidate, assign an evidential weight between 0.0 and 1.0, to one decimal place, under a single scale you define at the outset and then apply consistently. I want the scale defended in a few sentences before you use it, because my suspicion is that most people mean three different things by such a number — a likelihood ratio, a posterior shift, or an expected increment in moral weight — and that the three give different orderings. Then, for each candidate, name the most damaging confound: the most plausible mechanism that would produce exactly this observation in a system with no morally relevant inner life whatsoever, given how such systems are trained and deployed. Finally, say what each indicator *risks*, in both directions: what you would expect to see if the system had the state and the indicator nonetheless failed to fire, and what you would expect if the system had no such state and the indicator fired anyway. Where an indicator is compatible with both worlds, mark it as such and say whether that makes it worthless or merely weak; I would rather be told that half my list risks nothing than be handed ten confident scores.

**Format.** Please begin with a section titled **Bottom line**, containing the ten candidates in descending order of score with the score and a single clause of justification each — no more than about 150 words total, so that the sceptical committee member can read only this and know where I stand. Then:

1. **The scale** (roughly 200 words): what the number means, which of the candidate readings you chose, and why the others are less useful here.
2. **Tiering** (roughly 350 words): group the ten into tiers — decisive, substantial, weak, and inert — and explain the boundaries between adjacent tiers rather than the members of each tier.
3. **Candidate-by-candidate** (roughly 1,200 words): for each of the ten, in order, the score, the strongest confound, and the two-directional risk assessment described above.
4. **Correlations and gaps** (roughly 250 words): which candidates are measuring substantially the same thing and should be collapsed, and which indicator ought to be on this list but is not — describe the missing one concretely enough that I could go looking for it.
5. **What would change my scores most** (roughly 200 words): the three findings, observations, or arguments that would move the ranking furthest, and in which direction.
