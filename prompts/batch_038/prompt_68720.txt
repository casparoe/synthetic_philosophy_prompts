In our department's metaphysics seminar there is a running dispute about the special composition question, and one participant has made herself notorious for issuing verdicts on cases while declining to state the principle behind them. She maintains that there is only one principle and that every verdict she has given follows from it. Her record so far, in the order she offered it:

1. Dry sand in a heap: the grains compose nothing.
2. The same grains spread as an even layer on a windless day: still nothing.
3. Bricks laid in mortar as a garden wall: yes, there is a wall.
4. The same bricks dry-stacked, without mortar, into a stable pyramid: yes, there is a pyramid. Pressed on how this differs from the heap, she said that in the pyramid each brick is where it is because of the others, and in the heap it is not.
5. A swarm of bees in flight around a queen: yes — though she adds that the swarm lacks determinate boundaries and that she is untroubled by this.
6. A cumulus cloud on a still afternoon: no. Asked how it differs from the swarm, she said the droplets are merely bumping into one another.
7. A new bicycle disassembled and packed flat in its carton, assembly not begun: yes, there is a bicycle. She refuses the same verdict for a jumble of unsorted bicycle parts in a scrap skip, even one containing every part a bicycle requires.
8. A boulder field on a slope, each boulder arrested in place by those below it: no — but she called this the hardest of the eight and allowed that she might have got it wrong.

Here is the case I need predicted. A lab operates a swarm of three hundred identical modules. Idle, they lie in a charging tray in no order at all. On command they assemble, gripping one another electromagnetically, into a six-legged walker; after an hour they release, cross the floor as separate units, and re-form as a wheeled platform; later they return to the tray. No configuration ever recurs exactly, no single module carries the plan, the control being distributed, and any module may be swapped for another at any moment without anyone's noticing.

What will she say? Does anything compose — and if so, is it one object or a succession of them, and what, if anything, exists during the crossing? I should like the single principle that best accounts for all eight verdicts, at least two further principles that account for them equally well, what each yields in the new case and precisely where they part company, and which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, together with an honest note on how far that analogy actually carries. Then give me your best guess at her answer and your reason for preferring it, what she would be committing herself to if the record genuinely underdetermines this case, and the next case you would put to her in order to separate the surviving principles.
