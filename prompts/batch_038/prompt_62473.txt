I teach secondary-school history, and the same argumentative shape turns up every year when we reach figures like Columbus or Jefferson. A student concedes the fact — that people were held in bondage, that some contemporaries protested at the time — and then says that we cannot judge a man of the sixteenth or eighteenth century by our standards. Three features of this exchange puzzle me, and I do not know whether any of them is a recognised thing.

The first is that the plea is confined to morals. Nobody excuses an ancient thinker's account of the physical world on the grounds that the evidence had not yet been gathered; we simply say he was mistaken, and we go on teaching him. The same man's treatment of other human beings is insulated by his century.

The second is that the plea is elastic in a way that tracks how much the audience likes the figure: an admired man is measured against the loosest norms available in his own period, a disliked man of that same period against ours.

The third is the one I most want a word for. When I produce dissenting contemporaries — clerics in the 1500s denouncing the treatment of indigenous peoples, abolitionists among Jefferson's own acquaintances — the reply is never a retreat from the defence but a relocation of its criterion: those critics were a handful, they had no power, nothing the man could have learned from them would have altered what he did. Concede that, and the criterion moves again, to whether acting on it would have cost him too much. Each new criterion is individually sensible, and the defence as a whole is never at risk.

Is there an established concept or literature covering this, as a whole or the third feature in particular? I would want the nearest existing notions identified and then distinguished from what I have described: I know there is a general debate about judging the past by present standards, and separate work on whether ignorance of morality excuses, but neither seems to capture a defence that survives every counterexample by changing what it demands. And if nothing covers the third feature, what would you call it, how would you define it, and what would count as a case that is *not* it — for instance, a legitimate revision of the criterion rather than a retreat? A bare label will not help me; I need the boundaries.
