My institute's research office has circulated a call for twelve-month pilot projects. The application is a structured abstract of no more than 300 words, and it will be read by a mixed panel: several philosophers and one economist with no training in ethics. I would like you to draft that abstract.

The half-formed idea behind it is this. Work on moral compromise is almost entirely about permission — when one may settle with people whose values one regards as wrong, what settlement costs the compromiser's integrity, whether the resulting action leaves a residue calling for acknowledgment or regret. What is missing is the positive question: can there be gains from trade? Two parties whose value systems are incommensurable, not merely differently weighted over the same considerations, might each rank a negotiated compromise above every outcome reachable without cooperation. If that is possible, compromise is not only tragic concession but mutual benefit, and something like the economics of exchange would apply to moral disagreement. My hunch, which the project should test rather than assume, is that it cannot be had for free: for each party's own ranking to register the other's satisfaction as a gain, something must make that ranking responsive to it, and there seem to be only three candidates — a shared second-order value such as fairness or the avoidance of conflict, the value of the ongoing relationship itself, or a first-order interest in not being obstructed. Each, if it does the work, appears to reintroduce a common measure and to dissolve the very incommensurability that made the case interesting.

The anchor case should be of a particular kind: a negotiated settlement over a site that one party treats as sacred and non-substitutable while the other needs it for housing — not a case where the parties weigh familiar considerations differently.

Please deliver the abstract as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, in this order:

1. The research question, stated so that it has a determinate answer, including what would count as having answered it.
2. The two standard deflections of that question — the permission-focused literature, and the economist's default assumption of a common metric — and why neither engages it.
3. The method, which must stay ordinal throughout and must not presuppose transferable utility, together with the reason for preferring it to the obvious alternatives.
4. What a successful result would look like and what would count as failure; for calibration, one failure mode I want addressed is that an ordinal definition of a gain from trade may turn out to be vacuous, since any agreement can be described as a gain once the parties' rankings are freely posited.
5. An honest assessment of what one researcher can do in twelve months, and the specific way the project would balloon if left unchecked.

Do not supply a bibliography or a literature review by citation; refer to positions and arguments descriptively and I will add the references myself. Write so that the economist can follow the reasoning without knowing the philosophical terminology.
