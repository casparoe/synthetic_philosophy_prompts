Could you write me a dialogue for me? Here's the set-up I want.

N. is a lexicographer. For twenty-five years she compiled a dictionary of an endangered language together with T., its last fluent speaker, who also edited her English prose on the side. T. died four years ago. N. is still at work: she writes new entries in his idiom — his coinages, his etymological hunches, his particular way of hedging a doubtful gloss — and her colleagues say she's become him on the page. She can't always tell, about her own recent intuitions, which ones are hers any more; the voice in her head that catches a bad sentence sounds like him. Last year she wrote down a term for a kinship concept she had no notes on, and a box of T.'s field papers that surfaced afterwards contained exactly that term.

Two characters arguing about what any of this means for the self:

- One takes a Hofstadterian line (GEB, I Am a Strange Loop): an I is a pattern, a self-reinforcing loop, and patterns come in degrees and can be instantiated in more than one head at once. So some genuine portion of T. is still around, running in N. — not as metaphor or memorial but as live machinery. And the smearing goes both ways: part of N. was running on his hardware and went with him, which is why the boundary is unreadable from the inside now.

- The other thinks that's a type/token confusion dressed up as metaphysics. On their view a self isn't a shape that can be instantiated; it's a single causally closed loop that maintains itself and has stakes belonging to it and to nothing else. N. is running a very high-fidelity model of T. — an astonishing thing for a model to be — but a model has no stakes of its own, every stake in that skull belongs to N., and it gets switched off whenever she fancies lunch. T. isn't surviving. N. is just extremely good.

Ground rules: both of them sharp, neither a strawman. I especially don't want the second one to be a flat-footed "that's just influence, dear" — give them the strongest version of the closed-loop criterion and let them press the awkward questions: if the same mechanism happens inside one skull (a half-forgotten childhood resurfacing in your handwriting), do we call that a second self, and if not, why does skin make a difference? Is the distributivist committed to a folk song or a religion being a distributed person? The distributivist gets real answers and real counter-punches back, and gets to point out that the closed-loop view owes an explanation of why T.'s death feels to N. like an amputation rather than a bereavement, and why the kinship term showed up at all.

I want genuine back-and-forth — each picking up the other's last point instead of reloading — at least one concession that changes the shape of the argument, and the box of field papers should be argued over properly from both sides, becuase it's the best evidence either of them has. Don't resolve it; end on whatever crux is still live. Around 1200–1800 words, and in British English please.
