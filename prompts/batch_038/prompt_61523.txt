I'm drafting a paper for a workshop on well-being and biotechnology, and I keep running into the same wall. The topic is the hedonistic imperative — the claim that we should use genetic and neurotechnological means to phase out suffering in all sentient life, not by adjusting circumstances but by recalibrating the hedonic treadmill itself so that the biological floor sits above zero, with the result that life is lived on gradients of increasing well-being rather than on a gradient between pain and its absence. I find the project's moral premise unassailable and its critics' stock responses unconvincing, but I want to see whether the unconvincingness can be made into a real argument rather than a mood. Almost every objection I encounter reduces to one of two familiar gestures: the wireheading worry, descended from the rat that presses a lever for intracranial self-stimulation until it collapses, and the experience-machine worry, descended from Nozick. Both are deployed as if they settled the matter, and both are usually aimed at the project as a whole rather than at any particular version of it. My working thesis is that these objections are not merely weak but dialectically illegitimate, because whatever intuitive force they have comes from a theory of value that the premises of the imperative already exclude.

Please argue that thesis, and argue it as strongly as you honestly can. The structure I have in mind turns on a distinction the critics blur. There are at least three separable interventions: (a) direct, unmediated stimulation of reward circuitry, decoupled from any activity in the world; (b) a uniform upward shift in hedonic set-point, so that the same ordinary activities — eating, working, conversation, sex, grief — are experienced as considerably better than they now are, while their objects stay fixed; (c) an expansion of the range and the discriminative resolution of affect, so that both good and bad states become more finely differentiated while the lowest admissible state is raised above the current neutral point. My claim is that the wireheading and experience-machine objections are objections to (a), that they are simply not addressed to (b) or (c), and that when they are pressed against (b) or (c) they must borrow premises about agency, achievement, authenticity, or the value of contact with reality that are foreign to hedonism about welfare and cannot be introduced mid-argument. The same analysis should apply to the objection that pain is informative: nociception, damage-avoidance and aversive learning are functional roles, and one can in principle discharge the function while abolishing the unpleasantness, replacing suffering with states that are avoided but not bad. I'd like the argument to end by classifying whatever resistance remains, showing that each residue is either a covert appeal to desire-satisfaction or an objective-list theory, an intuition about species-typical functioning with no normative work to do, or a preference for the actual over the merely possible of a kind that the objector would elsewhere disown.

At the same time, I don't want a piece of advocacy. Be explicit about where the argument overreaches and where I might be wrong. In particular: whether (c) is coherent at all, given that a range can be widened at the top only by widening it somewhere, and that any floor defined relative to a mean may drift with the mean; whether hedonic tone may partly encode relative social rank rather than absolute condition — if it does, a shift applied uniformly across a population might be neutralized in exactly the way that a uniform raise in income fails to change anyone's standing; whether preserving the informational content of pain while removing its badness leaves a concept of pain worth having; and whether my dialectical move proves too much, since an argument that shows every intuition against maximizing pleasant experience to be question-begging would equally license the lever-pressing life that the project's own proponents want to disown. Aim for something in the neighbourhood of fifteen hundred words, addressed to readers who are sympathetic to reducing suffering but suspicious of biotechnology as the means. Please keep it self-contained: no bibliography, no citations, no reference list, and no invented page numbers or quotations. Name positions and thought experiments only where they are already common currency — hedonism, desire-satisfaction theories, objective-list theories, the experience machine, the self-stimulating rat — and spend your effort on the reasoning rather than on the scholarship.
