I'm supposed to circulate a short "where I stand" memo for an interdisciplinary reading group — half philosophy, half sociology, meets in a church hall, which everyone finds funny — that's spent two semesters going in circles about whether religion makes people morally better, and I'd like someone to poke at my position before I send it. My whole angle has been that "more moral" is doing several different jobs at once and nobody in the group keeps them apart, so I've been trying to hold all of the following apart explicitly:

(1) there are mind-independent moral facts and religion isn't needed to ground them or to find them — I'm a realist, not a divine-command person; (2) the individual-level data are real enough: in the US at least, religiously involved people give more money and more hours on average, and I don't think waving that away as self-report bias is honest; (3) but moral worth attaches to motives, so giving that's partly driven by hope of reward, fear of punishment, or standing in your congregation isn't *more moral* giving even when there's more of it — I lean Kantian here; (4) separately, practices and institutions get evaluated by their consequences, so a practice that reliably routes more help to people who need it is a better practice regardless of what's going on in anyone's heart; (5) when I write "more moral" I mean to keep four things distinct — rightness of acts, worth of motives, virtue of character, justice of institutions — and I think most of our arguments are just silent slides between those slots; (6) I also think generalized trust, the "most people can be trusted" item, names a moral attitude, a kind of second-personal openness to strangers, and not merely a prediction about how strangers will behave; (7) but I accept that trust levels across societies are mostly explained by institutional quality and material security rather than by anybody's character — Denmark is high-trust and very secular and I don't think it needs religion to stay that way; (8) religious belief as such is formed on bad evidence and its an epistemic defect; (9) and it's wrong to try to get people to hold beliefs because of the good consequences of holding them, so I'm not going to advocate belief-in-belief.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and how hard? I don't need my four-way distinction explained back to me — I want to know where it leaks. A few specific worries, though I might be worrying about the wrong ones. I suspect (3) and (4) are a contradiction I'm papering over with (5), but I can't tell whether the slot distinction really licenses holding both, or whether (4) is a consequentialist premise that (3) has no business sitting next to. I also have a nagging sense that (2), (4), (8) and (9) can't all sit still: if religion reliably delivers the good in (2) and I evaluate practices by consequences, it looks like I owe some pro-religion conclusion that (8) and (9) then forbid me from acting on, which makes (4) look inert. Is that a real bind, or is there a coherent "back the practice without backing the creed" move that escapes it — and if so, what does that move have to assume about *why* the practice works? And check (6) against (7): I currently think they're fine together and I can't say why, which makes me nervous that they're not. Rank whatever tensions you find by severity rather than listing them flat, say for each what would have to go and what the cheapest repair costs me, and then tell me which single revision buys the most cleanup across the whole set.
