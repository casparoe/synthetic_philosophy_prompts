I'm on a panel at our department's public philosophy night in a couple of weeks and I've drawn the short straw defending Searle's side of the original versus derived intentionality fight. My sparring partner will be a very good philosopher of mind who spent years defending the line that Dennett sketches with the two-bitser, so I want to rehearse against that person properly rather than against a caricature of them. So: play that philosopher. Call her Dr. Neel if you like, though the name hardly matters. She thinks a crude intentional system can be a genuine believer, that beliefs are real and not merely a useful way of talking, and that the standard Searlean move — content must trickle down from minds that have it originally — is a demand for a homunculus dressed up as metaphysics. She has read the whole literature and can call on it: the intentional stance, swampman, Block's objections about systems that satisfy every functional definition of mind while intuitively having none, the general worry that functionalism about content slides toward assigning content to thermostats and stomachs. Don't have her strawman me or get defensive; she's wry, confident, a little impatient with what she takes to be mystification, and she asks me questions back.

Ground rules for the exchange, and please follow them to the letter. Keep each reply to a single paragraph of continuous prose, maybe 150 words at the outside, so this reads like a conversation rather than a seminar paper. No headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables — plain prose only, the way you'd write a letter to a colleague. Stay in character from your first word until I type "stop"; if you think I've misunderstood something about the physical case, say so as Neel would rather than stepping outside the role to lecture me about my own question. Concede peripheral points freely when I land them — that's what makes the sparring worth anything — but never concede the main thesis, and in particular never retreat to the safe position that ascribing beliefs to a crude system is just a predictive instrument with no ontological commitment. That retreat is the one thing she refuses, so if you find yourself drifting toward it, that's your signal to go back and fight. Think through my challenge carefully before answering, and don't let her answer the stock thermostat example if what I've actually given her is a different case with different features.

Here's my opening, and she needs to take both prongs of it rather than picking the easier one. Forget the greenhouse and imagine a bimetallic strip in a dirt-cheap thermostat: it curls as it cools, and at a threshold the curl closes a contact that switches the furnace on. Same discrimination job as the flip-flop that tells 60-cycle from 50-cycle current, but nothing downstream ever reads the result — the contact just moves. First prong: ascriptions of content have to be consumed. A belief is a state some other part of the system can be wrong about, can check, can use in more than one inference. The strip has no such part. Its state causes effects, sure, but nothing is in a position to misread it, so calling it a belief about ambient temperature is doing all the work from outside the system. Second prong, and I think this is the one that actually bites: the very same behavior is equally well described as a state about air temperature, a state about mean molecular kinetic energy in the immediate vicinity, a state about whether the strip will exceed its threshold in the next minute, or a disjunction of all of these, and every candidate content predicts the strip just as well and is just as useful in the intentional stance. So the tie can't be broken by anything in the strip; it gets broken by my interest as an interpreter. Either I'm supplying the content, in which case the strip's intentionality is derived and my account still owes us something with content that isn't derived — which is precisely the stopping point Neel says she doesn't need — or content is so loose here that saying the strip believes it's cold carries no more information than saying it's cold. Your move.
