I'm in the second half of a machine translation course and today my prof shut down a question with a line he clearly loves. Someone asked whether a system that scores great BLEU on the held-out set actually understands what it's translating, and he waved it off, citing Dijkstra's crack about Turing: asking whether a machine can think is "about as relevant as the question of whether Submarines Can Swim." Then he moved on to beam search. Half the class took it as a mic drop, the other half (me) left confused about what exactly got refuted, if anything. I roughly know the Hofstadter-side objection — a thing that has only ever seen strings, never wanted to say anything to anybody, is doing something shallow, and whatever competence it shows is somehow borrowed from the humans who wrote the corpus and trained it — and I can't tell whether the Dijkstra line is an answer to that or a way of not answering it.

So what are the different precise claims that one sentence could be making? I want a menu I can bring to office hours, not your favorite reading defended. Candidates I keep generating but can't rank: that "think"/"understand" are simply defined so as to exclude machines, making the question verbal; that the word is being stretched analogically, the way we stretch "swim" to cover a submarine, so people are equivocating between a literal and a derived sense; that the question is answerable in principle but so boring it isn't worth anyone's time; that it's a category mistake becuase there's no fact of the matter at all; and something like "the only real question is whether the thing does the job, and the genuineness question is that one wearing a costume." For each, tell me whether it's a platitude nobody should resist, a substantive thesis that needs an argument, or just false — and be specific about what would make it false, since I suspect at least one of them is. Also note that the word he used was "relevant," not "answerable" or "meaningful," which seems like it might matter for which of these he's actually committed to.

The part I care about most: which of those readings, if true, would license his dismissal? The analogy is doing a lot of work and I don't know if it's work it can carry. A submarine really does propel itself through water; what it arguably lacks is a body plan, a history of having to get somewhere under its own steam, and having been designed without reference to fish. Is that supposed to carry over — i.e. the translation system's competence is parasitic on the humans whose text it learned from — or does the analogy break right there, since the point of a translation is that someone who wasn't involved in making it has to be able to understand it? And if the slogan works at all, does it work symmetrically, so that "does it really understand?" is exactly as empty as "does it really swim?", or does it only cut against one side of the debate? A clean list of readings with verdicts, plus a line on which one his argument actually needed, would be perfect.
