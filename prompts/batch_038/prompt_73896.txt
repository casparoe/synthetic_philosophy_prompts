I am preparing the handout for the session of my graduate seminar devoted to the combination problem, and I would like it to work as a sorting instrument rather than as a survey. The aim is that a student who works through it discovers which position she is already committed to, and encounters no name for that position until she arrives at the leaf that bears it.

Please write a decision tree with the following properties.

**Entry point.** Open with a single commitment stated in one sentence, one that every student who reaches the tree can accept without prejudging any of the questions below it — roughly, that having an experience is a real, non-eliminable feature of the world, and that no complete catalogue of the structural, dispositional, and functional facts about matter would thereby exhaust what it is like to be one of us. Everything downstream of that sentence is a further question about what has experiences, how many, and how they are related.

**Branch questions.** Six to eight of them. Each must be a claim or a concrete case that a student can adjudicate on its own merits; none may mention a theory, a school, or any word ending in *-ism*. At least two of the questions should be cases rather than abstractions — for instance, a scattered-cloud case (if the intrinsic phenomenal properties of every neuron in a brain were reproduced, one for one, in a widely dispersed cloud of dust grains that stood in no causal or functional relation to one another, would there be a subject there, or only a great many unowned qualitative goings-on?), or a division case (if a single subject's experiential life could be shown to contain, in fully determinate form, the experiential lives of many subjects, has anything been explained about the many, or only relocated the problem?). Where a question turns on a distinction students habitually conflate — intrinsic versus relational or dispositional properties, constitution versus numerical identity, fundamentality versus priority, unity of a subject versus unity of a representational content — gloss the distinction in a parenthetical aside in ordinary language, without jargon.

**The splits that must occur somewhere in the tree.** Whether fundamental experience belongs to very many small subjects or to one comprehensive one. Whether what combines, on the view in question, is subjects, or qualities that acquire a subject only when suitably arranged, or nothing at all. Whether the macro-level subject is numerically identical to some collection of micro-subjects or is a further thing constituted by them. Whether the relation between the fundamental layer and the human mind is one of constitution or of strong emergence, and if the latter, whether the emergence is of subjects or only of subject-level unity. Whether a view that retains phenomenal qualities at the fundamental level while denying them any bearer still deserves the family name at all. And whether the direction of explanation can be reversed: if the comprehensive subject comes first and finite subjects are derived from it, say in a parenthetical what would have to be true for that derivation to be anything other than a restatement of the original difficulty running backwards.

**Leaves.** Nine to twelve of them. The tree must reach, among whatever else you think the logical space demands: a bottom-up view on which subjects genuinely compose; a top-down view on which the one comprehensive subject is fundamental and we are aspects or partial manifestations of it; a view that posits protophenomenal properties which are not themselves experiential but jointly ground experience; a view that keeps fundamental qualities without fundamental subjects; a view on which the fundamental layer fixes only the intrinsic nature of physical dispositions, with experience arising from that nature; a strong-emergentist view that accepts a fundamental experiential layer but denies that subjects combine at all; and a view that concedes defeat on combination and concludes that the finite subject was never there to be accounted for. Give each leaf its customary name if it has one, or say plainly that it lacks one and supply a descriptive label of your own. Do not attribute any leaf to an individual philosopher, living or dead; the students should not come away thinking the exercise was about who said what. Each leaf also gets one or two sentences identifying the objection it is least able to answer and whether it has any reply worth the name.

**Unoccupied regions.** For every combination of answers your questions permit that no live view occupies, state which region it is and diagnose the vacancy: is the view incoherent, does it collapse into a neighbouring leaf on inspection, is it coherent but unattractive for reasons its would-be defenders cannot meet, or has nobody simply thought to defend it? I expect at least three such regions, and I would rather have them named as gaps than silently pruned from the tree.

**Inseparable pairs.** If two of your leaves cannot in fact be distinguished by the questions you have posed — and I would be surprised if none can — flag the pair explicitly and state what further commitment, if any, would tell them apart. Do not resolve the difficulty by redefining one of the leaves.

No citations, no quotations, no reading list, and no historical preamble. Plain indented text is perfectly adequate for the format; what I am buying is the architecture of the questions.
