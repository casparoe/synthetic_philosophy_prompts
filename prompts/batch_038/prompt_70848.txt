I sit on a state sentencing-revision commission, and one of my colleagues, a committed free-will skeptic, builds his entire reform package on a single analogy. It comes out of the public-health model of punishment associated with Pereboom and defended by Caruso against Dennett in their exchange on just deserts: incarcerating a dangerous offender is morally on all fours with quarantining the carrier of a contagious disease. Neither the carrier nor the offender is ultimately responsible for the condition that makes them dangerous; in both cases the state's only legitimate aim is to protect third parties; in both cases the isolation must be the least restrictive that achieves that protection; and in both cases the person is released once they cease to be a threat. From this he derives three conclusions he wants the commission to adopt. First, sentences should be set entirely by actuarial risk: the term is the shortest confinement that brings the individual's projected danger down to an acceptable baseline, and retributive proportionality has no independent role, neither lengthening nor shortening it. Second, prisons should be governed by the ethics of a hospital ward, so that no feature of confinement may be worse than containment and treatment strictly require, and any thought that the experience should be deliberately burdensome drops out of the justification altogether. Third, because carriers are not blamed, the condemnatory function of punishment is a residue of retributivist metaphysics; recasting the enterprise as prevention is not merely more accurate but more compassionate, which he says will make the package politically viable.

I am not asking you to adjudicate free will, and I know the quarantine model is a serious, live position with able defenders. I want an audit of the analogy itself, at the level of detail where analogies usually fail.

1. Lay out the correspondences explicitly: pathogen, carrier, mode of transmission, vulnerable third parties, the quarantine order, the release criterion, the least-restrictive-means constraint, vaccination and sanitation, contact tracing and screening programs, and the epidemiologist's risk estimates. Say what plays each role on the punishment side, and flag every slot where the target has no natural candidate at all.

2. Sort the disanalogies into those that are fatal to the three conclusions and those that defenders could absorb or argue around. I suspect several of the standard objections — the offender chose and the carrier did not; disease is involuntary; risk estimates are population-level while sentences are individual — are less damaging than they look, and that the real trouble lies elsewhere. Tell me where.

3. Identify which features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports, and which of those are doing unacknowledged work. Candidates I can see: that dangerousness is a settled, measurable property of a person rather than a relation between a person and a set of circumstances; that there is a determinate threshold analogous to ceasing to be infectious; that the confined person is a patient owed care rather than an agent owed a response; that the harm of confinement is a pure cost with no expressive dimension; that quarantine is presumptively brief and temporary.

4. Run the analogy in the direction my colleague declines to run it. If containment is the only legitimate aim, does the model license pre-emptive confinement of the high-risk never-offender, given that public health isolates carriers before they infect anyone? Does it render the gravity of the past act irrelevant, so that a trivial offense by a high-risk person and an atrocity by a low-risk person come out the same? And can it accommodate someone who is now no danger at all — the aged defendant, the single-occasion offender — while still explaining what, if anything, the state owes the victim?

5. Tell me what the compatibilist side actually gains here. Is holding a person responsible a form of respect that quarantine structurally cannot deliver, as I take Dennett's line to be, or is that a separate argument the analogy neither supports nor refutes? Could a retributivist borrow the quarantine model as institutional design while keeping desert in the justification — and if so, what is left of my colleague's argument once that option is on the table?

Then give me your verdict on each of the three conclusions separately: does it survive the audit intact, survive only in weakened form, or rest entirely on the analogy's holding? And if the analogy cannot carry the weight, propose a better one, or say that no single-source analogy fits the target and explain what that reveals about the actual shape of the disagreement.
