I'm three years into a jurisprudence PhD and I'm stuck on the chapter of my dissertation that's supposed to salvage something from Devlin after Hart. I basically concede Hart's two hits: there's no evidence that a society collapses when its shared morality changes, and Devlin equivocates between the identity of a society and the identity of the moral code a society happens to endorse. So in the draft I stipulate a stripped-down position and call it Devlin-lite: the state may permissibly criminalise conduct that is (i) regarded by a large majority in that political community as gravely immoral, (ii) carried out in private between consenting adults, (iii) productive of no physical, psychological or economic harm to any non-consenting person, and (iv) not even offensive to involuntary observers — where the *only* justificatory ground offered is that open tolerance of the conduct erodes a shared moral practice that is constitutive of that community's way of life, and where we stipulate further that there is no credible risk of social breakdown, institutional decay, or anything like it. So the disintegration thesis is gone by construction, and what's left is a claim about the loss of a form of life as a bad thing in itself, not as a cause of other bad things.

What I want from you is your credence that Devlin-lite is correct — meaning: that there really is a genuine pro tanto reason of that kind that can sometimes outweigh the liberal presumption against criminalising private conduct, not merely that some people think there is. And I want it decomposed, with a number and a line of justification for each term, plus how the terms combine (and tell me if you think they're independent, because I suspect they aren't). The axes I'd guess matter are: whether a constitutive shared practice can be a non-instrumental good at all rather than a repackaging of majority squeamishness; whether Devlin's epistemic filter for identifying what the community's morality even is (disgust and indignation, the man in the jury box) tracks anything real; and whether punishment is a means that can actually preserve such a practice or whether it self-defeats by converting shared commitment into compliance-through-fear, which would be a different and worse thing. Add whatever other terms you think belong — I have a suspicion that one of them should be about whether condition (iii) can ever be satisfied, i.e. whether "harm" in the harm principle is already doing the normative work so that no conduct qualifies. Then tell me which term my final number is most sensitive to and what would move it. Also, bluntly: does my stipulation actually escape Hart, or have I just relabelled the disintegration thesis and hidden the corpse in condition (iv)?

For calibration purposes, my own credence has been drifting around 0.25 and my co-author's is closer to 0.6 — she thinks the constitutive-good premise is nearly free and I think it does all the work, so I suspect that's where we diverge, but I can't tell whether she's just more sceptical about the harm principle than she admits. Give me your number, then tell me which of us is more likely wrong and on which term specifically.
