My department has just secured funding for a small three-year research network, and I — a third-year doctoral student in the philosophy of technology — have been asked to draft its intellectual programme. The network comprises two postdoctoral researchers (one working philosophically, one doing fieldwork), an annual workshop, and a final edited volume. The funding body has given it a deliberately provocative remit: to determine whether the thesis that artefacts have politics, in the sense made familiar by Langdon Winner's 1980 essay 'Do Artifacts Have Politics?' and its later reworking in *The Whale and the Reactor*, is a substantive claim at all, and if so, what sort of thing the politics consists in. I need to convert that remit into a ranked set of research questions, and I want a defended ordering rather than a map of the debate.

Some context that I think should shape the ranking. First, the funders have told us in terms that they consider the exchange between Winner, Latour, and Woolgar and Grint exhausted: a volume that merely re-adjudicates between inscription and interpretation will not count as a success, and nor will one that dissolves into a checklist for design practice. At least one question must therefore be philosophically load-bearing in a non-exegetical way, and its resolution must change how one would describe a concrete technical dispute. Second, the fieldwork postdoc will be embedded in a municipal authority deploying an automated ranking system to prioritise applicants for social housing. We will be able to read the procurement documents and the eligibility specification, and to interview both the officers who drafted the specification and those who hear appeals against its outputs; we will not be able to audit the system itself, since the vendor's model is commercially protected. Questions answerable through interpretive access to documents and to frontline discretion are thus tractable for us, whereas questions requiring counterfactual evidence about the system's effects on behaviour over several years are not.

Third, my own working diagnosis — which I would like you to attack rather than accept — is that the debate is deadlocked because 'politics' shifts between at least three registers that are rarely distinguished: a distributive register (who bears the costs and who takes the benefits of an arrangement), an agential register (what forms of action an arrangement authorises, discourages or forecloses), and a contestatory register (whether the arrangement is the kind of thing that can become the object of collective dispute and revision at all). If that diagnosis is right, much of the apparent disagreement between Winner and his critics is a disagreement about which register is in play.

Here are the candidate questions I have drafted. Please rank them for this network and explain the ranking:

(a) The inscription question: can a normative commitment survive into an artefact independently of the intentions of those who designed it, or is such ascription always a matter of uptake — and, in a case like ours, is the true locus of inscription the specification document rather than the system?

(b) The boundary problem: is the politics of a component (a threshold, a weighting) the same kind of thing as the politics of the system, and can any account of artefactual politics avoid committing itself to a mereology that says where an artefact begins and ends?

(c) The technical code question: does Feenberg's notion of a technical code do genuine explanatory work, or does it merely redescribe who is permitted to participate in setting standards?

(d) The responsibility question: if artefacts carry politics in the agential register, does it follow that responsibility for their operation can be located anywhere, and what becomes of the many-hands problem?

(e) The determinism question: is the charge of technological determinism in fact a charge about explanation — single-factor, exogenous causation — rather than about the ascription of political properties, such that Winner and Joerges are not contradicting one another at all?

(f) The indeterminacy question: does the claim that artefacts are texts whose meaning is settled in use render the politics question unanswerable, or does it merely relocate that question into use, and what would settle the dispute?

For each, tell me how much other work in the programme depends on its answer, how tractable it is given the interpretive-but-not-audit access I have described, whether resolving it would alter the description of the municipal case, and how neglected it is relative to the volume of writing it has attracted. Where these criteria conflict — I expect them to — say which you are weighting and why. Then tell me what would move a question up or down over the next eighteen months: a court decision on automated eligibility determinations, a change in public procurement rules, a credible empirical result about how much discretion appeals officers actually exercise, or a shift in the philosophy of AI agency.

Finally, be blunt about what to cut. I suspect (d) is the most overrated of the implications people draw from this thesis, and I would like you either to defend that suspicion or to explain why the responsibility question is doing more work than I think. If some question I have not listed should displace one of these, put it into the ranking and say which one it pushes out. What I am after is an agenda I can put in front of the funders with the reasoning exposed — not a survey, and not a bibliography.
