Retired biochemist, thirty-odd years at a bench, now reading game theory in the evenings. I wrote out an argument about the dollar auction — you know the one, highest bidder pays his bid and gets the dollar, second-highest pays his bid and gets nothing — and I don't trust it, because if it's right it proves far too much. The steps:

1. Money already bid is gone whichever way you go, so it can't enter the calculation. Only what happens next matters.
2. If I'm the low bidder, folding locks in my loss and buys me nothing at all. Bidding one more increment costs a few cents and, if my opponent folds, wins me the dollar. A few cents for a dollar is a good trade, so bidding again beats folding.
3. But my opponent is in exactly the same position, and by (2) he bids again too. So it holds at the next increment, and the next.
4. So neither of us ever folds, and we both end up paying several times what the dollar is worth.
5. Since (2) and (3) hold no matter what the other player does, entering the auction is irrational in the first place, and so is answering the opening bid.
6. Now generalize. Any contest where each extra increment is small compared to the prize, and where quitting forfets everything you've already spent, has the same shape: a drug development race, a lawsuit in its fourth year, the last two years of a grant project that plainly isn't working.
7. So nobody should ever enter any of those either.
8. But obviously they do get entered, by careful well-advised people, and they often end early with somebody comfortably ahead. So (7) is false.
9. So something in (1)–(3) is false. (1) I'd fight to keep — I've spent my whole career telling grad students not to count sunk costs — and (2) looks like plain arithmetic.

Which step breaks, and why did it look airtight to me when I wrote it down? And once I've fixed it, what does the repaired version predict about the contests in (6) — can escalation ever be the rational move there, or am I stuck saying every one of those people was making a mistake?
