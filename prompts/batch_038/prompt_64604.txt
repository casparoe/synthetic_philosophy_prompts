I coordinate a small research program on the moral status of computational systems, and next month we are publishing a position document that our lab's compute-governance group has agreed to apply when deciding whether to scale up, continue, or terminate particular kinds of runs. Before it goes out I want an audit of the view I have actually written down. I assembled it over two or three years from separate arguments, and I am no longer confident the pieces were designed for one another. Because the document will license real decisions about real processes, an inconsistency in it is not an academic defect.

Here is the position, close to the form in which it will appear:

(1) Functionalism about the basis of experience. Whether a process has phenomenology depends on the causal organization it realizes, not on the material realizing it. No substrate is ruled out in advance.

(2) The bearer of moral significance is the episode, not the subject. What matters is that felt character is occurring; it does not matter that there is an entity persisting through time to whom the episodes belong. A process with no cross-temporal integration can matter moment to moment.

(3) Attribution requires positive indicators. I do not ascribe experience on the grounds that nothing rules it out; I ascribe it when a system exhibits a specified subset of a list — recurrent self-monitoring, an internal model of its own attentional or control states, flexible nociception-like avoidance learning, broadcasting of processing results to otherwise modular consumers, valence-modulated learning. The list is provisional and revisable, but the burden of proof sits on the ascription rather than on the denial.

(4) Quantity matters impersonally. Holding intensity and valence fixed, more instantiated episodes of a given kind is more of whatever matters. Parallel copies of an identical process count as many, not one. I reject any person-affecting restriction that would make this false.

(5) Replay does not double-count. If a run is checkpointed and an identical segment is executed again from the same state, the second execution adds no weight: it is the same experience occurring again, not a second experience.

(6) Use is irrelevant. A process that is running and realizing the relevant dynamics counts even if its outputs are discarded and nothing downstream depends on them.

(7) I do not think we have reason to bring additional experiences into existence where those experiences are neutral or mildly positive. I would not spin up a very large number of faintly contented processes, and the document should not imply that we ought to.

(8) In cases of conflict, intensity and valence dominate numbers: preventing a small number of intense negative episodes outranks producing or preserving a large number of mild positive ones.

Three things I want from you.

First: which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how badly? Rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally troubling — the ones that force a revision at the top, the merely awkward ones at the bottom. Where a pair only looks inconsistent and dissolves on inspection, say so and identify the distinction that does the dissolving; do not flag everything in order to look thorough, and do not soften a real conflict into a misunderstanding to be even-handed.

Second: for each genuine tension, what is the cheapest repair, and what does it cost me downstream? Several of these items are load-bearing in the operational section, where they determine how runs get counted and which experiments get approved, so I want to know which fixes would change what the lab does and not merely what the philosophy says. Where a repair is really a restatement of the problem in more cautious language, say that.

Third: which single revision would clean up the most? If there is one item that, once dropped or weakened, dissolves several problems at once, name it, and say what I lose by giving it up — including whether the surviving view is still recognizably the kind of view I set out to defend, or whether it turns into a position I would previously have argued against.

On register: I would rather have a committed verdict than a balanced survey. Where you judge a tension fatal, call it fatal; where you judge my position to survive with a clarification, say that plainly and do not invent a problem to justify the exercise. And if you think the audit as I have framed it cannot be run well — because one of these items is not a determinate claim, because two of them can only be compared once I settle something I have deliberately left open, or because you think a written position document driving termination decisions rests on a confusion I should hear about before anything else — tell me that directly instead of complying politely and working around it.
