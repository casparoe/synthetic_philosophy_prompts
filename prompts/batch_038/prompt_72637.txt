Our safety reading group has spent something like eight months on the low-impact question — whether you can define "don't cause a big impact" without a full specification of value — and we have gone completely flat, which is frustrating because everyone in the room knows both moves already.

On one side are the reachability people. Impact is about how much you narrow the space of futures: penalize a policy for reducing the ability to still get back to states you could otherwise reach, or for reducing your capacity to pursue some set of auxiliary goals later, relative to a baseline where you did nothing. On the other side are the people who say that's just cheating with extra steps. You had to choose which auxiliary goals, or which states count as equivalent-enough to be reachable, or which baseline is the counterfactual, and every one of those choices smuggles in a ranking of what matters. So you havent avoided needing values, you've buried them in a parameter where nobody will look at them. And the reachability people reply that some value-ladenness is unavoidable in any nontrivial safety property, so the real question is whether the leakage is small and robust rather than whether it's zero. That reply has never landed, and the objection has never been answered either.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: "impact" is doing two distinct jobs and the camps are measuring different quantities. One is counterfactual-difference — how much would the world have been otherwise — and on that notion you genuinely can't escape value judgments. The other is modal — how many futures that were open are still open — and on that one you mostly can, but you lose the thing people actually cared about, which was that the agent didn't wreck anything that mattered. If that's right the debate isnt stalled because it's hard; it's stalled because two debates are sharing a name.

I've also been playing with a third idea, which may be dumb. Score a policy by how much it narrows the goal-space of other agents in the environment, where those agents' goals are read off their actual behavior rather than stipulated by me, so the weighting over which goals matter comes out of the world instead of out of the designer. Call it delegated weighting. My worry is that it just inherits the objection one level up: I still picked which other agents count, and I still decided that behavior is evidence of what matters.

So: diagnose this. Is it a substantive disagreement resting on a factual or formal question — and if so, which question exactly — a verbal one about "impact," two different questions under one heading, a bedrock disagreement about how much value-ladenness is acceptable in a safety property that nobody can argue for, or an ill-posed question? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says rather than surveying the candidate measures, and tell me where my hypothesis and the delegated-weighting idea land. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing, a different question entirely — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why.

Write it as continuous prose, like an email, no headings or bullets or bold or tables. And commit to a diagnosis with your reasons instead of laying out the options and leaving me to pick; if you think I'm wrong about the two-jobs thing, say so plainly.
