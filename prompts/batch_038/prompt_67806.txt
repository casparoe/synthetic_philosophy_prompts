Three chapters into a thesis on relational accounts of personhood — mostly the ubuntu-adjacent literature — and my supervisor has now told me for the third time that I keep weighing theories against each other without ever saying what would make one adequate. Fair hit. So before I read another word of it I want the spec.

Purpose, so you know what counts as adequate here: I want an account that could actually settle institutional questions about people whose relationships have been destroyed or withheld — prolonged solitary confinement, advanced dementia, communities dispersed by enslavement or forced removal, the dead who are still named by descendants. It has to do work, not just sound right in a seminar.

Write the desiderata: what any theory of what it takes to be a person has to deliver. For each one give the case or consideration behind it, not just the label. And be explicit about which you take to be genuine requirements and which are really the partisan preferences of one camp wearing a neutral costume — is the demand that personhood must not depend on anyone else's attitudes a requirement, or just what individualists insist on? Mark the non-negotiables.

Stuff I want covered, plus whatever I've missed:

- standing versus capacity, and having personhood versus having more of it;
- whether relations constitute a person or only reveal one — the direction of explanation;
- the Menkiti line that personhood is earned and can be forfeited, against Gyekye's more moderate reply that there's a floor from birth;
- Mbiti's living dead, the idea that ancestors remain persons only while someone alive still remembers their names. Anything that ties personhood to being remembered owes an account of the unnamed;
- the asymetry problem, which I think is the heart of the whole thing: can a theory let relationships add to personhood without thereby letting the withdrawal of relationships subtract it? If not, say so plainly, because that's exactly where my supervisor will push;
- consistency with what the developmental and dementia literatures actually show, and with the fact that the law assigns personhood to companies and ships without blinking.

Then tell me which subset can't all be satisfied at once, if any. My suspicion is that a no-oppressor's-veto requirement — a hostile community cannot reduce your personhood by refusing to recognise it — is jointly unsatisfiable with relations being constitutive rather than merely evidential, and if that's right the list is itself a result rather than a preamble. Also say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates.

Finish with a brief pass, a paragraph each and no more, on how a Lockean psychological-continuity account, a Kantian dignity account, a Metz-style relational account and a capabilities/threshold account do against the list. Short — the criteria are the point, not the scorecard.

Two last things. British English throughout. And don't hand this over as though it were settled: flag the two or three requirements you're least sure are real requirements rather than my own prejudices smuggled in, say what argument or case would make you drop each of them, and say where you think your reasoning here is most likely to go wrong.
