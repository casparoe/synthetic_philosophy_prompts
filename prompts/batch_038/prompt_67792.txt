I need help building something I should have built a decade ago, and I'm probably the worst person to build it alone. Day job: I teach political philosophy. Side gig: I'm one of four founders and the current chair of the membership committee of a cohousing community — 47 households, 90 acres, a common house, scheduled work days, the whole apparatus. We get maybe eight or nine serious applications a year now. Three of us read each one, we meet for ninety minutes, and we make a recommendation that goes to the full membership at the monthly meeting; the bylaws need a supermajority to overturn us, so in practice the recommendation is close to decisive. Our substantive criterion is one clause and it's a mess: applicants must be "in sympathy with the shared purposes of the community." That's all it says. Everything else is fair-housing law, which our lawyer has walked us through twice and which I mostly follow, plus a list of things we may not consider at all. For what it's worth, we botched one three years back — turned down a family for reasons nobody can now reconstruct except that they didn't feel like us, and they went off and started a thriving farm co-op forty miles down the road. I still wince about it.

Here's the problem, and it's exactly the problem I teach, which is why I can't see it straight. I've spent twenty years running the seminar where one side says the self is prior to its ends and the other says that's a description of nothing that has ever lived, and I've genuinely never known which side I'm on when it matters. The liberal in me says you have no business screening people for what they value, only for what they'll do: you can require that a household sends somebody to the work days and pays the assessment on time, you can't require that they think the common house is sacred. The communitarian in me says a community that screens only conduct isn't a community, it's a subdivision with a really nice barn, and the reason any of us moved out here is that we're partly made of shared ends that no covenant can write down. Both of those sound right to me in the abstract, and I have watched them produce opposite votes in the same room on the same application. I've been calling the distinction I want the conduct/belief line, which is obviously too crude — plenty of conduct is expressive of belief, and plenty of stated belief is cheap talk delivered pleasantly to a committee. So: what I want from you is a procedure, not an essay on the tension. A sequence of questions my two fellow committee members — a retired plumber and a pediatric nurse practitioner, neither of whom has read any of this and neither of whom should have to — can work through on an application in about an hour. For each question, what counts as a sufficient answer. Where the procedure should simply produce a no. Where it should stop and hand the case up to the full membership because the issue is what we want to be rather than what the bylaws permit. And explicit flagging of which steps are judgment calls in procedural costume, because otherwise the person smuggling my own politics in and calling it method will be me.

Then run it on three cases that are close to real. (1) A couple in their fifties, both with demanding jobs an hour away, financialy impeccable, who told us plainly in the interview that they want a quiet house and a garden: full assessment, work days as scheduled, and no potlucks — not hostile to us, just not interested. (2) The adult son of a founding household who left at eighteen, was estranged for fifteen years, and now wants to come back with a partner who has written to people on our waitlist that she considers the place a cult and is coming for the school district only. (3) A household that would rescue us — they'd buy into the barn conversion at a premium and take on a third of the well-drilling debt — and is broadly disliked: loud, already litigious about the parking rules, and one of them has a firm opinion about everything, delivered at length, at every meeting. For each, tell me what the procedure outputs and where I'm going to hate it. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do. My suspicion is that any procedure honest enough to admit case (1) dissolves us into a housing development, and any procedure that keeps us a community rejects case (1) on grounds I'd be ashamed to put in writing — and I would much rather you said so bluntly and showed me where the trade sits than built me something that pretends the trade isn't there.
