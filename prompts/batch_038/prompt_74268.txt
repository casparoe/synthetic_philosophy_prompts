I'm an economics PhD student auditing a philosophy seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and last week one of my classmates brought in an argument that has been stuck in my throat ever since. We had just worked through the standard result that in a finite game of perfect information, common knowledge of rationality among the players delivers the backwards-induction outcome — so in the centipede game the first player takes the pot at the first node, and the long cooperative path never gets going. Her claim was that the very same assumptions, applied carefully rather than loosely, show something far stronger: that no group of rational players can ever be in a state of common knowledge of rationality at all, and hence that the entire project of characterizing solution concepts by epistemic conditions is empty. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it.

(1) The players are rational, and their rationality together with the structure of the game is common knowledge among them. (2) If (1) holds, then each player knows that play will follow the backwards-induction path; and since common knowledge is closed under entailment, this holds at every node of the game, whether or not that node is ever reached. (3) In the centipede game, (2) implies that player 1 takes at the first node, so every node thereafter is one that each player knows will never be reached. (4) Now suppose player 1 instead plays across. Player 2 is thereby located at a node which, by (2), she knows she will never occupy — and at which (2) nonetheless purports to assign her beliefs about the continuation of play. (5) No player can coherently both know that she is not at a node and know what she believes at that node: knowledge is factive, so her being at the node makes the content of (2) false there, and what is false is not known. (6) So at every node that (1) rules out, (1) itself fails: the players cannot there be rational and commonly know it. (7) But a rational player must be able to deliberate coherently at every node of the game she is actually playing, including nodes to which she assigns probability zero, because she cannot exclude being deposited at one by an opponent's move; a player who cannot deliberate at a node is not rational at it. (8) Therefore rationality and common knowledge of rationality are jointly unattainable: (1) can never obtain, and any solution concept derived from it is satisfied only vacuously.

I cannot accept (8), and I suspect something slips between (2) and (5), though my instinct is unreliable here because (7) also looks shaky and I can't tell which of the two is load-bearing. So: tell me which step you reject, and why it looked innocent when we wrote it on the board. I'd particularly like to know whether the argument turns on the reading of "knows" — knowledge in the factive sense versus probability-one belief accompanied by well-defined conditional beliefs — and whether it survives if we substitute the weaker notion throughout. Then set out the repairs available to the foundations programme (restricting the epistemic model to the nodes the players can actually reach; treating an off-path node as an occasion for belief revision rather than as a place where knowledge simply persists; weakening common knowledge to rationality plus some consistency constraint on off-path beliefs) and say what each one costs, especially whether any of them still delivers a unique outcome rather than a large permissive set. Finally, apply the generalization test I can't stop thinking about: if (8) were sound, wouldn't the same reasoning show that nobody can commonly know any contingent proposition whose falsity would leave them unable to reason — including, say, a class's common knowledge that a surprise exam will not fall on Friday? Tell me whether that is a reductio of my classmate's argument or a genuine result about common knowledge in general, because if it is the latter I want to understand what has gone wrong with common knowledge rather than with game theory. Please don't settle for pronouncing the argument invalid; I want to know what she gets to keep. If she is forced to concede that a rational player who has witnessed a deviation may rationally abandon backwards-induction reasoning, I'd like to hear whether that concession is a defeat of her argument or in fact the interesting theorem that was hiding inside it.
