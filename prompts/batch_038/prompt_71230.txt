Ok so I'm three years into a novel set in a country where the franchise is earned. You sit a civics-and-numeracy exam at 21: pass and your vote counts 1.0, fail and it counts 0.25 — you can still vote, it just weighs less. There's also an institution I call the Audit: every ten years a sortition jury of 400 ordinary citizens reviews whether the exam actually predicted decent outcomes, and it can rewrite the exam or scrap it. A friend who reads political philosophy helped me build the chairman of the exam board's defense of all this, and then, being a pain, he flipped it into an argument that seems to blow up the entire book — both camps in it. Roughly as he gave it to me:

1. Any case for weighting votes by competence needs the correctness of political decisions to be independent of the procedure that produces them. If outcomes are just whatever the procedure spits out, "better-informed voters choose better" has no content.
2. If correctness is independent, then who is better informed is independent too — a fact of the matter, discoverable empirically.
3. Discovering it requires a test. But the claim that a given test tracks the standard is itself a competence claim: it says the test's authors know who knows.
4. That claim can't be established by a further test of the test-writers without regress, and it can't be established by appeal to the independent standard, since locating the standard's knowers is exactly what we set out to do.
5. So the boundary of the franchise — who counts as qualified, and where the pass mark sits — can only be fixed by a decision that is not itself qualified.
6. If that fixing decision is made by an unqualified body, drawn at random or open to everybody, then the epistemic advantage of the qualified franchise is downstream of precisely the kind of judgment the epistocrat says is unfit to rule.
7. So either the boundary is arbitrary, in which case the regime's edge over a coin flip is unearned, or the boundary is democratic, in which case epistocracy is just democracy with a turnstile.
8. Either way no procedure derives authority from competence, because competence can never be certified except circularly or arbitrarily. And notice this isn't specific to voting: by the same reasoning no licensing exam certifies a surgeon, since the exam's validity rests on an uncertified judgment about exam-writers.

Step 8 is what made me suspicious, because my sister is a vascular surgeon and she is extremely certified. But I don't want to just wave it off, because 3 through 5 feel *right* about the Audit — the Audit is a sortition jury with no qualifcations at all and it holds a veto over the exam, and I wrote it that way deliberately, because I could feel that something was off about the exam board and coudn't name it. The Audit is the scene I'm proudest of.

So: which step do I reject? I'd like to keep (1) — I want the chairman to be an epistocrat with real convictions, not a shrugging relativist — and I'd like to keep whatever it is that forces me to keep the Audit in the novel. Tell me what a Condorcet-style jury theorem defense of the exam says about (3) and (4), what a Raz-ish service conception of authority says about them, and what each of those costs the chairman. If instead the thing is valid and I just have to sacrifice a premise, tell me which sacrifice is cheapest and what stops being true once I make it. And if there's an equivocation hiding in there, name the two senses and point at the step that trades between them.
