ok, so I'm on a task force in my state legislature and we've been asked to take a position on the interstate transmission line fight — the multi-state line that our utility commission has been slow-walking for years, and the federal backstop that lets a national regulator step in and permit it when states don't act. Before I write anything I want to know whether my own views on federalism even hang together, because I suspect they don't and I can't tell where the problem is.

Here's the package, roughly:

1. I'm a subsidiaritarian. Things should be decided at the lowest level that can actually handle them, and the burden of proof is on anyone who wants to move something up a level.
2. Following that, I think federal preemption of state law is usually a bad idea — but I also think the civil rights statutes of the 1960s are the paradigm case of legitimate federal action, and I'd argue with anyone who calls them overreach.
3. I put a lot of weight on Tiebout-style competition: people sorting themselves across jurisdictions disciplines governments in a way that elections alone can't, and that's a big part of what makes federalism worth having.
4. But the deepest value of federalism for me isn't efficiency, it's the ability of a place to be its own kind of place — distinctive local communities surviving national homogenization.
5. I lean on the Hayekian knowledge argument constantly: local knowledge is dispersed and tacit, a central authority can never aggregate it, so decentralization is epistemically better and not just morally better.
6. I don't trust courts to draw these lines. I think the boundaries get worked out politically, by legislatures and intergovernmental bargaining, and judges who invent federalism doctrines make things worse.
7. I do support federal minimum floors in a few areas — workplace safety, air and water — where I think interstate competition would otherwise produce a race to the bottom.
8. And I think the EU handles this better than we do: they've got subsidiarity written into the treaties, and I'd like to see something like it here.

The one I keep losing sleep over is whether 1 and 2 can both be true. That's the pair I'd want fixed first if it's actually broken.

So: audit the set. Rank the tensions by how bad they really are rather than listing everything as equally worrying, and for each one say what specifically would have to give — which belief I'd have to weaken, restrict, or drop, and what I'd lose by doing it. Be explicit about which pairs only look like they conflict and why. And don't tell me federalism is hard; I want to know which of my own arguments is load-bearing past its capacity, since I have a feeling at least one of them is being asked to do work it can't do. When you're done, tell me which single belief the transmission decision is actually going to turn on.
