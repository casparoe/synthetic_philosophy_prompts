You are a philosopher specializing in rational choice and decision theory, with a firm grip on the debate over whether the sunk cost effect is ever rational rather than merely a bias. Please think carefully and work through the cases explicitly rather than writing a general essay on sunk costs.

I sit on the investment committee of a venture fund. One partner, N., holds the deciding vote on our deep-tech portfolio. Her record over five years, with what she said in committee at the time:

1. Fusion materials, $35M in over five years. The physics failed; the team and lab equipment were absorbed elsewhere in our portfolio at a discount; N. had never discussed the company outside committee. Vote: wind down at once.
2. Battery chemistry, $50M in. The lead product failed, but the failure data pointed at an unexpected electrolyte effect. Vote: $12M follow-on for the pivot.
3. Satellite imaging, $20M in, weak on its own numbers, but two of our other holdings were about to sign large contracts with it. Vote: continue.
4. Quantum sensing, $60M in. Metrics flat, addressable market collapsed, but N. had championed the company to our LPs eight months earlier and wanted to keep working with the founder. Vote: reduced follow-on.
5. Industrial robotics, $18M in. The forward projection was clearly negative and, so far as we could see, nothing else had changed; the company was one milestone short of the endpoint in its original plan. Vote: continue another year. Her only explanation: "you don't walk away from something like this at this stage."

The case now in front of her: a synthetic biology company, $70M in over seven years. The lead program's forward projection is negative both on our analysts' numbers and on an outside expert's independent review. The data, strains, and tooling are bespoke to a target that no longer exists, and are worthless to us and to everyone else. N. has never mentioned the company outside committee. No other holding depends on it. Both founders are leaving the industry. The decisive trials have been run and read out, so another year buys no information. It is one milestone short of the endpoint in its original plan; reaching that endpoint costs the $15M follow-on and yields something with no market. What would be irrecoverably lost is seven years and $70M.

Predict N.'s vote. Then:

- Lay out the candidate rules consistent with 1–5 — at minimum a purely prospective rule that counts new evidence, residual assets, reputation, and portfolio effects as forward-looking value; a rule on which the size of a past outlay is evidence about the soundness of her own earlier judgment; a rule on which rendering a large past outlay worthless is itself a reason; and a rule requiring a higher bar for reversal than for entry. Say which 1–5 leave open, and where they come apart on case 6.
- Give your best guess with reasons, identifying the closest analog to 6 among 1–5 and where that analogy strains.
- Describe one concrete further case that would discriminate among the rules surviving your analysis.
- If the rest of us should vote as N. does, what principle are we thereby adopting? If we should not, where precisely does her rule go wrong?

No citations or references are needed; I want your own reconstruction of her reasoning.
