I'm a research assistant on the curatorial team for an exhibition at a regional archaeology museum, provisionally titled Endings: Seven Societies at the Edge, which opens in the spring, and I have just lost the better part of an evening to an argument with a friend of mine — an anthropology doctoral student who has read far more of the collapse literature than I have, my own training being in philosophy — about how our lead curator will rule on the last section of the show. The premise of the exhibition is that each case receives one of two words in its wall text, 'collapse' or 'transformation', and that Dr. Brenner alone chooses between them; there is a standing bet of a bottle of wine on what she does with the final entry. Her rulings so far, and the reasons she gave at panel, are these:

- Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, around 1200 BCE: collapse. The palatial administrations of Mycenaean Greece and the Hittite empire went within a couple of generations, Linear B and Hittite cuneiform fell out of use, the number of occupied settlements dropped sharply, and nothing of comparable complexity reappeared in the region for centuries. When a colleague pointed out that the populations obviously remained and that successor kingdoms in northern Syria and southeastern Anatolia held on for at least three centuries more, she said that was exactly the point: what died was the order, not the people, and the order did not come back.
- Classic Maya southern lowlands, ninth century: collapse, though she insisted on the plural and on dating it city by city. Dated monuments stop, the dynastic courts dissolve, the region's population falls heavily. That the northern Yucatán was entering its own florescence and that Maya people and languages are alive today she allowed as a gloss; she did not change the word.
- Norse Greenland: collapse. Both settlements were wholly given up, and there is no continuation to point to.
- Rapa Nui: transformation. She rejected the familiar self-destruction story outright — the forest loss owed a great deal to introduced rats, the population held until Europeans arrived, and the catastrophe that followed came from outside, in slave raiding and epidemic disease. When someone noted that statue quarrying had ceased long before contact, she answered that the end of a practice is not the end of a people.
- Cahokia: transformation, and specifically dispersal. The great center on the American Bottom emptied, but Mississippian life continued across the region and the descendant nations are still here; she said the panel would not write that a people had destroyed themselves when what they had done was leave.
- Angkor: transformation. The court moved south in the fifteenth century, the kingdom continued in reduced form, the region stayed populous, and Angkor Wat has never been empty.
- Western Roman Empire: transformation. Government, law, and the church passed into the successor kingdoms and the episcopate; there is no clean break to point at.
- Indus Valley cities: transformation. The cities were given up and the writing stopped, but the population moved into the countryside and its practices resurface in later South Asian village life.

She also has a line she repeats at nearly every meeting, something to the effect that a society with grandchildren has not died, it has changed, and I cannot make that square with the Maya entry, which she kept.

The section we have left is the Ancestral Puebloan Southwest: the Chacoan great-house system at its height, then the thirteenth-century depopulation of the Mesa Verde region. Our draft gives the visitor prolonged drought, the aggregation of scattered farmsteads into large defensible villages, the cessation of great-house and great-kiva construction, the end of whatever regional integration Chaco had supplied, and then — over two or three generations — near-total abandonment of the region, with households moving south and east toward the Rio Grande, the Tewa basin, and the Hopi and Zuni mesas. The cultural-preservation offices of several descendant communities have written to us asking that the section avoid 'collapse' and 'abandonment' and speak instead of migration, on the ground that their ancestors chose to leave and that their own villages are the continuation. My friend is confident Brenner rules transformation, and thinks the letters settle the matter before the evidence is reached. I have bet the other way, on three grounds: that the Mesa Verde region was genuinely emptied rather than thinned, and stayed empty in a way the countryside around Cahokia did not; that the ceremonial and political order — the great houses, the great kivas, the roads and outlier communities — was not carried forward anywhere, since what appears afterward in the Rio Grande pueblos is a different settlement and ceremonial order however much continuity of people and practice there may be; and that her Greenland entry shows she is willing to call a small, non-literate society that simply walked away from a place a collapse when nothing took its place.

What I want is not your view on whether the Southwest 'really' collapsed, though I would like that at the end. First, lay out the candidate criteria that Brenner's eight verdicts are jointly consistent with, stated precisely enough to be applied to a case nobody has described yet; there is more than one, and I want to see which verdicts each is needed to explain and which it strains — in particular, what separates the Indus cities from the Late Bronze Age in her treatment when both lost their writing and their central places, and what separates Cahokia from Greenland when in both a region was largely given up. Second, apply each candidate to the Ancestral Puebloan section and say what it predicts. Third, give me your best guess at her ruling, with the reason for preferring that criterion over the others, and say how confident you would be. Fourth, we have short reserve entries drafted for Teotihuacan, Great Zimbabwe, Tiwanaku, and the fall of the Western Zhou, any of which could be promoted in order to settle this: tell me which would best discriminate among the criteria you propose, and what specific feature of it does the work. Fifth, since I sit on the drafting team as well, tell me what the panel should write if it aims at consistency with her record rather than with her slogan, and — if the record genuinely does not decide the case — what principle we would be adopting by choosing one way rather than the other. Finally, say whether you think she is in fact tracking two variables at once and running them together, and whether the exhibition ought to have a third word available to her.
