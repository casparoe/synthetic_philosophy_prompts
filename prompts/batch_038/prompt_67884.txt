I cover neuroscience and I'm working on a piece about "trust your future self," and a grad student friend sketched this argument for me. It's supposed to show that believing in evidence at all is incoherent, which is a great hook except I'm pretty sure it's wrong and I can't say where.

(1) Rationality requires that your current credence in any claim match what you expect your own later credence to be.
(2) Your future self will have gone through the same training you have, so you expect her to conditionalize on whatever she learns — that is, to fully adopt whatever view the evidence supports.
(3) From (1) and (2), your current credence already equals what you expect the evidence to justify.
(4) If you already know what the evidence will justify, gathering it can't change what you should believe, so planning to investigate is planning to be irrational.
(5) Nobody's required to be irrational by gathering evidence, so (1) is false.

He says the whole idea of deference to your future self is bankrupt and there's no principled way to salvage any of it. Which step do you actually reject? I want to know why it looked innocent when I first read the thing, what it costs to give up — and if you kill (2), why the usual sure-loss betting setups don't settle the matter against you. If it makes a difference, I'd rather hold onto (1) than (5), but I'd also like to know whether any limited version of listening to your future self survives.
