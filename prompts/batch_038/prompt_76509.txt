So I've been at it with my flatmate for the best part of three hours and I'd like someone who isn't either of us to weigh in. I'm doing an evening philosophy course — we're on the emotions module — and we went through the usual three ways of carving them up: the feelings view, where the emotion just is your awareness of your own bodily changes (James, basically, the one where you're afraid because you're trembling); the judgement view, where being afraid of the dog on the path is a matter of judging the dog dangerous (Solomon, Nussbaum); and then the newer line that says neither of those works and emotions are much more like perceptions — a quasi-perceptual awareness of value, so that fear is your seeing the dog *as* fearsome, the way you see a face as angry. The lecturer's example against the judgement view was the spider: you can be terrified of something you sincerely judge to be harmless, and if the fear just were the judgement you'd be flatly contradicting yourself, which you're plainly not. What she called it was a recalcitrant emotion. The perception people say that's no problem at all, because optical illusions do exactly the same thing — you can know full well the two lines are the same length and still see one as longer, and nobody thinks you're contradicting yourself about that.

My flatmate has run off with this and turned it into a decision-making rule, and I cant tell whether he's being clever or having a laugh. He says: if emotions are perceptions of value then they're evidence in the same way eyesight is, so when he felt a wash of dread about a job offer last month that dread was him registering something real about the offer, and filing it away as "just anxiety" is the equivalent of closing your eyes and deciding the room is probably fine. His clincher was that he isn't being naive about it — you discount perception when you know the conditions are bad (dark, drunk, need glasses), and likewise you discount the dread when you know you've had no sleep, to much coffee and a row with your mum. So there's a discipline to it, it's not just follow your feelings. When I said the whole thing leaves you with no way to criticise an emotion, he said no, it leaves you able to criticise one in exactly the way you'd criticise a sighting.

Here's my question, and it's a practical one rather than a seminar one. Taking his reading as given — emotions as value-perceptions, illusions allowed for — what does the calibration actually look like? With a stick in water you can reach in and feel it, or ask somebody else, or measure it; the point being you've got independent ways of checking whether the deliverance is right. So what's the equivalent procedure for my fear of flying, or the flat prickle of wrongness I get around one particular colleague, or the fact that I feel completely fine about a decision my sister thinks is a disaster? How do you tell which of those are tracking something and which are noise? And is he right that the spider fear is exactly like the Müller-Lyer lines — same length, same epistemic standing, just a stubborn appearance that survives what you know?
