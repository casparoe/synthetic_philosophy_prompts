Our journal's statistics editor has recused herself from next month's decision — she co-authored one of the papers in the submission's own reference list — and the rest of the editorial board has to write the letter. Before we guess, I want to reconstruct how she thinks. For three years she has appended a short rationale to every recommendation she makes on a submission involving an inferential claim, and I have transcribed nine of them.

1. A direct, preregistered, well-powered replication of a widely cited priming effect; significant, about a third the magnitude reported in the original literature, interval excluding zero. Publish, on the claim that the effect is real but smaller than the published record suggests.
2. An exploratory, unregistered study, 45 per cell, p = .03 on the primary outcome. The authors disclosed everything: every condition run, every exclusion, every measure collected, and they labeled the work exploratory. Reject as a basis for the claim — disclosing what you did does not change what the design licenses.
3. A Bayesian reanalysis of an existing dataset with weakly informative priors, reporting credible intervals and Bayes factors. Accept, conditional on the authors defending the prior and showing the conclusion survives a different one.
4. A preregistered study in which the authors found, before analyzing the outcome, that the manipulation had failed for roughly a fifth of the sample; they added an exclusion rule and logged the deviation. Accept — a departure from a registration is not fatal if it is logged and made blind to the outcome.
5. A study of n = 15,000 from an administrative dataset, p < .0001, difference too small to matter, presented as strong support for the authors' theory. Reject — the design was excellent at detecting a difference no one should care about, and the slide from finding a difference to supporting a substantive theory is the real error.
6. A failed replication with n = 30 whose authors concluded the original effect does not exist. Reject — the study could not have detected the effect had it been present, so the null licenses nothing.
7. Data collection continued until the test crossed significance, fully disclosed. Reject.
8. A sequential design with a pre-specified spending function, interim looks logged, stopped at the third. Accept.
9. A well-powered equivalence test against a justified smallest-effect-of-interest margin, finding the effect indistinguishable from zero. Accept — a negative result earns publication precisely when the design makes it informative.

The submission before us: preregistered, n = 900, testing whether a well-known effect reverses direction under a particular condition. Result p = .003 in the reversal direction, interval excluding zero. The prior literature is twelve published studies, all under 60 participants, all reporting the effect in the original direction. No Bayesian analysis, no equivalence margin. The authors claim the reversal is established.

Predict her recommendation. Then do the harder work: reconstruct the rule she appears to be running. Give me at least three general principles, each stated precisely enough to reproduce all nine verdicts above, show where they coincide and where they come apart, and say which one you would bet on for this submission and why. Since the board must actually decide, also say what the letter should recommend, which of the nine is the closest precedent, and — if her record genuinely underdetermines the case — what principle the board would be committing itself to under each option. Finally, tell me what sort of submission I could put in front of her to settle which rule she operates by, and which of the nine verdicts is most awkward for the rule you favor.

No bibliography and no literature review; name positions and machinery, not papers.
