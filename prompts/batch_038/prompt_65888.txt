Next month I am presenting at a two-day workshop on logical empiricism and the social sciences, jointly hosted by a history-of-science program and a political theory center, and what I need help with is the discussion period rather than the paper, which is essentially finished. My thesis is that Carnap's politics of planning is usually treated either as a biographical curiosity or as a piece of Neurath's thought borrowed by proximity, and that both treatments miss the fact that his framework machinery supplies a real argumentative structure for it. On my reading, the principle of tolerance together with the internal/external distinction of the early 1950s implies that questions about how an economy is to be coordinated are not questions about the true nature of economic reality but pragmatic questions about which constitutive framework to adopt; the criteria governing that choice are not truth-conducive but have to do with the fruitfulness of the framework; and the particular fruitfulness criterion Carnap is implicitly committed to is one I call the maximization of deliberate decidability, namely the extent to which outcomes are settled by reasons that could be laid out and argued about rather than by drift, custom, or signals nobody chose. I argue that this meta-value is what keeps his late career talk of scientific humanism from collapsing into an expression of temperament, and that the 1929 manifesto's remarks about the Circle's sympathies with movements toward a reorganization of economic and social life are best read as an early, unargued version of the same commitment. The slot is twenty-five minutes with twenty for questions, though in my experience the questions start around minute twelve.

The room is unhelpful in three different directions and I want to be ready for all of them. Two historians of the Circle work primarily on Neurath and consider the calculation debate settled; they will want to know why anything I attribute to Carnap is not better attributed to Neurath, whose writing on calculation in kind and on inventory actually engages planning head-on. Several political theorists work on legitimacy and coercion and will press the point that tolerance is formulated for a single agent choosing a language, whereas a social order is imposed on people who did not choose it, so the analogy between frameworks and polities does not survive contact with the question of who is doing the choosing. And there is at least one philosopher of economics who holds that markets versus planning is a factual question with an answer and that deflating it is either trivial or evasive. My former advisor will also be there, and he thinks I overstate Carnap's coherence on these matters while being too generous about my own coinages.

For each objection, give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, then a note on where that reply is softest and whether the right move is to concede the point or to hold it. Tell me separately what I should concede in the opening five minutes so that the concessions are not extracted from me later, and identify the single question I am hoping nobody raises: my own guess is that it is either the charge that my meta-value makes Carnap's position untestable, since any planning outcome can be redescribed as a framework choice, or the comparison between framework decisionism and Weimar decisionism that someone in that room has certainly read about, but I would like you to say which of the two is more dangerous for me and why.
