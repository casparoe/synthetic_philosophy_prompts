Syllabus question for a grad seminar week I'm building on explanation in the human sciences, and I want to sanity check my framing before it goes in the packet.

The week is organized around "would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler?" — except I've come around to thinking that's not a hard question, it's a broken one, and I want to teach it as such. Carr settled this: outcomes come out of structural forces, and the individuals riding on top of them aren't causes in the sense historians care about. So once you subtract the personalities there's no truth condition left hanging around for the what-if to latch onto — there's nothing to be right or wrong about, and people who argue it are just doing metaphysics in a history costume.

My worry is pedagogical rather than substantive. A couple of my students are going to walk in wanting to treat the top-down versus bottom-up accounts of how the regime's worst policies actually developed as a live empirical dispute with a right answer, and if I tell them the Hitler question is empty they'll think I'm shutting them down with a slogan instead of an argument.

So: what's the cleanest way to show them that the structuralist line isn't a rival answer to the counterfactual, but a demonstration that there's no counterfactual there to answer? And do you think there's any version of the Hitler question that survives that lesson, or should I just drop it and teach the structural material straight?
