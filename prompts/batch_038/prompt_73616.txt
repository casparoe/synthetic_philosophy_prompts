I'm in a philosophy of probability seminar this term and for the final we get to do a "the philosophy turns up somewhere in culture" piece, and I picked Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead, mainly because the opening is basically a probability problem staged: the two of them kill time flipping a coin, Rosencrantz calls heads every time, and it comes up heads ninety-two times running. Guildenstern gets more and more worked up and ends up somewhere like the view that they must be caught in forces that are un-, sub- or supernatural — i.e. that what's broken isn't the outcome but the chance setup itself.

The thing I need help with: my seminar partner says the scene is a dramatization of Bertrand's paradox. His argument is that the principle of indifference only delivers a probability once you've fixed how you're carving up the space of possibilities, and the whole play is about two men who never get to fix theirs, because it's been fixed for them by Hamlet, by the Player, by whoever wrote the script. I think that's a stretch, because Bertrand's paradox lives on a continuum — the chord in the circle, the 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 depending on whether you randomise endpoints or radius or midpoint — and there's no parametrization ambiguity about a two-sided coin. A streak like that is improbable on every member of the natural family of hypotheses about the coin, which feels like a different disease entirely, maybe closer to Hume, or to those puzzles where you can't tell which sequence you're a member of.
