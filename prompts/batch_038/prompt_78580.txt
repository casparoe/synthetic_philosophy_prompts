I retired four years ago from a comparative physiology laboratory, where my working life was spent asking when an organism — as opposed to a collection of its organs — has stopped doing its job. Since then I have been reading philosophy of medicine and, rather to my surprise, have been appointed the lay member of our regional referral network's Determination of Death Review Panel, which hears contested determinations referred to it by five hospitals. I have discovered that the panel decides by precedent in much the way a field advances by a sequence of well-chosen experiments, and that in eleven years nobody has ever written down the rule it is running. The chair has asked me to prepare a memo ahead of next month's case. I want your help with the inference, not with the literature: no names, dates, statutes, case law, or surveys of positions, all of which I can obtain for myself. What I cannot obtain is the reasoning, and that is what follows.

Our seven contested determinations:

1. Traumatic whole-brain death, confirmed by two examinations and by absent intracerebral flow; ventilated; diabetes insipidus requiring vasopressin. Ruling: dead, unanimous. Minute: the body no longer regulates anything in relation to the world; the machine breathes for it.
2. Complete transection at the first cervical vertebra after a fall. Ventilator-dependent, fully conscious and communicating, brainstem intact, but total loss of autonomic control below the lesion: poikilothermia, pressor-dependent, no bladder or bowel control. Ruling: alive, unanimous. Minute: "integration need not be total to be integration; what remains still belongs to an organism rather than to an assortment of organs."
3. A woman at sixteen weeks' gestation with total brain infarction confirmed angiographically, maintained on support at the family's insistence, with the argument pressed that a body sustaining a pregnancy is manifestly integrated. She was supported to twenty-five weeks and a viable infant was delivered. Ruling: the woman dead; the argument explicitly rejected. Minute: "the work being done here is the fetus's and the machine's; her body is the site of it, not the author of it."
4. Controlled donation after circulatory death: withdrawal of support, asystole, five minutes of continuously absent circulation on the arterial tracing, declaration, then sternotomy, cannulation, and clamping of the aortic arch vessels before regional perfusion was begun. Two members dissented on the ground that the clamp was itself what made cerebral reperfusion impossible, so that the team produced the very condition it cited. Ruling: declaration upheld. Minute: "irreversibility is not a metaphysical property of a body but a statement about what will be done to it; we did not intend to restore the circulation and we did not."
5. An anencephalic newborn, breathing spontaneously, brainstem reflexes present, who died on the fourth day; the parents asked whether the child might be declared dead at birth so that organs could be procured while viable. Ruling: alive. Minute: "the absence of everything we most value is not the absence of the organism."
6. Whole-brain death by every clinical and flow criterion, but with a documented intact hypothalamic-pituitary axis: no diabetes insipidus, preserved cortisol response, sodium normal for four days without replacement. The family argued that a standard requiring cessation of all functions of the entire brain had not been met while hormonal function persisted. Ruling: dead. Minute: "hormonal function is not among the functions whose loss makes an organism dead, whether it persists on its own or is supplied from a bag."
7. Severe anoxic injury after a drowning: three weeks in, isoelectric electroencephalogram, no command-following, no cortical evoked responses, but corneal reflexes present and sporadic respiratory efforts against the ventilator; a laparotomy wound healed; febrile and leukocyte responses mounted against two infections and cleared; weight gained on enteral feeding. Ruling: alive. Minute: "it takes in, distributes, expends; it defends its boundaries; it repairs itself."

The new case. A woman of sixty-one with a catastrophic intracerebral hemorrhage; some brainstem function preserved, so the whole-brain standard is not met. The family elects controlled withdrawal with intent to donate. Extubation at 09:12; asystole on the arterial tracing at 09:41; five minutes of continuously absent circulation observed; declared dead on the circulatory standard at 09:46, per our protocol. Cannulation is begun. At 09:52 — six minutes after the declaration, and before any perfusion has been established — an organized rhythm returns without any intervention, and arterial pressure climbs over the next few minutes to 78/44. She remains apneic and unresponsive with absent brainstem reflexes. Support is reinstated at the family's request. By day six her heart beats unaided, she is warm without a warming device, her kidneys work, her surgical wounds are healing, she has cleared one infection with a mounted febrile response, her pituitary axis has not failed and requires no hormonal replacement, and her sodium is normal. She has no brainstem reflexes, no respiratory drive, no cortical activity, and absent intracerebral flow on angiography. She is ventilated and enterally fed by pump. Two questions are before the panel: whether the declaration at 09:46 was correct when made, and whether she is dead now.

What I need from you:

(a) State every rule — formulated as a criterion a clinician could actually apply at a bedside — that is consistent with all seven determinations. For each, say which precedents it was evidently built for, which it accommodates only under strain, and what that strain costs it.
(b) Predict how this panel will rule on both questions, with your confidence and your reasons; identify the closest analog among the seven and say precisely where the analogy gives out.
(c) State the principle the panel would be committing five hospitals to for the next decade if it ruled the other way on each question. I want both branches written out explicitly, because I do not think the members will recognize that they are choosing.
(d) Tell me whether any two of our seven determinations are genuinely inconsistent rather than merely uncomfortable, and if so which one the panel will eventually have to abandon and why.
(e) Name one further case that would force the surviving rules apart — one that could realistically be referred to a panel like ours, not a contrivance — and say what each rule pronounces about it.
