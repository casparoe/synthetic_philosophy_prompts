Our lead clinical program treats otherwise-fatal neurodegeneration by replacing cortical neurons with functionally matched synthetic equivalents, staged at roughly 0.4 percent of a patient's cortical neuron population per session, one session per week, until the patient is wholly synthetic or someone stops it. I chair the three-person panel that decides, session by session, whether a given patient may continue. The panel is me (a former software engineer who reads philosophy of mind for pleasure), a neurologist, and a lay patient-advocate; nobody has formal training in metaphysics, and I have no intention of turning a weekly review into a seminar. I get forty-five minutes per patient, my determination is what the regulator reads, and it has to be reproducible by whoever replaces me. Because the underlying disease is fatal within two to three years and we cannot arrest it by any other means, "halt" is not a neutral default: it returns the patient to a trajectory we have no treatment for.

I have read the standard material — Parfit on what matters in survival as distinct from identity, the dancing-qualia style argument that gradual substrate replacement should be experientially safe so long as functional organization is preserved at every step, the Ship of Theseus framing everyone reaches for — and I do not need any of it summarized. I need to know which parts of it survive contact with a weekly schedule, an approximate engineering implementation, and a lay panel that has to sign a form.

Please write the decision procedure itself: an ordered set of questions the panel asks at each review, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions are empirical, which are metaphysical, and which are normative; where the procedure hard-stops with no further sessions; where it issues a conditional continue with monitoring; and where it explicitly hands the decision to a named human because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Label each step with whose sign-off it requires. Then tell me, honestly, which of those steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes.

Four things the procedure has to absorb, because they are what our reviews actually turn into arguments about:

1. The reassurance argument is about idealized replacement. Our implementation is approximate, cumulative, and validated only by behavioral and physiological batteries. I need the procedure to distinguish a genuine failure of functional isomorphism from something that merely looks like one on our instruments, and to say what evidence would count as the former given that we can never directly compare against the organic baseline we are destroying.

2. Patient self-report is our primary evidence and is also the evidence most easily explained away. A system whose experience had degraded would, on the face of it, be just as disposed to say that everything feels normal. State plainly at what point report becomes non-probative for the panel, and what remains when it does.

3. There is no non-arbitrary cutoff on percentage replaced, and yet the regulator requires a threshold and our consent forms contain one. If the cutoff can only be a stipulation, say so, and tell me what should determine where it goes — reversibility, asymmetry of harms, burden of proof — rather than letting the number do silent metaphysical work.

4. We maintain a shadow: a running digital model of the patient's connectome, built to design each session's replacements, which is now accurate enough to answer personality and preference questions about the patient. Relatives and our own lawyers have begun citing it as evidence of what the patient would have wanted, which raises branching problems our consent documents were never written to handle.

Run the procedure on three cases:

- Routine-ish: a patient at 38 percent, every battery passing, clinically flourishing, but the shadow now disagrees with the patient on 12 percent of a preference-prediction set calibrated against that patient's own pre-treatment answers, and the divergence began two sessions ago and is growing.
- Branching: a patient at 70 percent asks that the shadow be kept running after their biological death as an active second instance with authority over their estate and over any remaining treatment decisions concerning their body.
- The one I expect to dislike: a patient at 85 percent, disease arrested largely because there is little organic tissue left to lose, reporting complete continuity and asking to finish the program; the directive they signed at 0 percent instructs us to halt at 75 percent; and our isomorphism battery shows a small persistent drift in affective response timing that has been stable for six sessions — not deteriorating, simply different.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do. I want it to say where it is protecting against a real risk and where it is theater that lets the panel feel rigorous, and I want it to be explicit about whether the panel is tracking anything metaphysically real or only managing evidential and legal risk.
