I co-referee stuff for my PI's lab and we keep having the same fight in journal club: someone proposes a function for a trait, someone else says it's a just-so story, and then a third person says the spandrel objection is itself unfalsifiable because literally any trait can be redescribed as a byproduct of *something*. Nobody ever says what would settle it, so before I read one more paper in this literature I want the spec written down. Give me the desiderata -- the requirements any adequate account of when a functional explanation of a trait is licensed has to meet -- with the concrete case or consideration behind each one, plus a flag for whether it's a hard requirement or really just the preference of one camp (the Williams-style strict adaptationists, the Gould/Lewontin pluralists, the optimality-model people, the developmental-bias people). Things I want in the mix: the etiological gap between "this trait does F now" and "F is why the trait is there"; the demand that the trait be shown to be a better solution to the design problem than the alternatives that were actually available; phlyogenetic non-independence and why shared ancestry stops traits from being independent data points; what it takes to rule out a developmental byproduct, an allometric consequence, or drift given effective population size; and whether the criteria have to be able to license *positive* spandrel/exaptation claims rather than only vetoing adaptationist ones.

My own half-formed worry, which I'd like you to actually engage with rather than nod at: I suspect "available alternatives" is doing unearned work for one-off traits -- the human chin, descended larynx, menopause -- where nobody can enumerate the space of forms that were developmentally and historically reachable. If that's right, a criterion built on comparing the trait to its alternatives quietly makes all adaptionism inadmissible, and that's a result about the list itself, not about any particular trait. So tell me which of your requirements can't be jointly satisfied, or which single one knocks out the most candidates, and put that verdict inside the item where the collision happens.

Format: exactly five numbered items and nothing else -- no intro paragraph, no wrap-up, no bibliography or reading list (I'll handle the references myself, I just don't want you inventing them). Each item is one desideratum in two or three sentences: the requirement, the case that motivates it, and the hard/soft tag. Skip the scorecard of which camp passes and which fails; the requirements are what I'm after, and the camps are only there so I can tell whose prejudice a given requirement is.
