I teach an eighty-minute seminar for eighteen third-year philosophy students on practical rationality. They are comfortable with dominance reasoning and expected-value comparisons but have had no formal game theory, and they have not encountered the centipede game. Design an in-class activity in which they collide with the backward-induction paradox themselves: the tension between the step-by-step argument that appears to force the first player to end play immediately, and the observable fact that pairs who keep passing end up better off than that argument recommends. They should not hear the name of the game until the debrief.

Build the activity around a finite alternating-offers game of six decision nodes, played in pairs, one play per pairing, with these properties. At every node, ending the game must yield the player to move strictly more than passing would on the assumption that the other player ends things at their own next turn; and both players must be better off the longer play continues. Give me the exact figures you would print on the cards, and show me the arithmetic establishing that the first condition really is strict — I am wary of schemes in which a doubling pot and a two-thirds split make ending and passing-then-being-ended exactly equal, which quietly ruins the induction.

Stakes must be real but must not involve money. Say what the accumulated points convert into, and how you will keep the stakes from being so trivial that students stop treating the choices seriously.

Each decision must be accompanied by a written prediction of what the other player will do at the next node, together with the student's reason. Explain how you will collect these slips and how you will use them in the debrief to expose the point I most want the class to see: that the inference licensing an early ending at a late node depends on assumptions about the other player that the very arrival at that late node undercuts.

Partway through, have half the room play against a scripted partner, run by me or a teaching assistant, that passes at its first two turns. Use the contrast with the unscripted condition to surface the reputation-style explanation on which even a slight chance that one's partner is not a straightforward maximizer makes early passing rational — and then tell me how to keep that explanation from becoming a tidy dissolution of the paradox rather than a sharpening of it.

Include a minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, and a quick pairing procedure that does not let friends pair up deliberately. In the debrief, map particular moments of play onto the last-node inference, the induction step, and the problem of belief at nodes off the predicted path. Give me your response to two predictable objections: from the student who says they passed because they are a decent person rather than because they reasoned badly — say here whether an appeal to other-regarding preferences rescues or deepens the difficulty — and from the student who says the whole result is an artifact of the small stakes. Then tell me what will go wrong the first time I run it, including whether demanding written predictions alters the behavior I am trying to observe. Finish with a frank account of which feature of the game the classroom version cannot reproduce, and what I will have to supply by hand.
