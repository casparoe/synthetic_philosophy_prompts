Three months ago I was appointed to the Referendum Design Commission for Vestmark, an autonomous region of Maravia, and the more precisely I state my own views to my colleagues, the less they seem to cohere. I would like you to help me extract a principle from my verdicts — or to tell me plainly that none is available and which verdicts must therefore go.

The facts below are all stipulated and complete; please work from them rather than from any analogous case you may have in mind. Vestmark has 2.1 million people within a state of 30 million. It has its own language and its own private-law tradition; it entered the state by conquest in the 1840s rather than by treaty or plebiscite; it has had continuous self-government since 1979. The Sarnau Valley, in the south of the region, holds 18 percent of the regional population, is contiguous, is majority-Maravian-speaking, and voted roughly three to one for parties opposed to independence in each of the last two regional elections. Inside the valley sits the city of Sarnau, population 340,000, which voted about 70 percent for pro-independence parties. The Kevri, an indigenous people with no territorial concentration of their own, are 6 percent of the region and were overwhelmingly pro-independence. Between 1974 and 1989 the central government ran a settlement program that moved some 200,000 Maravian-speaking families into the valley and into three northern mining towns; the program was documented at the time, in the government's own language, as intended to alter the region's demographic composition. My parents came north under it in 1983. I was born here. My younger brother left for the capital twenty-six years ago and has not lived here since, though he still owns the family house. My wife is Kevri. My next-door neighbour arrived from abroad last year to work at the port and is not a citizen.

My verdicts:

1. That all of Maravia should vote on whether Vestmark may leave: no. Not merely impractical, but wrong. A state-wide vote lets 28 million people dispose of a question that is about 2 million.
2. That Vestmark as a whole should vote, and that a bare majority on a high turnout settles it: yes. I do not think a supermajority is required in principle. But I do think the losing side is entitled to put the question again after a reasonable interval, and a rule closing the question for twenty years strikes me as an injustice rather than a settlement.
3. That the Sarnau Valley, voting clearly to remain in Maravia, should be permitted to leave Vestmark and stay in the old state: yes.
4. That the city of Sarnau, voting clearly to secede, should be permitted to leave the valley and join the new state: no. The only difference I can articulate is that the valley is escaping a decision made by people above it while the city is escaping a decision made by people around it. I know how thin that sounds.
5. That my neighbour of one year should vote: yes. That my brother should not: no, he should not vote, despite having been born here.
6. That the central government's proposal — a ten-year durational residency requirement layered on top of the ordinary national franchise — should be adopted: no, emphatically not. As drawn it excludes essentially nobody but the settlement families of 1974–1989 and their children born outside the region. It would disenfranchise my parents after forty-two years here while enfranchising my neighbour after one. I think a referendum conducted under that rule would not be an act of self-determination at all, whatever result it returned.
7. That resident 16- and 17-year-olds should vote, and that long-term resident non-citizens should vote: yes to both, because the decision fixes which state they will wake up in and neither had any part in constituting that state.
8. That the Kevri should have a separate roll on which only Kevri vote, with the result counting as a veto or as a weighted second tally: no. But that a Vestmark secession undertaken without entrenched protections for Kevri land, language, and representation would be defective in a way that a merely narrow result is not: yes.
9. That if a lawful referendum were refused and the region held an unauthorized one under documented repression, returning an overwhelming result, a unilateral declaration of independence should be recognized: yes. That the same declaration, absent the repression, should be recognized: no.
10. That the new state, once independent, may bar the Sarnau Valley from ever leaving it: no. That seems flatly inconsistent with 3 — but if 3 and 4 are both right, the new state must refuse Sarnau, and I cannot see how to hold both.

The draft before the commission does two further things I want your principle to rule on. It requires a double majority: a majority region-wide and a majority in each of the region's seven historic provinces, of which the Sarnau Valley is one. And it opens a five-year adjustment window following any vote for independence, during which any municipality above a threshold population may petition for a plebiscite on transferring to Maravia; the new state would be obliged to honour a clear result but empowered to set the threshold itself.

What I am asking for:

- State the principle, or a small ordered set of candidate principles, precisely enough that I could tell whether it applies to a case I have not thought of. Define every term you introduce; I have to be able to read this aloud at a public hearing, so no technical shorthand.
- Say which of my ten verdicts it delivers and which it costs me. Where it costs me one, tell me what I should hold instead. Do not try to save all ten by adding clauses: I would rather a simple principle that overturns two of my verdicts than a baroque one that saves all of them by exception.
- Address the three pairs that seem to break it: 3 against 4, 5 against 6, and 2 against 10. In particular, tell me whether my revulsion in 6 is principled or a fudge — I accept residence as the criterion when it includes my neighbour and reject it when it excludes my parents, and I suspect I am simply reacting to the design of the rule rather than its content.
- Say what your principle implies for cases I have not listed: the three northern mining towns, which are majority-settler and voted against independence but are contiguous with neither the valley nor each other; an offshore archipelago of 40,000 administered from Vestmark for 150 years but never part of the historic territory, which wants to become its own state rather than join either side; the southernmost district of the valley, which wants to leave the valley while staying in Vestmark; and whether the adjustment window can be finite at all, and if it can, what the petition threshold should be and on what grounds.
- Tell me whether the opt-out logic in 3 generates a regress — if any unit may exit, may any sub-unit exit the exit, indefinitely — and where your principle stops it and why that stopping point is not arbitrary.
- Finally, tell me whether my list is conflating two questions that need separate answers: which body decides, and who constitutes that body. If it is, say which of my verdicts belong to which question, and whether one can be settled before the other.
