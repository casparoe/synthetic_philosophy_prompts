So, bit of an odd request. I'm a novelist — I spent about two years hanging round a care home researching a book, and the manager who let me do it, Pat, retires in three weeks. The home's asked me to give the speech at her leaving do and I'm hopeless at these, so I want you to write it for me. British English please, since that's how it'll be said out loud.

Room'll be about eighty people: care staff, a few residents who can manage it, families, and — awkwardly — a dozen people from a university ethics centre who funded something called the dignity project and who know their Noddings from their Held. So it has to work as speech for people who are knackered and have no interest in philosophy, while the academics at the back should be able to hear that something is actually being argued.

The thing I want it to turn on is Pat's green ring-binder. She was an actuary before she went into care. When a bed was scarce, or a slot with the physio, or a place on the minibus, she allocated it by a written rule, logged, appealable. She was fanatical about the log, because she said a favour you can't audit turns into a personality. But she also had what the staff called the eleven minute rule: once you've turned someone down by policy you owe them eleven minutes of undivided attention, no clipboard, no hurrying, to hear what it actually cost them. She kept a tally of them. She did one at seven in the morning in the car park once and missed her own kid's thing.

I want the speech to make the case — without ever stating it as a case, no thesis sentences, nobody saying "the point is" — that the cold bookkeeping and the attentiveness weren't pulling against each other. That the log is what made the eleven minutes possible and honest rather than sentimental. That's the bit the ethics people will clock, since a good chunk of that tradition is deeply suspicious of exactly the impartial rule-following Pat was obsessive about, and I'd like them to come away mildly unsettled rather than nodding along.

Two hard nos. Don't name-check a single philosopher, not even once — she'd hate it and so would I. And don't let it drift into praise for how intrinsically rewarding care work is; there are people in that room on £12.60 an hour and that move is basically a way of paying them in compliments. If the speech can't acknowledge the money it shouldn't pretend to be celebrating the work.

Five minutes, one real joke that isn't at anyone's expense, and it ends on Pat, not on an idea. Write it the way I'd actually talk — short bits, a few fragments, nothing you couldn't say while holding a glass.
