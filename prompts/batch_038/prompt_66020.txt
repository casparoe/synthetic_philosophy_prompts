I am a novelist, and I have reached the structural keystone of my third book without being able to decide whether the keystone will bear weight. The set piece is a public demonstration in a mid-seventeenth-century anatomical theatre. A clockmaker has built a mechanical spaniel — springs, cams, a bladder of compressed air where the heart would be — and a canon has laid a wager around it: if the artificial dog can be shown to be nothing but the disposition of its parts, then the living dog whimpering at the philosopher's feet is nothing but the disposition of its parts either, and the difference between them is one of craftsmanship rather than of kind. My philosopher character has to answer. The trouble is that I have read enough to suspect the answer I want to give him is a cartoon of the position, and that the real position has an internal joint in it that I cannot locate.

What I need is not history, and certainly not a reading list. I need the conceptual anatomy of the Cartesian claim about animal minds, because my scene turns on which of its components is doing the work.

1. The received version fuses at least three theses: that animals have no immaterial soul; that everything an animal does admits of a mechanical explanation; and that animals feel nothing. Please lay out the relations of entailment among these. Which, if any, follow from the real distinction between mind and body? Which are independent empirical hypotheses? Where is there room for a thinker to hold one and deny another without inconsistency?

2. Descartes elsewhere concedes that he does not deny sensation to animals insofar as sensation depends on a bodily organ. I want this phrase taken seriously rather than waved away. On his own account, sensations are modes of a thinking thing, produced in it by states of the brain in virtue of an institution of nature. If there is no thinking thing, what is left for the concession to attach to? Is there a coherent third category here — a purely bodily analogue of sensation, a disposition to react, a functional state that does the causal work of pain without being pain — or is the phrase strategic, retreating to a sense of the word that carries none of the phenomenology the reader was promised? This is the question my novel lives or dies on, so please be exact about what such a residue would have to be, and whether his system has the conceptual furniture to house it.

3. The two criteria he offers for telling a thinking being from a machine — the use of genuine language, and acting from understanding across all circumstances rather than by a fixed disposition of organs — need to be classified. Are they constitutive marks of thought, such that failing them is what it is to lack a mind, or are they fallible indicators, evidence only? The classification matters, because on the first reading the argument is immune to a cleverer automaton and on the second it is not. And: does his handling of the objection from human infants and from the deaf and dumb, where he allows that they invent signs of their own, smuggle back a graduated notion of thought that the sharp dualism cannot tolerate?

4. Finally, tell me what the strongest non-evasive reply available to my philosopher character actually is — the one a serious Cartesian could make in that theatre in front of witnesses — and what he has to surrender elsewhere in his system to make it. If the honest answer is that the wager cannot be settled from inside the position, I would rather know that now and build the scene around the impasse. Where competent readers of Descartes disagree about any of this, mark the disagreement rather than smoothing it over for my convenience.
