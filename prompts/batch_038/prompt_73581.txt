At a small interdisciplinary workshop I moderated last spring, two panelists — call them P and C — got into a sustained exchange about panpsychism that neither the room nor the follow-up correspondence resolved. I was assigned to write up the session, and the write-up has to say who was right about what. I took no position during the exchange and hold none now, so the report is as neutral as I can make it. Here is the argument as it actually ran.

P opened (1) by insisting that the case for panpsychism is negative rather than positive: what recommends it is that it eliminates brute emergence. If experience is fundamental, there is no need for a law connecting wholly non-experiential arrangements of matter to the appearance of experience — no unexplained surcharge on an otherwise complete physical account. The hard problem gets its grip precisely because structure and function seem unable to logically entail experience; make experience basic and the gap closes by construction.

C replied (2) that this relocates the gap rather than closing it. Panpsychism needs principles of combination, and these are more expensive than the emergentist's single law: the panpsychist must posit both micro-experiential properties and a novel kind of law governing how they compose, and that law must do the hardest work of all — accounting for the unity of a single field of consciousness. Worse, combination principles are not the sort of thing that can be confirmed or disconfirmed in the way structural laws can.

P's third move was that C had mis-described the package. The micro-experiential properties are supposed to be the categorical grounds of the dispositional properties physics describes, since physics characterizes matter only structurally and relationally. On that reading, combination is not a bolted-on extra law: the unified macro-experience is the categorical realization of the very macro-structure physics already posits. One package, no explanatory surcharge.

C then pressed what I will call the mosaic case (4). Take a billion microsubjects, each with a maximally simple experience — a homogeneous field of a single faint quality. Grant, for argument's sake, that there is a fact about the relational structure they instantiate. What makes their experiences literally compose into one subject with a richly differentiated field like mine? Relations among experiences are not themselves experiences had by anyone. If the structure is something the microsubjects stand in, it is not something anyone enjoys. Hence the dilemma: either the macro-subject is a further entity over and above the microsubjects and their structure, in which case emergence is back, or there is no macro-subject at all.

P took the second horn (5), and this is where the exchange became interesting. P denied robust phenomenal unity. The intuition that my experience is a single unified field may itself be a construction of the processes that generate reports about experience; split-brain results and inattentional blindness suggest unity is limited, graded, and partly retrospective. So P held that macro-subjects exist only to the extent that binding genuinely occurs, and that the everyday picture of a rich, seamless field is inflated by narrative. That is a re-description of the datum, P said, not a denial of it.

C's response (6) was that this concession costs the view its selling point. If the real subjects are mostly microsubjects whose intrinsic natures we cannot characterize from outside or from inside, the theory stops making contact with the phenomenology it was introduced to explain. It says nothing about which systems are subjects, and no observation would distinguish it from the claim that matter has an inner nature of an entirely unrelated kind, or from the claim that it has no inner nature at all. On C's telling the view is now insulated, and — the sharper charge — it has forfeited the acquaintance-based evidence that motivated it in the first place, since the one thing we supposedly know from the inside is exactly what P has just explained away.

P made two replies (7). First, insulation does not equal vacuity: the view is supported by inference to the best explanation rather than prediction, and the relevant comparison class is other accounts of consciousness, all of which are equally untestable in their details. Second, acquaintance still favors P: the only intrinsic natures we are acquainted with are experiential, so positing experiential natures at the base is the most conservative extrapolation available rather than an arbitrary stipulation.

C's final move (8) was to suggest that P faces an equally-costed rival rather than a refutation: cosmopsychism, on which there is one fundamental subject whose experience decomposes into ours. That view also avoids brute emergence, also supplies categorical grounds, and begins from a subject one might have independent reason to treat as unified. C added, candidly, that cosmopsychism needs a de-combination principle that looks like the mirror image of the combination principle C had just objected to — but argued that this at least shows the dialectic is underdetermined, not that P has solved anything.

What I need from you is an adjudication of this exchange, not a summary of the literature.

- Go through moves (1)–(8) in order and rate each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating. Where a move contains a strong and a weak component, rate the components separately.
- Flag any move that turns on a shift in the meaning of a term. I suspect "subject," "unified," and "brute" each do some equivocal work somewhere, but I want you to say where, and whether the shift is fatal to the move or merely expository.
- State explicitly which sub-claims each side is entitled to keep after the exchange and which they must give up. In particular: does P's move (5) leave a position with content that could turn out to be wrong, and does C's move (6) follow from it or overshoot?
- Identify what both of them left on the table. I have in mind such things as whether the dispute is about explanation or about grounding, and whether "brute" names a genuine theoretical cost or an aesthetic preference — but tell me what else they both missed, including whether the mosaic case is doing more work than either party noticed.
- End with an overall verdict: who was more right, and how much of the disagreement is substantive rather than a difference in what each party is willing to accept as a theoretical cost.

Please give me the reasoning applied to these eight moves as stated. No references or suggestions for further reading — I want the adjudication itself.
