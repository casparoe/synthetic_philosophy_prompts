I direct a graduate seminar in the philosophy of language this term, and one of my doctoral students has submitted what she intends to develop into a dissertation chapter. I have been asked to write her a letter, and before I do I want to settle for myself whether her central argument can be repaired or whether it is quietly disastrous. She is a careful and sympathetic reader of Grice, and she says she has not attacked the framework from outside but has simply applied his own commitments consistently. Below is my reconstruction of her argument in numbered form; I have kept the wording close to hers and supplied one running example of my own, which she approves of.

Her target is the distinction between what is said and what is conversationally implicated. (1) On Grice's 1957 analysis, a speaker means something non-naturally by an utterance just in case she intends some audience to think that p, intends the audience to recognize that intention, and intends that recognition to be at least part of the audience's reason for thinking that p. (2) Therefore what a speaker communicates on an occasion is constituted by that intention-recognition structure rather than by any convention; the conventions of the language enter, at most, as part of the evidence the audience uses to identify the intention. (3) That is independently confirmed by the fact that conventions are overridable on any occasion whatever — irony, malapropism, and private stipulation each show that no conventional rule can be rendered immune to contextual defeat. (4) But the evidence available to an audience does not single out one candidate content. Consider an email from a colleague consisting of the sentence "I've read the draft." The recipient's total evidence is equally consistent with her having read it in full, with her having read only the first section, with her having read it and withholding comments, with her having read it and approving, and with her having read exactly enough to discharge an obligation. Neither the words nor the situation ranks these candidates. (5) What is said, if it is a genuine level of content, must be unique: it is what the speaker stands committed to, what makes her a liar if she disbelieves it, and what the implicature machinery takes as its input. (6) Hence on such an occasion nothing is determinately what is said. (7) Conversational implicature, as Grice characterizes it, is what the audience must suppose the speaker to have in mind in order to reconcile her saying what she said with the presumption that she is observing the Cooperative Principle and its maxims; if there is no determinate what is said, there is nothing to reconcile, and so no conversational implicature. (8) Consequently, Grice's own apparatus, applied consistently, eliminates both of its central notions at once: nothing is said and nothing is implicated. Either conventions do constitutive work after all, or the distinction she built the apparatus to draw is a fiction.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me which step you reject and why that step looked innocuous to a reader as attentive as she is; tell me specifically what rejecting it costs the intention-first picture in (1) through (3), since that is the part of the argument I have myself defended in print and am least willing to surrender; and tell me, if you judge the argument valid, which of my two commitments — the intention-first picture or the said/implicated distinction — is the cheaper sacrifice and what the survivor has to absorb. If you think the argument can be weakened into something both true and interesting, say what the weakened conclusion is and what the weakening costs her chapter. Finally, please push back on me rather than working obligingly inside my framing: if you judge the argument sound and my resistance to (8) mere sentiment, say so plainly; and if you think my reconstruction is unfaithful, or that the question as I have posed it rests on a confused view of what the Gricean notion of saying was ever supposed to do, tell me that first instead of answering the question as asked. Please write in American English.
