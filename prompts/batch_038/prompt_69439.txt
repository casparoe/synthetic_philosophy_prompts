our rowing club is 110 years old and on saturday we adopt a brand new rulebook, replacing the one we've limped along on since the seventies. I'm the outgoing secretary and I've been asked to give the opening adress at the general meeting — four minutes or so, before the first vote of the day.

the bit that has me awake at 3am: the new amendment clause says any provision of the rules, including that clause itself, can be changed by a two-thirds vote. And the very first motion on the agenda, ten minutes after I sit down, is to use it to entrench something forever — that the club may never sell the boathouse. It'll carry easily. So I have to stand up and tell eighty people whether that motion can be valid at all, because half the room thinks obviously yes and the other half thinks you can't use a rule to abolish itself, and I refuse to fudge it in front of them.

the puzzle as I have it: the clause authorising the amendment is the clause the amendment kills. At the moment of the vote it's in force and hands us the power; a second later it's gone and there's nothing left to authorise anything. And if you say it did validly authorise its own replacement, then the entrenchment looks self-defeating, since the surviving rulebook still says everything in it — that bit included — is amendable, so what exactly did saturday buy us? Either bootstraps or a very expensive gesture. There's also a retired lawyer in the club who'll bring up the fact that the German basic law has provisions that can never be amended, as though that settles anything for a rowing club.

audience: about eighty people. Two are lawyers, one is a postgrad in constitutional theory who'll spot a Hart reference a mile off and would rather you didn't turn the thing into a seminar. Everyone else is there about the rowing and mainly wants to know if they can still book the boathouse for the regatta.

Write the address. Exactly five numbered movements, each three to five sentences you could actually say out loud, nothing before movement 1 and nothing after movement 5. It has to come down on a side — is saturday's motion valid, and can some later two-thirds undo what it does — and argue that side in the open, with the reasons visible in the speech rather than implied. No hedging, no on-the-one-hand, no leaving it for us to sort out later. One of the five movements has to be plain enough that a member could repeat its point accurately to thier kid in the car park, and I want one moment of real humour that the non-lawyers laugh at without the retired lawyer feeling like the target. Don't cite anything, don't name cases or constitutions beyond the one I mentioned, and only nod at Hart if it earns its keep.
