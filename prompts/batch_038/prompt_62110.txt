In six weeks I give the opening research seminar at an institute for Earth-system and sustainability science — forty-five minutes of talk, twenty minutes of discussion. I am an archaeologist, and I will be the only person in the room who does fieldwork on past societies. The audience is roughly thirty: resilience ecologists who think in regime shifts, hysteresis, tipping elements and planetary boundaries; two Earth-system modelers who will want to know what my variables are; an environmental economist working on long-run growth who cares about whether anything I say is measurable; one scholar of science and technology studies; and the institute's founding director, whose widely read book opens with the ninth-century Maya lowlands as a caution about overshoot. Their shared instinct is that the archaeological record exists to supply analogies for present-day risk. My talk tells them it does not, and that the two cases they rely on most are being read backwards.

The thesis. "Collapse" as used in that literature commits a unit error, and correcting it removes the policy inference drawn from it. I distinguish three things normally run together under one word. Complexity collapse: an order sheds administrative, monumental and hierarchical layers because their maintenance costs have stopped being repaid by their returns. Legitimation collapse: subjects cease to be willing to pay those costs — inscriptions stop, dated monuments stop, cities are not rebuilt — while food production and ordinary life carry on. Demographic collapse: people die or leave in numbers. My claim is that in the canonical cases the sequence runs legitimation, then complexity, with demographic collapse either absent or badly overstated. For the southern Maya lowlands, the paleoclimate record does show a run of droughts through the Terminal Classic and the cities were genuinely abandoned, but the northern lowlands flourished immediately afterwards, and settlement survey under tropical forest cover cannot by itself separate a population that died from a population that walked away and stopped commissioning dated stone. For Rapa Nui, the loss of the palm forest is better explained by introduced rats and very slow regeneration than by the transport of statues, and the demographic catastrophe belongs after European contact — epidemic disease and slave raiding — not to a pre-contact self-inflicted end.

Two further claims are where I am most exposed, and I intend to make both explicitly. First, polities are not the kind of thing that dies: populations die, and institutions are either transmitted or fail to be transmitted, and the two rarely coincide; using one word for the end of a dynasty and the end of a people is precisely what makes the analogy to the present feel available when it is not. Second, what was irreversibly lost in the ninth century, on my account, was neither population nor complexity but transmitted knowledge — a writing system, a calendrical astronomy, craft sequences — none of it passed on. Resilience science has no category for that loss, because a regime shift is by construction value-neutral, and I plan to say so to a room that lives inside that vocabulary.

Prepare me for the discussion. I want the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk if it landed unanswered and how likely it is to come. Phrase each the way these people would phrase it, in their own idiom — hysteresis, early-warning signals, identification, falsifiability, coupled human-environment systems, the politics of who counts a loss — not in an archaeologist's. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, say plainly what I should concede at the outset and what I must hold to keep the thesis intact, and mark where the reply is softest, including any objection whose only honest answer is that my taxonomy risks being unfalsifiable because every kind of loss has been sorted into one of its three boxes. Tell me which two or three should be conceded before anyone asks them, because the room will respect that more than a defense. Then give me the single reply that has to land if the seminar is to be useful rather than merely provocative, and finally the question I am hoping nobody asks — you will have a view about what that is from the material above, and I want it stated, along with the shortest true answer I could give if it comes. No references, no reading list, no suggestions of further literature; I want the objections and the answers only.
