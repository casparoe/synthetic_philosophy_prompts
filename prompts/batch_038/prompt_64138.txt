For three years I have sat in a research group split down the middle over path dependence, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the split, not a verdict on either half of it.

Camp A holds that history is genuinely locked in. Early choices get amplified by increasing returns, network effects, learning curves and switching costs, so accidents freeze into standards: QWERTY, VHS over Betamax (where the "inferior" part is itself contested), a railway gauge inherited from earlier wagonways, whichever reactor design a nuclear program happened to start with. Their claim is counterfactual — run the sequence again with a slightly different opening and you arrive somewhere else, and stay there.

Camp B holds that the outcomes would have arrived anyway. The space of viable solutions is narrow, functional equivalents recur, and what looks frozen is mostly what convergence looks like from the inside. Lock-in cases are salient precisely because they are odd; nobody writes up the thousands of arrangements that settled where any sequence would have settled them.

What puzzles me is this. Both camps accept the same mechanisms — both invoke increasing returns and network effects without embarrassment — and both work from the same case list. When A offers a case, B redescribes it at a coarser grain as a convergence: fine, that keyboard, but the function it serves was coming regardless. When B offers a convergence, A redescribes it at a finer grain as an accident that stuck: fine, some keyboard, but this layout, for a century, when it need not have been. Each side also charges the other with selection effects, and neither charge ever lands.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and meant to be tested rather than flattered, is that the dispute is not about mechanisms, not about the historical record, and not even really about counterfactuals, but about an unstated criterion for when outcomes in two different possible histories count as the same outcome. Fix the grain finely and everything is contingent; fix it coarsely and everything converges. Since neither side declares its grain, every case confirms both theses at once and the argument cannot terminate. On that reading the disagreement is neither verbal nor empirical but a missing convention — structurally closer to a measure problem than to a historical one.

Is that right? Sort out what kind of disagreement this actually is: substantive and empirical, resting on claims about how strong increasing returns are relative to how flat the solution space is, which data might in principle settle; verbal, turning on "path dependent" or "locked in"; two questions sharing a heading, one metaphysical about the openness of the past and one practical about whether early intervention is worth paying for; a standoff in which both theses have been qualified past the point of risk; or an ill-posed question. Ground the diagnosis in what each side says and in how they handle one another's cases, and tell me whether the biological version of the same argument — replaying the history of life against the ubiquity of convergent form — is stalled for the same reason or a different one.

Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a declared convention for outcome identity, a formal model containing a parameter the two sides genuinely disagree about, a natural experiment or replay-style design, a reformulation of the question, or a demonstration that one side's thesis is empty. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for that, and say whether it is a defect in the debate or a sign that the debate is doing real work anyway — for instance in the policy version, where the only thing anyone wants to know is whether to spend money on early-stage interventions.
