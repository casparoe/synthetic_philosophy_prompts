Just spent close to two hours arguing with a friend about organ donation protocols and I want a third party, because I think we're both talking past each other and I can't tell which one of us is confused.

The practice, as concretely as I can state it: somebody in the ICU has a devastating, non-survivable brain injury but does not meet the neurological criteria for death — heart is still going on the vent. The family decides to withdraw life support, and the patient had wanted to be a donor. So instead of extubating in the ICU they wheel him into the operating room, extubate there with the surgical team standing by, and when the heart stops they observe a stand-off period — somwhere between two and five minutes depending on the center, from what I can gather — during which nobody touches him and nobody attempts resuscitation even if the monitor does something weird. Then they declare death on the grounds that circulatory and respiratory function have ceased irreversibly, and immediately begin the recovery. And in some programs, after that declaration, they clamp off the vessels going up toward the head and run a pump to restore warm blood flow through the abdomen so the organs hold up better.

His position: the whole thing is a fudge, the stand-off period is theater, and the clamping is a flat admission that what everyone involved actually cares about is the brain. My position: nothing incoherent about it, circulatory death and brain death are just two routes to the same event. And then neither of us could say what the same event was, which is where it got annoying.

So — what account of death does this practice actually presuppose? In particular, is "irreversible" doing descriptive work here (a biological fact about the body at that minute) or normative work (a decision that we will not restart it, given that the team standing right there could restart it if it wanted to)? Does the practice need the two legal definitions of death to be one underlying phenomenon, or would it run exactly the same if they were two different things that happen to overlap in these cases? Which rival views does it quietly rule out — a consciousness-based or higher-brain definition, death as a process rather than an instant, death as a status conferred rather than discovered — and which of those would in fact license the practice just as well? I want the strict presuppositions separated from the ones that merely fit nicely, and I want to be told where the practice is underdetermined.

Then two follow-ups. My friend says a hospital could cut the stand-off to sixty seconds and nothing philosophical would change, only the odds; is he right, or is the length of the wait doing conceptual work rather than statistical work? And what would actually have to change in the protocol — not in the paperwork, in the acts themselves — if you swapped the presupposed view for a consciousness-based one, or if you dropped the requirement that the donor be dead at the time of recovery altogether?
