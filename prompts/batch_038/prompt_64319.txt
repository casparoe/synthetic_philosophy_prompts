I would like a carefully argued reconstruction of what Charles Darwin would most probably have made of four developments that postdate his death in 1882. The reconstruction should be driven by his own published commitments about the moral sense and about religion, not by what can be made to look plausible in his voice.

**The textual basis I want you to reason from.** In *The Descent of Man* Darwin advances what he treats as a highly probable proposition: that "any animal whatever, endowed with well-marked social instincts, the parental and filial affections being here included, would inevitably acquire a moral sense or conscience, as soon as its intellectual powers had become as well, or nearly as well developed, as in man." Conscience, on this account, is a consequence of the particular instincts a species happens to carry. He then tests the idea against hive-bees: were human beings reared under precisely the conditions of hive-bees, our unmarried daughters would regard the killing of their brothers as a sacred duty and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters, and no one would see anything wrong in it — yet such creatures would still have a conscience, still feel an inward monitor, because the machinery would be identical even where the deliverances were monstrous to us. On the faculties themselves he writes that "These faculties are variable; and we have every reason to believe that the variations tend to be inherited," and he concludes that they were progressively advanced by natural selection in our social ancestors. He nonetheless calls the moral sense "the most noble of all the attributes of man," and he describes the widening of sympathy — from the tribe to the nation, to strangers of every race, and finally, as he thought, to the lower animals — as genuine moral advancement rather than as drift. Late in life, writing to William Graham, whose book had argued that natural law implies purpose, he confessed: "But then with me the horrid doubt always arises whether the convictions of man's mind, which has been developed from the mind of the lower animals, are of any value or at all trustworthy. Would any one trust in the convictions of a monkey's mind, if there are any convictions in such a mind?" He was also in the habit of setting aside questions he judged unripe, remarking of how the mental powers first arose that "These are problems for the distant future, if they are ever to be solved by man."

**The four developments.**

1. *Directed modification of the instincts.* Germline editing now permits two proposals to be taken seriously. The first would edit laying hens and pigs so that they are far less capable of distress, leaving intensive confinement intact and reducing not the cruelty of the conditions but the animal's capacity to mind them. The second would edit human embryos toward variants associated with high agreeableness and low aggression, defended as a public-health measure on the ground that our sentiments were calibrated for small foraging bands and are now catastrophically mismatched with our technological powers. Darwin's hive-bee case has ceased to be a thought experiment and become a schedule of work.

2. *The horrid doubt hardened into an argument.* Philosophers now argue that because the moral sense was shaped by reproductive success rather than by truth, its deliverances carry no more authority than the bee-daughters' sense of sacred duty, so that moral claims may be useful but cannot be true or false in any robust sense. Does Darwin possess the resources to resist this, or was the horrid doubt already the argument with the nerve removed? Would he have said so in print?

3. *Engineered extinction and the suffering built into nature.* A gene drive is proposed that would spread sterility through the mosquito species principally responsible for malaria, aiming at its elimination in the wild, and its advocates argue this is a duty because natural arrangements impose vast gratuitous suffering — the very consideration that weighed most heavily with Darwin when he dwelt on parasitic wasps whose larvae feed inside the living bodies of caterpillars. He had watched introduced rats, cats and pigs strip island faunas and held no sentimental view of extinction as such; but extinction had always been, for him, the ordinary fate of the losing side of a competition, never a project undertaken by the winner out of compassion. Which is this?

4. *Moral communities designed without belief.* Well-funded organizations now construct congregational life, ritual, confession, and systematic moral instruction with no supernatural content, reasoning that religion historically functioned to bind groups and to reinforce community opinion with supernatural sanctions, and that the function can be preserved once the belief is discarded. Darwin treated belief in spiritual agencies as a late acquisition of the cultivated human mind rather than an original endowment, and expected unbelief to spread among the educated. Would he regard this as an honest completion of his own account of religion's usefulness, or as a failure to see what in religion was actually doing the work?

**Requirements on your answer.** Reason in Darwin's own vocabulary — social instincts, sympathy, habit, variation and inheritance, the judgment of the community, natural selection, the moral sense — translating each modern proposal into it, rather than reasoning in contemporary ethical jargon and then assigning him conclusions. For each of the four, keep separate what his published principles commit him to, what his temperament would have led him actually to say (extreme caution, unwillingness to be thought an attacker of religion, reluctance to publish on matters he considered unripe, solicitude for the feelings of religious friends and family), and what he would have declined to answer or deferred. Identify at least one published position he would have revised, and say what evidence would have revised it. Mark clearly where the reconstruction is underdetermined and where you are extrapolating. Do not provide references, a bibliography, or a reading list; work from the material above and your own knowledge. Close by naming which of the four he would have considered most dangerous, and why.
