I'm in a weekly interpretability reading group — eight of us, about half ML grad students and half philosophy grad students — and last Thursday's session collapsed into an argument that never actually got resolved. I've been asked to write up the summary for our group wiki and I'm stuck, because I honestly can't tell who was winning. Here's the case they were fighting over, stated as neutrally as I can manage: a classifier model correctly flags a certain kind of buried trick in arithmetic word problems on the standard eval, and it also correctly flags the trick when the problems are rewritten so that the trick is cued by completely different surface features. The team identified a set of internal components whose activation was necessary for the flagging in *both* versions, and which, when patched in from a run where the model flagged, was sufficient to produce flagging in both. But in the rewritten version there was an extra cluster of components that also mattered: knocking that cluster out badly hurt performance on the rewritten problems and did nothing at all to the standard ones. Sasha said that's plainly the same mechanism doing the same job in both cases — a mechanism just *is* a causal role in the system, the shared set plays that role in both, and the extra cluster is redundant scaffolding you'd expect any real network to grow around a function. Noor said no, it's two mechanisms that share a part, the way two cars can share the same brake supplier without being the same car: the extra cluster is doing genuine work in one case and not the other, so the two runs don't compute the same thing, and if you call that "the same mechanism" your anomaly detector is going to wave through exactly the cases it exists to catch.

Then it got worse, in an interesting way. Sasha said Noor's standard can never be satisfied — there is always *some* differential activation between two inputs, so by her rule no mechanism is ever identical to itself across two prompts, which makes the criterion useless rather than strict. Noor said uselessness isn't falsehood: if mechanisms really are that input-sensitive then a detector that screams constantly is the honest one, and she'd rather have a useless alarm than a comfortable one. Sasha came back with the metaphysics — mechanisms are types, not tokens, types are individuated by causal role, and causal role comes in degrees, so "same mechanism" is graded and the real question is how much overlap, weighted by how much each component contributes. Noor said fine, but the moment you make it graded you've stopped answering the question the detector asks, which is binary, and then your alarm is reporting on your threshold rather than on the model. I took notes so this is close to verbatim in structure, though I've cleaned up the wording. Near the end Noor offered a concrete test — call it interventional signature match: two runs instantiate the same mechanism iff the same minimal set of components is both necessary and sufficient for the output in both runs. Sasha said redundancy wrecks that immediately, because if there are two sufficient paths in one run and one in the other, the minimal sufficient sets differ even when nothing interesting has changed. Noor conceded the redundancy point but said Sasha's graded-causal-role view can't handle the opposite case, where the extra components are doing something genuinely different rather than something redundant, and neither of them could say how you'd tell those two situations apart from the inside.
