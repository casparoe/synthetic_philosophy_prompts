Ok, semi-panicked question from someone who is ~3 months out from defending.

Chapter 2 of my dissertation is a model of household saving where agents form expectations about future returns by anchoring on the average belief of their reference group — call it socially anchored expectations. Mechanically, each household plugs the neighborhood-mean forecast into its Euler equation instead of forming a private one. The model does what I need it to do: it reproduces a steep gradient in saving rates as a function of within-neighborhood income dispersion, and it does it with basically one free parameter.

Problem: I ran the survey module too, and the survey data is brutal to me. Expectation reports are overwhelmingly idiosyncratic. The correlation between a household's own forecast and its reference group's mean forecast is indistinguishable from zero, and it doesn't get any bigger in high-dispersion neighborhoods — which is exactly where the saving gradient shows up. So the mechanism assumption is false, and it's false in the precise corner of the data my prediction is about. My committee member says thats not a detail, that's the whole ballgame, and that I can't hide behind Friedman.

I don't get her objection, and I'd like to know whether I'm the confused one here. My understanding of the 1953 methodology essay has been:

1. Unrealism in assumptions isn't a bug, it's close to being the mark of a good theory — the more unrealistic a theory's assumptions, the more significant the theory, since something that described reality exactly wouldn't be a theory at all, just a copy. So if anything my survey result should count in my favor?

2. Assumptions and predictions live in seperate boxes and you're only allowed to test the second one. Somebody waving survey evidence at an assumption is making the one move the essay puts out of bounds.

3. Friedman was an instrumentalist anyway — that's where the whole F-twist business comes from, and the charge was that he only cares about prediction, never about whether the story is true. So insisting my expectation-formation mechanism be literally borne out is importing a truth requirement he rejects.

4. And even if you throw out 1–3, isn't this just Duhem–Quine? I can soak up the survey result by adjusting an auxiliary, so "your assumption is false" and "one of your auxiliaries is off" aren't really two different objections.

Question: is there any case where evidence directly against an assumption actually bites on a Friedmanite reading, or does the F-twist mean my survey module is a nice-to-have that can't in principle damage the model? I want a straight answer on where the line is, because right now I can't figure out whether my chapter is insulated or whether it's built on nothing. Please no reading list — I've got the essay and a stack of the replies to it, I just can't tell which of my four claims is the load-bearing mistake.
