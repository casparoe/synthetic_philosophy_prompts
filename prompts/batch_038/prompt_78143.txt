I am a fourth-year doctoral student in structural engineering, serving as the graduate member of a municipal review panel. We must attach a number to the probability that a proposed pedestrian bridge suffers a load-bearing failure within fifty years. The design has never been built before: it uses a composite-to-steel joint detail found on nineteen bridges worldwide, in a span arrangement used only three times, and it sits in a climate unlike any of those sites.

The three engineers on the panel have each produced a figure, all defensible, differing by nearly two orders of magnitude. The first used the nineteen bridges sharing the joint detail (two failures recorded, both during construction, both traced to causes unrelated to the joint — she counted them anyway, on the grounds that excluding them would be choosing the answer). The second used all four thousand pedestrian bridges in the national register, giving roughly 0.3 percent. The third argues that the only honest estimate is conditioned on everything we actually know about this bridge — its site, traffic, fabricator, inspection regime — and observes that the set of structures sharing all of those properties has exactly one member, so there is no frequency to report and the answer must be a range bounded by judgment.

My instinct is that the third engineer is right in some sense I cannot articulate, that his proposal is nonetheless useless for a permit decision, and that the first two are not merely imprecise but are answering different questions that we have been treating as one question. The panel must publish a single figure. I would like to know whether the three estimates are answers to the same question at all, and if they are not, whether any non-arbitrary principle determines which question a permit decision ought to be answering.
