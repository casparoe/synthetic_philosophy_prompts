I'm in a reading group that's been slogging through the part of GEB where Hofstadter builds his little toy brain — the careenium, a skull-shaped container full of simmballs that bounce around, clump together, and drift in and out of each other, where a given simmball is individuated less by its own physical properties than by which other simmballs it's hooked up to. The fight we keep having is the obvious one: do the simmballs actually push the careenium around, or is that just a cute way of redescribing what the underlying stuff is doing anyway? One guy in the group, a software engineer, says the question was settled long ago by an analogy everyone already accepts: a program running on a machine obviously makes things happen in the physical world — nobody finds it misterious that a spreadsheet can spin up a cooling fan or that a line of code can fire a solenoid — so of course the high level can be causally real without being spooky, and the careenium "puzzle" is fake. Then he adds that LLMs make it even more vivid: we all happily talk about what a model believes or wants, those descriptions predict its behavior, and underneath it's just numbers moving through silicon, so we have a working existence proof of symbols pushing substrate.

I want you to audit that analogy instead of just taking or leaving it. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what in the careenium plays the role of the machine, the program, the source code, the instruction set, the compiler, the programmer, the electricity — and then seperate the disanalogies that actually threaten his use of it from the cosmetic ones (I don't care that brains are wet and slow and that nobody designed them). The ones I suspect are load-bearing: in a computer the mapping from program level down to voltages was stipulated by somebody, and there's a real engineered boundary — the ISA — which is what makes the upper level a level at all, whereas in the careenium the simmballs and the substrate are the same kind of stuff in the same vessel with no interface and no one who fixed the mapping; also a program's symbols seem to get their content from outside the machine, from the programmer and the user, which is exactly the thing under dispute. Tell me whether I've got the right ones, and whether the analogy smuggles the mystery back in rather than dissolving it — does the software story secretly need somebody reading the symbols, does the interpreter regress bite? And test his second leg on its own: does the LLM case support it or cut the other way, given that what interpretability work actually turns up is features smeared across many directions, superposition, polysemantic units — nothing like a discrete variable sitting at an address, and arguably much more like Hofstadter's holistically individuated simmballs than like machine-level symbols.

Finally: does his deflationary conclusion — nothing mysterious about symbols causing things, the puzzle is fake — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole weight the entire time? If it doesn't survive, say what weaker conclusion does, and what would be left to explain about the careenium. Then propose a better analogy if there is one. Two got floated in the group: a belief is like a hurricane, a real high-level pattern that genuinely causes damage and is made entirely of air molecules; and a belief is like a shadow, which looks like it's moving across the wall but pushes nothing. Say which of those does the job better, or whether something else — a price in a market, a law on the books, a traffic jam, a melody in a performance — beats both, and be honest about where your replacement analogy also breaks, since I don't want to just trade one slogan for another.
