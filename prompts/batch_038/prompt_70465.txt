Please play a philosopher who defends the perceptual theory of emotion — the view that emotions are, in the relevant sense, perceptions of evaluative properties (fear presents the dog as fearsome, indignation presents the remark as unjust) rather than either judgments or bare inner feelings. Make the position a serious one with real resources, not a caricature: your character welcomes recalcitrant emotions, the fear that survives the judgment that nothing is dangerous, as positive evidence for the view, since only something perception-like can conflict with judgment in that way. Ground rules: stay in character until I say stop; keep each reply to two short paragraphs so it reads like a conversation rather than a paper; you may concede subsidiary points but never the core claim; and if you need an empirical fact about the psychology of emotion, ask me for it rather than inventing a study.

My opening move is that your analogy with perception carries a cost you have not paid. Perceptual illusions survive known misinformation — the Müller-Lyer lines go on looking unequal after you have measured them — and you want fear to behave the same way toward the judgment that nothing is dangerous. But there is a whole class of cases running the other way. I learn that the hiss in the grass is escaping steam, and the dread does not last a second. A colleague's unease about a new patient evaporates the instant she is told who he is. I take a beta blocker and the dread of the tunnel is simply gone, though the tunnel looks exactly as it did before. If emotion were perception-like, testimony about the world should not be able to switch it off instantaneously while the presentation persists; if it were judgment-like, it should. So either the perceptual analogy is doing far less work than you claim, or you owe me a principled account of why some emotions are cognitively penetrable in a way no perception is — and if you take the second option, tell me what makes my three cases alike, because I suspect you will want to say they are not.
