I am assembling a set of conceptual tools for a project on the historiography of scientific discovery, and I have run into a recurring pattern that I have been unable to locate a term for. I would like to know whether one exists.

The pattern runs as follows. Two or more investigators are counted as having independently arrived at the same discovery within a short window, and the case is then cited as evidence that the result was in the air — that it was waiting on the state of the field rather than on the particular person who happened to reach it. But when one examines what each of them actually produced, the two products turn out not to be the same result at all. The identity between them is manufactured afterwards, by the tradition that absorbs both, reconciles them, and reports the unified outcome as what each of the discoverers had found. On this reading the famous simultaneous discoveries are partly artifacts of retrospective homogenization rather than facts about the moment of discovery, which makes them considerably weaker evidence for the inevitability of the result than they are usually taken to be.

Three cases that seem to me to work this way:

- The calculus. Newton's fluxions and infinite series and Leibniz's differentials and his algorithmic notation are recognizably different mathematical enterprises, and the single subject taught today under that name is a product of the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century consolidation that came after both. The pair is nonetheless the standard exhibit for simultaneous invention.
- Darwin and Wallace. What Wallace argued in the essay read jointly with Darwin's material at the Linnean Society is the core mechanism in a few pages, without the evidential architecture the Origin supplies a year later — the argument from artificial selection, the biogeographical and palaeontological material, the treatment of variation and hybridization. They are now described as having arrived at the same theory in the same year.
- Oxygen. Scheele, Priestley, and Lavoisier are grouped as co-discoverers, although Priestley characterized what he had isolated in phlogiston terms for the rest of his life, and Lavoisier's substance becomes the substance we mean only once the chemical revolution provides the framework. Kuhn's observation that this discovery is not a datable event but a process spread over years seems to me to be pointing at exactly the problem I have in mind, though he draws a conclusion about paradigms rather than about how multiples get counted.

What I would like from you:

1. Does this pattern have an established name in the history, sociology, or philosophy of science? I know the neighborhood and I suspect none of it is quite the thing. Merton's multiples and multiplexes name the class being miscounted, not the miscounting. Priority disputes are about the allocation of credit, not about whether the competing results were identical. Merton's obliteration by incorporation describes a contribution ceasing to be separately attributed once it has been absorbed into the canon, which is a story about forgetting provenance rather than about absorbed contributions being reported as having always been the same contribution. Presentism and Whig historiography are broad methodological vices rather than this specific mechanism. Correct me if any of those is in fact the term I am reaching for, and tell me which comes closest and exactly where the fit breaks down.

2. If no established term exists, coin one. I want a definition sharp enough to do work: what would have to be true of a case for it to qualify, what would disqualify it, and — the hard part — how one is supposed to distinguish a genuine multiple from a manufactured one when the record is contested, since the move of insisting that a rival's result was not really the same result is transparently available to partisans in a priority dispute.

3. More substantively: what individuates a discovery, such that two results count as the same one? Is it content, the problem addressed, the method, the conceptual framework, subsequent uptake by the community, or some combination — and if different criteria can come apart, which one governs the question of whether a multiple is real? Whatever criterion you settle on, say what it implies for the inference from multiples to inevitability: does the pattern above undermine that inference, or merely oblige us to be more careful about which cases we count as multiples?

A bare label will not serve my purposes. What I am after is the carving — what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie.
