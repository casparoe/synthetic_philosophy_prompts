I run a tiny film-and-philosophy-of-mind group — eight people, my living room, one movie a month — and last term we did Her, which immediately collapsed into everyone agreeing it was "really about dating apps" and nobody engaging with the mind stuff at all. This month it's Ex Machina and I want to hand out a one-page framing note beforehand that pushes people toward epiphenomenalism specifically, because I'm convinced the movie is sneakily about the paradox of phenomenal judgment (the thing Chalmers names in The Conscious Mind — if experience is causally inert, then the sentence a person utters when they report a pain wasn't caused by the pain, it was caused by the neural stuff underneath, and the accuracy of all our first-person reports turns into a lucky coincidence). Nobody in my group has done much philosophy beyond an intro course, so the note has to do some work, and I'd rather you write the full reading and let me cut it down.

Here's the angle I want you to develop, and then stress-test. Caleb's entire job in the film is to decide whether Ava's self-ascriptions of inner life are caused by the experiences they describe or only correlate with them — which is exactly the question the paradox says we can't answer, and it's the question epiphenomenalism says we can't answer even about ourselves. Then there's the blackout structure, which I think is the key: the same body produces two incompatible sets of reports depending on whether the power is cut, and both Caleb and the viewer have to work out which set is honest, with no access to what's producing either one. My group has been playing with a coinage I want you to use and then argue with — call a system in the "Ava condition" when its self-ascriptions of experience would come out character-for-character the same whether or not there was any experience there. Does the film show Caleb sitting in the Ava condition too, given that epiphenomenalism is supposed to be a thesis about all of us? And where do the other characters land: Kyoko, who never reports anything at all and is treated as a kind of evidence; Nathan, who seems to think the reports are the only thing there could ever be; Ava at the end, walking into the crowd without looking back. Also, the epiphenomenalist has a reply available — something about acquaintance rather than causation doing the epistemic work — so tell me whether the film leaves any room for it or whether it forecloses it.

I want the reading faithful in both directions. Point me at specific moments (the session rooms, the blackouts, Kyoko, the ending) and say what the film gets right about the paradox that an argument on the page doesn't — the thing you can only show by making an audience sit through it — but also say plainly where I'm overreaching, i.e. where the movie is just about manipulation, gender and captivity and has no interest in metaphysics at all. Don't quote the script at me; I don't remember the dialogue and I'd rather you not invent any. And two things at the end: tell me what would change your mind here — which consideration or argument would make you say the epiphenomenalist lens is a stretch imposed on the film rather than found in it — and flag where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, especially if it's leaning on a contested construal of the paradox or on a reading of the blackouts that the film never actually settles.
