I'm the incoming chair of the history department at a small college and on Friday I have to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop we're hosting. It's called "The Standards We Can't Shed: anachronism and moral judgment in historical practice," and the title was picked by Professor Marion Oduya, who is retiring at the end of the year and who will be sitting in the front row. Write the speech for me — five or six minutes out loud.

The situation is the hard part. The workshop is in a building that got renamed last spring. It used to be named after the college's second president, a nineteenth-century guy who, among other things, aranged for students — several of them Black, some of them teenagers — to work off their tuition as labor on local farms and in local households, on terms the students had no hand in setting. A student petition got the name taken off. Three of the trustees who voted against taking it off are attending the workshop as guests and they know exactly who they are. Two of the students who wrote the petition are also registered. The college president will sit in for the first hour. Nobody has asked me to settle any of this, and I've promised the planning committee my remarks won't settle it — the two days are supposed to do that work, badly and in public, if they do it at all. But I also can't act like the building isn't the building. Every out-of-town guest who came through the registration table said some version of "so, about the name."

Second hard part. Oduya spent forty years arguing against two cheats. One is the "judge them by their own lights" cheat — she thinks that phrase is mostly used as a permission slip to stop thinking. The other is its mirror image, the confident presentist who treats the archive as a prosecution file. And she had an actual working rule that every one of her students had to obey, which I want to be the spine of the speech. She called it the witness requirement: you may not say of a historical actor that they should have known better unless you can name somebody alive at the time, inside their world, using a vocabulary that was available to them, who did know better and said so. Not somebody who would have known better in hindsight. A contemporary witness. Her point was that this turns a mood into a research task, and that when you can't produce a witness, your indignation is very often just your own century talking to itself.

So: build the speech around the witness requirement. Explain it once, plainly enough that the non-historians in the room — Oduya's husband, the trustees, a couple of parents who drove in — get it instantly and don't feel condescended to. Then do the reflexive move and apply it to us, in that room, on that day. I think the honest landing spot is that the requirement cuts against the workshop's own comfort: whatever those students wrote in their petition last spring is precisely the kind of contemporaneous witness testimony that would let a historian sixty years from now say of the trustees, and of me, that we should have known better. I want the speech to say that out loud and then not gloat, because the same rule runs the other way and protects Oduya from me — I'm not entitled to fault her for what she didn't take up in the eighties unless I can name a witness who told her so at the time.

Some constraints on the writing:

- It has to sound like a person talking. Warmth, and two or three genuinely funny moments; the room is tense and laughter is a tool, but nothing cheap at the trustees' expense and nothing that lands as a lecture.
- Absolutely no "the tension between these perspectives is a productive one and I look forward to our discussion." That is the exact sentence I'm trying not to say. If it ends on a question, the question has to be uncomfortable for me specifically, not a generic tee-up for the first panel.
- Don't quote anyone real. You can name Butterfield, or Carr, or the intellectual-history crowd's worry about anachronism, but paraphrase them; don't put words in a real historian's mouth. Oduya and the witness requirement are invented for this occasion, so "quoting" her is completely fine.
- The word presentism should not appear in the first half. When it finally shows up it should show up as something somebody says about us, not as a term I stop to define.
- Thank exactly one funding body. We have a federal grant and a local foundation and last year's speaker thanked six organizations in a row and it killed the room.
- End on something Oduya can recieve from the front row without wincing.
