I am a third-year student in a course on scientific realism, and for our closing seminar I have to present one novel read through the pessimistic meta-induction. I have chosen H. G. Wells's The Time Machine. The Time Traveller reasons from a handful of observations gathered in a single valley over a few days — the Eloi, the Morlocks, the beach of the far future, the eclipsed and cooling sun — to a sweeping cosmology of degeneration and exhaustion, and from where we now stand that cosmology is itself a past theory, superseded in most of its detail while some of its shape survives. My tutor proposed a framing I would like you to test rather than accept: call it the retro-PMI test. One asks of a work's science not whether it turned out to be true, but whether the text itself hands its reader resources for treating its own best theory as a past theory.

Please run the test on the novel. Where does Wells supply those resources — the frame narrator's withholding of belief, the Traveller's own revisions and self-corrections, the derelict Palace of Green Porcelain as an image of curatorial confidence coming to nothing — and where is he simply didactic, asking us to accept the cosmology? Does the novel thereby dramatize something the realism debate states only argumentatively, for instance the worry that the standard lists of dead-but-successful theories are a biased sample, or the selective realist's insistence that some structural content is what gets retained across theory change? Describe scenes and exchanges rather than quoting them; I want no quotations, no bibliography, and no reading list. Close by saying plainly what the novel contributes to the debate that an argument on its own could not.
