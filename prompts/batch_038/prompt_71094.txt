I co-teach a seminar with the econ and sociology people each fall — nominally about work and family policy, actually a place where the philosophers and the social scientists talk past each other for ten weeks. Last week it finally turned into a real argument rather than parallel monologues, and I chaired it, stayed out of the content, and came away genuinely unsure who had won. So I want a referee. The question on the table was narrow: in a public paid-leave program, should part of the entitlement be earmarked for the second parent — non-transferable, use-it-or-lose-it — or should the whole thing be a household pot the couple allocates as it likes? Ines is a labor economist, Dan does political philosophy, mostly family justice. They went back and fourth for about forty minutes.

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it, in order:

1. Ines opened by saying the earmarking question is not really about fairness to fathers, it's about who absorbs the child in a couple's career. A transferable pot is a household bargaining problem, and the household solves it the same way nearly every time: whoever has the lower current wage takes the leave, and because that's usually the mother, the entitlement becomes a maternal entitlement in practice. Make weeks non-transferable and the calculus changes — she pointed at the Norwegian experience, where father take-up went from a couple of percent to something like seventy within a few years of the first reserved weeks, and said Quebec showed the same jump. Nothing else in the policy toolkit, she claimed, moves that number.

2. Dan said that's exactly the trouble. The household has local knowledge the state doesn't — whether the mother is breastfeeding, whether one partner is on probation at work, whether one income is lumpy, whether a grandparent is dying. A couple that decides to convert the father's weeks into cash-equivalent time for the mother is not making an error you get to confiscate; you've destroyed value for that family and, summed across families, destroyed a lot of it, in exchange for a distributional aim you could pursue more cheaply. He added that earmarking works on people by exploiting loss aversion rather than by persuading them, and that if the view is that couples are systematically mistaken, the honest instrument is information, not forfeiture.

3. Ines said his cost-benefit arithmetic quietly treats preferences and employer beliefs as fixed, and that's the whole ballgame. Employers can't observe which individual woman will take a long absence, so they discount the whole category in hiring and in the assignment of client work and promotions — which is why the wage effect of a first child shows up as a divergence in trajectories rather than in hours worked. If reserved weeks eventually make long absence a gender-neutral event, the statistical basis for the discount erodes. So the quota isn't purely redistributive; part of what it buys is the removal of a distortion the labor market already had.

4. Dan said her mechanism has to move beliefs and behavior at the same time, and there's no reason it does. Take-up of reserved weeks is never total. If, say, six in ten eligible fathers actually take them, an employer updating on observed behavior still expects a longer absence from a woman than from a man, while the pool of workers who might vanish for months has gotten bigger — so in the short run you may have raised the expected cost of hiring anyone of childbearing age and told yourself a story about having lowered it. Her long-run convergence is asserted, not shown, and the transition could be self-defeating.

5. Ines conceded the design point but not the conclusion: a flat, badly paid quota gets used by whoever has the lowest opportunity cost, so the replacement rate matters a lot. But she said generosity is not the lever — a fully paid transferable pot still leaves every non-wage asymmetry intact, and it lets the household resolve those asymmetries in the traditional direction at public expense. Generosity makes the traditional default cheaper; earmarking changes the default. She also said that when Dan talks as though a household pot is the neutral baseline he's mistaken, because transferability is itself a choice about what families should look like, so there is no neutral option available to him.

6. Dan replied that if the operative variable is the default and not the forfeiture, then she should want the cheaper instrument: auto-assign weeks to the father with an option for the couple to jointly reassign them. Same default shift, no destroyed value, no confiscation. And he said her neutrality point is a two-edged sword — if every rule embeds a picture of the family, then "it isn't neutral" stops being an argument for anything in particular, including the picture on which both parents are in the labor force and a single-earner household shows up as a residual category that pays into the scheme and draws nothing.

7. Ines said the single-earner household is hardly the residual category, since spousal and survivor benefits and the tax treatment of a secondary earner have subsidized that arrangement for decades, and a payroll-funded leave scheme is a transfer between households in which the dual-earner family is the one paying for other people's absences too.

What I want is a move-by-move adjudication, not a summary of the literature and no references please. For each of the seven, give me a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right with two or three sentences on why, and be specific about which part of the move earns the rating — several of these do useful work and then overreach in the same breath, and I want the two separated out. Then tell me who is more right overall and roughly how much: is this a case where one of them has the better of it, or are they disagreeing about different things (behavior change versus legitimacy, say) without noticing? Identify the single empirical premise the whole dispute hinges on, the one where if it went the other way most of these moves would flip. And finally, tell me what neither of them said — I have a nagging feeling that the household-bargaining story gets invoked by both of them at the moment of the first birth and then dropped, even though the things that actually determine the mother's position inside the household over the next twenty years are re-entry, pension credits, and divorce risk, and I don't think either of them ever cashed that out. If that's right, say so and say how it changes the verdict; if I'm wrong, tell me what the real blind spot was instead.
