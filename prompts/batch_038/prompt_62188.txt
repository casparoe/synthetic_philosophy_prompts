I spent thirty-seven years as a marine invertebrate systematist — gastropods and bivalves, largely from the northeast Atlantic — of which the last eleven were devoted to a regional biodiversity-monitoring programme that reported, annually, on species richness across a set of fixed survey stations. I retired eighteen months ago and have since been reading the philosophy of biology seriously for the first time, which is a disorienting experience when one has spent four decades producing species lists without ever being asked what one thought one was listing. The monitoring committee is now revising its reporting standards, and one of its members — a philosopher with an interest in environmental policy — has objected that our counts are arbitrary, resting on tacit metaphysical assumptions we have never articulated. I have therefore been asked to submit a written statement of my own commitments, and I would like a rigorous consistency check on the draft before it goes in. I am not looking for a survey of the literature, and I would rather not be told which of my views has a name; I want to know whether the package holds together, and where it does not.

Here is the package. (i) Species are historical individuals: spatiotemporally bounded particulars whose parts are organisms, not classes or kinds with defining properties. I find this attractive precisely because it seems to explain why species have no essences and yet are not fictions. (ii) There is no single correct species concept; criteria based on reproductive isolation, on diagnosable phylogenetic distinctness, and on ecological-niche occupation are each legitimate within their proper domain, and the choice among them should be governed by the question one is asking. (iii) Species are nonetheless mind-independent: where a lineage has split, there is a fact of the matter, and an inventory is a report about the world rather than a filing convenience. I am unmoved by the suggestion that taxonomists impose their boundaries in any sense that makes the boundaries ours rather than the organisms. (iv) Prokaryotes are the exception, and cheerfully so: I regard bacterial species as operational conveniences fixed by nucleotide-similarity thresholds, because lateral gene transfer means there is no single lineage boundary to be discovered there; this costs me nothing, since (iii) was never meant to apply where lineages of the relevant sort do not exist. (v) For monitoring purposes, species richness is the only metric I trust. I reject phylogenetic-diversity and functional-diversity indices as replacements, because they build in weighting decisions that no body of data can settle. (vi) I take ring complexes — the Ensatina salamanders circling California's Central Valley being the case I know best from the literature — to show that lineage splitting is continuous, with no natural place to stop; accordingly I do not expect boundaries to be sharp even in principle. (vii) On particular cases: I hold that Neanderthals and anatomically modern humans were distinct species notwithstanding the documented gene flow; that the intergrading populations along a ring are conspecific while the terminal forms that meet and do not interbreed are distinct; and that a pair of morphologically diagnosable but freely hybridizing gastropod populations I worked on myself count as a single species, since there the gene flow is pervasive rather than marginal. (viii) As a matter of method, when philosophical reconstruction and taxonomic practice diverge, I defer to what working systematists actually do; I am suspicious of theorising about taxa from an armchair.

Please audit this rather than explain it to me. Work through the commitments pairwise and identify which are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be — I suspect at least one of the conflicts I worry about is not real, and I would like to be told which, and why. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list, and for each one state what has to give, what the least costly revision would be, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package; a revision to (v), for instance, is not costless for a committee that has to publish a number every year. I am especially interested in two things you should address explicitly: whether (vii) contains any verdict that my own stated principles cannot deliver, given that the three cases look structurally similar to me but are judged differently, and whether (viii) is compatible with holding (i) and (iii) at all. Finally, tell me which single revision would buy the largest gain in overall coherence, and whether the resulting position would still be one I could defend to the committee without conceding that our species counts are arbitrary.
