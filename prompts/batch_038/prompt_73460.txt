Six weeks ago I was appointed general manager of a precision machining company of roughly 340 people, having spent the eleven years before that on the floor and then as a supervisor. The founding family has agreed in principle to sell the entire company to an employee stock ownership plan trust, closing next spring, and I sit on the conversion committee with the controller, two of the family's representatives, three elected operators, and an outside consultant. The consultant has been selling the committee on a single claim: that once the trust holds the shares, exploitation ends by definition, because the workers now own the surplus they produce; and that whatever dissatisfaction remains on the floor must therefore be alienation — a cultural residue that the firm's new participation programs will gradually dissolve. Several of the operators have accepted this framing. I have come to think it is confused: that the two words name distinct things which can move in opposite directions, and that employee ownership may not settle either.

Over the next eighteen months this committee will face roughly a dozen decisions of that kind, about one per monthly meeting, each with real money and real jobs attached. I would like a written procedure I can run on any such proposal and hand to the committee: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a determinate finding about exploitation, where it terminates in a determinate finding about alienation, and where it stops and returns the question to the committee because what remains is a matter of values rather than fact. Before the steps, give me a few paragraphs on what the distinction itself consists in, since I will have to defend the procedure to people who have been told the two words are synonyms.

Three constraints. First, the two axes must be assessed independently, and the procedure must be able to report a proposal that reduces one while worsening the other. Second, no step may require the committee to compute a magnitude nobody in the room can measure; if surplus value cannot be operationalized by a controller and three machinists in ninety minutes, the procedure must route around it and say what it is substituting, and why the substitution is defensible. Third, it must not depend on asking workers whether they feel fulfilled, because I suspect that someone who has spent twenty years with no control over the workday stops reporting that absence as a complaint. Mark explicitly every step that is conceptual judgment wearing the clothing of a measurement.

Then run the procedure on three live proposals:

1. Automating the deburring cell — the job our own survey ranked worst in the plant. Nine operators would be displaced into retraining; throughput would rise; the additional margin would accrue to the trust and be allocated to participant accounts in proportion to salary and years of service.

2. Staffing a new second-shift flex tier with forty workers hired through an agency at a lower hourly rate, running the same machines and doing the same work as our employees, but not eligible to participate in the trust.

3. A participation program already drafted: a fifteen-minute daily huddle in which each cell sets its own targets and any operator may stop the line, plus a suggestion board with cash awards, with no change to job content, staffing, or pay.

If the procedure returns a verdict on any of these that the committee will not want to hear, say so plainly rather than softening it, and close by stating what the procedure cannot determine at all. I am not looking for sources or a reading list; I need something that can be read aloud in a conference room.
