I have long been troubled by a debate in Palaeolithic archaeology that seems incapable of resolution, and I would like a diagnosis of its structure rather than another survey of the competing views.

The objects are the small portable female figurines of the Upper Palaeolithic — the ones conventionally labelled 'Venus' figurines — carved from mammoth ivory, bone, antler, and soft stone, and in some regions modelled in clay and fired, distributed from the Pyrenees to Siberia across a span of tens of thousands of years. The question at issue is what they were for. The main readings, as I understand them: (i) cult objects in some fertility or mother-goddess religion; (ii) sexually charged images produced by men for men; (iii) depictions of dressed women, on the grounds that close examination of the fine surface incisions on certain figurines reveals headgear, bandeaux, and skirts, which would make the figurines evidence about textile production and about the status of the women who made and wore such garments; (iv) self-representations by women, or teaching and mnemonic devices; (v) tokens in exchange networks, or markers of group identity carried over long distances; and (vi) a deflationary view on which there is no determinate function to recover — non-utilitarian making that leaves no recoverable meaning behind.

Three features of the corpus strike me as jointly constraining. First, an extreme stylistic stereotypy — accentuated torsos, diminished or absent faces, similar proportions — sustained over enormous distances and time depth. Second, local variability in exactly the details that a symbolic reading would want to be meaningful: headdress, ornament, size, material, posture. Third, breakage and deposition: very many figurines survive only as fragments, some appear to have been broken deliberately, before or after firing, and they are typically recovered from ordinary domestic debris, hearths, and middens rather than from anything that looks like a shrine.

My own working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than adopt, is a kind of triple-explanans test: an interpretation of these objects deserves credit only if it simultaneously accounts for the trans-regional stereotypy, the local variability, and the fragmentation-and-deposition pattern. As far as I can tell no current reading does all three, and each camp treats a failure on one dimension as a fatal objection to rival readings while treating the same failure in its own reading as beside the point. My further suspicion is that the debate is stalled not for want of evidence but because the governing question is malformed: it presupposes a single tradition where there were plainly many, and it presupposes a functional teleology — that the objects had a purpose of the sort tools have — which may simply not apply.

What I want from you:

1. A diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, grounded in what each side actually asserts and how each responds to the awkward facts. Is it partly verbal — turning on 'cult', 'religion', 'art', 'self-portrait', 'goddess'? Is it about standards of evidence, in particular the admissibility and direction of ethnographic analogy to recent hunter-gatherers? Is it about which facts count as data at all — whether breakage is evidence of discard, of ritual destruction, or of nothing but post-depositional damage? Is it about background values, specifically which agents prehistory is permitted to have? Or is it bedrock intuition that neither side can argue for? Say which of these you think is doing the most work, and why.

2. An assessment of my triple-explanans test. Is it the right shape for a test here? Is it so demanding that no interpretation of any prehistoric object could pass it, in which case it condemns everything equally and discriminates nothing? Or does it genuinely separate the readings, and if so which survive it and in what sense?

3. A verdict on whether the question 'what were these figurines for?' is well posed. If it is not, state the well-posed question or questions underneath it, and identify which camps would still be talking past one another even given perfect evidence.

4. An account of what would count as progress: which conceivable findings — use-wear and residue analysis, spatial and contextual statistics, direct dating and provenance work, ancient DNA or protein traces from handling, experimental replication, or the rare discovery of imagery that depicts such objects in use — would move the debate, and which would merely add a further reading to the list. I want the distinction between the two drawn explicitly, not assumed.

5. A comparison with a case where object-to-person inference was overturned by method. The tenth-century chamber grave at Birka in Sweden, excavated in the late nineteenth century, was described for more than a century as the burial of a high-status male warrior on the strength of its weapons, horses, and gaming set, until ancient DNA showed the skeleton to be female; the ensuing argument then turned on whether the grave goods licensed any claim about the deceased's role in life at all. Does that episode show that progress is possible in this kind of inference, or does it show that even a decisive scientific result simply relocates the disagreement one level up? Apply the lesson to the figurines.

Finally, tell me honestly whether you expect this debate ever to resolve, and whether its persistence reflects a general limit on reconstructing belief and institution from objects, or something specific — and therefore fixable — about this corpus. If you think my hypothesis is wrong, say so plainly and say what you would put in its place.
