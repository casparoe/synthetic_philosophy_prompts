A friend and I spent most of a four-hour drive arguing about the free energy principle, and neither of us came away convinced. His position is essentially the popular gloss: living systems are in the business of avoiding surprise, they partition themselves off from their environment by way of a Markov blanket, and then they perceive and act so as to keep sampling the sensory states their own model expects, which is why organisms resist the second law and stay inside a narrow band of physiological and sensory conditions. My objection was that the gloss either collapses into triviality or comes apart from the mathematics it is supposed to summarize, because variational free energy is only an upper bound on surprisal: surprisal equals free energy plus the KL divergence between the approximate posterior and the true posterior over hidden states. A system can therefore lower its free energy while its long-run surprisal rises, and the looseness of the bound is itself a feature the model controls, since an agent can be confident or diffuse. He replied that this cannot happen in any system worth calling adaptive, that when free energy and surprisal come apart the organism is simply badly designed and gets eliminated, and that I was playing with a degenerate case. I would rather settle this with a constructed example than with another drive.

So: build me a thought experiment in which the two quantities come apart in the direction I claimed, and in which the divergence is genuinely adaptive rather than pathological — an agent that ends up with higher long-run surprisal than a matched control while achieving lower variational free energy relative to its own generative model, and that does better by an ordinary measure of task success or fitness. The constraints are these. The setting must be plain and naturalistic: a small foraging animal, a chemotactic bacterium, a simple robot with a handful of sensors, and not a human mulling over philosophy. Do not use the dark room problem, and do not resolve the case by invoking the epistemic term in expected free energy; if exploration features in your scenario, you must show that it is doing something other than what that term already handles, since curiosity-driven sampling is the reply my friend will reach for first. The agent's generative model must be specified concretely enough that I can check your work: two or three hidden states, a small set of possible observations, explicit priors, and at least one worked free-energy computation, categorical or Gaussian, with the numbers shown. Keep the scenario itself to no more than two paragraphs and put the arithmetic after it.

Then do the audit. State precisely which quantity goes up, which goes down, over what timescale, and relative to which model; explain why the avoid-surprise gloss predicts the opposite behaviour, and what it would have to add to itself to accommodate the case; and tell me whether your construction turns on the KL term loosening, so that the agent's model becomes less confident while fitting better, or on the priors over outcomes being re-set, because those are different diagnoses and I suspect only one of them is legitimate. Finally, be honest about the design rather than selling it to me: identify the single feature of the case that makes the two quantities diverge, say what happens if I strip that feature out, and give my friend his strongest reply — the best argument that, properly described, this is no counterexample at all, whether because surprisal is only meaningful relative to a system's own model, or because the fair comparison is average surprisal under the agent's own long-run sampling statistics, or because the matched control would in fact win once the environment is fully specified. If you conclude that the case cannot be built without one of those replies succeeding, say so plainly and show exactly where the construction breaks.
