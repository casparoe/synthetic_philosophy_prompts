I am four years into a PhD in computational psychiatry, and my thesis amounts to a defence of a fairly strong version of the claim that the brain is a computer. Let me state the position as precisely as I can, because I want it attacked in its strong form rather than as a straw man. The claim is not the trivial one that neurons transmit signals and that anything which transmits signals can be redescribed as computing something. It is this: there is a level of description at which the brain literally runs an approximate Bayesian inference over a hierarchical generative model of the causes of its sensory input; at that level the quantities being manipulated — likelihoods, priors, precisions — are real rather than merely useful fictions, in something like the sense in which voltages in a circuit are real; and the neurobiological detail underneath is implementation, so that a different substrate realising the same inference would thereby realise the same cognitive process. I also hold that this is an empirical thesis that could have turned out false: the world might have contained perceptual systems whose behaviour is not explicable as inference under any generative model at all, and the fact that it does not is a substantive discovery about the world rather than a fact about how we choose to talk.

My case currently rests on three legs, and I should say what they are, since I suspect the strength of any given defeater varies by leg. First, cue combination: when observers are given two noisy sources of information about the same quantity, their combined judgements sit close to the reliability-weighted average of the two, and the weights shift in the right direction when the experimenter degrades one source. Second, precision-weighting in neural populations: the gain of adaptation and aftereffect phenomena scales with the reliability of the driving stimulus in a way that looks like the system discounting an unreliable channel, and an analogous scaling shows up in the amplitude of neural responses to predictable versus unpredictable input. Third, the systematicity of illusion: perceptual distortions are not noise but structured, and their structure is recoverable from the statistics of natural scenes, which is roughly what you would expect if a distortion is a prior doing its job. I have already thought hard about two obvious worries and believe I have answers. One is that precision is not independently measurable — it is read off from the very behaviour it is meant to explain, which makes the second leg look circular; my answer is that parameter values fitted to one paradigm predict performance in a different paradigm, though I concede that the number of such successful transfers is small. The other is the objection that an agent whose only imperative is to minimise prediction error should sit motionless in a dark room; my answer is that the generative model includes a prior over the agent's own active exploration, so a dark room is precisely what such an agent predicts it will not be in. I mention these only to signal that I am not looking for them a third time.

What I want is a red-team. Give me everything that would count against the position as I have stated it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and do not pad the list with the standard objections to computationalism in general unless they genuinely bite on this version. For each one I want to know what would specifically have to be observed, proved, or demonstrated — concrete enough that I could recognise it if it happened — and I want at least two defeaters that I have not anticipated here. I also want to be told which of them a committed proponent of the view would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to which ones only critics assert, because I strongly suspect I have been reading the friendlier half of the literature. Then tell me honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: it has absorbed a great many objections by adding machinery — higher-order priors, precision over precision, action folded into the same formalism — and I want to know whether that trajectory looks like normal scientific refinement or like a tell. If it has been insulated, say whether that is a problem for a thesis of this kind or whether the insulation is doing legitimate work. Finally, I want the predictions that distinguish this view from its live rivals, namely dynamical-systems accounts that explain the same data without positing representations, and pure associative-learning accounts that explain it without positing inference; if there are no such predictions, I would rather know that now than during my viva.

Format: begin with a single table of defeaters, one row per defeater, most severe first, using exactly these columns. `id`: D1, D2, and so on, in your ranking order. `defeater`: one sentence stating what would count against the position. `category`: exactly one of empirical finding, formal result, philosophical argument, rival explanation. `trigger`: what would concretely have to be observed, proved, or demonstrated, specific enough that I could tell whether it had happened. `severity`: exactly one of fatal, serious, awkward, cosmetic. `best_reply`: the strongest reply available to a proponent, in two sentences or fewer. `weak_point`: where that reply is least secure. `proponent_stance`: exactly one of would concede, contested, would dismiss — meaning how a committed defender of the view would treat it. Keep every cell to a sentence or two. After the table, give me one prose section of no more than three paragraphs delivering the falsifiability verdict and the top three defeaters in order, and then a second table of distinguishing predictions with exactly three columns: `prediction` (what my view predicts about some phenomenon), `rival` (which rival account, and what it predicts about the same phenomenon), `consequence` (what happens to my position if the rival's prediction comes out right).
