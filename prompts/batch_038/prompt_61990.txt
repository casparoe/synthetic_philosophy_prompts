Last term I audited a reading group devoted to the methodology of thought experiments, and its final session was consumed by a dispute between two regulars — I will call them N and R — over a case the convenor had circulated beforehand. The case, dubbed the Vault, runs roughly as follows. An interstellar probe is being loaded for a four-thousand-year flight to a habitable world. Its payload includes a machine that, on arrival, will gestate and raise a founding human community, supplied with language, technical training, and the practical knowledge needed to survive. The launch vehicle, however, has mass for either the cultural package — Earth's archive of literature, music, philosophy, and history, together with the means to teach it — or a redundant shielding unit that makes the mission's success effectively certain, but not both. Without the shielding there is a 40 percent chance the probe is destroyed in transit and no human being ever exists on the far world; with it, arrival is near-certain, but the children will grow up with no Homer, no Newton, and no memory that any of it existed. The convenor's stated conclusion: nearly everyone chooses the shielding, which shows that we do not in fact believe an unread archive has value of its own, and that value requires a valuer.

The exchange then proceeded in four moves.

1. N: the case is a vividness trick. The 40 percent figure and the image of children convert the question into a rescue dilemma, so respondents are answering "would you rather everyone die or grow up philistine?" rather than the intended question about impersonal value. Turn the dial — set the risk at 1 percent — and the reported intuition reverses. A verdict that tracks a knob you can adjust is measuring the knob.

2. R: that diagnosis proves too much. Nearly every thought experiment has knobs: number of lives, magnitude of harm, distance of the agent from the outcome. If parameter sensitivity were disqualifying, all of them would be, which is a stipulation rather than a finding. R also denies that the reversal N predicts indicates rhetoric; it shows only that the concept at issue is comparative. Value can be genuine and still tradeable, and forcing a trade-off is precisely what such cases are for. The real defect, R argues, lies elsewhere: the Vault quietly assumes that a life without literature is a deprivation for the children, when the case is constructed so that no one who could have had it ever exists. It slides from worse-for-someone to worse-full-stop, and borrows the plausibility of the first to pay for the second.

3. N: N grants that equivocation is a genuine hazard but says R has misidentified whose intuition is being borrowed. Pressed, people who choose the shielding say the children would not miss what they never had. That is already a person-affecting claim, not a smuggled comparison. So the Vault is not equivocating covertly; it is faithfully recording an intuition whose content is person-affecting — and the convenor's conclusion is a non sequitur, because no-one-is-deprived is perfectly compatible with holding that an unread archive is an impersonal loss. On this reading the case fails as an inference, not as a piece of rhetoric.

4. R: R replies that N has now conceded the point at issue. If the elicited intuition is person-affecting, that is exactly what the convenor claims to have elicited, so the non sequitur charge reduces to a quibble about how the conclusion is phrased. R's final position: the case is serviceable provided the intermediate question — is anyone deprived? — is put explicitly, after which the intuition it tracks proves stable rather than rhetorical.

They left on bad terms, both agreeing that the Vault is somehow flawed while holding diagnostic tests that appear mutually exclusive, and neither would accept the other's general criterion. Could you adjudicate this stepwise? For each of the four moves, say whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and explain what turns on the difference between the alternatives. Then address three questions directly: whether R's proves-too-much objection actually defeats N's neutralization test or merely limits its scope; whether move 3 succeeds in converting a dispute about rhetoric into a charge of invalid inference; and whether N and R are ultimately disagreeing about the Vault or describing two distinct failure modes that happen to co-occur in it. Finally, identify any consideration that bears on the case and that both of them overlooked.
