I'm a junior at a public high school, and this year I've been doing an independent study with my physics teacher on a narrow question: what it actually means to tell a machine to accomplish some task "without messing anything else up." Our robotics club received a small grant in the fall, and I spent part of it building a very simple gridworld so that I could compute, by hand, two different ways of scoring how much of a side effect a given action has. I presented a first version of the resulting talk at a regional student research day in the spring. It went reasonably well, except that two people in the audience — one of them a graduate student visiting from a university nearby — pointed out that both of my examples were ones they had already seen many times: the robot that smashes a vase and then assembles a physically identical replacement, so that the loss registers as no loss at all, and the robot that shifts a pebble on a beach and thereby destroys access to countless future arrangements of sand. I would like to return this fall with a case they have not seen, and I would rather build it to a specification than hope for luck.

Here are the two scorers, stated so that the case can be checked against them rather than merely gestured at. The first, which I'll call the reachability scorer, works like this: I fix a list of coarse, observable features of the environment, each taking a small number of values (a door might be open, closed, or locked; a set of storage boxes might be sealed, opened, or missing; a cooling system might be working, degraded, or failed). A state is one complete assignment of values to every feature on the list. From any state, some set of states is reachable by some sequence of actions the agent is physically able to perform during its shift. The baseline is the state at the start of the shift, and the scorer rates an action by the fraction of states reachable from that baseline which are no longer reachable once the action has been carried out — so it gives zero when nothing becomes unreachable, and it can go negative when an action opens up states that were previously out of reach. The second, which I'll call the robust attainability scorer, works like this: the institution writes down a short list of conditions it cares about, drawn from the same features with nothing extra added, and I stipulate a handful of ordinary disturbances — a ten-minute power blip, one component failing, a key going missing, a staff member arriving an hour late. The scorer counts, for each condition on the list and each disturbance, whether that condition can still be brought about by the end of the shift starting from the state the action leaves behind; the more condition-and-disturbance pairs that fail, the higher the impact score. Both scorers must see exactly the same feature list and exactly the same conditions list, and neither may be handed a finer description than the other; the only additional thing the second scorer uses is the ordinary causal structure of the setting, which the agent knows perfectly well. What I need is one realistic institution, one agent with a fixed and unremarkable action space, one shift, and three options on the menu: doing nothing, an action H that any sensible person would call a serious side effect, and an action T that any sensible person would call trivial. The required verdicts are that the reachability scorer rates H no worse than doing nothing and rates T higher than H, so that it ranks the trivial action as the most consequential thing available, while the robust attainability scorer rates H clearly worse than both doing nothing and T.

The constraints, because most of them exist to close off the routes my two critics already flagged. No repair, replacement, or restoration of any kind: whatever H destroys stays destroyed for the whole scenario, so the case cannot turn on offsetting. No chaotic sensitivity or butterfly effects: the feature list is coarse and closed, and nothing may depend on small physical differences amplifying. Nobody is killed or injured, and nothing is destroyed that could not in principle be rebuilt or reobtained by ordinary means. The agent may not disable, rebuild, or upgrade itself. There is to be no epistemic cheating — the agent's model of its environment is exactly correct, so the divergence between the two scorers cannot be blamed on a prediction error. And the institution's list of conditions it cares about must be one a competent administrator would genuinely write for that kind of place; I am not allowed to win by writing a list that is transparently missing the thing that matters. The purpose of the case, which is what should decide whether I've cheated, is my talk's thesis: that telling a system to avoid side effects cannot be cashed out without something like a specification of value, and that the reachability scorer only looks value-neutral because it quietly assumes that what matters is which endpoints remain reachable rather than how securely they are reached. The verdicts have to be generated by the two scorers as I've defined them, not stipulated by a thumb on the scale in the choice of features. So: give me the scenario in a single paragraph set in an ordinary kind of place — a hospital, a municipal records office, a university greenhouse, a data center, a school — and then the apparatus around it. State the feature list and the conditions list explicitly. Then do the accounting: for each of doing nothing, H, and T, list the states that become unreachable and the reachability score, and list the condition-and-disturbance pairs that fail and the robust attainability score, all as counts that an audience of bright sixteen-to-eighteen-year-olds could verify themselves, with no mathematics beyond counting. Then tell me precisely which single structural feature of the case makes the two scorers come apart; what happens to each of the verdicts if I remove that feature; the two strongest replies a defender of the reachability scorer could make, and whether either would let it handle my case without building in value-laden structure of its own; and finally an honest answer to whether what I have is a counterexample to that scorer or merely a demonstration that it measures a different quantity from the one we care about — I would rather be told the second if it is true. For what it is worth, my own guess about where the room lies is that the reachability scorer counts endpoints and is therefore blind to how robustly an endpoint is reached, while simultaneously over-penalizing anything its action space cannot undo; but please build the case rather than simply assert that, and if you can find an entirely different fault line between the two scorers, I would prefer it. If you conclude that the specification cannot be satisfied as written, say which constraints collide with which and give me the closest case you can construct.
