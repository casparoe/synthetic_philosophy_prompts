I sit on the panel at a national metrology institute that adopts the official reference values for a set of physical constants -- the numbers other labs, standards bodies and industry calibrate against. We meet in three weeks and there's a mess.

A group using a completely different experimental technique from the usual one has published a measurement of one of our constants that lands about 6 sigma away from the value we currently adopt, with an uncertainty budget that three seperate referees have tried to break and nobody's found a hole in. Our standing practice is to wait for independent replication before moving an adopted value, and half the panel wants to do exactly that here. The other half -- me, sort of -- is uncomfortable, because you can always say "unknown systematics" about any outlier, forever, which makes our adopted value unfalsifiable in a way I find pretty gross.

I've been reading around the Humean / anti-Humean debate about laws of nature and I think it's actually load-bearing here, even though I can't get the panel to take the framing seriously. The Humean says the laws, and by extension our adopted constants, are just the most compact informative summary of what's actually been measured -- so a well-characterized new data point changes the summary right now, and "wait for replication" is a choice about the summarizing method rather than a fact about the world. The governing-laws person says the constants are part of what produces the data, so one discrepant run is overwhelmingly more likely to be a subtly broken apparatus than a shift in what governs, and the prior for stability is doing real work.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. I want it to recommend an actual decision rule we could adopt going forward -- a standing procedure for what it takes to move an adopted value, not just a verdict on this one case -- and separately say what we should do about the current 6-sigma result, with a timeframe attached. Take the replication-norm side seriously: the history of one gorgeous unreplicated measurement turning out to have a systematic nobody budgeted for is genuinely long, and the memo shouldn't wave that away. But it also can't leave us with a rule that no single experiment could ever overturn, however good the experiment is. If the two halves of the dispute imply different procedures, say which one you're siding with and what it costs us.

Keep it under 500 words, bottom line up front in the first two sentences. No citations or references or name-dropping philosophers -- nobody on this panel reads that stuff and it'd just make them roll their eyes -- do the argument in plain lab language.
