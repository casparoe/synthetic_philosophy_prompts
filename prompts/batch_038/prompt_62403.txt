I run a fifteen-student graduate seminar on AI alignment; we have one three-hour session on adversarial debate between two capable systems as a safety technique, and the students will have read the case for it — that checking an argument is cheaper than producing one, so an honest debater can always win by pointing at the flaw. I want them to feel where that reasoning breaks: an argument every step of which survives inspection once inspected, where the difficulty lies entirely in locating the step worth inspecting.

Design an exercise that runs on the distinction between the cost of verifying a step and the cost of localizing it. Give me rules, materials, a disputed claim any of them could in principle settle given enough time, a judge holding a strictly limited budget of audits they must allocate themselves, and a scoring scheme that needs no real money. The deceptive and honest sides must have genuinely asymmetric tasks, and the honest side must be able to win — otherwise the lesson collapses into "the liar always wins."

Then a debrief mapping what happened onto the safety argument: what an equilibrium in which both sides obfuscate would look like from the judge's seat, what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run, and which feature of superhuman debate the exercise cannot reproduce and I must supply by hand.
