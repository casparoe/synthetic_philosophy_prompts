I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, and next week I am presenting a draft paper at our department's metaethics workshop. The slot is thirty minutes, the draft currently runs to about 8,500 words and needs to come down to 6,000, and my supervisor has told me bluntly that section four does not work and that the paper as a whole may be arguing against a position nobody actually holds. I would like a careful second opinion before I start cutting. The paper concerns the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism, and its thesis is deliberately revisionist: I want to argue that the problem has been misdiagnosed as a charge of incoherence, and that the real difficulty expressivism faces is one of explanatory redundancy. What follows are my section notes, which track the argument closely enough that you should be able to reconstruct the reasoning without seeing the prose.

Section 1 restates the problem in the form I take to be doing the real work. If the meaning of a moral sentence is given by the state of mind the speaker is using it to express, then a sentence occurring as the antecedent of a conditional is not being used to express anything, and so, on that account of meaning, has no meaning; yet an expression must contribute the same sense whether asserted or unasserted, otherwise the inference from a conditional and its antecedent could not be valid. I note in passing that this formulation was aimed at the prescriptivist and ascriptivist accounts of moral judgement rather than at the more sophisticated expressivist semantics that followed, and that a good deal of the subsequent dialectic has consisted in pressing the old formulation against new machinery that was never its target.

Section 2 develops my reframing in three steps. First, no current expressivist semantics is actually incoherent: Gibbard's plan-laden and factored-state accounts and Schroeder's being-for programme both offer recursive clauses that assign a state of mind to every complex sentence, in exactly the same structural way that a truth-conditional semantics assigns a truth condition. Compositionality is secured by construction, and I see no way to press a straightforward contradiction. Second, the interesting question is therefore what does the work inside that recursion. The clause for a conditional is, in each case, borrowed rather than earned: the state of mind assigned to a conditional is a higher-order attitude — disapproving of combining acceptance of the antecedent with non-acceptance of the consequent, or some analogue — and which higher-order attitude is the right one is settled by appeal to the same pattern of combination that already governs the descriptive case. Gibbard's own remark that plans combine according to the patterns beliefs combine in is, I suggest, an admission of exactly this; likewise the positing of a single unifying relation in order to make the attitude-clauses mirror the classical ones. Third, if the formal structure that does the compositional work is imported from a prior account of the logical constants, then the attitude-assignment layer is doing no independent explanatory work: it decorates a composition that was already fixed.

Section 3 turns this into a dilemma. Either the logical vocabulary receives its content independently, from classical patterns of inference and composition, in which case expressivism is parasitic on a descriptive account of composition and my objection converges on the familiar complaint that quasi-realism merely puts on the semantic clothing of realism; or the logical vocabulary is itself explained expressivistically, in which case the theory owes an account of what fixes the patterns for purely descriptive conditionals as well, and must distinguish believing-that from planning-to without presupposing the very compositional structure it is trying to explain.

Section 4 — the section my supervisor objects to — proposes a stress test on mixed compounds. Consider the disjunction whose first disjunct is a moral sentence and whose second is a trivially descriptive one, together with the disjunctive syllogism that runs from that disjunction and the negation of the moral disjunct to the descriptive one. On the expressivist's own view the two disjuncts express states of mind of fundamentally different kinds, so any single unifying attitude for the disjunction must be specified in terms of a relation between an attitude and a belief. My claim is that the only natural way to specify such a relation is by reference to which combinations of world and plan are ruled out, and that this is already a representational notion: the expressivist has to know the answer to the composition question before the attitude-clauses for mixed cases can be written down at all. Section 5 concedes the obvious expressivist reply — that the attitude-clauses are there to explain the practical features of moral judgement rather than to explain composition, so that no redundancy arises — and answers that this reply, if accepted, means the theory is an account of moral psychology rather than a semantics of moral sentences, and that the Frege–Geach problem was never a live semantic objection in the first place. My conclusion is therefore deflationary rather than devastating: expressivism is not refuted by embedding, but its semantic ambitions are idle.
