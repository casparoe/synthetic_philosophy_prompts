Our formation program (secular now, but it grew out of a seminary) teaches character almost entirely through lives: Plutarch's paired biographies, two or three saints' lives kept for the structure even though nobody believes them, and a couple of modern biographies a year. Every year someone on the faculty objects that reading about admirable people isnt moral education, and the rest of us say it obviously is, and then nobody can say what the mechanism is supposed to be. I'd rather settle the spec before I read another account of it.

So: what does a theory of learning virtue from a life actually have to deliver to be worth anything? The purpose is narrow and I'll fix it — good enough to tell us what to assign and how, not good enough to be a full theory of moral development.

Give me the desiderata, each with the case or problem behind it. Here's what I'd already put on the list; push back on any of these if you think it doesn't belong:

- Plutarch's own stated method. Somewhere at the front of the Alexander he says he's writing lives and not histories, and that a slight thing — a remark, a joke — reveals character better than the biggest battles. An account has to explain why grain of detail beats scale of deed, or reject the claim outright.
- the hagiography datum. The stylized, largely invented saint's life demonstrably works on readers. Our students report being *more* moved by the sanitized lives than by the honest modern biography we assign right next to them. If truth-tracking is what does the work here, that has to be explained and not just explained away.
- the fiction/real-life discrimination. If narrative is what moves you, a novel should do it just as well and the fact that the person existed is doing nothing; if reality is what moves you, see the previous bullet. A theory has to pick a side and pay for it.
- transfer. The exemplar is a Roman general or a desert ascetic, the reader is a twenty-year-old in a city. What is supposed to carry over, and why isn't it just the rule they could have read in a book?
- situationism. The results that made trait-based virtue ethics nervous — the obedience and helping studies where trivial features of the setting swamp the person's character — seem to undercut exactly what exemplar pedagogy assumes. Does an account have to be consistent with them, or can it take a stand against them?
- admiration sliding into hero-worship, and the reader's habit of choosing exemplars who already resemble them. Is that a defect the theory can fix, or a feature it has to build in?
- bad lives. We seem to learn plenty from the worse man, and Plutarch pairs them deliberately so they can be compared. Does the account handle instruction from vice, or only from virtue?

Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are prejudices — mine, or a particular camp's. Then tell me whether some subset can't be jointly satisfied, because if the hagiography datum and the fiction/real-life requirement genuinely can't both be met, the list itself is the finding and I want that said plainly rather than buried. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most accounts.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which requirement is doing the most work, and whether the list is jointly satisfiable — then the list and the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate my question back at me. Very briefly at the end, two lines each on how an admiration-based exemplarism, a narrative-identity account, and a moral-perception-or-phronesis-through-cases account do against the list. No bibliography, no titles, I want the criteria.
