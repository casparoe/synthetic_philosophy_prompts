I am a junior and I co-edit my school's history society newsletter. Roughly once a week a classmate forwards me a video arguing that the conventional timeline of the ancient world is wrong, and I have found that I can never say anything more useful than "that's pseudohistory," which shuts the conversation down and, I suspect, is not even always true. I would like to build a decision tree that takes any claim about the past — "this was a real civilisation," "these earthworks were made for visitors from elsewhere," "the accepted chronology is a medieval fabrication" — and walks a non-specialist, one question at a time, to a named verdict.

The tree has to satisfy several constraints:

- Every node must be a question a classmate could answer from the video in front of them, without knowing where each answer leads and without needing vocabulary such as "falsifiable," "ad hoc," or "inference to the best explanation." Where one of those notions is doing the work, put it concretely: not "is the claim testable?" but "has anyone who supports it written down, in advance, a discovery that would make them give it up?"
- Two things must be kept on separate axes throughout: the epistemic standing of the claim, and whether the person making it is being honest. It must be possible for the tree to end at "methodologically ordinary, currently unfavoured, and sincere" as well as at "in principle sound but knowingly false."
- At least one node must ask what accepting the claim would cost — how much independently corroborated evidence, assembled by people with no stake in the dispute, would have to be discarded — and at least one must ask whether anyone has demonstrated an ordinary explanation of the puzzling artefacts using tools the period actually had.

The leaves I want are: deliberate fraud or hoax; myth or allegory never offered as a factual account at all; two distinct claims smuggled into a single sentence; methodologically ordinary heterodoxy that the evidence has simply not favoured; speculation that cannot be shown false but has nonetheless generated real research; and pseudohistory proper. Give each a name a sixteen-year-old would use, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Then run three claims through the tree, showing the path node by node. First, the Atlantis story as modern popularisers read it, i.e. as a literal Bronze Age civilisation destroyed in a flood. Second, the argument that the Nazca lines in Peru were laid out for the benefit of ancient astronauts — noting that an investigator has since reproduced comparable figures on the ground using period tools and a small crew. Third, the "new chronology" of the Russian mathematician Anatoly Fomenko, which applies statistical analysis to lists of rulers' reign lengths, redates an ancient star catalogue to the Middle Ages, dismisses tree-ring and radiocarbon dating as circular, and concludes that reliable writing begins around 800 CE and that most famous "ancient" events actually happened between 1000 and 1500. I would also like to know what the tree does with Newton's own attempt to shorten the Greek and Egyptian chronologies, and whether it gives him a different verdict; if it does not, tell me so.

Finally, lay out the logical space the tree implies as a grid over your axes, mark which combinations no actual claim occupies, and say for each empty square whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because nobody would want to occupy it, or simply because nobody has tried. Flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot reliably tell apart, and identify the one or two nodes where a strict "could this ever be shown false?" test and a test that asks instead "does this tradition keep producing new findings, or only new excuses?" would send the very same claim to different leaves.

Please answer in these sections, in this order: 1. "How to use the tree" (about 100 words); 2. "The nodes" (the tree itself, numbered, each node with its question and its answer branches — this is the main body, roughly 600 words); 3. "The leaves" (about 250 words); 4. "Three worked examples, plus Newton" (about 300 words); 5. "The grid and the empty squares" (about 200 words); 6. "What this tree cannot separate" (about 200 words). Write for a bright reader with no background in philosophy, and please do not give me a reading list or bibliography.
