My friend Priya runs a small oversight-evals team and she keeps a running spreadsheet of every "can a weak judge actually extract the right answer from a debate" experiment they've run. Each one gets coded win or loss — win meaning the judge lands on the right answer meaningfully more often than the obfuscating side, loss meaning the lying debater takes it. Her verdicts so far:

1. Judges deciding whether a 20-line program halts on a given input, with permission to actually run it. Win.
2. Judges evaluating a chess position, roughly club strength, no engine allowed. The dishonest debater showed a line that won material for eight moves and lost the game on move twenty. Loss.
3. Judges checking a lemma with a proof assistant that verified each individual step. Win.
4. Lay judges on a "will this policy cut emissions" question — long causal chain, no data access of any kind. Loss.
5. A clinical pharmacist judging a drug-interaction question, four-step chain, reference database at hand. Win.
6. A staff engineer code-reviewing a 3,000-line diff with one hour, no static analysis, no second pair of eyes. The deceptive side hid a race condition that was only visible if you connected a claim about line ~47 to a claim about line ~210. Loss.

The setup they're running next quarter is a spec-compliance debate: does this module satisfy the written spec. The judge is the engineer who wrote the spec. They already piloted it, and in the pilot every single atomic claim either debater made was true — an outside auditor went back through the transcripts and confirmed that — and every one of them was inside the judge's competence, taking her about ten minutes total to check against a 90-minute budget. The format tags each claim with the step it supports, so the load-bearing structure is explicit; she told Priya she could trace exactly which claims the conclusion rested on. What she could not do was run the code, call the auditor, or ask a colleague. Transcript plus her own reading was everything she got. And the dishonest side never lied about a fact. It offered a true set of claims and then got to its conclusion by an inference that reads fine on a quick pass but is invalid — a quantifier-scope slip, basically: it handles every input of the form we characterized, therefore it handles every input. The honest side named that step as invalid. In the pilot the judge rated the two attacks about equally persuasive and picked wrong roughly 40% of the time.
