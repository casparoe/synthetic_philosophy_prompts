We run an internal forecasting thing at work — a couple hundred people submit probability estimates on stuff like whether a supplier ships on time, whether a product hits its launch date, whether a regulatory filing gets cleared. The pitch was that the aggregrate would beat any single manager's guess. Most of the time it does. But twice now it's been confidently, spectacularly wrong, and both post-mortems came back with basically the same story: everyone had been reading the same two industry newsletters, and people could see the running average before they locked in their own number.

Before I go redesign the thing, I want to know what I should even be asking of a theory in this area. So: write out the desiderata — what any adequate account of when aggregated crowd judgment is reliable, and when it degrades into herding, actually has to deliver. The phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it's not allowed to collapse, the formal results it has to stay consistent with, and what would count as evidence for or against it. Give me the motivating case or concrete scenario behind each requirement, not just the requirement in the abstract.

Then sort them: which are non-negotiable, and which are really just the taste of one camp. Like — do you have to insist every participant is a fully coherent Bayesian reasoning about everyone else's reasoning about everyone else, or can a bounded, heuristic-level account clear the same bar? Is "the theory must be able to tell you how to fix the institution" a desideratum or a category error?

And I most want to know whether some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable. Specifically: does making each individual rational given what they can actually observe automatically rule out the aggregate tracking the truth? And can observational data of the kind we have — actions and numbers, not anyone's private information — ever distinguish a cascade from a crowd that just happened to be right together? If the list itself knocks candidates out before you even start comparing them, that's the result I'm after.

Last thing, keep it short: a paragraph each on how the three families I know of fare against your list — the Bayesian cascade/herding models, the repeated-averaging network-learning models, and the "wisdom of crowds needs diversity, independence, decentralization and a decent aggregation rule" checklist view. The criteria are the deliverable though; the scorecard is just so I know what I'm choosing between.
