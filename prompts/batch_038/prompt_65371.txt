Write a satirical auction-house catalogue entry of about 600–700 words for a seventeenth-century Dutch interior, inventing the painter, the forger, and the house itself. The piece should contain four parts: a lot essay in the house's lofty register, a numbered condition report, a provenance list, and a short note to bidders. Structurally, the joke is that the lot is a forgery and every department knows it, but the sale survives by retreating one rung at a time down the standard attribution ladder, so that the entry opens as a work by the master and closes somewhere near "after a lost original that may itself have been a copy." The target is the profession's half-stated creed that a difference which cannot be seen cannot matter — a creed the house invokes in nearly every paragraph while its own conduct quietly refutes it, through the estimate, the insurance, the indemnity, a scholar's technically true hedge, and a conservation report that recasts an anachronistic pigment as evidence of experimental technique. Let the whole thing function as a reductio rather than a lecture: no character may state a theory or use "authenticity" as a philosophical term of art, nothing may wink at the reader, and the humor should come from getting both the philosophy of forgery and the actual idiom of catalogue copy exactly right, so that a specialist in either recognizes the piece as fair. Keep the register impeccably commercial and the irony fully deadpan throughout. Please write in American English.
