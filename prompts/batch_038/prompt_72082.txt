I am a PhD student TA-ing a third-year undergraduate seminar on the history of the ethics of truthfulness, and I have to grade and return a batch of closed-book, two-hour in-class essays by Friday. Our marking scheme weights exposition of the assigned argument at 40, critical assessment at 30, accuracy about the texts at 20, and organisation and clarity at 10; the scheme also states explicitly that students must keep distinct (a) the argument from the purpose for which signs and speech exist, (b) the argument that the liar's own soul is the primary victim of the lie, and (c) the argument from the dissolution of trust between people, and that they lose credit for presenting these as one argument. Below is the question and one student's answer. The two assigned treatises are Augustine's *On Lying* and the later *Against Lying*, addressed to Consentius.

**Question.** Augustine holds that lying is wrong even when a lie would save an innocent life. Explain the argument he gives for this, say what he thinks a person in that situation should do instead, and assess whether that alternative escapes the objection that seems to defeat his prohibition.

**Student answer.**

(1) Augustine defines a lie as an utterance or sign that goes against what the speaker holds in the heart, joined to the will that the hearer take it for what the speaker really thinks. What matters is the double heart: one thing inside, another put outside. It follows that someone who asserts what she believes to be true does not lie even if she is mistaken, and Augustine is emphatic that errors of this kind are not lies.

(2) Speech and signs were instituted so that people could make known to one another what is in their minds. To use them to produce in a hearer the belief that one's mind contains something it does not is to turn the instrument against the purpose it exists for. So a lie is not wrong because of what it costs the hearer: it is wrong in the doing, and its first victim is the liar, whose soul is damaged by any lie more than the body of the person she might have saved by one. Augustine adds that lying destroys the trust without which human association is impossible, so the wrongness of lying is in the end a wrong against the community.

(3) Because every lie is the same kind of act and no good effect can be produced by an act that is corrupt in itself, Augustine holds that all lies are equally culpable. The lie told to save a life from a murderer is no more serious and no less serious than a lie about doctrine; there is no scale of gravity for him to appeal to, and this is why he cannot grant exceptions.

(4) In the hard case — the innocent man hidden in your house and the murderer at the door asking where he is — Augustine's answer is not to lie but to decline: one may hide the truth, fall silent, or refuse to answer, and none of these is a lie, because lying requires an utterance. This is the same licence he grants against the Priscillianists in *On Lying*, where he allows that a believer under interrogation may keep the faith in his heart while denying it with his lips, provided the heart does not consent — the position later developed by casuists as mental reservation.

(5) Augustine extends the principle to Scripture. In his exchange with Jerome over Paul's rebuke of Peter at Antioch, Augustine follows Jerome and Origen in holding that the apostles arranged the confrontation in advance as a lesson for later readers, since the alternative — that Peter really erred and Paul really reproached him — would put a lie inside a canonical text.

(6) The fallback does not escape the objection. Silence and concealment are chosen for exactly the reason a lie would be chosen, and they achieve the same thing: the murderer is misdirected. If lying is wrong because of what it causes the hearer to believe, then the difference between saying he is not here and saying nothing is technical rather than moral, and Augustine's refusal to draw that conclusion looks arbitrary. But if the wrongness sits in the misuse of the sign and in the damage to the liar's own heart, then the difference does hold, since nothing false has been signified and the heart has not been split. So the objection's force depends on which of Augustine's arguments is doing the work, and the prohibition survives only on the second.
