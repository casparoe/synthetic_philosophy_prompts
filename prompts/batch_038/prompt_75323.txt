I have served for three years on the Adult Directives and Capacity Panel of a regional public hospital network, and I have been asked to write the orientation memo that explains to two incoming members how the Panel actually decides cases. The memo is due in a week and I have drafted and discarded four versions of the same paragraph, because I cannot state our rule. I suspect we do not have one, or rather that we have several which have never been forced apart — until the matter scheduled for next Tuesday. Here is the track record as I have reconstructed it from the files; for two of the six determinations I could obtain only the outcome and the summary line, not the full written reasons, which I flag where relevant. (1) A retired carpenter, directive written at 58 after watching his mother die on a ventilator, refusing artificial nutrition and intubation once he no longer recognized his grandchildren by name. Advanced dementia at 71; cheerful, delighted by ice cream and by westerns on the radio, pulling at his feeding tube; two of his three adult children objected. The Panel honored the directive, nutrition was withdrawn, and he died within the month; the stated reason was that the directive expressed the values he had lived by and that his present contentment was not evidence he would have revised them. (2) A woman with Huntington's disease, directive at 38 refusing life-prolonging treatment once she could no longer read a novel for pleasure. At 41, with a neurologist documenting intact capacity but noting low mood, she wrote a note saying she would rather have more time than she had expected and asking her family not to be hasty. At 44 she was past the threshold, no longer reading, content, and answering yes when asked whether she wanted to live. The Panel declined to follow the original directive and treated her aspiration pneumonia; she lived another fourteen months and her family reported she was glad of them. The stated reason was that the most recent competent expression of her wishes governs. (3) A 29-year-old with schizoaffective disorder who, during a stable period, had executed what we call a Ulysses clause: advance consent to a named antipsychotic in any future manic episode, with language instructing us to disregard her refusals at the time. Manic, refusing, not dangerous, and assessed as lacking capacity for the treatment decision. The Panel authorized treatment, reasoning that her earlier self had spoken to precisely this situation and that the later refusal was, in the file's phrase, a symptom rather than a choice. (4) A former pastor, directive at 50 refusing transfusion on religious grounds. He left the congregation at 57, remarried outside the faith, told his new wife that chapter of his life was closed, and never amended the document. He presented unconscious from a gastrointestinal bleed, with no capacity at any point; his wife asked that he be transfused. The Panel honored the refusal and he died. The summary line I have says only that a change of heart is legally inert absent a revocation made with capacity, and that the Panel would not speculate about which of his two selves was the real one. (5) A 77-year-old whose directive, signed at 70 on a standard form at a community health fair and never discussed with anyone, refused heroic measures. After a severe stroke she was minimally responsive; her daughter reported years of remarks about wanting to live to see her great-grandchildren start school. The Panel authorized continued antibiotics and artificial nutrition, reasoning that a form signed without reflection did not carry the weight of an expression of her values. (6) A 61-year-old with Lewy body dementia and fluctuating capacity, directive at 55 refusing nursing-home placement and requesting to die at home. On lucid mornings he asked for a hospice referral; on confused afternoons he was frightened and begged to be taken to the hospital. The Panel deferred, ordered a structured capacity log over six weeks, then authorized hospice on the strength of what it called his lucid and repeated wishes, arranging medication for the frightened episodes rather than transfer.

The new case is this. A 49-year-old woman — call her R. — with a progressive motor neuron disease diagnosed at 42; repeated neuropsychological testing has shown no cognitive or behavioral involvement. Her directive was written at 38, before any diagnosis, after her father died of the same disease, and it set two conditions: no artificial ventilation or nutrition once she required ventilatory support continuously, and once she could no longer feed herself. She did not make loss of communication a condition, and this matters, because she still communicates fluently with an eye-gaze device and is articulate, funny, and combative about politics. At 44, two years into her illness and with intact capacity documented, she added a paragraph in her own hand. In it she said that she knew people in her situation sometimes come to want more time, that she might become one of them, and that if she asked to be kept alive after reaching the threshold, the Panel and her family should treat the request as the illness and the fear speaking rather than as her. She went further, and this is what has us arguing: she named her own future arguments in advance, saying that she would probably tell us she had been too proud at 38, and that she would probably say some days were good, and that neither statement should be taken as a change of mind. She asked to be held to what she was saying then, while she could still say it clearly. Both threshold conditions are now met. She has asked, on three separate occasions, that treatment continue; one of those occasions involved an independent psychiatrist she requested herself, and all three capacity assessments found her to have decision-making capacity for treatment decisions. She has said that her 44-year-old self was too proud and wrong; that she is not suffering as she had imagined she would; that she enjoys her nieces' visits and a serialized show and the arguments with her husband; and that she is tired, and that some days are bad. At 47, with capacity intact, she wrote a letter that both sides quote, saying that she did not want to be a burden but did not want to be gone either, and asking her husband to do whatever would let him live with himself. Her husband declines to decide and asks the Panel to choose; her sister says honor the directive; her neurologist says withdrawal of ventilation would likely bring death within days and that she is not otherwise suffering. Our statute, as we have always read it, permits a declarant with capacity to revoke a directive at any time and in any manner, and we have never had to decide whether that right can be waived in advance, which is exactly what R.'s clause purports to do. My supervisor has a name for what she thinks the clause claims: anticipatory authority, the idea that an earlier self's authority over a later self peaks when the earlier self correctly forecast the later self's dissent and addressed it pre-emptively, since the dissent is then the very thing the earlier self was speaking about. I think that is a philosophical claim rather than a legal one, and I do not know whether our precedents already support it.

What I want from you, for the memo and for my own clarity, is the following. First, predict the Panel's determination and give me a confidence level, and identify which of the six precedents is the closest analog and why, together with the precedent the losing side will invoke and what it would have to say to distinguish it. Second, lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all six outcomes — I expect at least four, and I expect some of them to be hybrids rather than anything I have listed — and show for each how it accounts for every one of the six, noting any determination it can accommodate only by an ad hoc distinction. Then say which of those rules survive the new case and what each surviving rule implies about it. Third, tell me which rule you think the Panel is in fact running and defend the preference: in particular, whether our practice gives weight to the earlier self as such, to the most reflective expression, to the expression made at the patient's best, or to something else, and whether anticipatory authority names something already latent in precedents one, three, and four, or something new whose adoption would change how we decide future cases. Fourth, describe a further case — ideally a minimal variation on R.'s facts, changing one feature at a time — that would force two of the surviving rules apart, so that the Panel could not rule consistently with both; I want to put it in the memo as a hypothetical for the new members. Fifth, for each of the two most likely determinations, state plainly what principle the Panel would be adopting by ruling that way and name one consequence of that principle in other kinds of cases that I doubt the Panel would welcome. Finally, list any fact you consider missing and material, and say how its resolution would move your prediction. You need not cite anything, but if any of the candidate rules corresponds to a position with a familiar name in the literature on advance directives and personal identity, say so, and tell me whether the version our precedents support is the standard one or a variant of it.
