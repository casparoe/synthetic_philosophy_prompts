I co-teach a graduate seminar with the history department — half philosophy students, half Europeanists — and this term we paired a unit on the July 1914 crisis with a unit on collective responsibility. It went sideways fast. Two of the regulars got into it at the pub afterwards and I sat there uselessly eating nuts and taking no notes, so this is reconstruced from memory as best I can. Ines works on the July crisis; Ben does moral philosophy of groups and agency. Neither would drop it, and I genuinely couldn't referee it. Maybe you can.

The exchange, in eight moves:

1. Ines: the Fischer line still holds up better than the everyone-was-sleepwalking line. Berlin used the July crisis to start a war it had been steering toward for years, and the September 1914 war-aims programme tells you what they wanted out of it. So responsibility for 1914 isn't spread evenly across the great powers — it's distributed by who decided what.

2. Ben: fine, but "Germany decided" is not something that happened. The Kaiser, Bethmann, Moltke, the Wilhelmstrasse, the general staff — those are the agents. When you say Germany is responsible you're either (a) talking about a dozen men, in which case the nation-word is decoration, or (b) blaming tens of millions who had no say, including the SPD, which was the loudest anti-war party in Europe right up until the afternoon it voted for war credits in August 1914.

3. Ines: neither. What produced the war was a decision structure — mobilization timetables, war-planning doctrine, an alliance system with automatic triggers — and its outputs are not what any individual inside it intended. Under the general staff's planning logic, "mobilize" and "march through Belgium" were effectively the same act. Structures like that are the right subject of "Germany did it," and they aren't just the sum of the men currently occupying them.

4. Ben: that's an explanation, not an accusation. You've described a machine with terrible outputs. Blame needs a subject that can register and respond to reasons, and a mobilization schedule can't. So on your own account you've explained the war without finding anyone to blame — which may be the truth, but then the war-guilt article at Versailles was a category error, and Fischer's verdict is moral vocabulary doing historiographical work.

5. Ines: the men who built and maintained the machine knew exactly what it committed them to. Moltke knew that mobilizing against Russia meant an attack on France through Belgium. Keeping in place a system whose outputs you foresee is itself an action. So structure and agency aren't competitors here: I blame the men for the structure, and I blame the structure through them.

6. Ben: then your collective talk did no independent work. You've landed on individuals plus a foreseeability condition, which is where I started — you just took the scenic route.

7. Ines: there's one place it can't be dissolved, which is liability. Somebody had to pay for northern France, and it wasn't going to be Wilhelm's private estate. And here's the bit philosophers always get wrong: Article 231 never says "guilt" anywhere. Roughly, it has Germany accepting responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war, and it was drafted as the legal hook for reparations. The Allies were startled by how furiously Germans reacted, because they thought they'd written an accountant's clause, not a verdict. So the document everybody cites as the paradigm case of blaming a nation isn't a moral judgment at all. Payment just has to attach to something that outlives its members.

8. Ben: which is my point! If 231 is a liability clause and not a blame clause, then holding nations responsible is really the practice of allocating costs to a continuing entity — a legal fiction with a useful function. Guilt still requires a mind.
