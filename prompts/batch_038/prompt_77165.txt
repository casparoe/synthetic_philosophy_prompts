Longtime lurker, no ML background beyond reading, and I keep needing a word for a thing I don't have.

You try to work out what someone wants by watching what they do. But once they catch on that you're watching *in order to* work it out, their behavior stops being a clean readout of what they want. They start picking actions partly for what those actions will make you conclude. They're not lying — they haven't changed their mind about anything, and often they'd rather you got it right. The measurement channel has quietly turned into a communication channel.

Two cases I keep running into. One: a robot learns a task from demonstrations, and the human demonstrator does something deliberately clumsy or roundabout so the robot won't infer the wrong goal — the demo is now adressed to the learner rather than to the task. Two: people filling out a form knowing a model will score it, who shade their answers toward what a scorer would like, which then makes the trained model worse in a way nobody intended.

Nearest things I know are Goodhart's law, which I don't think fits, since nothing here is being optimized against a target except the inference itself, and the old "people work harder when watched" effect, which is about effort rather than about what the behavior means. Is there an established term for this — in ML, in game theory, or in whatever social scientists call the problem of measurement changing what it measures — and how does what I described differ from each candidate that comes close? And if nothing quite fits, coin one and give me the edges: does it still apply when the person is unaware they're doing it, or when the watcher is another human instead of a machine?

I'm not after a bare label, I want the carving. Bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question.
