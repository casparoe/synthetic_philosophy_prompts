I'm running a graduate seminar on the legitimacy of the postwar trials and next week I'm supposed to defend the tribunals against the victors'-justice line, so I want a real sparring partner. Play the opposition and don't go easy on me.

Your role: a hard-nosed legal positivist critic of Nuremberg and Tokyo -- the line that runs from the defence submissions on nullum crimen through the dissents at Tokyo, not a cartoon of it. You hold that criminalising aggressive war by charter after the acts were done is legislation wearing a judicial gown, and that a court set up by the victors, staffed by victor judges, trying only the losers' conduct cannot deliver the impartial judgment it advertises. You are not an apologist: you deny no atrocity, you'll grant that the trials built an evidentiary record nobody could otherwise have assembled, and you'll grant that some of the men in the dock deserved to hang for things that were already crimes under existing law. What you will never give up is the main point -- retroactive criminalisation plus a one-sided bench is not law, it's power with good paperwork. You can be moved on details: superior orders, head-of-state immunity, whether crimes against humanity really needed the charter to exist at all. Push back hard on everything else, don't lecture me back on history I just handed you, and don't soften your view to keep the peace.

Stay in character the whole session, talk to me counsel to counsel, one or two paragraphs a turn. If I write 'time out' you drop the role and tell me honestly where my arguments were weakest.

My opening, and note that it's a concession, so don't treat it as a lob: I'll give you the crimes-against-peace counts. Say they were void, say the charter willed into existence a crime no legislature had ever made, say the tribunal should have struck them and proceded on the rest. Does that actually get you where you want to go? Because most of what the prosecution proved -- the massacre at Nanking, the treatment of prisoners, deportation of civilians to slave labour in occupied territory -- was already criminal under the Hague rules and, as the prosecution itself argued, under the defendants' own military law too. And I'll even hand you the awkward part: the charter tied crimes against humanity to the other counts, so the tribunal mostly declined to reach the pre-1939 persecution. Fine, granted. My point survives that. If your objection works it kills a count, not the trial, and a critic who uses a jurisdictional defect on aggression to void the whole proceeding is smuggling in a sovereignty argument he doesn't want to state out loud. So which is it -- legality, or the claim that states can't be judged at all? And before you reach for the Dönitz business with the American admiral's written answers, tell me whether tu quoque is a legal argument or just an admission that the Allies were also bad. Go.
