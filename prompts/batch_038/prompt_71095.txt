I'm in the final year of a political theory degree and currently taking a seminar on competition and collective action in technology sectors. Last week's section was on multipolar traps, and during it a classmate dismissed a proposal — that a leading firm should submit its flagship system to an expensive, slow, third-party safety audit even though no competitor has agreed to do the same — with the sentence "you can't unilaterally disarm." Several people nodded, the topic moved on, and I want to be able to say next week precisely what that sentence can and cannot do.

For my paper I've modelled the case as follows. Firm A and Firm B each choose either to run a nine-month independent audit before deploying, or to deploy on schedule. If both audit, each keeps its share and a widely anticipated public failure is averted. If only one audits, that firm forfeits roughly a year of first-mover advantage and the other takes the market. If neither audits, the failure occurs within a few years and the activity is banned outright. My classmate treats the matter as settled by the slogan. My suspicion is that the slogan smuggles in assumptions about which game this actually is, about who "you" is, about what "disarm" amounts to, and about what kind of "can't" is in play — and that on the least demanding reading under which it comes out true, it doesn't get him his conclusion at all.

So: lay out, as a numbered menu I can bring to section, the distinct precise claims that "you can't unilaterally disarm" might be making, from the platitudinous to the radical. For each, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim that is probably true, one that is false, or a non sequitur presented as a fact about the world; what it presupposes about the structure of the interaction (one-shot prisoner's dilemma, iterated play, stag hunt, chicken), about verifiability and credible commitment, and about the scope of "you" and of "disarm" (a full halt versus a slowdown, a disclosure, an inspection regime); and which of the readings, if any, would genuinely license dismissing my audit proposal. Then say what the strongest true reading leaves of my classmate's argument, and what a counterexample would have to look like — I have in mind candidates such as a state's declared moratorium on destructive direct-ascent anti-satellite missile testing, South Africa's dismantling of its own nuclear arsenal, and the Soviet unilateral testing moratorium of the mid-1980s that Washington never matched — and whether each is a real counterexample or merely a case in which the slogan's conditions were not met.

Please also be explicit about the limits of your own assessment: which of your verdicts you are least confident in, what consideration or case would move you to a different verdict on the readings that matter most here, and where this style of analysis is most likely to go wrong — including if you have assumed a structure my case doesn't actually have.
