I am auditing a graduate reading group in the philosophy of economics at my university, and last Thursday's session produced an argument I could not resolve and would like adjudicated. We had read two things: the chapter of Keynes's 1921 *Treatise on Probability* devoted to the weight of arguments, and his February 1937 *Quarterly Journal of Economics* article summarizing the *General Theory*, in which the following passage appears: "By 'uncertain' knowledge, let me explain, I do not mean merely to distinguish what is known for certain from what is only probable. The game of roulette is not subject, in this sense, to uncertainty; nor is the prospect of a Victory bond being drawn." ... "About these matters there is no scientific basis on which to form any calculable probability whatever. We simply do not know." ... "Nevertheless, the necessity for action and for decision compels us as practical men to do our best to overlook this awkward fact and to behave exactly as we should if we had behind us a good Benthamite calculation of a series of prospective advantages and disadvantages, each multiplied by its appropriate probability, waiting to be summed." Two of the regulars — I will call them Tobias and Ines, and I stress that I was only taking notes and have no side to defend — argued for the better part of an hour, and afterwards asked me to get an outside opinion. Here is the exchange as I recorded it:

1. Tobias: The 1937 passage shows Keynes holding that some questions have no probability at all, not even an imprecise one. The roulette sentence is the key: roulette is not uncertain not because it is low-stakes but because there is a known physical set-up and hence a calculable answer, whereas the price of copper twenty years hence has no such basis. So the uncertainty of 1937 is continuous with the *Treatise*'s claim that many probability relations are non-numerical, and it is why the Benthamite calculation cannot govern long-term investment.
2. Ines: That conflates two things Keynes kept apart. In the *Treatise*, probability is an objective logical relation between a hypothesis and the evidence proposition; when the evidence is thin the relation may be non-numerical, but there is still a definite relation, and our inability to assign a number is not the absence of a probability. "No scientific basis on which to form any calculable probability" is a remark about calculability from our evidence, not about the nonexistence of the relation. The 1937 text therefore licenses no radical thesis of fundamental uncertainty.
3. Tobias: Then weight becomes otiose. Keynes holds that adding relevant evidence can leave a conclusion's probability unchanged while increasing the weight of the argument, so weight must track something other than the relation itself — namely how much of the relevant evidence we possess. That is precisely the second-order or imprecise-probability idea, in which a wide interval means little evidence; and the limit case, the whole interval, is exactly what "we simply do not know" amounts to. A substantive doctrine, not a platitude.
4. Ines: The analogy fails at three points. First, Keynes's probabilities are only partially ordered: many pairs are simply non-comparable rather than bounded by an interval, so they are not representable by sets of numbers. Second, Keynes's probability is not a doxastic state, whereas a set of priors represents an agent's beliefs, and he explicitly denies that a proposition is probable because we think it so. Third, Keynesian weight rises monotonically with relevant evidence, whereas imprecision in the modern sense can persist no matter how much evidence you have. And on Ramsey's telling, weight was never part of the probability relation at all but a practical matter about whether to act on more or less evidence — so treating it as a dimension of uncertainty smuggles decision theory into logic.
5. Tobias: Grant the monotonicity point. Weight still does normative work: Keynes at least raises whether, other things equal, a heavier argument is preferable to a lighter one, and if the answer is yes, then choice is governed by a constraint that maximizing expected utility with a single prior cannot express. That is the content of the Benthamite sentence — his complaint is that classical theory abstracts from the fact that in the interesting cases the calculation has no determinate deliverance.
6. Ines: On your continuity reading the 1937 passage is nearly empty. Everyone agrees we lack knowledge about yields decades hence. If that is all, Keynes's objection to expected-value reasoning is an empirical point about sparse data, and the "uncertainty tradition" built on him is importing what it wants. Note also that your own roulette example cuts against you: roulette is not uncertain for Keynes because we have a great deal of relevant evidence and a numerical answer — the sentence distinguishes heavy from thin evidence, which is my reading.
7. Tobias: Keynes's conditions for applying the principle of indifference — that the field be divisible into alternatives with no known interdependence — together with his insistence that statistical methods require presuppositions of a limited-variety sort before they license equiprobability, mean that in genuinely organic situations no numerical probability arises at all, not merely no precise one. Long-run yields are organic in that sense.
8. Ines: That is still a technical claim about the conditions under which numerical probabilities obtain, which supports continuity, not a metaphysics of unknowability. And if you insist the result is interesting rather than deflationary, you owe an account of what the objective logical relations are doing: if the practical upshot is indistinguishable from ordinary Bayesian humility about sparse data, Keynes's objectivism is idle machinery.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than globally. For each of the eight moves, give a rating on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, with two or three sentences of justification keyed to the content of the move — not to its rhetoric. Then answer three questions directly: (a) is Keynesian weight a genuine dimension of uncertainty alongside probability, or does it reduce to something already expressible in a probability assignment, or to a preference about acting on more evidence? (b) does the mapping onto imprecise probability survive Ines's three objections, and if not, which of the three is fatal? (c) is Tobias's continuity reading deflationary in the way Ines claims, or does her own reading leave Keynes's objectivism unexplained in the way he claims? Also flag any move in which one of them misstates what Keynes actually says in either text, and say what the two of them together failed to bring up that would have changed the shape of the argument. Finally, commit to a verdict on who was more right overall, and say whether the residual disagreement between them is exegetical, substantive, or both.
