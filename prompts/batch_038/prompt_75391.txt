I have been working through decision theory on and off since the Sequences, and for the last few years I have run a small reading group that meets in a bookshop back room — a mix of people who read the technical material and people who will only engage with it through narrative. That asymmetry has made me dissatisfied with the stock parables we circulate. The St. Petersburg casino, the utility-monopoly tycoon, the paperclip maximizer, the genie who takes your instructions too literally: all of them assume that a prospect has a well-defined value and then quarrel about how big it is or whether you should maximize it. What I want is a story about the prior failure — a prospect whose expected value does not exist, not because the sum blows up to infinity but because the sum depends on an ordering that nothing in the world fixes. Please write a short story, roughly three thousand words, that takes this seriously as the engine of its plot rather than as an aside.

Here is the seed, which you are welcome to develop, rename, or re-set as you like. In a grain port whose whole trade runs on credit, the municipal exchange issues notes backed by a perpetual wager that the city maintains in perpetuity: a draw is made every day forever, and on day n the city owes the note-holder one-nth of a standard measure of grain if the draw comes out one way, while the holder owes the city one-nth if it comes out the other. Tallied in date order, the city's net exposure is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ···, which converges to ln 2, and that figure is written into the charter as the reserve's value. But the series converges only conditionally: tallied in a different order it can be made to converge to any number whatsoever, or to diverge. The city also markets a second instrument, structurally similar except that every term is a payment to the holder and the amounts shrink like one-nth, so its expected value is infinite while almost every finite run of it pays out a pittance; a faction wants the notes re-backed with that instead, on the grounds that a city should be solvent in the largest available sense. I want the story to turn on the moment this stops being an accountant's curiosity — a clerk, an auditor, a creditor's agent, someone low enough in the institution to be plausible — realizing that the ordering is a convention rather than a discovery, and that whoever controls the convention controls the money supply. Two things I would insist on. First, the characters should understand that rearranging a conditionally convergent series is simply a theorem, not a trick or a fraud; the real question the story should press is why the chronological ordering deserves to be privileged at all, and what a holder is owed if it does not. Second, everyone arguing for a particular resolution — fix the ordering by charter, bound the utility of grain, truncate the tail, adopt some generalized notion of a limit that restores order-independence, or declare the instrument meaningless — should be given their strongest case in their own voice, and none of them should be a fool.

After the story, please append a short note, a few hundred words at most, telling me where the fiction takes liberties and how much of what it assumes is actually agreed on. I would like you to separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested: I take it as uncontroversial that a conditionally convergent series has no order-independent sum and that its terms can be rearranged to any limit, but I do not want the story or the note to present as consensus the further claim that this refutes expected-value reasoning, nor to treat any particular repair as the accepted one, since as far as I can tell bounding utility, truncation, richer number systems or generalized limits, and ordering-relative value are all live proposals with none clearly winning. Tell me plainly which of these you regard as the majority position, which as respectable minority positions, and which as marginal, and flag any point where my own description of the setup quietly presupposes one of them — including whether a stream of draws extending forever is even a coherent object to make decisions about. I do not want a bibliography or a list of further reading; I have no way to check it and would rather you put that effort into the prose.
