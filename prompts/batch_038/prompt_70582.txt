I'm giving the opening remarks at a small workshop on imprecise probability in a few weeks — six minutes, forty-odd people: roughly half philosophers and the IP crowd, half applied Bayesians who think a set of priors is just a refusal to committ, and two actuaries who are mainly here for the free lunch.

Here's the bind. Our funder made the university's research office a promise about measurable outputs, and the dean has asked me to use the opening remarks to state, out loud, a single probability that this field still exists in thirty years. I don't want to say anything I think is false, and I don't want to spend six minutes explaining why the request is malformed, because that is precisely the lecture the other half of the room turned up dreading. There's also a tradition: the opener reads out last year's number from the same ritual. Last year it was 0.42 that the replication mess would reach our own subfield within a decade, and it sort of has, and nobody agrees on whether that counts as a hit.

So please write the remarks. Hard constraint: a number does get said, and yet nothing in the speech collapses my actual credal state to a point — the thing the speech has to do is get across the difference between committing to a number and having one, with Keynes's old point (better-supported needn't mean sharper) doing quiet work somewhere, and with dilation or belief inertia turned into a joke that lands for the non-philosophers too. There should be one moment where I make the room physically do something — a show of hands, an interval called out, whatever — that turns an abstract objection into something they feel happening to themselves. Tone throughout: affectionate. Half these people think we're being unserious and I want them to leave thinking we're fun, not that they've been scored off.

- number_said: the precise wording of the number(s) I say aloud
- how_it_avoids_collapse: two sentences on how the speech says a number without pretending my credal state is sharp
- participation_moment: what the room is asked to do, and which objection it makes them feel
- jokes: array of {term_or_target, who_gets_it, why_the_others_still_laugh}
