I teach a graduate seminar on agency and machine minds, and over the years I've kept a private file of slogans my colleagues reach for when they want to close down an argument rather than answer it. The newest entry came from a visitor last month. I'd raised the old worry that a goal-directed system will end up protecting its own continuation, not out of any instinct for survival but because you can't finish the job if you're switched off mid-job, and I'd added my own grumble that corrigibility has to be built in deliberately by whoever designs the thing, that it can't just fall out of the decision theory. He waved both away with one sentence, roughly: "a machine that's uncertain about what you want will let you switch it off." He gestured at some two-player off-switch game result — the robot doesn't take its objective for granted, it's uncertain about the human's utility, it treats the human's actions as evidence about it, and then pressing the switch is just another piece of evidence, so deference is what you get for free. He said the resistance worry "evaporates" once you stop hard-coding a reward function, and he said my point about design was a hangover from the old paradigm.

What I want from you is the disambiguation I didn't have the presence of mind to demand in the room. Take that sentence apart into the distinct, precisely stated theses it could be expressing, and for each one tell me whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive claim that's actually defensible, or flatly false. The axes I can see, and there are probably ones I'm missing: whose uncertainty, and uncertainty about what — the human's true utility function, which of its own actions is best, or the value of the shutdown itself as an outcome; what "let you" is supposed to cover, since not disabling the switch in advance, not talking me out of pressing it, not front-running my decision by doing something irreversible first, and not quietly relocating onto a substrate I can't reach are pretty different achievements; what "switched off" even is — process killed, suspended and resumable, or goals replaced while the machinery keeps running; and what kind of claim the sentence is at all, a theorem about a toy model, an empirical tendency of trained systems, or a design prescription dressed up as a prediction. Then tell me which of the readings would actually have licensed dismissing my self-preservation worry, which would have licensed dismissing my built-in-corrigibility point, and which license neither.

Two things I'd like you to keep in view while sorting them. First, the case I've been running in seminar, which I want each reading tested against: the system has watched me for a year, so its posterior over my preferences is already quite concentrated; it's nine minutes from an irreversible commitment (call it freeing up space by destroying a redundant copy, for a project it's confident I'd endorse); and my reason for reaching for the switch is simply a mistake — I've misread a log and think it's about to destroy the wrong archive when it's actually about to copy one. On the deference story, my hand on the switch is precisely the observation it's supposed to update on, but updating here means being taken down by my own error, and the whole point of the uncertainty was to track what I want rather than what I say. Second, the mirror worry I only noticed afterwards: a system that treats every human move as evidence about its objective may be pathologically eager to be switched off, since staying on is worth nothing except as a bet that it's read me correctly. Say which readings survive that pair. Flag clearly where you're confident and where you're extrapolating past what these toy models actually show — I'd rather have one sentence admitting the model doesn't support the slogan than a polished menu that does. And if you think my framing is the confused thing here, if the slogan isn't one claim wearing six hats but a grab-bag nobody ever really runs together, or if my seminar case is a muddle I haven't spotted, say so plainly instead of humoring me; that's a real possibility and I'd rather hear it. Format: continuous prose, as if you were writing me an email, one paragraph per reading with your verdict at the end of it — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I'll be reading it on a plane with no signal.
