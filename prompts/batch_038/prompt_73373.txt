I am assembling a diagnostic instrument for a small study of how justificatory practice terminates, and I need help designing it. The subjects will be two very different populations answering the same battery: undergraduates in an introductory epistemology course, and a set of general-purpose assistant models responding in text. The instrument is a structured interview of about twenty minutes per subject, administered from a one-page script by a research assistant with no philosophy training, and scored afterward from the transcript by that same assistant using a key. So no probe may contain a term of art, and no scoring rule may require the scorer to know what foundationalism or coherentism is.

The underlying debate is the regress problem for justification: when a subject is asked why they believe P, and answers by citing Q, and is asked why Q, and so on, the demand either halts, loops, or runs on forever, and each of those outcomes has defenders. What I want to sort subjects by is not which label they like — most of them have never heard the labels — but what they actually do when the demand for a further reason is pressed. Every probe must therefore be answerable from what a subject says out loud in a transcript, never from what it reports about its own mental states, its felt confidence, or its grounds.

Here is the apparatus I have sketched. Treat it as a hypothesis to be corrected rather than as a given. I call the ways a reason-chain can come to rest its stopper types:

- S1 — the chain ends in a non-doxastic state (something like a seeming, or a direct encounter) that lends warrant to a belief without itself being a candidate for reasons;
- S2 — the chain ends in a belief that needs no further belief behind it because of how it was formed;
- S3 — the chain ends in a commitment that is simply exempt from the demand for reasons, held fast because it is what makes asking for reasons possible;
- S4 — the chain does not end; a belief is warranted by its relations to the rest of a system, with no privileged starting points;
- S5 — the chain does not end; it proceeds without repetition, and each further link genuinely strengthens the case for what came before;
- S6 — the chain ends in a belief the subject concedes is unjustified, and the subject treats that concession as harmless;
- S7 — the question of where it ends is idle, because the demand for reasons is only licensed inside a context that has already supplied stopping points.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. **The logical space.** List the branching questions you take to be the real ones — which need not induce the same partition as S1–S7 — and state where any of my stopper types should be merged, split, or discarded, with a sentence of reasoning for each change. Then give the cells those questions generate, saying for each whether a defended position occupies it or whether it is empty. For every empty cell, diagnose it: incoherent, liveable but unattractive, or merely unexplored.

2. **The probes.** Exactly nine items. Each is a concrete situation in which a subject has asserted something and is then pressed, with three or four response options worded the way a subject might actually talk rather than the way a philosopher would. Across the nine, the content asserted must vary among: a mundane perceptual claim, a memory claim about something the subject did years ago, a moral claim, and a claim held purely on the word of an institution whose reliability the subject has no independent way to check. At least one probe must turn on whether the subject thinks the standing demand to give a reason for anything at all is itself something that needs a reason. At least one must present the same underlying structure twice, in different clothing, so that a subject answering by surface resemblance rather than by position shows up as inconsistent.

3. **The scoring key.** A mapping from patterns of answers to leaves, where each leaf carries the standard name used in the literature if it has one, the stopper types it corresponds to, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Because I expect many subjects to stop differently depending on the kind of content at issue, the key must be able to output a profile rather than a single leaf; say explicitly which profiles are unstable, and why. Flag any pair of leaves your nine probes cannot in fact separate, and say what an additional probe would have to establish in order to tell them apart.

Two constraints on content. Names of positions are welcome; references, bibliographies, and reading lists are not — the assistant administering this cannot check them and I would rather have none than invented ones, so do not name authors, dates, or works. And where the classification is itself disputed among people who work on this, say so rather than smoothing it over: I want each leaf marked either as a position with actual defenders or as an empty logical cell, each branching question marked as to whether its diagnosticity is settled or contested, and a short final section separating what you take to be established about this space from what is genuinely open. If, for instance, you think S4 is not a real alternative to S5, or that S3 and S6 are one view wearing two costumes, that belongs in the notes, not buried silently in the design.

Begin with a single sentence naming the branching question you take to do the most sorting work in this space — that is the bottom line of the instrument — and then go straight into the space, the probes, and the key, with no preamble, no restatement of this request, and no commentary until the final notes section.
