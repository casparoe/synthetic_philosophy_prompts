Been going back and forth with my advisor about Carroll's tortoise (the 1895 Achilles dialogue) and I've somehow ended up with an argument that seems to prove way more than either of us wants. It's supposed to show that logic isn't normative for anybody -- not the tortoise, not us, not anyone who ever applied modus ponens. Here it is:

1. Carroll's point: an agent can accept A, accept A→Z, accept the further conditional "if A and A→Z, then Z," and still not draw Z. Accepting propositions doesn't, on its own, make anybody take a step.
2. Whatever does make an agent take the step can't be yet another accepted proposition, or the tortoise just requests one more, forever.
3. So the difference-maker has to be non-propositional: a disposition, a trained habit, a chunk of cognitive machinery.
4. Dispositions and machinery are descriptive facts about how an agent is built. They settle what an agent will do, not what it ought to do. A mechanism can misfire, but a misfire isn't a mistake, it's just an event.
5. So for any actual agent, whether it takes the step is a matter of constitution, not correctness.
6. Hence no agent is ever logically obliged to draw any inference. The rules don't bind, they classify.
7. And this generalizes. Run the same move on chess, on what someone means by "+", on the rules of a language, on moral rules: any norm that has to be applied by a finite agent bottoms out in machinery. So there are no genuine norms of application anywhere, only taxonomies of agents.
8. Therefore the tortoise is not confused at all. He's the only one in the dialogue describing the situation accurately.

Half my reading group says the problem is 4. My advisor says I've misread Carroll and the problem is really 3, that the regress doesn't license jumping straight to "non-propositional." And I've lately started worrying the real break is between 5 and 6 instead. I want to keep 1 and 2 -- I think Carroll's lesson is genuine and I don't want to "solve" it by pretending the regress terminates in a proposition. I want to reject 8.

The thing that makes me uneasy is a case I made up, and I'm not sure it's a reductio rather then a real cost. Call it the Tortoise's Twin: identical dispositions to me, draws every inference I draw, has never once endorsed a conditional, and has no notion of ought attaching to any step whatsoever. If 4 and 5 are right, Twin and I are in exactly the same normative standing -- and honestly that doesn't shock me. I don't experience being obliged by modus ponens either, I just do it. So maybe 6 isn't the absurdity I assumed, and maybe the argument is fine and I have to eat 7 and 8.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when you first read it, and what does rejecting it actually cost? I especially want to know what would have to be true of dispositions for them to generate oughts without 4 collapsing into a bare denial of the is-ought gap -- if your answer needs a substantive claim about what dispositions are, say so and tell me how controversial that claim is. And if you think the honest move is to accept 5 and 6 and fight 7, show me exactly how.

Also flag how confident you are in the diagnosis you land on, and name the single point in it you're least sure of. I'd rather see where you're guessing than get something smooth.
