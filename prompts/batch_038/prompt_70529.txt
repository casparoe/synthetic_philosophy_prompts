I cover science for a magazine and every summer I get asked to run one session at a journalism school's writing workshop — 18 people, mostly first- and second-year grad students, 75 minutes, one room, no budget, so points or candy rather than real money. This year I want to build a game that makes them feel the pessimistic meta-induction from the inside instead of hearing me say the phrase.

The mechanic I've got in my head: each round they get a research finding — fictional-but-plausible, or real ones from 1900 on, your call, but please not the usual phlogiston/caloric/ether parade, they've all seen that list — and they have to commit a lede verb. "Is," "causes," "proves" at one end; "may," "suggests," "could be linked to" at the other. Then time jumps and the finding either survives, gets refined, or gets overturned, and they score. I want the scoring built so that confident verbs get punished early and hedged verbs get punished late, so no single policy works all the way through, and so that whether the pessimist is licensed ends up depending on the overturn rate in the deck — which they can literally count of their own score sheets. That's the thing I most want them walking out with: hedging by reflex is an epistemology with a track record, and the track record is an empirical question about a particular field.

So give me the rules, the deck contents (how many rounds, what kinds of findings), the point scheme, a timeline that fits 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps moments in play onto the actual argument — including the realist reply about mature theories being different, which I can gesture at but shouldn't be the one explaining. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone hedges everything and treats it as a joke), how to handle that, and be straight with me about which part of the debate a card game flat-out cannot reproduce.
