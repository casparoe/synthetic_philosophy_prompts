I'm putting together the final problem set for my upper-level seminar on practical rationality and I want one short-answer item built around Buridan's ass, but I don't want a question that can be answered by reciting the story or by listing three stock complaints about it. Here's the setup I have in mind: I show them a paragraph a student wrote last year and ask them to diagnose whats wrong with it. The paragraph goes roughly like this.

"The ass case shows that expected-utility theory is incomplete as a guide to action. Rationality requires you to maximise, but when two options are equally good it doesn't tell you which of them to take, so a maximiser who follows the theory faithfully just stands there and starves. Any complete account of rational choice therefore has to add a tie-breaking principle. Since the ass has no such principle available it is stuck: it can't rationally eat either bale, and so it eats neither."

The question should make them identify exactly where that reasoning goes wrong, not just gesture at the difference between what rationality permits and what it requires. What I'd like from you: the question as the students would actually see it on the sheet, including a tight word limit and the mark allocation (call it 20 marks, and they get about 40 minutes); a model answer; and a marking scheme that names the concrete moves that separate a 2:1 from a first, plus the wrong turns that should cost marks — students calling it a paradox, treating the starving as evidence about rationality rather than about the ass's cognitive limits, proposing a randomiser without noticing that the choice of randomiser just rebuilds the same symmetry one level up, that sort of thing.
