I am writing a chapter on coordination failure, and I want to work through the chlorofluorocarbon regime as my central case — principally because it is the one well-documented instance in which a global multipolar trap appears to have been escaped, and because I suspect the escape is what makes the case interesting and also what makes it awkward for the standard framing.

So that you need not go looking anything up, here are the facts I intend to rely on. Modelling published in 1974 suggested that inert CFCs released at ground level would eventually reach the stratosphere and catalytically destroy ozone. The United States, Canada and the Nordic states banned CFC propellants in aerosols in the late 1970s, while production for refrigeration, foam blowing and solvents continued and grew. The industry organised, funded its own atmospheric research and maintained that the hypothesis was speculative. In the mid-1980s the discovery that ozone over Antarctica was being lost far more severely and far earlier than any model had predicted changed the epistemic situation abruptly. A framework convention was agreed at Vienna in 1985, and a protocol with binding reduction targets at Montreal in 1987. DuPont — the largest producer in the world, and for a decade the intellectual leader of industry resistance — shifted position in the mid-1980s and in 1988 committed to ending CFC production altogether. A financial mechanism was added in 1990 under which industrialised parties met much of the compliance cost for developing ones. The substitutes that replaced the CFCs were ozone-safe but potent greenhouse gases, and it took until the Kigali amendment of 2016 to bring them under control. And in 2018 atmospheric measurements disclosed an unexpected rise in CFC-11 emissions, subsequently traced to unreported production in eastern China.

Please analyse this through the Moloch picture, but using two distinctions of my own rather than the usual vocabulary. The first is between payoff traps, where defection really is dominant given the true payoffs, and belief traps, where the payoffs would not sustain defection if accurately known, but each player's estimate of the world and of the other players' estimates does. The second is between traps that are dissolved, because the payoff structure genuinely changes, and traps that are merely suppressed, because defection has been made detectable and costly; my working hypothesis is that suppression decays at a rate set by the cost of detection, and that most celebrated escapes are suppressions.

Cut the episode into four phases — the period between the initial modelling and the first unilateral bans; the stalemate of the early 1980s; the five years from the Antarctic discovery to the financial mechanism; and the recent period covering Kigali and the CFC-11 disclosure. For each phase, state who the players actually are (noting that the player set changes, and that by the end it includes the compliance machinery of the protocol itself), what each one's strategy set is, whether unilateral restraint is dominated given the payoffs as they then stood, and what each player believes about the state of the world and about the others' beliefs. Then classify the phase as a payoff trap, a belief trap, or a mixture in which one is converting into the other. Where the two categories blur — as I expect they will in the stalemate, where some of the doubt was genuine and some of it was manufactured — say so explicitly, and say what the framework can and cannot do with belief that has been deliberately produced, since it ordinarily takes beliefs as given.

Then run the analysis in the other direction, and be as hard on the framework as the material allows. The trap broke without anyone's moral conversion: the largest producer defected once it had substitutes to sell and while its competitors were behind it. Does the structural picture have any place for that, or must it treat the defection as exogenous — a stroke of luck it can describe but not predict? If escape required a private firm to have already solved the technical problem, what does that imply about which traps are escapable in principle? Next: the substitutes that dissolved the ozone problem created a climate problem that took three decades to address. Is that recursion predicted by the theory, merely compatible with it, or invisible to it, and does it mean escape needs to be redefined as a transfer of the trap rather than its abolition? Next: the 2018 disclosure. Was the trap re-forming, or is this a categorically different thing — defection by a handful of producers inside a regime whose enforcement rests on self-reporting and on the goodwill of parties — and which of my two distinctions, if either, applies?

Be equally clear about where the framework falls silent rather than forcing it to speak: on radical uncertainty about the state of the world; on the asymmetry between costs concentrated in a few firms and benefits diffuse across decades and hemispheres; on the possibility of a large state acting unilaterally in the late 1970s and discovering the cost was trivial; and on the fact that the eventual agreement worked by constituting a club with trade restrictions against non-parties rather than by altering anyone's payoffs directly.

Close with a verdict. Is the ozone case a confirmation of the multipolar-trap picture, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated — and what would have to be added to the picture for it to accommodate this one? Finally, address a worry of mine about the framing itself. Structural explanations relocate agency from actors to the game, and there is a sense in which that is their whole point; but in a case where a well-resourced industry spent a decade arguing that the science was unsettled, the relocation risks doing exculpatory work it has not earned. Tell me how much of the explanation the structure can legitimately carry here, and how you would state the structural account so that it does not become an alibi.

I want the mapping and the argument, not a survey and not a reading list.
