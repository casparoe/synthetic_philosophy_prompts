In three weeks I am giving a forty-minute paper at a departmental seminar run jointly by our evolutionary biology group and our philosophy of science group, and I would like a proper question-period prep sheet rather than another defence of the view.

The thesis: in a laboratory system of experimentally evolved microbial collectives, group-level selection is not a harmless redescription of what is happening within groups but a distinct process making its own causal contribution — and the familiar move of absorbing the result into kin selection is unavailable in this system for a non-trivial reason, namely that high within-group relatedness is a downstream consequence of our selection regime rather than its mechanism.

Here is the argument as I intend to present it, so that the objections can be aimed at something concrete:

- We propagated 96 lines of a mat-forming microbe for 300 transfers. At each transfer the bottom tenth of a settling column was retained, and every new group was founded from a propagule of twenty cells drawn from a single parent group. Group-level differential propagation is therefore built into the design.
- In half the lines we periodically introduced a fast-growing variant that contributes nothing to the mat and so lowers its group's settling rate: the usual cheater.
- Price-equation partitioning with a contextual-analysis term yields, in both treatments, a substantial cross-level covariance term. In the cheater-introduction lines, within-group relatedness measured at mid-transfer falls sharply, yet the group-level variance in mat production and the cross-level term stay large.
- From this I argue two things. First, that our groups reproduce as groups — fragmentation preserves parent-group composition across the propagule stage — so this is the strong sense of multilevel selection, in which collectives themselves stand in parent–offspring relations, and not merely the weaker sense in which collectives are vehicles and only cells reproduce. Second, that because group-level variance persists when relatedness is experimentally depressed, a Hamilton's-rule reduction cannot be doing the explanatory work here, and the objection that group-selection results always dissolve into relatedness is answered by manipulation rather than by argument.

The audience: roughly a dozen empirical evolutionary biologists — behavioural ecologists, two microbial experimentalists, someone who works on cooperative breeding — formed on Williams and Dawkins, who hear the phrase group selection as a settled error; three philosophers of biology who work on the units debate, are fluent in the partition machinery and the Darwinian-individuality literature, and will want to know precisely which sense of multilevel selection I mean and whether my criteria for it are principled; one quantitative geneticist who fits mixed models and will attack the statistics rather than the metaphysics; and several graduate students, at least one of whom works on major transitions and may be on my side.

Please produce the following:

1. The objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage each would do, weighting likelihood and severity together. Eight to ten is about right.
2. For each, who raises it and how they will phrase it, including the prefatory remark they are likely to begin with — a behavioural ecologist and a philosopher of biology will not put the same complaint into the same words, and I want to recognise these in the room.
3. A reply I can deliver in about two minutes of speaking time, followed by one sentence on where that reply is softest: the follow-up that would expose it.
4. Where an objection depends on a contested reading of the formal or philosophical literature, say so, and say whether I can reframe my claim to sidestep it without surrendering anything I care about.
5. What I should concede explicitly in the first five minutes of the talk, in order to defuse the two most damaging objections before they are raised.
6. The one reply that has to land if the thesis is to survive the hour, and how I should set it up earlier in the talk so that it is available when I need it.
7. The question I am hoping nobody asks. Name it, tell me honestly how bad it is, and give me either a real answer or a dignified way of saying that I do not have one.

Do not pad any of this with references or a bibliography: I know the literature, and I cannot check a citation while standing at a lectern. Write the whole response in British English.
