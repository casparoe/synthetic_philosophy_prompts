I have been asked to draft the criteria by which a festival jury will award a single prize for artistic achievement in film, and I do not want to begin with a rubric. Before I decide what jurors are entitled to weigh, I want the specification underneath it: what any theory of the bearing of a work's ethical character on its artistic value has to deliver in order to count as adequate, where adequate here means good enough to be defended to twelve intelligent non-specialists who will disagree with one another about the morality of at least two of the films in competition. State the requirements generally — a theory that works only for narrative film is not what I am after — though film may supply your examples.

So: write the desiderata. For each, give the requirement and then the case or consideration that generates it; I want the reasoning behind the requirement, not a catalogue of labels. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any theory in this area and which turn out to be the doctrinal commitments of one camp dressed as neutral criteria. Where you think some subset cannot be jointly satisfied, say which and argue the incompatibility as tightly as you can — if that argument goes through, it is worth more to me than everything else in the answer.

Some of the data an adequate theory has to reproduce. Treat all of these as stipulated; I have constructed them to be clean.

(a) Production. A documentary in competition contains a sequence, universally singled out as its achievement, that the crew obtained by continuing to film a person in mortal danger rather than intervening. Nothing in the finished film records or rewards that decision, and no amount of attentive viewing could detect it. Does the discovery change the film's artistic value, or only our reasons for honoring it? If the former, by what mechanism, given that the film is unchanged?

(b) Later conduct. The director of an otherwise unobjectionable film is credibly accused of a serious wrong after completing it and before the festival. The film is frame for frame what it was. A theory that says only the work matters must explain why that response feels like evasion here; a theory that says the maker matters must explain why a prize should turn on a fact no viewer can perceive.

(c) Revealed intention. Years later we learn that a film long admired for its unflinching cruelty was built as a trap: the director intended to expose his audience's appetite for precisely what he showed them. The frames are identical, and the discovery changes our estimate for the better. If intentions matter here, why do they not matter in (a) — or why do they matter there too?

(d) The virtuous and the inert. A film of impeccable moral outlook: humane, sincere, competent in every craft department, and utterly inert, such that nobody who has seen it once wants to see it again or thinks of it afterward. Any theory on which moral merit contributes to artistic value must fail to deliver the verdict that this is a strong candidate for the prize.

(e) Genre relativity. A long, formally dazzling act of cruelty appears, unchanged in its particulars, in a slasher picture, in a realist family drama, and in an animated feature marketed to children. Our verdicts differ sharply. The theory must explain the difference without reducing artistic value to audience expectations.

(f) Excision. The festival also screens a print from which the sequence in (e) has been removed, and nearly everyone who sees both agrees that the shorter film is the worse film. Whatever else the theory says about that sequence, it must account for its being at once a defect and load-bearing — or explain why that intuition is confused.

(g) Complicity. A film recruits identification with a perpetrator so thoroughly that the viewer is made to want what the film simultaneously shows to be monstrous, and much of its power consists in that recruitment. Is the value located in the work or in the experience the work produces, and does a theory that answers otherwise lose anything?

Second, the distinctions. I expect a theory here to be judged partly on whether it keeps certain pairs apart: the ethical character of a work and the ethical conduct of its maker; what a work depicts and what it endorses; the responses a work prescribes and the responses it happens to cause; artistic value and aesthetic value; a local defect and a defect in the work's governing attitude; the claim that a work is defective and the claim that I have reason not to watch it; and a work's being wrong about something and a work's being wrong in the way it makes me feel. Tell me which of these are load-bearing, which are artifacts of a particular position's vocabulary, and which collapse under pressure — and where they collapse, what has to replace them.

Third, a constraint I do not know how to formulate. My jurors will disagree about the morality of the material, which is not a defect of the jury but the normal condition of criticism. I therefore need to know whether such a theory may let artistic value track the truth of a contested moral claim, or whether it must locate the defect in something that survives moral error — an attitude that fails by standards the work itself invites, say, rather than by the correct standards. Whichever way you take it, state it as a requirement and price it.

If any of (a) through (g) is not in fact a datum — if the intuition behind it is confused, or belongs to one camp's ideology rather than to critical practice — say so and strike it from the specification. I would rather have a short list of real requirements than a long list of demands a theory is entitled to refuse.

Finally, and briefly: how do the standing positions — that ethical defects are never artistic ones; that they can be, but only where they bear on a work's uptake; that a work is artistically defective whenever it prescribes responses that are ethically unmerited; and that ethical defects can themselves be a source of artistic value — fare against the list you end up with? A paragraph each, no citations, no literature review; I will not be checking your references. What I want from this section is a single verdict: which one requirement eliminates the most candidates. Leave the rubric to me, and keep the weight on the criteria, since the scoring is the part I could have guessed at.
