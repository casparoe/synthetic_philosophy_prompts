I run the citizen assembly program for a city of about 190k up here in the Great Lakes region — 38 residents picked by lottery, six Saturday sessions a year, they take up maybe 12 to 15 items and the council is legally required to respond to each one publicly within 90 days. Nothing about the process is exotic, but I'm the person who signs off on what goes on the agenda and how its framed, and my four facilitators come from social work, city planning, and high school teaching. Nobody on staff has read any political theory, including me until this winter.

Over the winter I finally slogged through Thucydides and Plato's trial dialogue, and what stuck with me wasn't the rhetoric, it was that all three famous Athenian blowups look like process failures I watch happen in my own room. Mytilene: they vote something monstrous on the strength of the day's anger, reopen it the next morning, reverse, and then literally have to race a second ship to catch the first. Sicily: the general who thinks the plan is insane tries to kill it by naming a force so enormous nobody could approve it, the assembly loves it and hands him everything, and later the letter arrives from the front and instead of bringing anybody home they double down. Socrates: the guilt vote is close, and it's the sentencing round where it goes bad, partly because of what the defendant says about himself, and there's no unringing it. And there's the fourth one, the generals after the sea battle, where they tried everybody at once against their own rules and the presiding officer who wouldn't put it to a vote turned out to be Socrates — I think that one is about procedure getting steamrolled, but you tell me.

What I want is a procedure my facilitators can actually run, not an essay about Athens. Give me a pre-vote checklist: the questions to ask about an item, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which answers are hard stops, meaning the item does not go to a vote this session at all; and which ones route the question out of the room — to the council, to a named staff person, to a delayed second session — because it's a question of values or of who eats the cost rather than a fact the assembly can settle. Budget: 25 minutes per item the week before, one facilitator, agenda packet only, no outside reading, no greek, no citations or reading list anywhere in it — if a step comes from something the Athenians did, one clause describing it is plenty, I'm not putting a bibliography in front of a social worker. Mark plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume, and say what the thing can't do at all, including where the cases just don't transfer (we're 38 people making recommendations to a council, not six thousand voting on a war, and nobody gets executed for a bad forecast — probably).

Then run the whole procedure on three live items. One: a "downtown safety initiative," cameras plus a big block of police overtime, which the assembly voted down 31 to 7 in March on cost and civil-liberties grounds; a council member wants it back on the June agenda with identical text, a new slide deck of April assault photos, and a petition with 4,000 signatures. My instinct is to call that a Mytilene and kill it, but I also know we tried a standing rule that nothing voted down can return for 12 months, and the council scrapped that rule after it blocked a genuinely improved version of a park design, so I want to know whether some narrower version survives or whether my instinct is simply wrong. Two: a riverfront plan where the only thing on the table is a $400,000 feasibility study, per the consultants, sitting in front of a 12-year, $90 million build whose phases each make the next one harder to walk away from, and nobody in the room thinks the study is controversial. Three: a resolution to censure our floodplain engineer and refer her to the state licensing board, because the model she signed said the levee system would hold a 100-year event and it didn't. It has majority support, the sponsor is close to the mayor, and the whole engineering staff has told me they resign if it passes. I expect your procedure to stop that one, and I need it strong enough that I can point at the checklist instead of at myself.
