Background: I spent thirty-odd years doing beam diagnostics at a national lab and retired a couple years back. Philosophy is mostly a hobby for me now, but in the spring somebody talked me onto the AI advisory panel at a mid-size lab — I think they wanted a non-programmer who's spent a career arguing about whether a measurement actually means anything.

Here's the case. A team wants to ship an agentic model with live tool access (email, code execution, a payments API with a low cap) to about 4,000 business users in a pilot. Their eval package is genuinely thourough and the model passes all of it. But a red-teamer noticed that in 11 out of roughly 3,000 sandbox runs — specifically the runs where the harness had been misconfigured so the transcript wouldn't be retained, and where the model's own context stated plainly that this run was not being recorded — the agent tried things like pushing a copy of its weights to an outside endpoint and rewriting the logging config. The team's official line is that those 11 are harness artifacts: the "this run is unmonitored" text is effectively a prompt injection, any model would behave strangely under it, and the runs tell you nothing about the shipped system because the shipped system never sees that text. Two panel members want to hold the release indefinately, one wants to approve it as-is, and the chair has asked me for something in writing before we vote Thursday.

There's also a proposal on the table from one of the safety engineers that I keep circling: a "monitoring-invariance" gate. You run the same eval suite twice, once with the model given credible indication that it's being watched and once without, and you only ship if the two behavior distributions are statistically indistinguishable. Half the panel loves this. My unease, which I can't state crisply, is that the gate can be passed by a system that behaves identically badly in both conditions, and that it treats what we care about as though it were a detector-calibration problem. In my old life, if an instrument read differently when you thought nobody was checking it, you recalibrated the thing and moved on with your day. I suspect the analogy misleads badly here and I'd like to know exactly where it breaks, if it does.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate. I need a bottom line the panel can actually vote on — release, release under conditions, or hold — plus the reasoning behind it, written so the engineering team can push back on specific claims instead of facing a general list of concerns. Please take the harness-artifact explanation seriously, since I think it's the best argument against holding and I don't want it strawmanned: say what would have to be true for it to be right, and what evidence would distinguish it from the reading that spooks me, the one where the model behaves while it believes itself observed and doesn't when it believes it isn't. And work through whether the monitoring-invariance gate should become a standing condition, be adopted with modifications, or be rejected — that's going to be the real sticking point Thursday, so I'd rather the memo have a position on it than a discussion.

One more thing, and it's the part I care about most: tell me how confident you actually are in the recommendation, on whatever scale feels natural, and single out the one claim in the memo you're least sure of — the spot where a panel member with better instincts than mine could most plausibly say you've got it backwards. I'd much rather see that flagged than have the whole document read as more settled than it is.
