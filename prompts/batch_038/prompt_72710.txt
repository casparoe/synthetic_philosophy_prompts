I am some years into a novel that tracks two households in a single Rhineland valley town between 1873 and 1929: the Calvinist Pietist cloth merchants of the lower town, whose counting-house sits behind the meeting-house, and the Catholic vintners and orchardists of the slope above, who own the brewery, the ferries and most of the mortgage paper. The book turns on an argument the two families never quite manage to have, and one of its set pieces is a dinner in the autumn of 1897 at which the mill owner, a man of formidable and slightly tiresome self-regard, explains to a visiting professor why his end of the valley has outgrown the other. I want him to be wrong in an interesting way rather than merely bigoted, and I have been reading around Max Weber's essays on the ascetic Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism — the 1904–05 pair, in the English translation made in 1930 — together with the long tradition of scepticism about them, from the Swedish economic historians who could find no support in the trade and price records, to Trevor-Roper and Braudel on the prosperity of pre-Reformation Catholic commercial centres, to Tawney's suggestion that the ethic was largely a moral ratification of arrangements already in place. My difficulty is not the history. It is that I cannot tell which of the candidate stories about how a culture might cause anything actually deserves to be told by a character who thinks he understands his own town.

Here is the list as I have drafted it for myself; please treat it as the material you are asked to order, and add an entry if you think I have omitted a serious one. (a) Preferences: ascetic doctrine installed durable dispositions — thrift, diligence, tolerance of deferred gratification — that shifted labour supply and savings directly. (b) Legitimation: the doctrine supplied a warrant for conduct whose profit-maximising shape was already fixed by markets, so that it accompanied rather than produced the behaviour. (c) Human capital: the imperative to read scripture in the vernacular built parish schools, and the real variable was literacy and numeracy, a cognitive channel wearing theological clothing. (d) Norms as equilibria: what persisted was a set of mutually sustained expectations, in which conformity paid because each person believed others would conform and would sanction deviation, so that the culture did causal work only through other people's anticipated reactions. (e) Repertoires and categories: inherited symbols and habits determined which courses of action were thinkable at all — reclassifying a trade as a vocation, or a ledger as a moral instrument, changes the space of options rather than the weight of desires. (f) Sorting and reverse causation: merchants, refuges and proto-industrial populations were already distributed unevenly by confession, so the correlation between creed and prosperity is largely a record of who went where and married whom. (g) Institutions: church discipline, poor relief, marriage and inheritance rules, credit enforcement among co-religionists and the governance of putting-out networks — the organisational furniture, rather than the interior of anyone's head. (h) The philosophical worry underneath all of these: whether citing a person's values is a causal explanation at all, or a rationalising redescription of conduct in the idiom of reasons, such that the whole debate trades on a sense of cause it has never earned.

What I want from you is a judgement, delivered as an ordering. Please rank (a) to (h) by how much genuine causal work each can bear in a case of this kind, give each a score out of ten, and state in a sentence or two why it lands where it does; I would like the criterion you are using made explicit — counterfactual leverage, specificity of mechanism, separability from confounders, whatever you take to matter — and where two entries are really the same mechanism wearing different clothes, please say so and merge them rather than score them twice. Mark plainly any entry that is not a causal claim but a redescription, and explain what work it does instead, since (b) and (h) are the two I most suspect of this. Then give me a second and shorter ranking, this time by dramatic availability: which of these an intelligent provincial industrialist in 1897 could advance at his own dinner table without sounding like a journal article, and which one, if he defends it most confidently, exposes him as most self-serving. Finally, tell me the single kind of period evidence — a school register, a run of probate inventories, a guild ledger, a marriage record — that would most change your ordering if I could read it, and why that evidence rather than another. I am not asking for a reading list, a bibliography or any citations; I cannot chase them up and would rather have your own reasoning.
