I've been chewing on the lying-vs-misleading thing for maybe fifteen years now — first hit it in a comment thread somewhere and never really recovered — and I want to see an actual argument about it rather than a both-sides survey.

Write me an essay on whether there's a real moral difference between lying and misleading: between asserting something you believe to be false, and saying something literally true while steering your listener into a belief you know is false. Take a position and defend it. A conditional verdict is fine ("the distinction carries weight only when X") but you have to commit to what X is, not gesture at it.

Here's the wrinkle I want you to actually wrestle with, because I think it breaks most of the stock accounts. Call it armored misleading. The speaker says something literally true, intends and expects the false inference, and also hands over the exact information that would defeat that inference — just in a form where uptake is guarenteed not to happen. Nothing is withheld, there's no secret the listener could have dug up, and yet my intuition says this sits much closer to lying than to innocent misleading does, and it sits there pretty firmly. Examples of the shape I mean:

- A bank CEO asked on live TV whether his institution is exposed to a failing counterparty, who says the exposures are described in the risk factors section of the annual report — true, and the relevant paragraph is in there, phrased so that only a forensic accountant could decode it.
- A surgical consent form that does list the mortality risk, in 6-point gray-on-gray type on page 31 of a 40-page document, with the patient having been handed it four minutes before being rolled back.
- A company that says it has never had a data breach, where the definition of "breach" it's relying on is disclosed in a footnote to the terms of service.

So what's doing the work in those cases? Is it the speaker's beliefs and intentions, in which case armored misleading just is a variety of lying and the literal-truth part is doing zero moral work? Is it something about what the listener is entitled to assume — like, that the speaker made the relevant information processible and not merely available? Or is the deflationary answer right, that our lying/misleading vocabulary was never tracking truth and falsity but something more like ease-of-deception, and once you notice that the whole distinction is way less load-bearing than it feels?

I also want you to steelman and then adjudicate the obvious pushback: that demanding more than literal truth just relocates the burden onto listeners being less gullible, and that a norm obliging you to make your true statements easy to correctly interpret is unworkably demanding and effectively hands the inattentive a veto on speech. Does that argument go through, or does it prove too much? If it proves too much, say where the line goes and what principled reason you have for putting it there.

Two more things. First, no survey mode — I don't want three positions described and a shrug. Argue, and tell me what you'd give up if it turned out wrong. Second, end by drawing out what your verdict implies for one of these: the US perjury doctrine that a literally true but unresponsive answer isn't perjury even when it's meant to mislead; informed consent as it's actually practiced in medicine; or how we should evaluate AI systems that produce technically-accurate outputs in a context where users will reliably misread them. Pick one and be concrete about what your view would change.

No bibliography, no reading list, no name-dropping — I want your reasoning and I want to be able to tell whether you actually have a view.
