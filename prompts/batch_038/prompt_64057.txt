I have a draft argument for a paper on Mill and free speech whose conclusion I am confident is false, and I would like help locating where the excess enters. To keep the discussion focused, stipulate that the cases in view are not the incendiary ones: I have set aside the sort of address delivered to an excited mob gathered outside a corn dealer's house that Mill himself treats as instigation rather than discussion, and likewise lies, threats, fraud, and breach of contract. What I have in mind are truthful statements about identifiable people, lawfully obtained and published without malice — an accurate account of a schoolteacher's psychiatric admission twenty years earlier; a compiled list of local residents who were arrested and never charged — where the publication cost the subjects their employment and, by the evidence available to the hypothetical regulator, their health.

(1) On Mill's principle, interference with a person's liberty is legitimate just in case it is necessary to prevent harm to others.
(2) Speech is an action like any other: something a person does in the world, with effects in the world; nothing about its being articulate removes it from the ordinary causal order.
(3) The publications described cause harm to others — measurable physiological stress in the subjects, loss of livelihood and standing, and an increased incidence of harassment and violence against members of the group to which a subject belongs.
(4) A harm does not become less real for being distributed. A practice that injures ten thousand people slightly has injured ten thousand people; this is already how we reason about secondhand smoke, effluent, and noise.
(5) Therefore the principle in (1) legitimizes restricting such publications wherever restriction is necessary to prevent that aggregate harm.
(6) But for almost any utterance there is someone whom it sets back: if not the subject of a true disclosure, then the person deprived of information, the person whose error is corrected, the audience whose settled expectations are upset. Utterances that set back no one's interests are a limiting case with no bearing on policy.
(7) Therefore the harm principle legitimizes restricting essentially all speech, and Mill's defense of free discussion, read through his own criterion, protects nothing in particular.

I do not accept (7). I also doubt that the argument simply begs the question at some obvious point, since each step looks like something a careful Millian would be embarrassed to deny outright. The steps I am least willing to lose are (2) and (4): if speech is a special category exempt from ordinary causal accounting, I cannot see how to say what makes it special without smuggling in the conclusion I want to reach, and a refusal to aggregate small harms strikes me as the most arbitrary move available in practical ethics.

Which step should be rejected, and why did it look acceptable on a first reading? Where the diagnosis is that a premise trades on two senses of a term — harm, setback, necessity, legitimacy, or whatever else — say which two senses and at which step the trade happens. Then price the repairs. For each premise I might give up instead, including (2) and (4), state what the Millian loses by abandoning it and what other parts of the account — the argument from truth, the argument from character and individuality, the worry about the tyranny of the majority — come loose along with it. Finally, tell me whether the argument is invalid, or valid but unsound, and if unsound, which single false premise it most needs.
