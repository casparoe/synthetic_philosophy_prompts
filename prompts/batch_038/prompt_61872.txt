I have been commissioned to write a poem for the opening evening of an exhibition on the history of money. It will be read aloud in the gallery devoted to paper and electronic currency, to an audience of museum visitors, economists, and a delegation from the payments industry who have views of their own. Our central bank is piloting a retail digital currency; alongside it, a privately issued token pegged to a major currency has become very widely held and — this is the point of the room it sits in — very widely unspent. The curators want a poem that argues about that situation without preaching, and the form I have settled on is a contemporary querist, after Berkeley's *The Querist*, first published in 1735, which pressed the case for an Irish national bank and a paper currency in numbered queries and almost never in declarative sentences.

Please write the poem, to these requirements.

**Form.** Eighteen to twenty-four numbered queries in iambic pentameter couplets, one query per couplet, with the numbers printed as Berkeley printed them, so that the thing on the page looks like an instrument. It should run about three minutes read aloud. Permit yourself exactly one slant rhyme and place it at the pivot described below, so that the couplet's habitual smoothness breaks at the moment the argument turns from what money is to what an issuer ought to do. No line may be declarative: every line sits inside a question, including questions that carry further questions within them, a device Berkeley used constantly — use at least two of those double queries, and in each let the second half complicate or reverse the first.

**Method.** Berkeley's argument is carried by the reader's own assent. At least one run of six consecutive queries must therefore be a strict chain: given an honest answer to each, the next admits only one reply, so that a reader who keeps answering arrives at the functionalist thesis — that money is a ticket or counter whose entire worth lies in setting people to work — without the poem ever stating it. For the ear rather than for borrowing, here are three queries from Part I:

> Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?
>
> Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating?
>
> Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?

**Content that must survive the transposition**, rendered in modern materials: that money is not wealth, since wealth comes from labour upon the elements and money only moves it; that a token passes current by public authority and general consent rather than by what it is made of; and, as the query that must do the work on the hoarded token, the point about velocity — that less money swiftly circulating is equivalent to more money slowly circulating. Let one or two queries carry, unnamed and unexplained, the resonance with Berkeley's account of signs in the seventh dialogue of *Alciphron*: a mark that signifies without resembling anything it signifies.

**Complications.** Two queries must be ones where the honest answer is no, so the sequence does not harden into a catechism. One query must be aimed squarely at the payments delegation and must concede something real to them: Berkeley held that an arbitrary change in the denomination of coin was a public cheat, and an issuer able to create tokens without limit and adjust their number is not obviously free of that charge. Leave that tension standing.

**The ending.** Berkeley's account has no room for value at rest. Money is what it does in motion; a token that is perfectly sound, perfectly held, and never spent has on his own principles ceased to be money, and yet it still commands property. The final query must land there and stop — unanswerable from inside the theory that produced it, and delivered as a question, so that the poem ends where Berkeley's does not.

**Prohibitions.** No pastiche diction: the syntax may be Berkeleyan, the vocabulary must be ours — no *doth*, no *stirreth*. Do not use Berkeley's own period illustrations of the anti-bullionist point, which cannot be separated from colonial contempt, and do not imitate his remarks on the Irish poor. No footnotes, no list of sources, and no further quotation of Berkeley. If you want an epigraph, invent one from the pilot scheme's own enabling instrument, after the manner of the verse Berkeley set at the head of the *Querist*; it may be the only declarative text in the piece, but it must not state the thesis.

Success looks like this: a historian of economic thought recognises the argument as Berkeley's, transposed; a listener who has never heard of him hears a poem with a clear arc and a hinge; and no one in the gallery can point to a single line that told them what to think.
