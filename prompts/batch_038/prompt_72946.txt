I am a junior, and every other Saturday I work on a trail crew at a nature preserve about twenty minutes from my house. At the trailhead there is a laminated card listing the seven Leave No Trace principles, and one of them is "Leave What You Find." The card explains what it means: photograph historical artifacts rather than touching them, put back the rocks and plants you pick up, do not build structures or dig trenches, and try not to carry non-native species in on your boot soles. Our faculty sponsor, who coaches the debate team and runs the small AI-and-ethics reading group I belong to, put that four-word principle at the top of last month's handout as, he said, the shortest available statement of the low-impact ideal — an advanced system should be built to leave the world as it found it. His use of it was not merely decorative. He argued that we need nothing resembling a complete theory of value in order to constrain a powerful agent, because "leave what you find" supposedly picks out a determinate class of changes the agent must refrain from without our ever having to settle what is good, and that this is why the low-impact program is more tractable than people assume.

One of the eleventh-graders in the group objected that the principle is either a platitude about hiking or a way of smuggling in exactly the value judgments it was supposed to spare us, and the three of us went in circles for a month. Meanwhile I built a toy model to test it: a gridworld in Python, ten by ten, with a robot that can push crates, open and shut doors, water a potted plant, and break a window. As my measure of impact I counted how many outcomes from a fixed list were still reachable twenty moves later than would have been reachable had the robot never been placed in the grid at all. The results unsettled me in ways I have not been able to articulate. A robot that opens a door and closes it again scores as having done nothing, which seems right; but a robot that leaves a door standing open so that a person can walk through scores a large impact even though nothing at all has been lost. A robot that carries a crate across the grid and carries it back scores zero, while a robot that leaves the crate in a different corner — a corner that is, so far as I can tell, equally usable for every purpose I can think of — is penalized heavily. And a robot that breaks one window and repairs a different one scores zero on my measure, which is precisely what "leave what you find" seems to forbid, since the window you found is still broken.

So I would like to know what the slogan is actually claiming when it is moved from a trail card to a design requirement. Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that "Leave What You Find" could be expressing in that new context, and for each one say what exactly it claims, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it would genuinely support my sponsor's conclusion that low impact can be specified without a specification of value? Please say as well, for each reading, whether my gridworld results count against it or are simply artifacts of the metric I chose, and identify which reading each of the three of us was most likely invoking when we talked past one another — including the possibility that the objector and my sponsor are not disagreeing at all but answering different questions under one phrase. If you conclude that no reading makes the slogan straightforwardly false, or that some reading I have not thought of does all the work, say that instead of fitting your answer to my expectations. I am writing this up for our school's spring research symposium and would rather present an honest analysis than a confident one, so please also tell me which of your verdicts you hold least securely, what argument or counterexample would lead you to revise it, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to break down. If you believe I have mischaracterized the disagreement in our group, or that my measure tracks something other than what we think it tracks, I would rather hear that plainly. I am not looking for a reading list or references, only the disambiguation and your assessment of it.
