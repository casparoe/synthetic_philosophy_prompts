I am the executive director of a small home-care nonprofit — about forty aides, clients across two counties, mostly Medicaid waiver-funded — and I co-chair our case-review panel with our director of nursing, who is the only person on the board with serious training in moral philosophy. She came to ethics late, after twenty years at the bedside, and reads Gilligan, Noddings, Held, and Tronto closely enough to argue with all of them; I came to it from economics and a utilitarian temperament, and I am routinely surprised by her votes. Next month the panel has to allocate one state-funded waiver slot, and I want to predict her position not by guessing at her mood but by working out which principle her record actually supports. I don't know the care-ethics literature well enough to name the candidate principles myself, and naming them is part of what I'm asking for: tell me which rules are consistent with her track record, which strand of care ethics each one comes from, which she is most likely running, and where my reconstruction of her reasoning may itself be distorted — I hear her arguments through utilitarian ears, so it's possible I've recorded some of these votes in a form that makes them look more principled, or more inconsistent, than they were.

Her votes over the past fourteen months, as best I can reconstruct them. P1: a consultant proposed reassigning aides by an algorithm that minimized total drive time, which would have saved roughly eight percent of paid hours but reshuffled about a third of our long-standing aide–client pairings; she voted it down and carried the board, saying that the hours we save are not the thing we are actually providing. P2: she led the vote moving money out of a heavily subscribed Wednesday social club for relatively independent clients and into additional personal-care hours for three bedbound clients with no family. P3: a daughter asked us to support a guardianship petition over her mother, who had early-stage dementia, wanted to stay in her own flat, and was managing with supports while accepting some risk; she refused and I recorded a dissent. P4: she backed a two-aide rule for all intimate personal care, which cost us about sixty thousand dollars a year and complicated scheduling, on the ground that aides working alone in hard placements were the ones quitting. P5: a donor offered a substantial gift for six new care packages conditional on clients appearing in our annual report and in a short promotional film with signed releases; she voted no, would not trade on the media element, and spent two months hunting an alternative funder. P6: where a family member was willing and able to be paid as the caregiver, she supported paying them out of our budget even in a case where I thought a professional aide was meaningfully better qualified at transfers and medication support. P7: during the winter staffing shortage we had to triage ten hours a week; she ranked clients by whether they could manage toileting and transfers unaided above clients whose main need was medication prompting, including one whose clinical risk score was higher but whose neighbour could be trained to prompt. P8: the county to our north asked us to extend our catchment, where assessed need is greater and no other provider will go; she voted against, arguing that our care works because of what we know locally and because our aides live there, and that a thin long-distance service would be worse care for existing clients and worse care for new ones. P9, which I include for completeness and cannot interpret at all: she also insisted we replace paper timesheets with an app, saying only that the aides hated the paper.

The new case is this. One waiver slot opens and we have two candidates. Candidate A is a 79-year-old man in our own county, moderately frail, living with the daughter who is his principal carer and who is close to breakdown herself while holding two jobs; his needs are currently manageable but escalating, one of our aides did respite with him eighteen months ago and he remembers her by name, and serving him costs ordinary travel time. Candidate B is a 34-year-old woman with advanced motor neurone disease in the northern county from P8, living with a full-time-employed partner and two teenage children, with no professional support of any kind; the only other provider there has an eighteen-month waiting list and has already declined her twice. Serving B means a ninety-minute drive each way for the aide, which roughly halves the care hours the slot actually buys, and on current trajectory she will lose usable speech within months, so the aide's ability to read her needs and to receive any response will progressively fall away. I lean toward B on need grounds and expect her to resist me, but I am not certain, and I am not certain which of her principles does the resisting.

So: identify at least four candidate principles that her record supports, including at least two that diverge on this slot, and for each say which precedents it explains cleanly, which it strains or leaves unexplained, and what it implies about A and B. Then give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasoning you'd defend, identify the single precedent in my list that does the most work in constraining the answer, and name the hypothetical future case that would best discriminate between the two leading candidate principles. Finally, tell me what I would have to offer her — in her own terms, not mine — if I wanted to move her to B, and be honest if the answer is that nothing short of a different theory would.

Please be explicit throughout about what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature. I understand that care ethicists disagree among themselves about whether their commitments yield distributive principles at all, about how far obligations extend beyond relationships one can actually sustain, about whether partiality toward intimates is the point or a liability, and about whether care ethics is a rival to impartialist theories or a corrective to them; do not present a contested reading as though it were the received view, and flag any place where a principle you attribute to a strand of care ethics is your own construction rather than a position that strand actually holds or has actually been criticized for. Also tell me where you are least sure, and whether P6 and P7 can really be squared with each other or whether one of them is probably noise. I'm reading this offline and cannot check references, so I'd rather you reason than cite: no bibliography, no list of works to consult.

Format: write the analysis in prose, then end with a single JSON object summarizing it, using exactly this schema — candidate_rules (array; each element has name, provenance in the care-ethics literature, statement of the principle in one sentence, explained_precedents as an array of the P-labels it covers, strained_precedents as an array of objects with id and why, verdict_on_new_case as "A", "B", or "reframes the choice", and reasoning in two to four sentences); best_guess (object with verdict, rule_she_is_running, probability as a number between 0 and 1, what_she_will_argue_in_the_meeting in two or three sentences in her register, and how_to_move_her_to_B, or the string "not movable within her own principles" if that is your judgment); most_constraining_precedent (object with id and why); discriminating_future_case (object with description and predictions, the latter mapping each leading rule's name to "A" or "B"); settled_vs_contested (array of objects with claim, status drawn from "settled", "contested among care ethicists", "contested in ethics generally", and note); and where_you_are_least_sure (string).
