You are to act as a professional philosopher whose specialty is decision-making under moral uncertainty, and who has also spent years coaching industry engineers through hostile interdisciplinary Q&A sessions. I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. Please think carefully and work in the two stages specified at the end of this prompt; do not skip the first one.

**The situation.** I have been invited to give a 25-minute talk with 15 minutes of questions at a university workshop on AI and ethics, co-hosted by the philosophy department and an ML lab. I am the only speaker on the program without an academic background in either field.

**The thesis I am defending.** I call the proposal a *normative ensemble*; I have also been calling its components *modes*, and the reduction of the space of moral theories to a small implementable set *normative quantization*. I expect to be attacked on all three names. The design:

1. A deployed system carries a fixed, small set of normative modes. Currently four: a constraint-based mode that returns a permissibility judgment rather than a score; a scalar outcome-based mode that returns a number; an acceptability mode that returns, for each option, whether the affected parties could reasonably reject it; and a particularist mode that scores options against prototypes of relationships and character without appealing to any general principle. I chose these four because they were legible to implement, not because I can argue they are the right four.
2. Each mode ranks the available options in its own units. Before aggregation, each mode's output is normalized by its own spread across the option set, so that no mode dominates the aggregate merely by using larger numbers or a steeper scale.
3. Aggregation is by expected choice-worthiness under a weight distribution over the modes — except that any mode returning "impermissible" for an option exercises a hard veto over it, whatever the weights say.
4. If normalized disagreement across the modes exceeds a threshold I call the *mode temperature*, the system does not act; it escalates to a human along with a structured account of which modes disagreed and over what.
5. The weights are updated from structured human feedback, subject to two constraints: no single mode's weight may exceed a cap, and realized behavior must stay inside what I call a *moral regret envelope* — a pre-specified bound on how much choice-worthiness the system is permitted to lose, relative to each individual mode, across a window of decisions.
6. The load-bearing claim, which is weaker than it looks: I am *not* claiming the ensemble makes better decisions than a single well-specified value function. I claim that even if exactly one moral theory is true and we cannot identify it, carrying the disagreement explicitly buys three things a consolidated objective cannot — auditability (a reviewer can see which considerations were in play and which lost), graceful degradation (one mis-specified component cannot silently poison the whole objective), and a principled place to hand control back to a human.

**Where I already know I am soft:** I have no criterion for individuating modes; the normalization in (2) is borrowed from statistics and I cannot defend it philosophically; (3) may be flatly incoherent, since a veto is a lexical priority claim and I do not know how to combine that with a weighted sum; (5) looks like it collapses back into the single reward model I am arguing against; and (6) is instrumental, so a philosopher may say I have made a point about software architecture rather than about morality.

**The audience** (please calibrate hard to this; the identical proposal draws completely different questions from these groups). About sixty people:

- Roughly 60% academic moral philosophers and metaethicists. Two of them work directly on decision-making under normative uncertainty and are the ones I most fear. One accepts the expected-choice-worthiness framework but holds that intertheoretic comparisons of value are far harder than my step (2) assumes, and doubts any of this survives contact with actual decisions. The other thinks moral theories are not the sort of thing that can be individuated, counted, and weighted at all, leans particularist, and will treat my four modes as caricatures that distort whatever they were drawn from.
- One moral realist who will press on coherence: if there is a true theory, is an ensemble not a way of building known error into the system deliberately? Someone adjacent will ask whether my proposal secretly presupposes anti-realism or relativism.
- About 25% ML researchers working on reward modeling, multi-objective optimization, and learning from human feedback. Expect: "this is a mixture of reward models with a constraint layer and an abstention rule — where is the philosophical content beyond that?" and "your normalization is scale-dependent and gameable; modes will inflate their spread, and the ensemble will learn to route around the veto."
- One person from an AI governance center who cares about auditability and liability rather than metaethics: when the modes disagree, what goes in the incident report, and who is accountable? Non-determinism is a regulatory liability, not a feature.
- One clinical bioethicist who will observe that escalation latency is not free, and that a permissibility veto in a hospital will refuse routine and necessary care.
- Two or three political philosophy graduate students who will ask whose morality became one of the four modes, and whether I have simply hardcoded a canonical Western liberal set and given it an engineering veneer.

**Stage 1 — brainstorm, and show me the work.** Before writing any prep sheet, generate twelve to fifteen candidate objections. (e) whether the objection targets the design, the philosophy, or my standing to be discussing either.

Then, separately:

- The single question I most hope nobody asks, with the honest answer to it, including the least damaging way to say that I have no answer if I do not.
- The one reply that has to land for the talk to count as a success: one sentence, and why the rest of it depends on that.
- What I should concede unprompted in my first five minutes, so the philosophers stop waiting for me to notice the problems on my own.
- A short glossary of the terms this audience will use that I am likely to misuse or fail to recognize on contact. Cover at minimum: incommensurability, lexical priority, side constraint, deontic, scalar consequentialism, particularism, aggregation, moral residue, fanaticism, and "normative" as they use it versus how I use it. For each, one plain sentence on what it means and one sentence on the mistake an engineer typically makes with it.
- Three sentences I can use to admit ignorance without losing the room.

**Constraints on your answer.** No citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no attributions to named philosophers, papers, or books — if you need to refer to a position, describe it rather than naming its holders.
