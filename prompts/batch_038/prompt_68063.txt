I TA a second-year logic course and for the week on naive set theory I want to hand out something better than the usual "here's the paradox, here's separation, next topic." The idea is a self-sorting quiz — call it "which repair of naive set theory do you actually hold?" — where students answer a bunch of questions about specific collections and the pattern of answers tells them which theory they've been silently committed to the whole time. Most of them walk in thinking there's exactly one paradox and exactly one fix, and that you just can't have a set of all sets, duh; a couple have heard the word "proper class" but couldn't tell you what it commits them to, and basically nobody has heard of NF or of anyone who thinks the universal set exists. I've got 40 minutes and half the room has no set theory beyond first year, so it needs to be doable without any machinery.

Here's the design I want. Eight to ten questions, each one about a concrete collection or a concrete claim about the language, never about labels — nothing along the lines of "do you prefer Zermelo-Fraenkel or Quine," and nothing that just asks "do you believe in sets?" Two to four answer options each. I'd like the questions to include: R itself; the collection of all self-membered things (I'm told ZF's verdict on that one is that it exists and is just the empty set, which is a lovely trap, and should be the question that separates the students who've actually absorbed foundation from the ones who've only heard the word); the universal set; the collection of all singletons; the collection of all ordinals; and one question where naive comprehension, ZF and NF really do give three different verdicts. I think the set of all singletons does that job — NF happily has it, and the map sending a thing to its singleton is a bijection onto it, so Cantor's theorem goes wrong for V there, while ZF calls it a proper class — but check me on that rather than taking my word for it. I also want one question about whether "R ∈ R" even has a truth value, with an option for true-and-false and an option for doesn't-say-anything, and one about statability: can your restriction on comprehension be written down as an axiom in the very language it's restricting, or is it more like an informal ban on certain sentences? I gather Russell's own arrangement in the Principia was the second kind, and that this is part of why the ramified version lost out, but again phrase it however you want as long as the question doesn't require knowing that history to answer.

Then the scoring key. Leaves should be: the ZF-style iterative conception, NBG/class theories, simple type theory, NF and stratification, a paraconsistent or dialetheist reading that keeps naive comprehension, the view that there is no fixed universe of all sets at all (the open-ended/indefinitely-extensible answer, whatever you want to call it), and one deflationary leaf for people who say there are no sets and the whole talk is a useful fiction. For each leaf, the signature answer pattern plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then tell me which combinations of answers no live theory occupies, and for each of those whether it's empty because it's provably inconsistent — and if so which two answers collide and why — because it's coherent but unattractive, or because nobody has looked there. Finally flag any pair of leaves the quiz can't actually separate; I suspect ZF and NBG will come out identical on every question about sets, and possibly the iterative conception and the no-fixed-universe view too. If that's right, say what extra question would separate them, or say plainly that nothing at this level does. Close with a short paragraph I can read aloud to the class about what the instrument is supposed to demonstrate, which as I see it is that this isn't one problem with one solution but a menu of different things you can give up, and the interesting question is which one you'd miss most.
