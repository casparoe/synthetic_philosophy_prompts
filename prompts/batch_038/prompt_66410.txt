Longtime reader, first real question — and I want a case analysis, not a tour of the literature or a summary of what Lewis says. I've read both.

The episode: the run of work from about 1988 to 1992 in which Thorne's Caltech group turned traversable wormholes into time machines. Facts, so you don't have to guess at them: the wormhole program got started as a favor to a novelist who needed a plausible fast way to cross the galaxy; Morris, Thorne and Yurtsever then showed that once you have a traversable wormhole you can convert it into a time machine by taking one mouth on a high-speed round trip (or putting the two mouths at different gravitational potentials), which creates closed timelike curves — at the price of matter that violates the averaged null energy condition; Hawking answered with the chronology protection conjecture, roughly that vacuum fluctuations diverge as the CTC horizon is about to form, so the machine never quite gets built. And separately, Echeverria, Klinkhammer and Thorne ran the billiard-ball calculation: a ball fired into a wormhole that deposits it a few seconds earlier, aimed so that its older self can collide with its younger self and prevent the launch. Their headline result is the one I care about. For the 'dangerous' initial trajectories — the ones where the naive collision-free motion would send the ball into itself — they did not find zero self-consistent solutions. They found infinitely many. The Cauchy problem comes out ill-posed in an unexpected way: not no solutions, but far too many. Two of the infinity are slight perturbations of the naive motion (a glancing self-blow), the rest are wildly different from it, and a later paper claimed that going quantum restores uniqueness at the level of probabilities even though it doesn't at the level of trajectories.

The lens: Lewis's 1976 diagnosis of the paradoxes of time travel — that the grandfather paradox is a pseudo-problem produced by equivocating on ability. Tim can shoot Grandfather, considered against his skills, weapon and opportunity; Tim cannot shoot Grandfather, considered against the full set of facts about the actual world, including the fact that Grandfather lived to father Tim's parent. Two legitimate but different senses of can, no contradiction, and the surviving sense is an ordinary local inability rather than anything metaphysically exotic.

So: map the episode onto that account, move by move, and then tell me where the account has nothing to say. Specifically:

1. Lewis's constraint runs through settled facts about the world. In the billiard-ball case the past is not settled by the initial data — it's settled by which of infinitely many consistent solutions obtains. Does Lewis's explanation of the time traveler's inability have any determinate content under those conditions, or does it just get a blank to fill in? Am I mangling him here, and if so where?

2. Does the infinite-multiplicity result vindicate the self-consistency principle or embarrass it? It seems to have delivered on no-contradictions and blown up the laws-pick-out-what-happens. Also: the glancing-blow solutions look conspiratorial in a way the paradox never was — is that a new problem or the old fine-tuning worry in costume?

3. Is Hawking's conjecture a rival answer to the paradox or a refusal of the question? And since the entire episode rests on hypothetical exotic matter, was it ever an episode that could adjudicate anything — or a thought experiment wearing a lab coat? Be blunt about this one.

I've been calling the cost of moving from the second to the third the multiplicity tax. Is that a real trilemma, or a category confusion dressed up as a taxonomy?
