I TA an upper-level seminar cross-listed between philosophy of mind and cog sci — it's basically about levels of explanation and causation — and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes closed book, worth 25 marks, built around Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment.

For anyone who hasn't read it, the setup: he asks you to imagine a frictionless pool-table-like chamber full of countless tiny magnetic marbles he calls simms, which careen around and occasionally clump into much bigger, much slower spherical clusters he calls simmballs; a simmball is constantly gaining and losing simms at its edges. Zoom out far enough in both space and time and the simms blur into a gray background, so all you can see is simmballs jostling, merging, splitting. Then he asks the question the whole thing exists to pose: who shoves whom around in there? At the simm level there's just magnetism and momentum, but from the zoomed-out level it looks like there's a logic to events, like the simmballs — which are supposed to stand for symbols — are doing the pushing. I'm pretty sure it's in GEB first and he revisits it in I Am a Strange Loop, but if you're not certain just say "Hofstadter" in the question text; the attribution isn't the point. The question has to hand students a compact self-contained version of that description so nobody is penalized for not having read either book.

What I don't want is something answerable by reciting the standard line (higher levels are real, downward causation, ideas cause ideas). I want students to have to earn a criterion. So build in some way of forcing the issue — for example a pair of variants on the careenium, one where clusters form and dissolve with no downstream effect on collision statistics and one where certain clusters, once formed, bias later trajectories and catalyze copies of themselves, and then ask in which of the two it's legitimate to say the simmballs push the simms and what makes the difference. Invent your own discriminator if you have a better one. What I want pulled out of them is the recognition that there are two distinct ways the macro claim can come out empty: either the micro story already exhausts the causation so the simmball talk is at best a redescription, or the level is something we impose by choosing where to zoom, so the logic is imported by the observer rather than found in the chamber. A good answer should also have something to say about what would have to be true of a system for a symbol-level description to be doing real work rather than being a convenient fiction, and about who supplies the mapping that makes a cluster a symbol of anything at all.

Deliverables: the exam question exactly as students will see it, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme totalling 25 with named must-hit points and the marks each is worth, plus a line or two on the mistakes that should cost marks. The ones I expect are treating supervenience as if it settled causal impotence, invoking multiple realizability like a magic word, and declaring the whole business a category mistake and going home.

And please make the model answer commit. I dont want it laying out the redescription view and the downward-causation view as equally respectable and then shrugging; it should pick a verdict on whether the simmballs do any pushing and argue for it. Same for the marking scheme — if you think either verdict can earn 25, spell out exactly what each route has to contain rather than writing credit either side.
