Our jurisprudence reading group has been playing Nomic on Thursday nights since October — you know the one, Hofstadter's game where amending the rules is itself a move, and winning is largely a matter of not getting ruled out of the game before everyone else. There's six of us, we've accumulated about forty rules, and weve got two that matter here. Rule 204 says: any rule in this game may be amended, repealed or replaced by a two-thirs majority, including this rule. Rule 301 — which we added in January, in a move passed under the old 204, which was itself a replacement of the founding clause we agreed at our very first session — says: a change to the rules counts as a legal move only if it is authorised by a rule in force at the moment the change takes effect. 301 is our Hart rule. I argued for it and I still think it's right, because I like the idea that the validity of a rule isn't a brute fact about it but a matter of where it came from.

Last week Priya moved to amend 204, and I said the move was void, and then I wrote my reason down properly and found I couldn't stop it. Here it is.

(1) By 301, the move is a legal move only if authorised by a rule in force when it takes effect.
(2) The only rule that could authorise an amendment to 204 is 204 itself: nothing else in the game confers power over it, and 301 is a condition on validity rather than a source of power.
(3) If the move succeeds, then from the moment it takes effect 204 is no longer in force — that's just what amending a rule consists in.
(4) So at the moment the amendment takes effect, the only rule authorising it isn't in force.
(5) So by 301 the move isn't a legal move, and the amendment of 204 is void.
(6) Nothing in (1)–(5) mentions our game except by name, so: no rule of change anywhere can ever amend itself. Self-amendment is impossible.
(7) But (6) leaves 204 permanently unamendable, and an unamendable rule is an entrenched one — and entrenchment is itself a change in what may be done to the rule.
(8) Either that change is brought about by an amendment, which (6) rules out, or by nothing at all, in which case a change to the rules has happened without a legal move, which 301 forbids all over again.
(9) And it runs backwards as well: since 301 came in under the old 204, and the old 204 came in under the founding clause, and the founding clause was itself a move made by six people with a bottle of wine, every move we've ever made is void and we haven't been playing a game at all, just talking in a kitchen.

The bit I can't live with is (6) generalising, because the same shape would show that no legislature can alter the rules governing its own power: no amendment clause could ever itself be amended, no entrenched provision unentrenched, no manner-and-form requirement changed by the very procedure it constrains. That's false as a matter of fact — it's done constantly — and yet I can't find the step where the argument cheats. Someone suggested we simply repeal 301 before touching 204, which obviously begs the question, since repealing 301 is itself a move that has to be authorised by 204.

So: which step do I cut, why did it look harmless when I wrote the list, and what do I owe once it's gone? I'd most like to keep (1), because 301 is the premise I actually believe, but I'd sooner be told it has to go than be talked out of the conclusion with hand-waving. And can you answer me twice — once for our Thursday game, where every source of authority has to be a rule written on a card, and once for a real legal system, because I suspect the two come apart at the bottom and I want to know what's down there if it isn't a rule, and whether that thing makes 301 false or merely beside the point. One more thing: if your diagnosis is that a rule can perfectly well authorise its own destruction, explain why that doesn't collapse into a rule authorising anything whatsoever, since the whole bite of 301 was supposed to be that authority has to come from somewhere.
