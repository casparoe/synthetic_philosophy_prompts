> (b) If there is a unique best world and God has a sufficient reason to choose it, was God free in creating this world? Does Leibniz's position here differ in substance from Spinoza's?

And here's the student's answer verbatim:

> Leibniz's system starts from the claim that God's understanding contains every possible world, where a world is a maximal set of individual substances that could all exist together without contradiction — this is what he means by compossibility. God surveys all of them and actualises the one with the most perfection, and perfection for Leibniz means the greatest variety combined with the greatest order, so the best world is the one that squeezes the most phenomena out of the fewest and simplest laws. On the question of whether the comparison is an adding-up of parts, the answer has to be yes: perfection is a quantity for Leibniz, and God is essentially solving an optimisation problem, weighing total richness against total simplicity and taking the maximum. That is also what the quoted passage is doing — it rules out voluntarism, since goodness isn't just whatever God decides, but it doesn't put a law above God either. The standards live in God's own understanding and in the essences of the possible things themselves, which have a kind of claim to exist in proportion to how perfect they are, so that God's act of creation is mostly a matter of ratifying the result of that competition among possibles.
>
> This makes part (c) awkward. If our world is the maximum, then a world exactly like ours except that the Lisbon earthquake never happened would contain everything ours contains and one less catastrophe, and would therefore score higher. God chose ours anyway, so the earthquake must be contributing a positive amount of goodness rather than merely being permitted. It is good in itself. This is consistent with the way Leibniz talks about evils as the dark patches in a painting that make the illuminated parts more striking, and with his view that God draws a greater good out of evils he allows.
>
> On part (b), the principle of sufficient reason means God must have a reason for choosing this world rather than another, and since only one world is the best there was only ever one reason available to him. So the actual world exists metaphysically necessarily. Leibniz wants to protect freedom by saying the necessity involved is only moral, i.e. that God is inclined by his goodness without being compelled, but that looks like a fig leaf: an agent who cannot do other than what his own nature determines is not free in any robust sense, and Leibniz ends up in precisely the necessitarianism he spends so much of the Theodicy accusing Spinoza of. The difference between them is vocabulary — Spinoza calls it substance, Leibniz calls it divine wisdom.
>
> Historically the optimism is what got attacked. Voltaire's Candide, written after the Lisbon earthquake, is a direct reply to the Monadology, which was published only after Leibniz's death, and in England Pope had already made the same point with his slogan that whatever is is right, which Leibniz borrows from him.
