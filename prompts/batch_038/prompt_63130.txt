I help to run a small community theatre company in the north of England. I have no academic training in any of this; I have simply been turning one question over for some months, and I would rather put it properly than keep circling it.

At a programming meeting last week our artistic director refused a piece — a quiet, extremely well-made play about an old man and his garden, with nothing political anywhere on its surface — and justified the refusal by quoting W. E. B. Du Bois to the room. The line was from his 1926 lecture Criteria of Negro Art: "Thus all art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists." From that single sentence he drew two conclusions. First, that a play which declines to take a side is not neutral but propaganda for things as they are. Second, that questions of craft and beauty are beside the point once that fact is recognised. He treated both as settled.

Two things trouble me and I cannot articulate either. The first is that I believe Du Bois himself, in an earlier piece for the same magazine, criticised precisely the insistence that art and propaganda be made one, holding it to be wrong and in the end harmful; I may be misremembering, but if I am not, then the word is plainly doing two different jobs in his own writing, and the director has picked up whichever one suits him. The second is that one sentence seems to be carrying at least four different claims at once — something about what art is, something about what artists intend, something about what audiences actually do with a work, and something about how a work ought to be judged — and I cannot tell which of them he is using at which moment.

Could you therefore lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims that sentence might be making, keeping those four levels apart, and say for each whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false? Please pay particular attention to "must", and to what turns on whether it states an unavoidable fact about art or a demand upon artists; and please say whether the claim, if it is a claim about all persuasion, can consistently be offered as evidence for itself. Finally, tell me which reading, if any, would genuinely license the director's two conclusions, and whether he would still accept that reading if it were applied to a play he loves.

Please write your answer in British English.
