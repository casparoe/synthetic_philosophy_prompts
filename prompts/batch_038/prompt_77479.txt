I am a junior and one of two student organizers of my school's philosophy club. We meet for forty minutes on Thursdays and typically draw fourteen to twenty students, sophomores through seniors, none of whom have studied probability or economics beyond algebra. I would like to run a session on the way a crowd of individually reasonable people can end up ignoring nearly all of the information it collectively possesses, because each of them defers to the visible choices of the people ahead. Please design a self-contained game we can play in a single meeting, using no phones, no money, and no materials more complicated than slips of paper, two opaque containers, and a whiteboard.

The requirements I have in mind are these. First, there should be a hidden state of the world with exactly two possibilities, and each player should receive a private indication that points toward one of them with a fixed accuracy that you choose. Tell me what accuracy you chose and why that value makes the herd behavior likely to begin around the third or fourth player rather than the twelfth, given that our attendance varies between fourteen and twenty. Second, players must act in a fixed public sequence, each seeing every earlier public choice but nothing whatsoever about earlier players' private indications. Third, separately from the public choice, every player should record a private guess on a sealed slip that is collected but not read until the end. When the slips are opened, both tallies go on the board side by side, so that the room sees a public record that looks almost unanimous sitting next to private information that is nearly evenly divided. I want that gap to be the moment the idea lands, and I want the players to be able to measure it rather than merely be told about it. Fourth, please include a short second round in which the reward for being right is unchanged but an additional reward goes to whoever ends up in the majority. The purpose of the second round is that afterward the players genuinely cannot say whether they followed the crowd because they were persuaded or because they were paid to; build the round so that this ambiguity is a real feature of the rules and not just something I assert, and write the discussion questions so the group argues about which motive was operating on them.

Along with the game, please give me: the exact wording of the instructions read to the players; a scoring scheme expressed in points redeemable for candy; a minute-by-minute timeline for the forty minutes; a mapping of specific moments in play onto specific parts of the idea; your recommendation on whether a public announcement made partway through the sequence would sharpen or wreck the effect, and if you keep it, where it should fall; the three things most likely to go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each, including what to do if the first two players happen to split and no herd ever forms; and a candid statement of which feature of the phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in discussion. The players should not hear the name of the effect until the discussion, and nothing I say before play begins should explain it to them.
