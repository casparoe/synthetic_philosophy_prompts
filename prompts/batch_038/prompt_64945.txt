Among historians of logical empiricism there is a dispute that has run for decades without resolution: was Carnap's philosophy connected to his politics, or merely coexistent with it? The material seems to support both readings. Carnap was a socialist throughout his adult life and co-authored the Vienna Circle's 1929 manifesto, which presented the scientific world-conception as bound up with the social transformation of the period, and he worked alongside Neurath, who engaged directly with the socialist planning debate, including the claim that a planned economy could reckon in kind rather than in money. Yet Carnap's published philosophy is non-cognitivist about value — value judgments express attitudes rather than assert anything — and tolerant about frameworks: no framework is uniquely correct, and choices among frameworks are settled by pragmatic criteria such as simplicity and fruitfulness. That looks like a philosophy that could not entail, or even tilt toward, any particular political order.

The two camps read the same corpus in opposite directions. Some argue that non-cognitivism, tolerance, and the program of a scientific world-conception jointly amount to a political philosophy, one on which framework choice is a collective practical decision of just the kind a democratic planner would have to make. Others argue that Carnap held the two in separate compartments, never derived a politics from his logic, and that finding a political philosophy in the movement is a retrospective projection onto a set of largely technical texts.

My hypothesis is that the dispute is not chiefly about what Carnap thought but about what would count as a connection between a philosophy and a politics — entailment, shared motivation, shared conception of rationality, shared institutional program — so that the two camps answer different questions under one heading. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually argues. If it fails, say what the disagreement is really about, and where the better argument lies. Either way, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a class of evidence, or a change of question — and whether progress should be expected at all, given how little Carnap said in print about what his technical work implied for planning.
