The question as it was set:

Q3. Throughout this course the term "nimbin" has been governed by the following stipulation, given in week 1: "Let 'nimbin' name the stuff — whatever it turns out to be — that produces the green shimmer sometimes seen over the harbour at Reykjavik in midwinter." Nobody at the time of the stipulation knew what produced the shimmer. Subsequent research established that it is produced by an ionized gas; call that gas G.

(a) Is "nimbin" a rigid designator? Is "nimbin = G" necessary or contingent, a priori or a posteriori? Justify each answer, and say explicitly what work the phrase "whatever it turns out to be" is doing in the stipulation. Close with a sentence or two on what would have happened to the stipulation if the research had instead turned up two wholly unrelated causes of the shimmer.

(b) Suppose the week-1 stipulation had instead read: "By 'nimbin' let us mean the ionized gas G." How do your answers to (a) change, and why?

(c) A student in an earlier cohort wrote: "If (a) is right, then some statements are necessary but a posteriori, so necessity and apriority come apart. But then the identity theorist can simply say that pain = C-fibre firing is necessary a posteriori, discovered by neuroscience, and Kripke's objection to the identity theory collapses." Assess this argument.

The student's answer:

(a) Nimbin is a rigid designator. The description in the stipulation is not the meaning of the word, and the clause "whatever it turns out to be" is the giveaway: it signals that the person stipulating is using the description to pick out which stuff she is naming, not to give the term a sense that it would then carry from world to world. So nimbin names G in every possible world, whether or not G produces any shimmer there, and whether or not anyone in that world has ever seen Reykjavik. "Nimbin = G" is therefore necessary, since a world where the two came apart would have to be a world where something is not itself. It is a posteriori though, because the only route to it is the atmospheric work — nothing in the stipulation tells you what nimbin is. Same combination as water = H2O. On the last part: if the research had turned up two unrelated causes, the stipulation would simply have misfired, because no single stuff answers to the description. Either nimbin fails to refer at all, or it gets ambiguously attached to both causes, and in neither case is there a necessary identity to report.

(b) Then nimbin is an abbreviation, the sentence "nimbin = G" is trivial for anyone who has the stipulation, and it is a priori rather than a posteriori — you don't need the atmospheric research to know it. But it is no longer necessary. This is the standard metre pattern: whoever does the stipulating knows something without evidence, and yet the world could have turned out otherwise, so what they know is contingent. That is what makes the pair of cases interesting, since the same stipulation done in two different ways delivers necessity in one and contingency in the other, and the difference between them is just whether the stipulator already knew the chemistry.

(c) The earlier cohort is basically right. Pain and C-fibre firing are both rigid designators — "pain" picks out that very state in every world, not whatever state happens to feel that way over there — so if the identity is true it is necessary, and the identity theorist is entitled to call it a necessary a posteriori truth that neuroscience discovered, exactly parallel to water = H2O. The appearance that the firing could occur without the pain is an illusion, and it is generated by the fact that we fix the reference of "pain" by a contingent property of the state, namely how it feels, so we can imagine the property without the thing it belongs to. Once you see that Kripke has nothing left to work with: all he is entitled to is that the identity is counterintuitive, which is not an argument against it.
