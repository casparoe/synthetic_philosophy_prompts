I'm deep in the second draft of a novel and there's a scene I keep circling without actually writing. Setting: about sixty years out. A mining consortium has figured out how to anneal bulk matter into what they market as "quiet" — a crystalline lattice whose parts are, in their own phrase, kept from talking to each other. Their pitch is that if panpsychism is right — if experience runs all the way down, so that even a single ion has some faint flicker of something-it-is-like — then ordinary matter, with all its tangled binding and feedback, is screaming. And their alloy is a mercy product. They sell it as anesthetic infrastructure. Cities are lined with the stuff. Nobody actually knows whether it works.

The scene is a materials engineer who helped design the lattice arguing with a physicist who's become the loudest voice against it. Write it as a dialogue, maybe 900–1200 words, and make it a real fight — I want them answering each other's actual points, not doing that thing where two people recite positions at each other in parallel and never quite touch.

What I care about:

- The engineer has to be a serious panpsychist, not a shill reading from a brochure. Give her the strongest version of the idea: that if experience tracks something like how tightly a system's parts are integrated and mutually informative, then a lattice deliberately engineered so its parts are nearly independent really is the closest thing to a general anesthetic that matter can be given. She should be able to say why she thinks a suffering claim is even coherent at that scale, and what she imagines it's like down there — vague is fine, hand-wavy isn't.
- The physicist takes the same metaphysics seriously, and that's what makes her objection bite. Let her press on the parts I cant work out myself: whether a barely-integrated system can have experiences at all, or whether the whole view collapses into "there is nothing it is like to be an ion"; whether badness can come in degrees without a subject for whom it is bad; and whether their alloy might be manufacturing a harm they have no instrument for — some population of tiny dissociated states that's worse than anything the loud matter ever produced, precisely because there's no unified subject left to object.
- I want at least one moment where one of them says something that genuinely unsettles the other, and where you can see the engineer's confidence crack in a way that isn't just her politely conceding.
- Keep the ethics tangled up in the physics of the argument rather than in consciousness science generally. No relitigating whether zombies are conceivable, no name-checking philosophers, papers, or thought experiments I could google. Two smart people who share a metaphysics and reach opposite conclusions about whether to line a city with this material.
- Don't resolve it and don't stack the deck. I want to finish it unsure who's right, but I also don't want a tie that feels engineered — they should both sound like they could win.

Oh, and no stage directions about the lighting or what anyone's wearing. I'll handle blocking. Voices only, plus the occasional gesture if it's actually doing work.
