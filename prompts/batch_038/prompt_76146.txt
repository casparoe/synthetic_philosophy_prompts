Please write a short story of between 1,800 and 2,500 words, under a title of your choosing, in British English. It is to be a work of fiction with characters and stakes rather than a disguised essay, but it should be built around a real intellectual problem, which I set out below together with everything a reader will need. No outside knowledge should be required to follow it.

**The idea it should be about**

From the mid-1980s Douglas Hofstadter, Melanie Mitchell and their collaborators built a family of programmes — Copycat is the best known — that work in deliberately tiny domains, answering questions of the form: if abc becomes abd, what does ijk become? Their claim, argued in Mitchell's 1993 monograph on Copycat and in Hofstadter's *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies* (1995), is that making an analogy is not a step taken after perception but a form of perception itself: to see what a string *is* — three letters, or a rising alphabet, or a singleton followed by a pair — is already to have begun solving the problem. Such systems therefore never apply fixed rules to fixed descriptions. They maintain a spreading network of concepts whose boundaries overlap, a workspace in which tentative descriptions are built and torn down, and a crowd of small opportunistic processes fired according to urgency, in what Hofstadter called a parallel terraced scan. When the workspace is a mess a temperature-like quantity runs high and the system tolerates destructive, radical moves; as structure firms, temperature falls and the alternatives quietly cease to be available. The characteristic event is the *slippage*: a concept that seemed perfectly determinate — "the rightmost letter" — turns out to be able to slide into a neighbour ("the rightmost letter of that kind", "the letter at the end of the last group"), and the slide is precisely what makes an old rule applicable to a case it was never framed for. On this view slippage is not a malfunction. It is the mechanism.

**The microdomain — please use it exactly as given, and do not extend it**

At a small port, cargo is recorded on tally-cords: a length of laid rope running from a wooden toggle at the fixed end to a free end, knotted from toggle outwards. There are three kinds of knot.

- **s**, a single knot: one unit of cargo.
- **d**, a double knot: two units.
- **f**, a figure-knot: worth nothing, but a junction binding two strands, so that the cord has a *near strand* (toggle to figure) and a *far strand* (figure to free end).

A cord is written from toggle to free end; thus `s s d f s` has a near strand of four units, a far strand of one, and a total of five.

Each quarter the old cord is "tied forward": a fresh cord reproduces it and is then extended at the free end according to a rule the office has never written down. The rule is transmitted only through matched pairs kept in the archive — forty years of them. Here are three, and they are typical:

1. `s s d f s` → `s s d f s s`
2. `s d s f s s` → `s d s f s s s`
3. `d d f s` → `d d f s s`

One regularity has made the archive useless as a teacher: by standing practice a quarter's cord is always closed with a single knot, the sign that the count has been told out in full. Every archived cord therefore ends in **s**. All of the following readings are consistent with every pair in the archive: *append a single knot*; *append a knot of whatever kind the cord ends in*; *raise the cord's total value by one unit*; *raise the far strand's value by one unit*; *lengthen the final run of like knots by one*.

Now, for the first time, a bonded consignment arrives, and a new regulation requires a bonded cord to be closed with a double knot. The cord to be tied forward is:

`s s d f s d`

Three answers are in play, and your story should contain all three, written out in the notation so the reader can check them:

- **P**: `s s d f s d s` — the rule concerns singles, or the adding of one unit, and the end of the cord is simply where the rope runs out.
- **Q**: `s s d f s d d` — the rule concerns whatever the cord ends in, and this cord ends in a double.
- **R**: `s s d f s s d` — the terminal double is not a count but a *seal*, and a seal is not part of the record; the concept of "the free end" slips from the end of the rope to the end of what the rope says, so the extension goes inside the seal and the seal is retied last.

**What the story must do**

Set it on the night the cord has to be tied, before the ship is loaded, and give the choice a real cost — the wrong cord enters the cargo wrongly, and someone bears it. Use at most three named characters. The clerk may consult the ledger books, which record unit totals for each quarter's pairs: that arithmetic is enough to eliminate **Q**, since every archived pair differs by exactly one unit and Q differs by two. Nothing whatever may decide between **P** and **R**. No further fact, no document, no memory, no dying word may settle it; she must arrive at one of them by seeing the cord differently, not by inferring. If you want a piece of evidence that appears decisive and is not — a sealed cord of her late predecessor's, tar-bound so that she cannot tell whether its extension was tied before or after the seal — you may use it, but it must remain genuinely ambiguous.

Show the high-temperature state as well as the freeze: at some point, hurried and cold and lamp-low, she should entertain a reading that is merely desperate (cut the cord, retie it, treat the double as a mistake, treat the whole practice as void), and later, once she has committed, the rejected readings should have become unavailable to her — she should be unable to reconstruct how **Q**, or the desperate move, ever looked possible.

Do not name Copycat, the slipnet, codelets, the workspace or temperature as technical terms anywhere in the story; those ideas are to be present only as the shape of events. Do not have any character expound a theory of rules, concepts or underdetermination, and do not close on a moral. End with the cord tied.

**After the story**

Append an author's note of roughly 300 words, separate from the fiction, in which you: (i) identify which of the story's turns are load-bearing on a contested view of analogy-making — in particular on the claim that a slippage is a perceptual event constrained by the salience of structure, rather than an unconstrained choice dressed up afterwards as reasoning — and say how much weight you would put on that claim; (ii) say plainly where you have taken liberties, whether with the microdomain's bookkeeping, with the architecture's machinery, or with how tidy you have made the fork between P and R; and (iii) say what would change your mind: which argument, example or piece of evidence would move you to a different verdict about which cord is right, or about the underlying view of concepts, and where you think your own reasoning about this microdomain is most likely to fail.
