I write a science-and-ideas column and I've got a piece due on the whole nudge-and-welfare fight, so before I file I want a sanity check on whether the position I've been defending actually hangs together. Here's what I keep asserting, in roughly the order I assert it:

(1) Preferences aren't inner states, they're patterns in what people actually choose — that's the point of the revealed preference move, and it's why I tell readers that utility is a book-keeping device, not a feeling anyone has ever measured.

(2) So a lot of the "we're predictably irrational" stuff is overblown: you can't call a choice a mistake relative to someone's preferences if their preferences just are their choices.

(3) And yet I think the default-setting work genuinely helps people — when the pension form or the cafeteria gets rearranged, folks end up better off, and the fact that they could opt out and don't is part of why I'm comfortable saying so.

(4) I'm also happy defending the way agencies do cost-benefit analysis, where benefits get measured by what people are willing to pay.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually pulling against each other and which only look like they are. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flat, and for each one say what would have to give and what I'd lose by giving it up — including whether (2) is doing any real work or is just a tautology wearing a smoking jacket. Don't send me to the literature, I know the names and I don't want a history lesson; I want the conceptual audit, and I'd rather cut one of these commitments than fudge it in print.
