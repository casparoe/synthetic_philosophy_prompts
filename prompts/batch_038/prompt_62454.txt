For something like fifteen years I have read the rationalist blogs and their comment sections, and for most of that time I have kept a private log of one particular commentator's pronouncements — a former physicist who posts under his initials and has become that community's resident authority on infinity, supertasks, and their discontents. He is careful, he is articulate, and in a decade I have never caught him reversing himself. What follows is my log of his verdicts, in my words but with his reasoning attached as faithfully as I can render it. I want to predict what he will say about two new cases before he says it, and I want the prediction to fall out of an explicit reconstruction of his rule rather than out of my priors about him.

The log:

1. **Zeno's runner.** Infinitely many shrinking intervals must be crossed to reach the finish. His verdict: dissolved rather than solved. The intervals and the times shrink together, so nothing worrying is completed. Anyone who sees a paradox here has mistaken a convergent sum for an impossible task.

2. **Thomson's lamp.** A switch is toggled at 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, … of a minute before noon. His verdict: the state at noon is undefined, "but be precise about what that is." It is not a contradiction, and it does not show the task impossible. The description fixes the lamp at every moment before noon and is simply silent about noon; Thomson mistook silence for absurdity.

3. **The shuttling machine.** A ball is moved between two trays at the same accelerating rhythm; where is it at noon? His verdict: same diagnosis as the lamp. The error is in the step from "no determinate position" to "such a machine cannot exist" — those are different claims, and only the first is supported.

4. **The vase.** At stage *n*, balls 10*n*−9 through 10*n* are added and ball *n* is removed. How many at noon? His verdict: none, and determinately none. Ask of each individual ball where it ends up; every ball has been removed by noon. The rival answer comes from taking the limit of the running count, and counting does not commute with limits.

5. **The vase, modified.** Identical, except that at stage *n* ball 10*n* is the one removed. His verdict: infinitely many remain, and just as determinately. Both answers are item-wise. The paradox was always in the arithmetic of the tally, never in the contents of the vase.

6. **The accelerating machine.** A device halves the time it takes per step, so that it has run through every natural number by noon and prints the answer to an undecidable question. His verdict, in two parts. First: there is no contradiction in the description; it is a perfectly intelligible machine. Second: it cannot exist — the steps would have to be executed at unboundedly increasing rates, with unbounded power and heat in a bounded region. "It is barred by physics, not by logic, and the two must not be traded on each other."

7. **Pressed on 6.** Suppose such a machine existed and printed an answer — could we trust the printout? His verdict: the printout would be a physical record like any other; what we could never do is distinguish such a machine from one that merely ran a very long time and printed early. "That is a limitation on us, not a defect in the answer."

8. **The eternal clerk.** An immortal being has written down one digit per day since eternity past and now possesses an actually infinite list. His verdict: possible and unremarkable. There is no moment at which the list was finished, so nothing was traversed. The intuition that an infinite cannot be gone through is an intuition about finite durations, misapplied.

9. **The endless clerk.** A being checks one number per day forever and is asked on "day ω" whether he has finished. His verdict: not a supertask at all. With no finite accumulation point nothing is compressed, and the question has the trivial answer *no*. He reserves the interesting questions for tasks whose stages crowd toward a moment.

10. **A reader's submission.** Infinitely many would-be assassins; the *k*-th is set to strike, if and only if the victim is still alive, at exactly noon plus 2^(−*k*) minutes. Each is perfectly reliable, and there is nobody else. His verdict: "a genuine paradox, and the only one on this list." Nothing here is silent — every assassin's behavior is fully determined — yet no consistent story exists about who strikes: the victim cannot survive past noon, and no assassin can be the one who kills him. Where the lamp's description was merely incomplete, this one is unsatisfiable, "and an unsatisfiable description is not a malformed question but an impossibility proof."

The two new cases, neither of which he has ruled on:

**A.** A relativist proposes a design that strips out every physical excuse listed in 6. A computer of wholly ordinary construction and power draw — one step per second, a pocket calculator's energy budget — is left in stable orbit far from a large rotating black hole. It enumerates proofs of a fixed conjecture in a fixed order and is wired to emit a single light pulse toward the hole if and only if it ever finds one. Separately, an astronaut falls in along a trajectory of the kind the relativity literature discusses under the name Malament–Hogarth: on it, the entire unbounded future of the distant external region lies in her past, and she reaches the point beyond which she is cut off from that region forever after a finite reading on her own wristwatch. So there is a moment τ, on an ordinary clock, by which she has received the pulse if one was ever sent. Nothing accelerates, nothing dissipates unbounded energy, and no stage is executed faster than the one before. I have not asked whether the geometry is physically realistic, and I am reporting the case as posed. The question he must rule on: at τ, is there a determinate fact about whether the pulse was sent — and does the astronaut thereby know the answer about the conjecture?

**B.** Another reader proposes running the lamp exactly as in 2, with one added clause: the lamp's state at noon is stipulated to be *on*. Is the resulting description now complete and consistent, so that the task is possible after all; still silent about noon, so that the stipulation is empty; or unsatisfiable, so that it collapses into his case 10?

What I want from you:

- At least two, and preferably three, distinct candidate rules that reproduce **all ten** of his verdicts — rules stated precisely enough to yield verdicts rather than moods, and genuinely different from one another, not three phrasings of the same disposition.
- For each candidate rule, what it predicts for A and for B, including whether he grants determinacy, grants possibility, and grants knowledge.
- Your single best guess for each case, with the reasons for preferring that rule — and an honest note about which parts of the log you are leaning on, since I suspect 7 and 10 are doing far more work than he would admit.
- The closest analog among the ten precedents for each new case, and precisely where the analogy strains.
- Whether any of his verdicts are in tension with one another, or whether a single rule absorbs all of them; in particular, whether 10's appeal to unsatisfiability is consistent with 2's and 3's refusal to move from a defective description to an impossible task.
- One further case, not already on my list, that would most sharply separate the leading candidates, with each candidate's prediction recorded in advance.
- A note on any part of the dispute that you think is merely verbal — for instance, whether "possible," "well-posed," and "determinate" are being used interchangeably across the log, and what turns on keeping them apart.

You have no web access, and I am not asking for citations, for placement in the literature, or for reading recommendations; everything you need is in the descriptions above. I would rather you flag any precedent whose description you think is too loose to support the verdict he gave it, since that would be a finding about my log rather than about him.
