write me a short story, maybe 1500–2000 words, that stages an argument from inside suffering-focused ethics — negative utilitarianism and the views adjacent to it — without ever naming the debate or sliding into essay mode. no philosophers quoted, no real books or papers referenced anywhere, and please dont tack on an afterword explaining what you meant.

here's the frame I want you to invent the details of. there's a public agency, call it something like the Weights Office, and its entire job is to maintain the conversion table the state runs on: how many small bads are equivalent to one atrocity. everything downstream depends on that table — hospital triage rules, whether a factory gets shut down, whether the budget goes to programs that amplify good experiences or programs that delete bad ones. the protagonist is the officer who has to revise it, and she's a sincere negative utilitarian, not a cartoon.

the plot should turn on a single case that the current table can't absorb. something like: a permanent intervention that would erase one specific kind of agony from a whole population, which also erases a band of mild discomfort, and which a small group of people are petitioning against on the grounds that what it removes is the thing they most want to keep. or an aggregation case — she has to rule on whether any finite number of migraines can outweigh one person being tortured for a year, and the ruling decides whether the project gets funded. pick one and commit.

what I actually care about:

- give her the strongest version of her own view and let the reader feel its pull. the sense that adding up little pleasures on one side of a ledger to cancel out one person's screaming on the other is a kind of accounting trick. the conviction that reducing agony has a claim that isn't just one preference among many. don't soften it into ordinary consequentialism.

- the complication has to come from inside the view too, not from the cheap "but suffering gives life meaning" line, which I'm bored of. better options: she discovers that filling in the table at all requires her to decide whether there's a threshold below which bads simply stop mattering, and either answer costs her something she's committed to. or she realizes she's been treating irreversibility — the fact that a mistake here can't be walked back — as if it were a reason rather than a fact, and then has to face what her view says about the difference between ending a suffering life and never starting one.

- work in the pinprick objection at least once, the old line that her position implies one small sting is worse than painlessly ending everything that exists. but let a character say it in dialogue, and let her actually answer it — well or badly, I don't care, but it has to be an answer she'd give, not a strawman she knocks down.

craft constraints: the reader should figure out what the table is by watching people use it, so no exposition dump in the first page. at least one scene is a hearing or a form being filled out line by line. no character gets a speech longer then four sentences. end on something concrete and unresolved — a number, a signature, a room — not on a thesis.
