I am editing a small farewell anthology for a colleague who has spent thirty years writing about persistence and change, and the volume is to open with a single poem on the Ship of Theseus. Please write it as a pantoum of five quatrains — twenty lines in all — and observe the form's rule of inheritance to the letter: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return, in that order, as the first and third lines of the quatrain that follows, and in the closing quatrain the third line of the opening quatrain comes back in the second position while the opening line itself comes back last, so that the poem ends on the line with which it began. Ten distinct lines will therefore each be used exactly twice. On sound, the recycling is unforgiving: a full ABAB rhyme in every quatrain would collapse the whole poem into a single rhyme family, so either commit to that one rhyme sound across all ten distinct lines or write unrhymed four-beat lines of roughly eight to ten syllables. Choose one course and hold to it; the rhythm must be steady enough that no repeated line reads as padding.

Beyond the mechanics, I want the form to carry the argument rather than illustrate it. Because the answering lines of one quatrain become the opening lines of the next, a pantoum contains two interleaved chains, and in the final quatrain those chains change places outright. Please set that against the two ships of the puzzle as Hobbes completed it: the vessel whose worn timbers are replaced one by one over the years and which sails on regardless, and the second vessel that a shipwright quietly builds up on the slipway out of the timber thrown away. Let one chain hold the claim of continuity — shape, use, the story the crew keeps telling — and the other the claim of the wood itself, every fibre of it original, and let the closing exchange of positions show that the two claims have swapped places and that nothing in the grain settles which ship is which. Observe, too, the form's own ideal that a repeated line should change its meaning on its second pass without changing a word: the same plank, named twice, should belong to a different ship the second time.

The poem should be a scene and not an essay — harbour, slipway, weather, hands, the smell of tar. It must not use the words 'identity', 'Theseus', 'paradox', 'puzzle' or 'philosophy', must not name Hobbes, and must nowhere announce its subject. A reader who has never met the puzzle should find it a good poem about a shipyard; a reader who has should be able to say which lines belong to which ship, and why the crossover in the last quatrain is where the difficulty lives.

After the poem, add a note of no more than 120 words setting out how the inheritance rule maps onto the two ships and precisely where the crossover bites. Please write both the poem and the note in British English, in spelling and idiom alike.
