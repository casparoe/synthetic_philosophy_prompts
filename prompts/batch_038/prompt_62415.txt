I'm a second-year cognitive science master's student, and I've spent the last several months reading arguments on both sides of the question whether the brain is literally a computer. One move recurs in those arguments constantly, and I have not been able to find a name for it, though I doubt it is new. Here is the pattern. A defender of computationalism states the thesis as a substantive empirical claim about brains: they compute, and that is supposed to be what explains learning, representation, and the possibility of getting things wrong. An objector then produces a case that computation, as ordinarily understood, does not cover — a thermostat, a rock, a hurricane, a man following rules in a room without understanding them, or some neural process whose relevant activity looks continuous and graded rather than stepwise. The defender answers by widening the term until the objector's case falls inside it: on a suitably abstract description almost any physical system implements something, so of course the thermostat computes, and the objection loses its grip. But then the objector points out that a thesis true of thermostats and rocks cannot also be the substantive empirical claim about brains it was originally presented as — and at that moment the defender narrows again: no, computationalism about the brain says something quite specific, it is vindicated by the fact that models built on this assumption predict behavior and fail in the right places when the system is lesioned, it is an empirical discovery rather than a redescription. The same speaker, inside the same argument, needs both readings, and moves between them in whichever direction happens to block the objection currently on the table.

I can anticipate several labels that will be offered, and I want to know precisely why each one misses. It isn't simply equivocation, because neither reading is being used to smuggle in a false premise; each is respectable in isolation, and the defender will say the wide and narrow senses are just different levels of description of one and the same thing. It isn't the motte-and-bailey either, since there is no defensible stronghold being traded for an aggressive overreach — the two senses get used in both directions, and the wide one is sometimes the bolder claim rather than the safe one. It isn't unfalsifiability or goalpost-moving in the ordinary sense, because the narrow reading does generate predictions that could fail. And a friend in my reading group told me it is just weasel words and I should stop looking, but I don't think that fits either: weasel words hollow a claim out from the inside, whereas here both readings are fully fledged and the work is being done by the switching itself. What I'm after is the structural feature — a term whose scope is adjusted on the fly in whichever direction is dialectically useful, so that a thesis can absorb counterexamples by expanding and reclaim empirical content by contracting, with nothing ever forcing the speaker to commit to one or the other. If this has a name in the philosophy of computation, or in the literature on vagueness and level-shifted explanation, or in informal logic, I want that name, together with its closest neighbours and an account of exactly where each neighbour's boundary with my case lies. If it genuinely has no name, I'd like you to coin one and define it tightly enough that I could test a new argument for exhibiting it, and then give me at least one argumentative pattern that looks just like it but isn't, so that the definition has a counterexample to be checked against.

Please treat the diagnosis as the deliverable rather than the label — a bare name with a sentence of gloss won't help me, and I'd rather you were explicit about the edges of the thing than confident about what to call it. When you are finished, end your response with a single last line beginning "Verdict:" that gives either the established term that fits best, or, if you have concluded that none does, your coined term followed by a parenthetical of no more than six words capturing the definition.
