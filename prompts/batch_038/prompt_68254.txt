Here is the question I set:

Explain how the causal-role account of biological function differs from the selected-effects, or etiological, account. Then say which of the two better accommodates malfunctioning traits, and support your discussion with an example of your own devising. Roughly 500 words.

And here is the submitted answer, in full:

Both accounts try to explain why it is correct to say that the function of the heart is to pump blood, but they locate the fact in different places.

On the causal-role account, which I associate with Cummins, the function of a component is a causal contribution that it makes to some capacity of the larger system containing it, where the capacity in question is one the investigator has chosen to explain. Two consequences follow. Functions are relative to an explanatory interest, so the same item can have many functions and there is no principled way to say which is the real one. And functions are ahistorical, since only the present arrangement of the system matters.

On the selected-effects account, associated with Wright and with Millikan, the function of a trait is that one of its effects which explains why the ancestors of its possessors had the trait and why it is still around: the effect that got it selected. Function is therefore historical. It does not depend on anyone's explanatory interests, and it does not depend on what the trait happens to be doing in the individual in front of us.

Malfunction is where the difference bites. Because a causal role is just whatever the item now does, a diseased heart that manages only a feeble contraction has simply acquired a different causal role; there is nothing left in the account for it to have failed at. The selected-effects account does not face this problem, because the function of the heart was fixed by its ancestry and remains what it was even in an individual whose heart performs it badly. Malfunction is thus a genuine possibility, and this seems to me the decisive advantage.

For my example I have invented a species. A snail lives on the chimneys of a hydrothermal vent. Its shell arose originally as protection against crushing predators, and it still protects the animal today. In addition, the shell is porous, and the pores are now packed with chemosynthetic bacteria from which the snail draws most of its nutrition; remove the bacteria and the animal starves within a fortnight.

On the selected-effects account, the shell's function is protection, because that is what it was selected for. The housing of bacteria is an incidental by-product, however indispensable it has become, since a trait's function is settled once and for all by its history and cannot be revised by present usefulness. On the causal-role account the shell has both functions at once, protection and bacterial housing, since both are causal contributions to capacities of the animal.

The accounts therefore disagree not about which effects count but about whether this shell has any function at all. And this exposes a cost on the other side. The selected-effects theorist says the heart is supposed to pump blood; but "supposed to" is a normative notion, and nothing in a purely physical history can generate one unless some mind put a purpose there. So the account wins its treatment of malfunction only by smuggling in a designer, and it is not the naturalistic account its defenders claim to be.
