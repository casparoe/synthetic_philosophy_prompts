I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, most of it at a national laboratory measuring things like phonon dispersion and thermal transport, and I retired eighteen months ago with the intention of finally reading philosophy properly rather than in fragments. My wife's book group has been working through a popular history of economic thought, and since I retain the habit of wanting to check a derivation rather than trust a stated result, I have been reading Hume's political essays alongside it — the ones on money, on interest, on the balance of trade, and on public credit, which I gather first appeared together in 1752 and were later folded into the collected essays.

The chapter devoted to Hume summarizes him as follows, and I should like to know how much of it I may rely on:

"Hume was the first economist to see clearly that money is a veil. Because prices adjust in proportion to the quantity of money, doubling the gold in a kingdom simply doubles all prices and changes nothing real — money is neutral, and this is why Hume dismissed the mercantilist policy of accumulating treasure as a mere superstition. He drew two further conclusions from the same principle. First, trade imbalances correct themselves: a deficit drains gold out of the country, domestic prices fall, its goods grow cheaper abroad, and exports recover until the deficit disappears, so the system is self-stabilising and any tariff or restriction can only distort it — free trade benefits every participant without exception. Second, since the level of prices is fixed by the quantity of money in circulation, paper credit merely inflames prices without adding to real wealth; Hume therefore urged a return to a purely metallic currency, which makes him the intellectual ancestor of every modern argument that government-issued paper money is inherently inflationary. (He did at least break correctly with Locke on interest: low interest rates and low profits go together, and both are consequences of flourishing commerce rather than of an abundant money supply.)"

Would you work through this claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of Hume would accept, which are defensible compressions of something he actually says, and which are misrepresentations? Two points in particular leave me uneasy. In my own discipline, a quantity that rescales every measurement while leaving all ratios intact is a choice of units and physically inert, and the money-is-a-veil thesis appeals to me for precisely that reason; does Hume really hold that a change in the money stock has no real effects, or does the analogy break somewhere he himself indicates? And the self-stabilising trade mechanism reads to me like an unconditional negative-feedback loop, whereas anyone who has designed a control system knows that such a loop has a domain of validity; did Hume attach conditions to it, or did he present it as automatic and universal? Finally, does the book's larger genealogical claim — that Hume belongs in the lineage of hard-money arguments against paper currency — survive once the details are corrected, given that my recollection of the essay on money is of him proposing something rather different, involving a public bank that locks up the coin and lets domestic circulation run on paper? Please give me your own assessment of what Hume held and where the popular account has slipped; I am not after references or a list of further reading, and I would rather be told plainly that a sentence is wrong than be given a hedged account of the scholarly debate about it.
