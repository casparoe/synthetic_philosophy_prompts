Hofstadter's *I Am a Strange Loop* advances the claim that a human self is not confined to one skull but distributed over numerous brains in varying degrees — a thesis he came to personally, after his wife's death in 1993, through the conviction that she was still present in him and in their children in more than a metaphorical sense.

Write a dialogue in which two equally well-equipped philosophers argue over that thesis, anchored in the following case. Iris has spent twenty years embedding herself in a different household each summer, deliberately installing her habits of attention, her jokes, her way of reasoning aloud. A degenerative condition is meanwhile erasing her own autobiographical memory. By the end, one host — Nadia — has absorbed her so thoroughly that Iris can no longer tell whether a given recollection is hers or Nadia's report of the same conversation.

One speaker defends the Hofstadterian line: selfhood admits of degrees, requires no single substrate or continuous body, and what Nadia now carries is literally part of Iris rather than merely a trace of her influence. The other resists: a self is essentially the one stream unified in co-consciousness at a time, so Iris has produced influence plus a powerful pattern of grief and recognition — not survival.

Have them press each other on whether influence and survival admit of a principled distinction or collapse into a threshold on a continuum, on what a theory that allows degrees owes to transitivity, and on whether Iris should fear dying. No caricatures; end with a real concession from one side or a precise statement of where they now disagree.
