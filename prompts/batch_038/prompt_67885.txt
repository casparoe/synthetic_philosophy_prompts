I keep coming back to this asymmetry in contract law and I can't figure out what it's actually committed to, so I want to lay the whole thing out and see if you can tell me what's underneath it.

Say I contract to sell you a house, prices jump, and I refuse to sign the deed over. You can get a court to order me to convey it, on the standard reasoning that land is unique and money can't put you where you would have been. Now say I sign a three-year employment contract and walk out after six months because someone offered me more. My employer can sue me for the difference — what it costs them to replace me — but no court will order me back to the job. Personal service contracts are the classic exception to specific performance, and the reasons given are that a court can't supervise the quality of coerced work, that compulsion would be a hardship, and in the US that it runs into the 13th Ammendment's ban on involuntary servitude (Bailey v Alabama struck down a state law that made it a crime for a worker to take an advance and then quit).

But the same system does three other things that seem to cut the other way. It's perfectly happy to let me sell my labor-power for forty years on the assumption I'm handing over something I own. Most states will enforce a reasonable non-compete, and courts going back to an 1852 English opera case, Lumley v Wagner, will issue a negative injunction barring a singer or an athlete or a star executive from working for a rival for the rest of the term — while insisting this isn't just specific performance through the back door. And meanwhile it refuses outright to enforce the consensual sale of other things attached to a person: kidneys, votes, yourself into slavery, none of which are for sale at any price.

Here's the part I can't get straight. The damages rationale says labor is replaceable — the boss is made whole by hiring somebody else — which makes my working sound like a fungible commodity, no different from a shipment of wheat. The 13th Amendment rationale says nearly the opposite: that there's something about my working that can't be transferred the way a deed can. Both can't be the deep reason. And the negative injunction sits weirder than either, because the court won't make me exert myself but it will happily turn my *not* exerting myself somewhere else into an enforceable asset. My working hypothesis — tell me if this is too cute — is that what the doctrine is really committed to isn't the inalienability of labor at all but the alienability of exclusivity: it treats my future abstention from other employers as property the current one can hold, while treating my actual activity as something nobody can be made to supply. On that reading the law has no problem with Marx's distinction between labor and labor-power, so long as labor-power gets cashed out as a claim on what I won't do rather than on what I will.

So: reconstruct the theory of self-ownership and alienability that this practice only makes sense under, and be strict about it. Seperate out what the doctrine genuinely presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with — supervision costs and contempt-of-court practicalities might explain the whole pattern with no philosophy down there at all, and if that's the right answer I want to hear it. Say where the practice is underdetermined, meaning which rival views would license it equally: libertarian self-ownership, the line that persons can't be treated as things, the market-inalienability view that some goods can be had and used but not sold, and a Marxian reading on which the whole arrangement is exploitation dressed up as liberty. Then tell me what would concretely have to change in the doctrine if we swapped the presupposed view for (a) full commodification of labor, enforcing employment contracts the way we enforce land sales, or (b) thoroughgoing inalienabilism. And flag anywhere above where I've got the law wrong, since I'm reconstructing this from a contracts class a while back.

One thing I need from you: keep clear about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on contract theory and labor law and which are live disputes, and don't hand me a contested reading as though it were the consensus. If most specialists think my exclusivity hypothesis is confused, say that plainly rather than being polite about it. No citations or reading list needed, I just want the reconstruction.
