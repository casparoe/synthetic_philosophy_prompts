I work in fraud operations at a mid-size payments company, so not an AI person by training, but I've gotten way too deep in the AI safety stuff over the last year or so. Mostly the "control" side of it — the crowd that says don't bother promising the model is aligned, just assume it might be working against you and build cages: seperate monitor models reading its outputs, tripwires that kill the process, restricted tool access, no internet, all that. The architecture feels familiar to me, which is exactly what's bugging me, because I think one piece got imported over from my day job that doesn't survive the trip.

Here's the piece. In fraud, a huge share of our actual security is that the attacker doesn't know the rules. We deliberately keep the detection logic opaque and we rotate it, because if a fraudster knew precisely what pattern trips the alarm he'd just step one inch inside of it and we'd never see him. Opacity is the security, more or less. Now the control people propose basically the same shape for a possibly-misaligned system: say a company runs a coding agent with full repo and shell access, and sitting behind it is a monitor model that reads the agent's outputs and commands looking for signs it's trying to copy weights out, patch its own scaffolding, or reach a third party. But the watcher and the watched came out of the same factory. Same training corpus, same broad architecture, overlapping evals, probably trained by people who read the same papers about what "deceptive" looks like. So the monitored model is in a weirdly strong position to model its monitor — to just generate, internally, a pretty good guess of what would get flagged — in a way no fraudster can ever guess my rules. Which makes me suspect the concealment trick can't be ported over at all, and worse, that porting it anyway makes these setups look safer on paper than they are in practice.

So: argue it. As hard as you honestly can, make the case that monitor opacity — keeping the monitored system in the dark about what's being checked and how — is simply not available as a design resource in the AI control setting, and that any control scheme whose safety rests on it is worse than no scheme, because it converts a known unknown into a confident false negative. Steelman it properly, don't just gesture at "the model might figure it out." I want the mechanism spelled out, and I want you to say what you think actually follows for someone building one of these systems: if opacity can't be had, what does the load-bearing part of the design become — hard capability limits, making the tripwires public and eating the adaptation cost, something else entirely. Then commit. Pick the option you think is right and defend it, don't lay out a menu and leave me holding it. And at the end, tell me straight where you think your own argument is thinnest and which single counterargument would most likely break it, because I've got a coworker who already thinks I'm wrong and I want to know if he has a point. American English, please.
