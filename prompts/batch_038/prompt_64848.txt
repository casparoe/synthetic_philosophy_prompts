Next month I am teaching a fifty-minute clinical ethics seminar to twelve second-year internal medicine residents on the authority of an advance directive when the person who wrote it and the person it now governs seem to want opposite things. In six weeks they will be drafting these documents at bedsides, so the aim is a usable structure rather than a settled position. I do not want the argument explained to me; I want a plan for how to run the explanation.

The case I intend to build the session around: Rosalind, now 79, wrote at 68, in her own hand, that if she ever reached a point where she could no longer recognize her daughter or hold a conversation, she did not want antibiotics for pneumonia or any other infection, "even if I seem happy then, and even if I ask to be treated." Her dementia has reached exactly that point. She does not recognize her daughter, she speaks in single words, and by every caregiver's account she is cheerful: she hums, she holds hands, and when asked in the simplest possible terms whether she wants to live, she says yes. Three days ago she developed aspiration pneumonia, which a course of antibiotics would very likely clear. Her daughter asks that the document be honored; the covering resident wants to treat.

I am also considering a second case, in which the directive is vague about the trigger — the writer refused "to go on as a burden, dependent and out of touch with the people I love," and the patient is now out of touch with everyone, is not a burden in any material sense because her care is privately paid, and is content. Please tell me which of the two should carry the session, and what a carrier case for this topic must contain and must conspicuously lack. A related design question: in the first case the directive's condition is unambiguously satisfied, which may make the residents' work too easy. Does that clarity help or hurt, and what changes in the rest of the plan if I use a directive whose condition is only contestably met?

Beyond that, I want: the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependence relations stated — what has to be in place before what will even register. One distinction I assume belongs early is the one between experiential and critical interests, between what makes her days pleasant and what made her life the life she took herself to be living; tell me whether it should be handed to them as vocabulary or elicited from the case, and whether I am wrong about its position. Tell me where to raise the question of whether the demented patient is still the same person: I fear that raising it early collapses the session into metaphysics, but I suspect the residents' instinct to treat the document as decisive already rests on an answer to it. Give me the misconceptions they will arrive with and the ones they will form during the hour, each with the exact point in the sequence where it must be headed off. My own list so far is that this is really a legal question, that a change of preferences voids the document, that everything turns on whether she is "still her," that her apparent cheerfulness settles what is in her interest, and — the one I most fear — that the whole exercise implies a life with dementia is not worth living. Add the ones I have missed.

There is also an asymmetry I do not know where to put: the two answers wrong different people at different times, and one of them wrongs someone who cannot register the wrong. Honoring the document ends the life of a patient who appears to want to go on; setting it aside leaves a life organized against everything the earlier self cared about, while harming no one capable of experiencing the harm. Say whether to present that as decisive or as something to be resisted, and where it goes.

Then: one closing question whose answers will separate residents who have grasped the structure from residents who have merely picked a side, together with what the two most revealing wrong answers would sound like. And what I should refuse to teach at this level and say so openly — identity theory, the empirical literature on how reliably wellbeing is reported in advanced dementia, jurisdictional variation in directive law — including a sentence for each that declines it without seeming evasive.

Finish by stating how confident you are in the ordering you recommend, and single out the recommendation you are least sure of, particularly if it depends on a contested claim about the wellbeing of people with advanced dementia rather than on a settled one.
