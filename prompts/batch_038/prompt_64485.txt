I am preparing a seminar handout on the methodological apparatus of Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments*, and I would like help working through a passage that most commentary passes over in silence because it looks merely programmatic. It comes from the opening of Part VII, "Of Systems of Moral Philosophy" — first the introduction to Section I, "Of the Questions which ought to be examined in a Theory of Moral Sentiments," and then two paragraphs from the introduction to Section II, "Of the different Accounts which have been given of the Nature of Virtue." I quote from a public-domain online transcription; ellipses mark omitted sentences, and the punctuation follows that transcription rather than any modern critical edition.

> From some one or other of those principles which I have been endeavouring to unfold, every system of morality that ever had any reputation in the world has, perhaps, ultimately been derived. As they are all of them, in this respect, founded upon natural principles, they are all of them in some measure in the right. But as many of them are derived from a partial and imperfect view of nature, there are many of them too in some respects in the wrong.
>
> . . .
>
> In treating of the principles of morals there are two questions to be considered. First, wherein does virtue consist? Or what is the tone of temper, and tenour of conduct, which constitutes the excellent and praise-worthy character, the character which is the natural object of esteem, honour, and approbation? And, secondly, by what power or faculty in the mind is it, that this character, whatever it be, is recommended. to us? Or in other words, how and by what means does it come to pass, that the mind prefers one tenour of conduct to another, denominates the one right and the other wrong; considers the one as the object of approbation, honour, and reward, and the other of blame, censure, and punishment?
>
> We examine the first question when we consider whether virtue consists in benevolence, as Dr Hutcheson imagines; or in acting suitably to the different relations we stand in, as Dr Clarke supposes; or in the wise and prudent pursuit of our own real and solid happiness, as has been the opinion of others.
>
> We examine the second question, when we consider, whether the virtuous character, whatever it consists in, be recommended to us by self-love, which makes us perceive that this character, both in ourselves and others, tends most to promote our own private interest; or by reason, which points out to us the difference between one character and another, in the same manner as it does that between truth and falsehood; or by a peculiar power of perception, called a moral sense, which this virtuous character gratifies and pleases, as the contrary disgusts and displeases it; or last of all, by some other principle in human nature, such as a modification of sympathy, or the like.
>
> . . .
>
> The different accounts which have been given of the nature of virtue, or of the temper of mind which constitutes the excellent and praise-worthy character, may be reduced to three different classes. According to some, the virtuous temper of mind does not consist in any one species of affections, but in the proper government and direction of all our affections, which may be either virtuous or vicious according to the objects which they pursue, and the degree of vehemence with which they pursue them. According to these authors, therefore, virtue consists in propriety.
>
> . . .
>
> The character of virtue, it is evident, must either be ascribed indifferently to all our affections, when under proper government and direction; or it must be confined to some one class or division of them. The great division of our affections is into the selfish and the benevolent. If the character of virtue, therefore, cannot be ascribed indifferently to all our affections, when under proper government and direction, it must be confined either to those which aim directly at our own private happiness, or to those which aim directly at that of others. If virtue, therefore, does not consist in propriety, it must consist either in prudence or in benevolence. Besides these three, it is scarce possible to imagine that any other account can be given of the nature of virtue. I shall endeavour to show hereafter how all the other accounts, which are seemingly different from any of these, coincide at bottom with some one or other of them.

What I need is not summary but analysis, on five points.

1. Reconstruct the final quoted paragraph as an explicit argument — premises, the disjunction it turns on, conclusion — and assess it. My own suspicion, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the division is not neutral: the first horn is already stated as ascription to all affections "under proper government and direction," which is precisely the propriety account Smith favours, so the trichotomy is generated by treating his own answer as the general case and his rivals as restrictions of it to one class of affections. If that is right, is the argument question-begging, or is it a legitimate theoretical claim of a familiar kind — that rival theories are recovered as limiting cases of a more general one? Say what Smith would have to demonstrate to earn the latter reading, and whether the quoted text gives him any of it.

2. Stress-test the exhaustiveness claim against accounts that do not obviously fall under any of the three heads: a view on which virtue consists in obedience to the will of God; a Stoic view on which it consists in living in agreement with nature; Mandeville's; and — deliberately anachronistic, offered only as a test case — a view on which virtue consists in the strength of a rational will acting from duty. For each, say which horn Smith must assign it to, and whether the assignment preserves what is distinctive about the rival view or quietly dissolves it. Which of these, if any, shows that the closing sentence ("coincide at bottom") is doing escape-hatch work?

3. Explicate the relation between the two questions. Are they independent for Smith, or does answering the second in his own way settle the first? Relatedly, his own answer is placed last and in the most hedged language in the passage — "some other principle in human nature, such as a modification of sympathy, or the like." What is "modification" doing: is sympathy a modification of some further principle, or is his view a modification of the moral-sense account he has just listed? And why would Smith describe his own position in the residual, catch-all slot?

4. Gloss the phrases that carry weight and are easy to misread in a modern idiom, giving the eighteenth-century sense and noting where a contemporary reader would go wrong: "ascribed indifferently to all our affections"; "the tone of temper, and tenour of conduct"; "the natural object of esteem, honour, and approbation"; "founded upon natural principles"; "the view or aspect of nature." On the third of these in particular: if virtue is the *natural* object of esteem, does Smith presuppose a fittingness relation antecedent to our sentiments, and is that consistent with answering the second question sentimentally? Treat "tone of temper" and "tenour of conduct" as a possible disposition-plus-pattern-of-manifestation pair and say whether the three candidate answers to the first question are even answering the same kind of question about the same kind of thing.

5. Finally, assess the ecumenical claim in the first quoted paragraph. What epistemic status does "they are all of them in some measure in the right" have — is it a finding, or a stipulation that no reputable system can be wholly mistaken? Describe what a system of morals would have to look like in order to resist assimilation to Smith's principles, and say whether his methodology in Part VII could in principle be falsified, or whether the burden is discharged entirely by the promised demonstration in the last sentence quoted.
