Quick one, and sorry if it's a weird ask — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so you're going to have to carry the technical weight here.

I'm about 90k words into a book. Two of the main characters are brothers. One is a metaphysician, a card-carrying Lewisian modal realist — the kind who insists possible worlds are concrete, real in exactly the way ours is, and spatiotemporally cut off from it, and who gets visibly annoyed at anyone who treats worlds as sets of sentences or maximal propositions. The other brother is the narrator, a carpenter, no patience for any of it.

There's a scene at their father's wake. The metaphysician gets pushed into saying a few words, panics, and instead tells a joke. I need the joke.

Problem is, everything I've drafted so far is one of two failures. Either it's the generic "there's a world where X" gag — Lewis walks into a bar, in some other world he doesn't, ha — which my one academic friend says is what you get from someone who's heard of possible worlds but doesn't know what's actually being claimed. Or it's the sentimental one, the "somewhere out there Dad's still alive" bit, and my friend says the character could never say that even as a joke, because on his own view the man in the other world isn't his father, it's a counterpart — a different guy who resembles Dad in whatever respects the context makes salient. Nothing is literally in two worlds. He'd think that consolation is a category error, and he'd be right to.

So: write the joke he tells. Speakable, setup and punchline, under about 60 words, and the laugh has to come out of the actual content of the view — the indexicality of actual, i.e. that it works like "here" or "now" and nobody's world is the one true one, and/or the counterpart business — not out of the bare fact that there are lots of worlds. It also has to work as a thing a man says at his own father's wake: funny, its a bit ugly, and it lets him get through the next ten minutes without crying.

Then give me:

1. Two alternates. One that only the four philosophers in the room would actually get, and one that a carpenter and a roomful of relatives could laugh at without knowing any metaphysics.
2. For each of the three, two or three sentences on what makes it land — I need to know which bits are load-bearing so I don't sand them off when I revise, and so I can defend it to my academic friend.
3. A note on which one would get a real laugh at an actual philosophy department party and which one only gets a groan. I want the character to be completely right about the theory and completely wrong about his audience, and I'd like to write that gap into the scene after the joke.

Also, don't put any words in the real Lewis's mouth unless you're sure they're his — paraphrase him, don't quote him — and skip the reading list, I can't check any of it and I don't want to.
