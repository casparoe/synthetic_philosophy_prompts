I've been trying to work out when it's actually reasonable to just believe what somebody tells you, and I want to see if my gut reactions add up to a principle. Here's what I actually think, case by case:

- A stranger at the station says my train's on platform 4: I believe him and walk off, even though I know nothing about him and can't see the board from where we're standing.
- A stranger at the same station says a certain supplement cures migraines: don't believe him for a second.
- My doctor says the biopsy came back benign: believe her completely, and I couldn't follow her reasoning even if she spelled it out.
- My uncle says the moon landing was staged: don't believe him, even though he's the most honest guy I know and he's usually dead right about engines and weather.
- A six year old says a dog bit her at the park: believe her. Same kid, same earnest face, says there's a monster under her bed: don't.
- Plumber says the entire pipe run has to be replaced: half believe him at best, becuase he's the one who gets paid for the extra work.
- Basically all climate scientists say the planet is warming: believe it fully, and you could argue their career incentives are way bigger than the plumber's.
- A friend tells me about a fight she had with her brother: I believe the gist, but I don't believe the exact words she quotes him saying, even though she's the only one who was there.

My default rule has been something like "believe people unless you've got a specific reason not to," but that clearly doesn't cover the uncle or the monster, where I don't need to know anything about the person to reject what they said — I reject it just from what was said. So is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't deliver and whether I should give one of them up instead of making the rule more complicated. Then tell me what it says about the one I keep going back and forth on: my brother in law is a hypochondriac but not a liar, and he says he's been getting chest pains — do I believe him the way I'd believe my sister saying the same thing, and if not, what exactly is doing the work there?

One more thing that doesn't fit and maybe should. When a coworker told me a meeting had moved and it hadn't, I wasted an afternoon but I don't feel like I did anything wrong. When a financial advisor told me to put money somewhere and it cost me real cash, I feel like I should have checked first. Same structure as far as I can tell — an informant, no way for me to verify, it turned out bad — but my verdicts are different. Does the principle have to explain that, or is one of those two feelings just a mistake?
