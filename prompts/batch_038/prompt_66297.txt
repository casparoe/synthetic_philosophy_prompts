I have reconstructed an argument that is meant to refute epiphenomenalism about phenomenal character, and while testing it I found that a generalized version of it proves considerably more than I am willing to accept. I would like a diagnosis. Please write in American English.

The argument, as I have it:

(1) If a phenomenal state has no causal powers, then nothing about that state can figure in the causal explanation of any belief, including the belief that one is in that state.
(2) Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal states have no causal powers; the physical events underlying them do all the causing.
(3) A belief counts as knowledge only if the fact that makes it true figures in the causal explanation of the belief's having that content rather than some other content.
(4) So on epiphenomenalism, no belief about one's own phenomenal states is knowledge; at best such beliefs are reliably and luckily correct.
(5) But my belief that I am in pain right now is the paradigm case of knowledge — if that one fails, my claim to know anything at all is in jeopardy.
(6) Therefore epiphenomenalism is false.

Taken by itself that strikes me as a good argument, and I am prepared to accept it. The trouble is what happens when (3) is applied without restriction:

(7) By (3), for any subject matter, if the facts of that domain cannot figure in the causal explanation of our beliefs about it, we have no knowledge of it.
(8) Arithmetical facts cannot figure in any causal explanation, since abstract objects are causally inert; the same is true of facts about unactualized possibilities and of counterfactuals; and, on the non-reductive physicalist's own account, higher-level facts about beliefs and desires are excluded from causal work by the microphysical facts that realize them, which is the exclusion reasoning that made epiphenomenalism attractive to begin with.
(9) So we have no arithmetical knowledge, no modal knowledge, and no knowledge of our own propositional attitudes either.
(10) Worse still, (3) undermines the case for the view it was supposed to rescue. The conceivability argument and the knowledge argument both begin from what our reports about experience tell us, and if those reports are not knowledge, those arguments have lost their premises.
(11) So the argument against epiphenomenalism, generalized, delivers something closer to eliminativism about the phenomenal than to a vindication of it: it consumes the reports I was trying to protect and then goes on to consume mathematics.

I keep suspecting that the damage is done somewhere between (3) and (8), but each time I try to isolate it, (3) looks like nothing more than the denial that a belief can be knowledge by sheer coincidence with the facts — and I do not want to deny that. I am most attached to (5), and I am unwilling to abandon the thought behind (3) that knowledge requires something stronger than a happy match between belief and world.

What I want from you: identify the step or steps that fail, and say precisely why each failed step nevertheless looked unobjectionable where it first appeared. Address in particular whether (3) is being read consistently across (4) and (8), whether the inference from the phenomenal state's causing nothing to the fact's playing no explanatory role is licensed when a psychophysical law guarantees covariation between the state and the belief, and whether the non-reductive physicalist in (8) is genuinely in the same predicament as the epiphenomenalist or is owed something by the realization relation that the epiphenomenalist cannot claim. Then price the alternatives: for (3) itself, for (1), for (5), and for the unrestricted generalization in (7), state what a defender gives up by rejecting it and which of those rejections is cheapest all things considered. I am not looking for references or a reading list — I have the literature and it is not what is missing. I want the argument taken apart.
