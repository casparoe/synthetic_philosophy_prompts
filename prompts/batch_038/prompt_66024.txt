I'm a software engineer, and I have no philosophy background at all, so please treat what follows as a layman's reconstruction and correct it wherever I've mangled the source rather than deferring to me. For about eighteen months I led a project that automated most of what my team did manually: incident triage, deployment verification, capacity checks. The stated rationale, which I wrote myself, was that it would free people for higher-value work. The team went from eleven to eight over the following year, the work was redistributed rather than removed, and the two people who left were among the best I'd worked with. Nobody in leadership lied, exactly, and I can't articulate what went wrong. In March I went to a repertory screening of Fritz Lang's Metropolis with a live organ accompaniment, more for the music than the film, and it has nagged at me ever since; I watched the 2010 restoration and I know it was adapted from Thea von Harbou's novel and that the surviving versions differ. Afterward I spent a week reading secondary summaries of Marx on machinery, which is the only Marx I've read.

Here is what I want you to work with. On the Marx side: the machinery chapters of Capital, Volume I, with their account of the machine as fixed capital, as congealed dead labour that confronts and subordinates the living worker, as the instrument for repressing workers' resistance, and as the arrangement that turns the worker into a mere appendage of a mechanism which moves on its own; and alongside that, the 1857–58 Grundrisse notebooks, particularly the stretch people call the Fragment on Machines, with the general intellect and the claim that capital is itself a moving contradiction, in that it presses to reduce labour time to a minimum while continuing to posit labour time as the sole measure and source of wealth. On the film side, please be concrete about sequences rather than about themes in the abstract: the shift-change marches with their rows of identical, bowed men; the explosion in the machine hall and Freder's hallucination of the machine as Moloch with workers being fed into its mouth; Freder taking the place of a collapsed man at the machine; Maria's sermon in the catacombs and her parable of the tower whose planners and builders could not understand one another; Rotwang's laboratory; the false Maria dancing for the rich in the Yoshiwara club; her burning, which reveals the machine underneath; the flooding of the workers' city; and the reconciliation on the cathedral steps, where the son joins the hands of the city's master and the foreman, with the closing intertitle saying that the mediator between head and hands must be the heart.

My own thesis, which I want you to test and, if it fails, to dismantle: the film contains two incompatible pictures of automation and does not know that it holds both. The Heart-Machine is Marx's machinery almost exactly — dead labour consuming living labour — and notably the film never once shows a job being eliminated; the machine requires the same or more human attendance after the disaster than before it, and Freder's own descent is a descent into labour intensification rather than displacement. The robot is something categorically different. It is never put to work, it enters no production process, it transfers no value, and Fredersen commissions it for a political purpose, to impersonate and discredit Maria. On Marx's categories it is not fixed capital at all; I suspect it is a counterfeit of labour, a fake worker, and that the concept belongs to a later century's thinking about simulation rather than to Marx, though I haven't read any of that either. So: does this reading survive, or am I distorting Marx by converting a claim about ideology into a claim about technology, and would the robot be better handled as ideology given a body, or as the general intellect sitting in an inventor's workshop and then requisitioned by the man who owns the city? Relatedly, is Rotwang the villain or the instrument, given that the order comes from Fredersen — I keep seeing the film described as blaming the machine, which strikes me as a misreading. And what does the handshake actually settle? If the contradiction is structural rather than a failure of mutual comprehension between head and hands, is that ending a resolution or a refusal to have one? Finally, tell me where the film resists the whole approach: its expressionist surfaces, its religious register, its evident delight in the beauty of the machine, the arguments people make about the politics of its mass choreography. I'd like this as criticism rather than as a survey — a couple of thousand words — and I'd like it to end by saying what, if anything, the film shows about automation that Marx's account does not capture. Please don't give me a bibliography, and don't reconstruct quotations from Marx or from the intertitles; if you're unsure about a passage or a scene, say so and work around it.
