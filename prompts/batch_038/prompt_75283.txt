I am in the third year of a doctorate, and my corpus is a small and rather ugly one: a handful of narrative sources, two notarial registers, a communal statute book, one funerary inscription, and a fifteenth-century epitome of a lost partisan history, all bearing on a disputed succession in a northern Italian commune in the 1340s. My methods seminar this term has run from Herodotus and Thucydides to present-day debates about testimony, and I have been doing something I half suspect is naive. I built a spreadsheet in which every report is scored for the author's access, his partisanship, and his independence from the other witnesses, and the resulting aggregate determines how much weight the report carries. The spreadsheet keeps outputting verdicts I find unacceptable, so I have gone back to my own judgments case by case and written them down. One thing from the seminar is stuck in my head and may be relevant: a classmate argues the whole enterprise is confused, because the ancient historian everyone treats as the paradigm of rigor tells his readers outright that he composed the speeches himself, rendering what the situation seemed to call for rather than what was said, and Herodotus's famous disclaimer — that recording what he is told is his business, while believing it is none of his business — is on this reading not a modest confession but a device for buying the reader's trust. If she is right, the disclaimers that make a source feel honest are the very features that should make us suspicious of it. I have not been able to decide whether I believe her.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Two chronicles narrate the same siege in strikingly similar language, and the later author demonstrably owned a copy of the earlier one. This is a single testimony, not two: the second adds nothing, however exactly it agrees.
2. Two chronicles narrate the same siege, one plainly using the other for the outline, but each preserves an incidental particular the other lacks — one names the engineer who built the siege tower, the other gives the price of bread inside the walls. Here the pair is worth noticeably more than one witness, though not twice as much.
3. A chronicler reports a rout suffered by his own faction, including the fact that its commander fled first. Strongly credible; among the best evidence I have.
4. A different author prefaces his work with a long confession of his own past political errors and goes on reporting things that damage his own reputation. Not more credible at all, and possibly less. Once I see the confession as self-presentation, the fact that he says unflattering things about himself stops counting in his favor.
5. The inscription erected by the winning party to catalogue its own achievements: I do not discard it, but I use it only for what it gives away incidentally — the names of offices, the fact and rough date of the conflict — and not for its central claim.
6. An otherwise dependable author's troop numbers: I throw out the numbers and keep the battle.
7. Silences differ. A contemporary who wrote at length about the commune, had every occasion to mention a certain decree, and never does: I take that silence as genuine evidence against the decree. A fragmentary register that happens not to mention it: no evidence either way. The epitome that does not mention it: also no evidence — but I cannot articulate what separates the epitome from the complete contemporary, beyond the vague thought that the epitomator was cutting for length and had no opinion about the decree at all.
8. A tradition first committed to writing two centuries after the events, which nevertheless preserves a verse line whose metre and diction belong to the earlier period, and a district name that had long since fallen out of use. I credit this considerably more than its date of recording would suggest.
9. Two sources agree on a detail; both are hostile to the same man, and I suspect without being able to prove that both drew on a lost pamphlet attacking him. I discount heavily — but I am uneasy, because I have no idea how much weight a merely suspected common ancestor should subtract.

My own tentative principle is a costliness test, which I will state crudely and let you restate better: a report is credible in proportion to the degree to which, had it been false, the author would have exposed himself to contradiction by an audience with the means, the motive, and the opportunity to contradict him. Case 3 fits, since the fugitive commander and his kin were alive and in the city; case 5 fits, since a public monument is maximally exposed; case 7 partly fits, since a reader who knew of the decree could have objected. But case 8 is where it comes apart. Nobody living could have checked that verse line or that obsolete district name, so on my account the report is costless, and yet I trust it more rather than less. Something close to the opposite seems to be doing the work there: the details carry weight not because they were expensive to lie about but because they would have been prohibitively expensive to invent and cheap to pass along honestly. Cases 4 and 9 are awkward in ways I cannot yet name.

So: is there a principle, or a short ordered set of principles, that reproduces as many of these nine verdicts as possible? Please state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a report I have not yet seen — clause by clause, telling me which case in my list motivates each clause — and tell me explicitly whether credibility is a property of the source or of the individual proposition asserted, since 5 and 6 suggest it must be the latter while my spreadsheet assumes the former. I would also like to know whether costliness and the unfakeability notion belong in one principle or two, and if two, which takes priority when they conflict, as they do in 8. Then give me the principle's verdicts on two cases I have not ruled on: a source that matches verifiable evidence in all ten places where I can check it and then asserts something I cannot check at all — does the batting average carry over, and if so, across which domains, since being right about topography may say nothing about being right about motives; and two hostile witnesses who agree on an incidental detail that serves neither of their interests and is mildly damaging to both. Finally, tell me plainly which of my nine verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and I ought to abandon. I would rather be shown that 3 and 4 are the same case seen from two angles, or that 8 has to go, than be handed an apparatus elaborate enough to save everything I happen to believe.
