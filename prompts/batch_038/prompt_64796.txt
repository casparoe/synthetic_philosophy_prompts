I write novels for a living, which is presumably why I was asked to chair the jury of our country's principal nonfiction prize. There are five of us. We meet on the fourteenth to settle the shortlist and, if the room allows it, the award; I must circulate a written opinion by the eleventh. I would like you to draft that opinion for me, in my voice.

The case. One of the six shortlisted books is a memoir whose hinge is an event at her mother's deathbed that the author describes as a vision and takes to have been an encounter with God. She trained as a laboratory scientist and left the field twenty years ago; the book is exact and unsentimental about the ward, the medications, and the hour, and it neither argues for the event nor explains it away. It simply reports it as the thing that reorganized her life, and one of its consequences, which she describes without emphasis, is that she forgave a brother she had not spoken to in two decades. When an interviewer asked whether any natural explanation of the event would change her mind, she replied that an explanation would not stop the event from being the reason she forgave him.

Two of my colleagues want the book off the shortlist, and their argument is not crude. The charter instructs the jury to consider whether a book tells the truth about the life it recounts, and they hold that a prize which cannot even state what would count as checking the central claim cannot apply that instruction to it; awarding the book, they say, would be indistinguishable from a public institution vouching for a miracle, and the prize carries state money. One of them has proposed instead that we create a parallel category for spiritual writing, which a fourth juror calls a polite way of shelving the book forever. The fifth juror observes that last year's winner was a memoir of the loss of faith, whose central claim is no more checkable, and that no one proposed excluding it.

Draft the opinion so that it does the following.

First, distinguish at least three different things my colleagues could mean when they say the book cannot be judged as nonfiction — that we cannot assess the truth of the vision, that we cannot assess the book, that we cannot rank it against books whose claims are checkable — and say which of these they actually need for their motion, and whether they have it.

Second, take seriously that the author does not offer the event as evidence for anything and would not treat an explanation as a refutation. Say what sort of commitment that is, whether it amounts to a claim a jury can judge at all, and what a jury is judging when it judges a book rather than a belief: the honesty of the account, its authority over a reader, the truth of its propositions, or some mixture the prize has never had to name until now.

Third, address the difference between the two jurors in my own room. They do not appear to contradict each other about any fact either could check, and yet they are not merely using the word God differently. Say what their disagreement is about, if it is about anything, and whether a vote is the right instrument for settling it.

Fourth, tell me whether the comparison with last year's winner holds.

Then commit. Recommend one of the four motions actually available to me on the fourteenth — award, exclude, reclassify, or shortlist with a citation stating what we are judging — give the reason in terms a colleague could reply to, and include a short paragraph stating the strongest version of the losing argument together with what it costs my recommendation, since I will be voted down if I pretend that argument does not exist.

I should add that I have been rereading Wittgenstein on religious belief, and I suspect the argument in our jury room is a version of the confusion he describes. Use those remarks as instruments. Do not lecture on them, do not attribute anything to anyone, and include no citations, notes, or reading list: this is a document five tired people will read in ten minutes and then vote on. Plain prose, no jargon, and end with two or three sentences I could read aloud to the room without embarrassment. Do not settle for declaring the dispute merely verbal, and do not retreat into the observation that reasonable people differ. I need a motion.
