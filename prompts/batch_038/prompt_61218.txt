I am a sixth-form student and the secretary of my school's philosophy society. We meet once a week for thirty minutes, with a regular attendance of about fifteen students aged sixteen to eighteen; none of them has studied philosophy formally, two are strong biologists, one has read a good deal of popular neuroscience, and every one of them will arrive assuming, without ever having considered the assumption, that there is somewhere inside their own head from which they watch. I have a whiteboard and a laptop and no handouts. Next Thursday it is my turn to lead, and my topic is Daniel Dennett's rejection of what he calls the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model he offers in its place. I have read enough of Consciousness Explained to know the view; what I do not know is how to sequence it for this room. I want a plan for the session, not an explanation of the theory, and I am not looking for further reading.

My draft order is this: begin with the facts that visual processing is distributed across many specialised areas and that there is a measurable delay between a stimulus reaching the retina and a subject's being able to report it; then introduce the theater as the picture those facts are supposed to refute; then present multiple drafts; then close with a demonstration. Tell me whether this order holds, and where its dependencies are broken. I suspect that opening with the neuroscientific facts makes the thesis look like an empirical discovery about the visual system, which I do not think it is, but I cannot say why, and I need to know what has to be in place before those facts can do any work at all.

For the demonstration I have two candidates. The first is the illusion in which two differently coloured dots are flashed in quick succession at different positions and observers report a single dot that travels across and changes colour partway along its path; I can run this on my laptop. The second is a tactile one I found described in passing: a rapid series of taps at the wrist followed by a series at the elbow is reported as taps spread evenly along the forearm, including at points that were never touched. Decide which of these two should carry the whole session, say what the chosen example must have in order to do that and what it must not invite, and say what the losing one fails at.

Then give me the misconception map for this particular audience: what they will bring with them, what they will construct during the session, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. Two members have already told me they expect the upshot to be that consciousness is an illusion; I am certain another will say the thesis amounts to nothing more than that the brain processes information in parallel, which is true and needs no thirty minutes.

I also want two specific judgements. Someone will ask who is watching if there is no theater, and I need to know whether that question is answered by a well-ordered session or is evidence that the ordering has failed. And I have come across his distinction between two ways of accounting for the colour-change illusion — one in which the brain settles the story before anything is experienced, and one in which the record is rewritten afterwards — together with his claim that no introspective evidence could tell the two apart. Say whether that belongs in a first session for this audience or should be cut, and if it belongs, where.

Close the plan with one question I can put to the room at the end whose answers would show me whether the idea has landed, rather than whether the phrase "no Cartesian theater" can be repeated back to me. Finally, tell me what I should refuse to take up at this level — I assume the hard problem of consciousness, and whatever he says about qualia — and how to decline those openly, since this audience will read a refusal to discuss something as a concession that it cannot be answered.
