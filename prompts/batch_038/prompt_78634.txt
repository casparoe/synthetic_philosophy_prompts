I am running a 100-minute session of a second-year metaphysics seminar — fourteen students, one prior week on vagueness, no exposure to metaontology — and I want them to arrive at the verbal/substantive distinction by getting trapped in one, not by being told about it. Please design the session as a runnable activity rather than as an outline of points.

What I can supply: a whiteboard, index cards, one printed sheet per student. No technology, no real money as stakes.

The core mechanic I want built: a term-elimination test of the kind used to diagnose a merely verbal dispute. Participants deliberate as a committee over a case in which a contested predicate decides a concrete institutional outcome — a heritage plaque, an insurance payout, a competition ruling, something with a deadline attached. At a point you specify, the committee is placed under a ban on the contested word and its cognates, and each side must restate the disagreement using a deliberately narrow permitted lexicon: parts, spatial arrangement, history of use, causal consequences, and recorded dispositions of speakers to apply labels. Each side must also state the other side's position in that lexicon to the other side's satisfaction before it may argue further. Design that lexicon precisely — tell me exactly which words are permitted, which are banned, and why the boundary sits where you put it — and give the committee a decision it has to reach while still under the ban.

Then give me a second round that reverses the lesson: a case that looks purely terminological, where the ban applies cleanly, but where the restatements come out in genuine conflict about the world rather than about words. I want the students unable to generalise in either direction, so make the two rounds structurally parallel on the surface and explain to me, not to them, what actually differs. For this second round I would like an ontological rather than a classificatory dispute — something in the neighbourhood of whether a largely restored object survives as one thing, or whether a scattered collection is a single thing — so the activity also raises whether quantifier-involving ontological disputes are ever more than verbal, and sets up both the deflationary answer and the structural answer that denies it.

Please include: a minute-by-minute timeline; role assignments, including someone whose job is to enforce the ban and someone whose job is to force a decision; the restatement procedure written out as explicit rules I can hand to students on a card; what the group should come away seeing at the end of each round; and a debrief that maps named moments in the activity onto the idea that a dispute is merely verbal when it can be reformulated away, onto the limit cases where reformulation succeeds syntactically but leaves disagreement intact, and onto the standing disagreement between those who think ontological vocabulary is interchangeable and those who think some of it carves better than its rivals. You may name the positions only in the debrief.

Then, without softening it: three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with what I should say or do when each happens; the feature of the metaontological debate the activity cannot reproduce, which I will therefore have to supply by argument afterwards; and how you would re-run the same design for a group with no philosophy background at all.

Finally, treat the design as a claim you are defending rather than a package you are delivering. Identify the single assumption about how the second round will actually play out on which the whole session depends, tell me what I would hear or see in the room that would show that assumption is false, and give me one fallback round to keep in my pocket. And say what would change your mind about the design itself: which objection to the eliminability test, or which argument for one of the two metaontological verdicts the activity is meant to leave open, would if correct lead you to tell me not to run this at all — and where you think your own reasoning about the pedagogy is most likely to fail.
