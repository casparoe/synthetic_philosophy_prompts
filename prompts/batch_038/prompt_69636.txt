I'm on a philosophy of science course with a meteorology side module and something's bugging me. Weather centres run ensembles of a few dozen slightly perturbed runs, turn the spread into numbers like "30% chance of rain", and then score themselves on the calibration of those numbers over decades. The perturbations aren't sampled from anything real — they're engineered to mimic the errors in the analysis of the current state. Meanwhile the same people say the atmosphere is deterministic but chaotic, so errors in the starting point amplify exponentially and two weeks is a hard predictability horizon — and then they publish projections out to 2100 off the same equations.

So what does the calibration-scoring practice presuppose about probability? The atmosphere is in exactly one state, not a spread of them, so where is the 30% supposed to live? If it's just a measure of our ignorance, why is a badly calibrated forecast a genuine failure rather than bad luck — and what licenses the particular ignorance-distribution they picked? And is the practice consistent with a two-week limit sitting right next to a confident 2100 projection?

Please give me a committed answer, not a menu: tell me which account of chance the practice actually needs, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say what a weather service would have to stop doing if it took the line that there's no objective chance without indeterminism seriously. British English, if you don't mind.
