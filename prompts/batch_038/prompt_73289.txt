I'm three years into a translation studies PhD and our workshop has this artifact I've become increasingly suspicious of. Everyone in the group translates the same source poem — this term it's a short rhymed French Renaissance epigram, very short lines, the kind where the rhyme scheme is doing a lot of work — and then we score each draft on a grid: content fidelity (0–5), form fidelity (0–5: meter, rhyme scheme, stanza shape, line length), target-language poemness (0–5: does it read as a poem in English rather than like a decoded foreign text), and economy (0–5: did you pull it off without padding, without prose-y expansion, without leaning on compensations from outside the line). Sum them, sometimes weighted, top total gets the slot in the department anthology. Nobody designed the grid deliberately, it just accumulated, i don't think there was ever a vote on it.

I want you to reconstruct what theory of the poem this practice presupposes, and to be specific rather than gesturing at "fidelity is complicated." Does the additive structure commit us to the view that a poem is a bundle of separable features, such that you can be down two points on form and earn them back on content — i.e. that form and content are commensurable, at least at the level of the score? Does "poemness" as its own axis presuppose a language-general property a text can have that isn't reducible to the other three, and if so what is it doing sitting next to them in the same sum? And does "economy" presuppose that translation is transfer under a conservation constraint — that there's a fixed budget of poem to be distributed, so that padding is a kind of counterfeiting rather than a legitimate addition?

Then the part that actually bugs me, because the grid is inconsistent here and I cant tell whether the inconsistency is in the grid or in me. We happily allow compensation for form: lose a rhyme in line 3, pick one up in line 6, and form fidelity treats the poem as a total count of formal features, basically position-insensitive. But nobody would accept the same move for content — you can't drop an image from the source and add a different, prettier one later to balance the books; that gets scored as a miss twice over. So form is treated as fungible across the poem and content as locally anchored. What view of meaning licenses that asymmetry? Is there a theory on which rhyme really is a mobile quantity while imagery is fixed to its place, or is the grid just quietly ranking the two axes by a habit nobody has examined?

Related and worse: when the source rhymes two words that are semantically linked — the amour/toujours sort of move, where the rhyme is enacting a conceptual yoking rather than decorating it — the grid has literally nowhere to put it. You either score it as content and let the translator off the formal hook, or score it as form and miss the fact that losing the rhyme loses an argument. What would have to be true about meaning for that to be a genuine dilemma rather than an artifact of a four-axis spreadsheet?

Also tell me where the practice is underdetermined. Could someone who thinks form and content are strictly inseparable — that there's no bundle there to score at all — still run the grid as a purely diagnostic instrument without buying any of its metaphysics, or does using it for a decade turn you into a separatist whether you like it or not? And what does the grid strictly presuppose versus merely fit well with? I suspect at least one axis is philosophically optional and I can't work out which.

Finally: if we replaced the presupposed picture with the view that a poem is a particular event in a particular language, so an English rendering is at best a related performance and not a partial transfer of anything, what would have to change — the grid, the anthology, the way we assign credit for the slot, or the workshop itself?
