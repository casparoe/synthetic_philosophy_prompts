I serve on the science committee of a regional land trust. For six years our director of conservation science has made every call on whether we commission evidence before committing funds, and because we are about to reopen one of those calls I have tabulated her rulings.

1. We held a fixed-price option on a two-hundred-hectare parcel. She commissioned a fortnight of botanical survey at trivial cost; a listed orchid's presence would have made us exercise the option, its absence would have made us walk. It came back absent, and we walked.

2. A university group offered us, free of charge, a three-year hydrological study of a wetland on which we had by then signed an unconditional purchase contract. She declined. No possible result could have altered anything.

3. Drone photogrammetry would have given near-exact counts of a colonial nesting population, but required low passes over the colony during the nesting window, with a documented risk of flushing birds, and the coordinates would have entered a public database that a specimen-trading ring was known to harvest. She refused. The counts would have fed an annual management plan that we revise every year regardless.

4. A funder offered to pay in full for a genetic audit of our captive-breeding stock: accurate, timely, and directly relevant to that season's pairing plan, which we revise each season. The contractor reported first to the funder's board, and we had watched the same contractor soften a finding for another grantee the year before. She refused.

5. A colleague proposed a cheap six-week soil assay on land adjoining a village where we were about to sign an easement, the easement running ten years with a break clause at year three. A positive result would have triggered a statutory duty of disclosure and remediation costing roughly ten times the value of the easement; the board had already resolved to sign and had made plain it would fund nothing beyond the statutory minimum. She declined the assay, and we signed.

6. Twice she has spent money we did not have on evidence she had strong reason to expect would be unfavourable. On one occasion she put four per cent of the annual acquisition budget into a hydrogeological study of a site the entire staff wanted; it came back bad and killed the purchase.

7. She approved a peat-depth survey whose findings everyone expected to be embarrassing to a partner agency, on the written condition, agreed by the board before fieldwork began, that we would publish the full results whichever way they fell and implement the management change they implied.

She gave reasons for none of these beyond a phrase or two, and she signs the expected-value-of-information calculation our policy manual requires for every one of them, including the refusals. So the arithmetic is there; what is not obvious is what rule the arithmetic is being made to serve. I know the theorem behind it — the result usually credited to I. J. Good, that for an agent who conditionalizes on what she learns and then chooses what her posterior recommends, freely available information is never expected to leave her worse off — and I know the familiar ways it is said to fail: information that costs money or arrives too late, information whose acquisition changes the state of the world it reports on, information that triggers duties the agent would otherwise not have had, and agents whose later selves cannot be relied upon to do what the posterior recommends. What I cannot tell is which of these she is tracking, or whether she is tracking something not on that list.

The case we are reopening. We hold a ten-year management agreement over a coastal heath, renewable next spring, the landowner being a water company. That agreement funds something like forty per cent of our other work; the board has already decided to renew, and there is a break clause at year five. An independent university group has offered a six-week hydrological survey of the heath at a cost we can absorb without displacing anything, with results in hand two months before the decision deadline. The survey is methodologically clean, reports to us alone, carries no embargo, has no funder with a stake in the outcome, and will not be filtered or summarised by anyone else. It involves no disturbance to the site and no publication of locations. What it measures is habitat condition rather than a regulated pollutant, so no statutory duty attaches to any finding. Its result would determine which of three management options we fund, those options being budgeted annually and switchable at short notice; we would genuinely act on that choice whichever way it came out. The complication is this. One possible result is that the company's abstraction is the proximate cause of the heath's drying. We know in advance that the board will not raise such a finding with the landowner and will not permit us to publish it, and we know that we would comply. The other possible result confirms what we have already been telling our donors.

What I would like from you:

- Set out the two or three decision rules that are jointly consistent with all seven verdicts, stated precisely enough that some further case would falsify each of them, and note for each rule which of the seven it fits most awkwardly.
- Say what each rule predicts for the heath survey, and which single feature of that case is doing the work in each prediction.
- Sort the candidate rules into those that are simply Good's theorem applied to a more accurate description of the decision problem — the signal she will actually receive, the duties knowledge actually creates, the costs she actually bears — and those that are genuine departures from it. For each departure, name the assumption of the theorem it denies.
- Give your best guess at her verdict, argued from the track record rather than from which rule you find sounder in the abstract. If you think one of the seven verdicts already settles the matter, say which one and why.
- Describe one further case, no more complicated than those above, that would separate the two leading candidates from each other, and state what each of them would say about it.

I am not looking for references or a survey of the literature on this theorem; I want the rules, their verdicts, and the discriminating case.
