I run the anaesthesia and pain service at a veterinary teaching hospital, so basically my whole working life consists of scoring other species' faces. Cats and dogs mostly. We use validated composite scales — grimace scoring (ear position, orbital tightening, muzzle tension, whiskers, head position), then posture, activity, response to palpation of the surgical site, plus the physiologic side of it (HR, BP, sometimes cortisol, pupil diameter in the OR). At the end of that we produce a number and the number tells us whether to top up an opioid. The bit people outside the field don't really clock is that we never once get a self-report. Every single thing I'm willing to call pain is an inference from behaviour and numbers, and I've gotten pretty comfortable with that, honestly. Then my partner, who's doing a part-time bioethics degree, came home from a reading group last week and described this condition — pain asymbolia, I think is the name — where people with damage somewhere around the insula/parietal region say they do feel the pain, can tell you where it is and whether it's sharp or burning, but just aren't bothered by it. No withdrawal, no distress, and from what she said some of them will let you apply the same stimulous again. She says philosophers make a big deal out of it and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since, because if she described it right then my scales would score those people as comfortable.

So here's what I want. Take the argument about what exactly makes pain bad — the versions I've managed to gather second-hand are roughly (a) the badness is in the felt quality itself, so that even an unmoved patient is still having something intrinsically awful, (b) the badness is in the aversive pull, the fact that the experience commands or motivates you to make it stop, which would mean an asymbolic has the sensation but not the badness, and (c) some middle view where the feeling is bad because of what it tells you about your own body being under threat — and restate that whole three-way fight in my working vocabulary. Nociception versus pain, aversive behaviour and escape, action units, preemptive and rescue analgesia, wind-up and central sensitisation, dissociation under ketamine, conditioned place aversion, the operant stuff where an animal will press a lever or cross a grid to switch off a noxious input. I know my own field cold and I have no philosophy training whatsoever, so don't soften the clinical side for me, and don't explain what a composite scale is. I want to see what the philosophers are actually disagreeing about once it's phrased entirely in terms of things I can measure, block, or manipulate.

Then give me the residue report, and this is the part I care about most. Sort it into three buckets: what translates cleanly, what only translates if you bend it out of shape (say how), and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — not a hard-to-measure counterpart, no counterpart. Be careful here, because there's an obvious cheap way to do this assignment. My framework is already close to behaviourist: if it doesn't produce a face change, a HR bump, or an avoidance response, I treat it as not-pain. So you can't just quietly adopt that as the translation and then announce that the philosophers' disagreement evaporates, because that's smuggling my assumptions in and calling it a finding. Same in the other direction — don't import their assumptions and then tell me my scales are measuring the wrong thing as if that were a neutral result of the translation. Once you've done it honestly, tell me which side the leftover material indicts. Is the residue evidence that my instruments are structurally blind to something real, i.e. that a composite scale literally cannot tell the difference between no pain and pain that isn't minded, and that this is a hole I should worry about in my own practice? Or is it evidence that the philosophers' distinction is less stable than it looks — maybe "feels it but doesn't mind it" isn't a coherent description of any experience anyone could have, and the asymbolia cases are being over-read. And finish with the concrete version: suppose a dog lands on my table with an insular/parietal lesion, is being handled for a procedure that would normally score high, and comes back at a 2 out of 24 while I'm palpating the site. In my vocabulary, is there any sentence I can even write down that states the question of whether I should treat? If not, what's the closest thing I can say, and what gets lost in saying it that way.
