Okay, this got weird and personal and I need someone to tell me where the argument actually breaks, because I've been going in circles for about three weeks.

Context: I sit on a small grants committee at an animal welfare nonprofit. Five of us, roughly $400k a year, mostly farmed fish welfare and cage-free work. Last spring another member made the case that we've been ignoring insects entirely, and the argument went like this:

1. If you're unsure whether some being is sentient, you should weight its interests by your probability that it's sentient. Any other policy is a gamble that they aren't, and the odds aren't in your favour.
2. P(insects are sentient) is low but definitely not zero — call it 0.1, maybe lower, but nobody who's actually read the nociception and associative-learning literature thinks it's 0.
3. The number of insects our species affects, through pesticides, habitat conversion, climate, and just the sheer size of the population, is maybe a million times the number of farmed fish, pigs and chickens we affect.
4. So expected insect suffering is on the order of 100,000 times the expected suffering of the animals our entire portfolio is about.
5. So we should shift essentially the whole budget to insects.

I voted for it, mostly because I couldn't answer (4). Then in January the same person came back with the next rung, and he didn't mean it as a joke: soil nematodes. Individuals outnumber us by something like a trillion to one, so even at P(sentience) = 10^-4 the identical multiplication says nematodes swamp insects, and then it's mites and springtails and things in the leaf litter nobody has named. He's serious about this. And I realised I don't know which of us is wrong, because the arithmetic that moved me on insects moves him on everything smaller. It's got to the point where i stand on the pavement instead of the grass when I'm waiting for the bus, which is not a person I want to be.

So which step is bad? I've tried two objections and neither one held up. First I said all the numbers are made up — but he just said that changes the constants, not the shape, and the shape is what's doing the work. Second I said you can't multiply a probability by a population like that, because P(sentient) isn't a per-individual coin flip, it's one bet on the whole class: a million insects are either all in or all out, so there's no averaging happening. He said that's precisely why it's worse, since correlated risk doesn't diversify away, and expected value is linear regardless of how the probabilities correlate. I think he's right about that and I'm stuck again.

I'd rather keep (1) than (5) — I don't want to be someone who discounts the uncertain just because it's convenient. But if keeping (1) genuinely commits me to a budget with nothing we currently fund in it, tell me that plainly. And then price the alternatives for me: what does it cost to give up (1)? What does it cost to give up (2) — say, by denying that moral weight flips on all at once at the sentience line and instead comes in degrees, so that a 10^-4 credence buys you 10^-4 of the weight rather than the whole animal? Is there anything wrong with (4) itself, or with the way the population figures and the credences are being treated as two independent knobs? Which of those exits is the cheapest, and what has to be true for me to take it?

And if the honest answer is that the argument is fine and my colleague is right and I should just follow the math wherever it goes, say that too — but then say what you'd tell someone who has to keep running a grants committee while believing it.
