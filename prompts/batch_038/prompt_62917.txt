I host a departmental podcast in which we record a forty-minute conversation with each visiting colloquium speaker the morning after the talk. Next month's guest is a philosopher of language who defends what I would describe as audience-indexed pluralism about the norm of assertion. On her view the speech act is not constituted by a single rule but by a family of rules, and which one governs a given assertion is fixed by what the audience needs in order to act on what is said: something close to a knowledge requirement where the audience's stakes are high, and a weaker requirement of justified or warranted belief where they are low. She holds that this preserves the data the knowledge-first camp insists on — the impropriety of asserting a lottery proposition on statistical grounds alone, and the infelicity of pairing an assertion with a concession that one does not know what one has just said — while explaining away the cases usually pressed the other way, such as assertions made on misleading higher-order evidence where the speaker seems not to know yet also seems uncriticizable, and selfless assertions by speakers who have excellent warrant for what they say but do not believe it.

Please draft eight to ten questions for the conversation, ordered so that the pressure increases as we go. They should engage the machinery of her position rather than invite her to restate it, and at least one should address each of the following: whether a rule that shifts with the audience's situation can still be constitutive of a type, given that constitutive rules are ordinarily supposed to hold invariantly across tokens of the practice; how a speaker is to determine which rule applies when the audience's stakes are opaque, or when there is no determinate audience at all, as with published testimony or journalism — and whether that determination problem reintroduces the very cases she means to explain away; whether her plural norm is redundant, since it looks derivable from a norm on belief together with a conversational requirement of adequate evidence; and what her account predicts about the disclaimer concession, that is, whether that piece of infelicity supports a knowledge rule or something weaker.

For at least four of the questions, add the follow-up I should use if she gives the answer I most expect, and name that expected answer in a phrase before the follow-up. End with a question about what pattern of competent speakers' judgments would count against her view. Assume she knows the literature and does not need it recapitulated, but assume the listeners do not, so each question should still be intelligible to someone who knows only that philosophers disagree about what one must have in hand to assert something properly.
