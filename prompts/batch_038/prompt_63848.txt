I came at taxonomy from the wrong end, I think. Years of reading rationalist blogs left me with a very strong prior that arguing about definitions is never a real disagreement — you notice the word is fuzzy, you stipulate a sharper one, you move on, and anyone who keeps fighting is just attached to the sound of the word. Then I started reading about the species problem and it clearly doesn't work like that, and I can't figure out why not.

Situation as I understand it: people have been arguing about what a species is for something like seventy-plus years. Same organisms, same field data, same museum drawers, and no convergence. The biological species concept crowd say a species is a set of populations that do or could interbreed and are cut off from other such sets by reproductive isolation — and they insist isolation is the causal boundary, not a proxy for it. The phylogenetic/diagnosability crowd say a species is the smallest cluster you can actually pick out by fixed characters and that sits at the tip of a lineage, on the grounds that a category you can't diagnose isn't science, it's bookkeeping. Ecological types say the real unit is defined by its niche and by whatever selection keeps it together. The species-are-individuals people say all of these are making a category mistake, since a species isn't a class with defining properties at all but a spatiotemporally extended particular, so asking for the property is asking the wrong sort of question. Pluralists say several concepts are legitimate and you pick per taxon and per question, and their opponents say that's a surrender that makes counts incomparable across groups. And then there's the move that says there's one theoretical notion — separately evolving metapopulation lineages — with many operational criteria for delimiting it, which sounds like a dissolution until you notice the criteria still disagree in actual cases and someone still has to say which one wins.

My hypotheses, offered as hypotheses, and I expect at least one is dumb:

1. The stall is about paradigm cases, not facts. Each concept was built around somebody's favorite taxon — sexually reproducing birds, hybridizing plants, bacteria trading genes sideways, ancient asexual lineages — and each side treats the other's paradigms as pathological edge cases. So they're not disagreeing about the world, they're disagreeing about which slice of life the word is answerable to, and no data settles that.
2. It's partly verbal but the verbal part has teeth: the term does regulatory work in conservation listings and biodiversity counts, so there's a standing incentive to keep it ambiguous and to insist the dispute is substantive.
3. I'm wrong and there's a hidden empirical question nobody has actually run: how much of life really has the discrete cluster structure that every one of these concepts quietly assumes. If most of the tree is reticulate or continuous, the debate isn't stalled, it's just unanswered.

Diagnose it. Which of these, or something else entirely? Ground the diagnosis in the moves people actually make — what does a reproductive-isolation person say when you hand them a bdelloid rotifer or a prokaryotic taxon; what does a diagnosability person say when you hand them an oak genus that hybridizes freely but stays morphologically coherent; what does a pluralist say when you ask why one word should carry three meanings at once. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical result, a formal criterion for how discrete clusters have to be, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. And be explicit about where my definitions-are-cheap deflationism is right here and where it misleads me, because I genuinely can't tell if that prior is a tool or a blindfold.

Don't give me a tour of the concepts and don't bother with citations or a reading list — I have no web access and wouldn't look them up anyway. I want the structure of the disagreement, not the map.
