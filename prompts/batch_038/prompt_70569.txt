A colleague of mine — I'll call her H. — has spent the past year arguing about when an experience happens, and she has settled into a pattern of verdicts so consistent that I want to see whether the next one can be predicted. Her framework is the familiar pair of labels borrowed from political history and made standard in the philosophy of mind by Dennett: in an Orwellian case, the brain delivers an experience and then goes back and rewrites the record of it, so the subject is left holding a memory of something that never happened; in a Stalinesque case, the brain stalls, reconciling the evidence before anything is presented at all, so that nothing is ever rewritten and the finished verdict arrives as though it had been the plan all along. H. is also willing to say that a case is neither — that whatever changed was not the experience.

Her verdicts so far:

1. Two spots of light, one red and one green, flash a short distance apart in quick succession. The subject sees a single spot travel between them and change color somewhere in mid-journey, although the information about the second color cannot have arrived in time to affect the earlier part of the path. Stalinesque, she says: the system withheld presentation until the second flash landed.
2. Taps delivered at the wrist and then, a fraction of a second later, at the elbow are felt as a row of taps marching up the forearm at points where nothing was touched. Stalinesque, on the same grounds.
3. After you move your eyes to a clock, the second hand appears to hang for longer than a second. Stalinesque: the interval is filled in rather than edited.
4. A shape is flashed and then covered by a mask within a few tens of milliseconds. The subject insists they saw nothing there, yet their forced-choice guesses about the shape run better than chance. Still Stalinesque. Her gloss: that a system can guess above chance is no evidence that it ever had an experience and then lost it.
5. A subject picks the more attractive of two photographs; the experimenter swaps the card and hands back the one they did not pick; the subject, noticing nothing, produces reasons for a preference they never expressed. Orwellian. Her gloss: this is not a subject saying something false, it is a subject who no longer has the earlier state.
6. People who watch a film of a collision and are asked how fast the cars were going give higher figures when the question uses a more violent verb. Neither, she says: no one's experience of the collision has been rewritten; what moved is the number they will offer when asked.

The case put to her this week is one she has not yet ruled on. A letter is flashed briefly and masked; the subject names it correctly and confidently, at once. Forty seconds later the experimenter asks again, remarking in passing that people often confuse that letter with another from the same set. The subject now names the other letter, sincerely and with confidence, and neither corrects themselves nor treats the first answer as a slip; they simply give a different one. But ten minutes after that, shown both letters, told plainly that the experimenter's remark may have pushed them, and asked which they actually saw, they pick the original letter without hesitation and volunteer that the second answer had felt like a guess.

What I would like from you:

(a) The candidate rules that jointly fit all six of H.'s verdicts. I suspect at least two are in play — one keyed to how long after the event the change occurs, one keyed to whether the earlier state can still be recovered — but there may be others that do the work just as well, and I want the full set rather than my hunches confirmed.

(b) What each surviving rule implies about the new case, including any that agree on the outcome for different reasons.

(c) Your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals — and an explicit statement of what she would be committing herself to by ruling as you predict.

(d) Which of the six earlier cases is the closest analog to the new one, and precisely where that analogy strains.

(e) A single further case that would force the surviving rules apart, and why that one rather than another.

(f) Which of her existing verdicts is load-bearing: the one she would most have to abandon for the underdetermination to resolve, and what her position would collapse into if she gave it up.
