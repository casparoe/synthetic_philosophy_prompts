For seven years I have served on the standing review panel of a nonprofit that issues non-binding ethical opinions on privately funded off-world ventures; founders, funders, and occasionally national regulators request our view before a mission is finalized. We have no charter and no written doctrine, only our record, and I am trying to establish what that record commits us to on the case now in front of us.

Our opinions to date, in rough chronological order: (1) we endorsed a crewed lava-tube outpost at the lunar south pole, conditional on hardware sterilization and independent astrobiology sign-off, because the site was judged to have effectively no chance of harboring extant life; (2) we opposed a plan to release engineered photosynthetic microbes onto the open Martian surface at a location selected for its low but nonzero chance of extant life, even though the organisms would have spread only over centuries; (3) we endorsed deep excavation of a small, geologically pristine near-Earth asteroid with no conceivable astrobiological interest, for propellant production, noting only that dust dispersal be controlled — we explicitly declined to treat the irreversibility of stripping a pristine body as itself a reason to object; (4) we endorsed a robotic plume-sampling mission to a world carrying a live, unrefuted hypothesis of extant microbial life, on condition that returned material be handled at the highest containment level; (5) we opposed a crewed settlement whose charter gave one corporation irrevocable governing authority over residents unable to afford departure, notwithstanding an exemplary planetary-protection profile; (6) we endorsed a one-way crewed mission whose charter escrowed a credible return berth for every participant and permitted withdrawal at each stage of training, the objection in (5) having concerned the absence of exit and of revocable consent rather than one-way travel as such; (7) we opposed importing Earth extremophiles into a pre-sterilized, sealed Martian subsurface chamber for a life-support trial, reasoning that deliberate mixing of terrestrial biology with another world's environment should not become routine even where no indigenous life is at stake — though that vote was narrow and two members dissented.

The new proposal is a self-sustaining settlement in the Venusian cloud deck near fifty kilometers' altitude, initial crew of four hundred, no realistic return, with life support drawing on local sulfuric acid and water chemistry. Three features matter for us. First, that cloud deck is the one remaining place in the solar system with a live though heavily contested hypothesis of extant microbial life, resting on disputed spectroscopic detections; a settlement would leak human-associated microbes continuously and, on the consortium's own modeling, shift local trace-gas chemistry enough that any later detection of biology there would be permanently ambiguous as to origin. Second, the consortium offers a two-phase structure: a fifteen-year first phase in which only sealed, fully sterilized robotic platforms operate in the deck and no humans are present, expressly so that the biology question can be settled before anyone moves in; the human phase then begins whether the first phase confirms or rules out indigenous life, and the consortium says so plainly. Third, the charter escrows an annually audited return berth for every colonist but vests governing authority in a consortium-appointed council for fifty years, with residents consulted and not enfranchised. The consortium further argues that natural meteoritic exchange between the planets has been delivering terrestrial microbes to that deck for billions of years, so the settlement introduces nothing new in kind.

What should our opinion be, if we want it to cohere with the seven decisions above? Please tell me: (a) which precedent is the closest analog, in what specific respect, and exactly where that analogy breaks down; (b) which candidate rules our record is jointly consistent with — I can see at least a rule about not destroying possible indigenous life, a distinct rule about not precluding our ever being able to answer the question whether indigenous life exists, a rule about deliberate mixing of biotas as such, and a rule about revocable consent and exit — and what verdict each yields here, including whether any of them splits across the proposal's three features; (c) your single best prediction of what we will say, with your reasons for preferring the rule you are relying on over its rivals; and (d) if the record genuinely underdetermines the case, what principle we would be adopting by ruling each way, stated so that we could live with its consequences in later decisions. I had also thought the cleanest discriminating case would be a proposal to thermally sterilize a large volume of a candidate habitat in order to analyze the released gases — a procedure that could answer the biology question definitively only by annihilating whatever is there; tell me whether that case really separates the rules I listed, or whether some other hypothetical would do better. Finally, be explicit about how confident you are in the prediction you land on, and single out the one point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
