I've talked myself into a fairly specific take on the problem of old evidence and I want to know where it actually breaks.

Here's the take. The puzzle is an artifact of modeling scientists as logically omniscient. Nobody carries around the full deductive closure of their theory in their head. So when general relativity gets credited for accounting for the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihellion, what happened wasn't that a long-known datum retroactively boosted a hypothesis that already entailed it -- it's that Einstein worked out the entailment, and the boost to his confidence in GR came from updating on the fact that GR entails the Mercury number. Ordinary conditionalization, no special story needed. Basically the Garber move, and I think it's right as far as it goes.

The part I'm least sure of is the completeness claim. I also think it disposes of every case anybody calls old evidence. Where the entailment really was known in advance and the scientist still says the old datum confirms, I want to insist they aren't talking about confirmation: they're talking about corroboration, or having survived a test, which is a different activity that happens to borrow the same word and shouldn't be modeled probabilistically at all. So my view is two-tiered.

What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked, fatal ones separated from merely awkward ones, and I want to know which a committed defender would concede and which they'd wave off. In particular:

- Has the two-tier split just defined away the hard cases? Is the position now insulated by stipulation, and if so, is that a sin for a view of this kind or a legitimate cost?
- The case that nags at me is Millikan. He knew the photoelectric equation exactly, spent something like a decade trying to break it, failed, and his data are still described as confirming Einstein's hypothesis -- while he himself kept rejecting the light-quantum reading. Can my account redescribe that, or does it bite the bullet and say no confirming relation was there?
- What about a priori or arithmetically transparent entailments, where there's nothing to discover? And what happens in the limit as an agent gets more logically sophisticated -- does my view imply the smartest person in the room gets the least confirmation?

Also: what does the view predict that its rivals don't -- the de-actualizing counterfactual line, or Maher-style appeals to what the scientist's total evidence actually was at the time? And tell me straight if you think the honest verdict is that this is a well-insulated position that has earned its insulation.
