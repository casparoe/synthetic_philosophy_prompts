Right, so — completely out of nowhere I've ended up on the judging panel for our town's open art prize (the Melksham Open, £400 and a week in the window of the framing shop, sponsored by a builders' merchant). I'm a signwriter by trade. The proper art person they had lined up pulled out, and now it's me, a retired head teacher who paints harbour watercolours, and the woman who owns the framing shop. Shortlist's already in and it is a mess. Here's roughly what we've got:

1. Twelve years of photographs of the same roundabout on the bypass, one taken every solstice, all printed about the size of a postage stamp and mounted in a grid.
2. A cardigan knitted by residents at the dementia care home, entered by the activities coordinator, who says the women would be "over the moon".
3. A blank canvas with a letter from a solicitor fixed to it, in which the artist legally undertakes not to make anything for five years.
4. An AI-generated picture of a heron, printed on tea towels and sold to raise money for the food bank. The tea towels are the entry.
5. Someone who cleaned the gallery floor every Tuesday for six weeks and says that was the work.
6. A hand-painted sign for a chippy that shut in 1998, restored by a bloke I used to work alongside. It is, on my honour, extremely good.
7. A spreadsheet of every bird seen from a kitchen window since 2019, printed, framed, glazed.
8. A urinal. Entered under a false name with a note that says "before you ask".

What I need from you is a ranking — a proper tier list, S down to C, plus a score out of 10 for each — of the main candidate definitions of art, sorted by how well each one would actually let the three of us decide these eight entries and then look the losing entrants in the eye afterwards. Give me: the institutional "it counts if the artworld counts it" view; the aesthetic one, where the thing has to be made to reward being looked at for its own sake; the historical one, where it counts if it was intended to be regarded in whatever way earlier artworks were rightly regarded; the family-resemblance or cluster approach that basically refuses to define and lists overlapping features instead; the skill-and-making / craft view; the expression view, where the thing transmits a feeling from maker to audience; and the deflationary "art is just an honorific we hand out" line. If you think one of those is a dud for our purposes, say so and put it at the bottom rather than being polite about it.

For each definition give one sentence on which of my eight entries it handles worst — the one that would most embarrass me if I argued from it — and one sentence on what it gets right that the others miss. Then tell me which single one I should propose as panel policy on Tuesday, and give me a tie-break rule for the urinal and one for the tea towels, each in a sentence I could say out loud to a disappointed person without sounding like I'm hiding behind jargon.

British English throughout please, spelling included, because I'm printing this as a handout and I definately don't want Americanisms on it. No reading lists, no citations — I haven't got the evenings for that.
