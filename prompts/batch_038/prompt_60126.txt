In commercial salmon and trout farming, the unit at which welfare is measured changes over the life of the animal. During rearing, welfare is tracked as a property of the pen — mortality rate, growth and feed conversion, stocking density, sea-lice loads, disease incidents — and one certification scheme is weighing a proposal for a composite index built from opercular beat rate, plasma cortisol and a crowding response, sampled from a fixed number of fish per pen and collapsed into a single score that either clears a threshold or fails it. At slaughter the unit becomes the individual: protocols specify a current and exposure time, or a blow placement, and require confirmation that the fish is unconscious and will not regain sensibility before bleeding out. Crustaceans get neither treatment; they are typically sold alive from tanks and killed by the cooking process itself, even in jurisdictions that have legislated to place decapods and cephalopods alongside vertebrates in the class of animals whose sentience must be given due regard in policy-making — a duty backed by committee reports rather than by any offence.

What theory of welfare does this patchwork presuppose? Does the pen-level regime commit its authors to welfare being a property of populations or production systems rather than of individuals, and what does that imply for an index whose every input is measured in a single fish? Does the slaughter regime commit them instead to the view that what matters morally is a momentary state of consciousness rather than the shape of a life, and can one regulator coherently hold both? Does the treatment of crustaceans presuppose that sentience is a binary status whose default setting differs by taxon — presumption for vertebrates, burden of proof for invertebrates — and is that asymmetry an evidential claim or a moral one? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit comfortably, mark the places where rival accounts (hedonistic, preference-based, telos-based, or a biological-integrity view) would license exactly the same rules, and say what would have to change in the metrics, the audit and the supply chain if welfare were instead treated as the sum of individual experiential lives over time.
