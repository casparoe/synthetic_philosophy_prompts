I'm drafting a section of a paper on Adam Smith's account of conscience, to be presented to a general moral-philosophy seminar (no Smith specialists, though one Hume scholar). Here is the draft, roughly as it stands:

Smith's impartial spectator is usually read as a device for correcting the biases of actual spectators: we generalize from the many partial viewpoints we encounter until we arrive at a standpoint that is nobody's in particular, and it is that standpoint which confers authority on our judgments about ourselves. I want to argue that this reading gets the direction of explanation backwards, and that once it is corrected a problem appears which is not the familiar charge of relativism.

On my reading, the impartial spectator is an equilibrium notion rather than a criterion. The process Smith describes in Part III — watching others judge, watching them judge us, internalizing judgments we cannot avoid, then imagining how we would appear to someone who had all the relevant information and none of our stakes — is a process of mutual adjustment. A spectator is impartial when further exposure to further viewpoints no longer changes its verdicts: the process has settled. Nothing outside the process certifies the settlement. This is why Smith's occasional theological remark that the general rules of morality are justly to be regarded as the laws of the deity cannot function as an external guarantor. In Smith the deity is represented as endorsing what the settled spectator approves, so the endorsement redescribes convergence rather than checking it independently.

If authority amounts to settled-ness, the obvious objection is that different communities settle differently. I think this misdiagnoses the difficulty, for two reasons. First, Smith need not deny divergence: he devotes a substantial part of the book to the influence of custom and fashion on moral sentiment, and he repeatedly portrays actual spectators as distorted by proximity, rank, and self-preference. Divergence is his data, not a refutation of him. Second, and more importantly, Smith already possesses a criterion that is not mere stability: scope. The corruption cases that worry him — the internalized judge being silenced, or bought off, or answering only to a narrow circle of admirers — are cases in which the process settled prematurely, because too few and too similar perspectives were integrated into it. The content of the impartiality requirement is therefore a claim about how wide the circle must be before settling counts.

This relocates the vulnerability. Smith's theory does not require convergence in fact across societies; it requires convergence under widening scope within the same agent: that if I keep enlarging the circle of perspectives I take account of, my verdicts stop changing, and stop changing at the same point as yours. That is a substantive empirical-psychological claim, and Smith's own account of how readily approbation is captured by custom gives us reason to doubt it. Custom does not merely supply different starting points that widening would wash out; it alters what counts as a viewpoint at all. The objection I want to press, then, is not that the spectator is relative to a community, but that scope-widening is not self-correcting, which leaves the theory with no way to distinguish a settled judgment from a captured one.
