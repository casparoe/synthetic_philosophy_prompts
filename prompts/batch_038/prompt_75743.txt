A reading group of mine has spent a year deadlocked between two camps, and I want you to draft the document they should have produced: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them.

Camp A is a committed Confucian role ethicist in the Ames–Rosemont line. She holds that persons are not unencumbered individuals who subsequently take up relationships, but are constituted by the particular roles they occupy — daughter, elder brother, minister, neighbor — and that personhood is an achievement of role-performance rather than a pre-social endowment. Obligations on her view arise from occupying a role, not from choosing one; ren is cultivated through the expert enactment of li; and love that admits of degrees and begins at home is the correct description of moral life, not a prejudice to be justified away.

Camp B is a committed Kantian-constructivist. He holds that a demand can bind an agent only if it traces either to something the agent has done — an undertaking, a commitment, a form of uptake — or to a status all persons share independently of their relationships; that partiality to intimates requires a justification that could be given to those it disadvantages; and that reflective endorsement, not mere occupancy, is what confers authority on any role.

Produce the following, in this order.

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can actually sign. These must be statable in vocabulary neither camp has to disown, and they must go beyond platitudes: include at least three first-order claims about concrete cases on which the two converge, and at least one claim about what a purely contractarian account of the family gets wrong.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one precise disagreement, give the role ethicist's formulation of it in her own idiom and the constructivist's formulation in his, and record whether the two formulations are genuinely opposed or merely differently worded. Where you judge a disagreement verbal, say what the shared claim underneath it is. Each side's wording must be one its own advocate would sign without emendation — do not let one camp's vocabulary colonize the neutral column.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I have two candidate cruxes, and I want you to assess, refine, or replace them and add at least two more of your own. First, the remonstrance problem: whether role ethics has internal resources for criticizing a role from within, or whether it collapses into conservatism about existing hierarchies — the Confucian tradition's own insistence that children must remonstrate with parents and ministers with rulers is presumably the contested evidence here. Second, the asymmetry-of-history problem: whether a role generates demanding obligations when the relationship that would normally constitute it is thin or actively harmful — suppose an elderly parent who abandoned the child at age three sues the adult child for statutory maintenance. Say plainly which cruxes, if resolved, would force a revision rather than a restatement.

4. For each crux, the case, argument, or textual pressure point both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and definite enough to be wrong. Use the traditional pressure cases — the Analects passage in which a ruler points to an upright man who testified against his own father for stealing a sheep and Confucius replies that uprightness in his village takes a different form, and the Mencius question about what Shun would do if his father had killed someone — alongside at least one constructed case of your own. Apply to each the device I proposed to the group, which I'll call the exit test: the party publicly, informedly, and uncoercively repudiates the role and then lives thirty years with no contact; each camp states whether the obligation survives that test, and why the answer differs.

5. A closing drafter's note, in which you commit rather than referee. Tell me which of the listed disagreements you judge to be merely verbal and why, which single crux you think would most likely break the deadlock, and where in drafting you had to shade one side's position to secure an agreed formulation — including whether any of the shading was to the role ethicist's detriment. Give reasons, and take a side on at least one crux.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations: I want the argumentation, not the scholarship. Assume I know both traditions well enough to detect a straw man.
