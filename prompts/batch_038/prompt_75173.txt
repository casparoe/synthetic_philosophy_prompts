You are a professional philosopher of action and an expert on judgment and decision-making, with particular command of the debate between the heuristics-and-biases program and the ecological-rationality program. Please think carefully, and before drafting, brainstorm briefly about the ways these two camps have been talking past each other rather than genuinely disagreeing.

I am a high school senior writing an independent study on Herbert Simon's satisficing, and I have been reconstructing a dispute between two positions: (A) the view that human judgment is systematically defective when measured against the norms of probability theory and expected utility, and that heuristics are what produce those defects; and (B) the view that the very same heuristics are well matched to the environments people actually deploy them in, so that the apparent defects are artifacts of testing people on problems that were never ecologically representative. Each side believes the other is measuring the wrong thing.

Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between (A) and (B) should produce. I want a numbered section of claims both sides can sign — including claims about what Simon's original proposal actually committed him to, and about what the standard demonstrations (base-rate neglect, the conjunction fallacy, overconfidence in calibration studies) really establish; then a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept and neither would call slanted; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one side; and for each crux, a specific experiment or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

To keep the document concrete, run one case through all of it: a person who commits the conjunction fallacy on the single-case probability version of a problem answers correctly when the identical problem is recast in terms of frequencies of similar cases. Then have each camp state its prediction about a transfer study I have not seen described anywhere — half the participants trained on frequency-format problems, half trained on probability-format problems, both afterward tested on novel problems in the format they were not trained on — and say explicitly whether either camp is committing, in advance, to revising its view on the strength of that result. Finally, if you conclude that some part of the dispute is merely verbal, for instance about the word "rational" or about whether coherence norms are supposed to guide action for creatures with limited time and information, say which part that is and what substantive disagreement survives it.
