In traditional Jewish liturgy the same texts pull in two directions at once, and I want to know what that double movement presupposes. Every morning, before the core prayers, the service sets out a block of passages describing the Temple's daily offering and the procedures governing the various sacrifices, and it closes by asking that our recitation of those passages be reckoned to us as though we had actually brought the offerings at their appointed times. Then on Sabbaths, new moons, and festivals an additional service is appended which rehearses the specific offerings that were brought on that day when the Temple stood and petitions that God rebuild the Temple speedily and restore us to it so that we may once again discharge those obligations as commanded. So the liturgy simultaneously treats verbal study as a sufficient substitute and asks that the substitution end.

Maimonides supplies a philosophical frame that looks hard to fit to this. In the third part of the Guide he explains the sacrificial law historically: the mode of worship universal among the nations of that era was animal sacrifice in temples, and since a people cannot be made to abandon abruptly a custom to which it is thoroughly habituated — he compares it to a nursing infant being weaned, and to what it would be like if a prophet today told us to serve God only in thought and never by prayer, fast, or petition — God did not abolish the practice but redirected it exclusively to Himself, making it a means toward the Law's primary end of instilling knowledge of God and eradicating idolatry. On that account the sacrifices were not commanded for their own sake. Yet in his legal code he rules that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and that the whole sacrificial order will be reinstated in his days as the Torah prescribes.

Please reconstruct the theory of value that the liturgical practice presupposes. Specifically: does petitioning for the restoration of sacrifice require that sacrifices retain some instrumental value for the restored community, and if so what could that be for a people no longer habituated to sacrificial worship — is such a petition a request that a concession be reimposed, and is that coherent? What does the morning formula asking that recitation be reckoned in place of performance presuppose: that the point of the sacrifice was a cognitive or affective state that words can induce equally well, that the physical act carries a value study can only approximate, or that the substitution is a matter of divine acceptance rather than of equivalent content? Distinguish sharply what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with — including the classic medieval critique associated with Nahmanides, on which the offerings have efficacy of their own in drawing the worshipper near, and the deflationary reading on which the restoration petition is a liturgical fossil preserved out of textual conservatism rather than belief. Tell me where the practice is underdetermined, such that both Maimonides' concessive account and a non-instrumental rival could license it unmodified.

Then use the modern revisions as a test. Some contemporary liturgies recast the festival petition in the past tense, describing what our ancestors offered rather than asking to offer again; others drop the sacrificial references altogether. What theory does each revision presuppose, and does the fact that these edits were intelligible and only mildly controversial show that the original practice was genuinely ambiguous, or rather that it was committed and that the edits amount to substantive rejections of that commitment? Finally: if the Guide's account were made explicit and fully believed by a community, what in the practice would have to change, and what could survive untouched?

Be disciplined about separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on Maimonides. That the Guide contains the historical-concession argument and that the code affirms a restored sacrificial order is not, I take it, in dispute; whether the concession argument states his considered view or a deliberately softened exoteric one, whether he thought the sacrifices would ideally lapse altogether, and how the two texts are to be squared are live questions. Flag which reading you are relying on at each step, and do not present a contested interpretation as the scholarly consensus.
