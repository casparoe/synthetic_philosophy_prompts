I edit Wikipedia, on and off, mostly small stuff, and there's a rule at the heart of the whole project that I cant get my head around. Two things happened to me recently that show what I mean.

One: I spent years working on old tube amplifiers, and there's a claim repeated in an article that I know is wrong. Not "I suspect" — I've had my hands on this hundreds of times. I tried to fix it and got reverted, and the revert was correct by the rules. Nobody argued I was mistaken. They just said it doesn't matter whether you're right, if it isn't in some reliable published source it can't go in, even if you know it's true.

Two, the mirror image: there was a claim in another article backed by three okay-looking sources that I'm confident is just an old error that got copied from book to book. I wanted to pull it. Told I couldn't — you can't delete sourced material because you think it's untrue, you need either a source contradicting it or a better source. So the article now states, flatly, something that most of the people working on it privately think is probably false.

So the site ends up saying things nobody involved believes, and refusing to say things everyone involved knows. That seems like it has to be driven by some particular idea about when you're allowed to say something, and it isn't "only say what you believe," and it isn't exactly "only say what you know" either.

Here's what I want. Reconstruct that idea as precisely as you can. What does the site treat as making it okay to put a sentence up? Is it about the evidence, or about who eats the blame if it turns out wrong, or is it secretly a rule about who's allowed to speak rather than about what's true? And who is doing the saying in the first place — the volunteer, the site, or the source being pointed at? Because the articles are written in their own plain voice, "the treaty was signed in 1848," not "according to these books the treaty was signed in 1848," even though the whole policy is one long disclaimer about belief. Does that plain voice mean the site is asserting it after all, and if so is the disclaimer just decoration?

Then tell me how much of this the practice actually needs. Would some different account of when it's okay to say something generate the exact same editing rules, so that you couldn't tell them apart from the practice alone? My guess is a couple would, and if that's right I want to know what case would make them come apart — something where they'd give different verdicts on the same edit.

Last part, and be honest with me. My own idea: drop the source rule, and instead every claim carries the confidence of whoever put it in; claims no current editor is actually confident about get pulled no matter how well sourced they are, and claims an editor is confident about but can't source stay up with a flag saying so. I think that fixes both of my cases. But I can also see that it turns the site into a running weather report of how various volunteers happen to feel, and I'm not sure that's an improvement. Would that version even be asserting anything? And what does my proposal have to assume that the real one doesn't?

No reading lists or references, please — I can't look anything up and I'd rather you just argued it out with me.
