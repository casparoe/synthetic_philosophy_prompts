**Assignment.** Write a short story of approximately 1,500 to 2,200 words that dramatizes the problem of *pure* time preference: whether the bare fact that a benefit or a harm falls further in the future is by itself a reason to count it for less, and if so, how much less. The story must treat this as a live practical question for its characters, not as a seminar topic.

**Background you may draw on.** All of the following is paraphrase; do not put any of it into quotation marks or into a character's mouth as scholarship. Sidgwick held that the mere fact of one moment of experience coming before another is no rational ground for regarding the earlier one more. Ramsey — whose name attaches to the standard decomposition of a social discount rate into a pure time-preference term, a term for the elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption, and a term for expected growth in consumption — considered pure time discounting ethically indefensible and a symptom of a failure of imagination. Parfit argued in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) against what he called a bias toward the near, while conceding that the weakening of psychological connections between earlier and later stages of a person might justify some discounting of one's own future. The Koopmans line of reply is that a creature with limited foresight and a limited capacity for concern cannot coherently care about everything at once, so that some positive impatience is a condition of agency rather than a defect in it. Contemporary disputes about climate policy have turned partly on this: the Stern Review's use of a near-zero pure time-preference parameter, on the order of a tenth of one percent per year, was attacked by economists who said the assumption, and not the evidence, was doing the normative work.

**The world of the story.** Set it in a polity of your own invention that has made the discount rate a matter of personal record rather than of macroeconomic policy. These details are required; you may add to them, but do not contradict them.

- On reaching majority, every citizen files a Declaration: a single annual percentage, the rate at which that person discounts future well-being, their own and other people's alike.
- The median of all live Declarations is the figure the state uses to price any public project with a horizon beyond fifty years. Because the median is recomputed continuously, one unusual Declaration can matter.
- A filed number binds its filer in both directions. Declare a high rate, and your later complaints carry almost no weight: you may sign away your own future cheaply, and the state will enforce the bargain against the person you subsequently become. Declare zero, and you are held to be indifferent between a benefit today and the identical benefit in eighty years; the state therefore declines to treat you as competent to make any binding commitment involving delay — no loan, no deferred wage, no plea bargain, no course of treatment — and appoints a guardian for such matters. Zero is not a virtue in this polity. It is an incapacity.

Your protagonist is an auditor who tests Declarations for internal consistency and investigates outliers. Two outliers exist: a citizen who filed zero and will not revise it, and a citizen whose rate is so high that almost nothing beyond a decade has any value to them at all. Meanwhile a long-horizon project of your choosing — make it physical, unglamorous, and slow — is about to be priced, and the median sits close enough to the tipping point that the auditor's own Declaration will settle it.

**What the story must accomplish.**

1. Include one scene in which two characters defend the same number for reasons that are not merely different but incompatible: one because later-ness as such counts for less; one because they expect future people to be richer, so that an equal gain to them is worth less at the margin; one because the future is uncertain and may never arrive to be enjoyed. No one in the scene need notice the confusion, but the reader must be able to see it in what is said and done.
2. Separate, through events rather than exposition, the intrapersonal case from the intergenerational one: discounting your own future, where continuity thins and the later person is in some sense a stranger, versus discounting the welfare of people who will exist regardless and between whom and you no such relation holds. One character should press the point that whatever license the first case grants does not transfer to the second.
3. Give the high-rate citizen a genuinely strong case. Limited foresight, the impossibility of caring well about everything simultaneously, the discipline that only near stakes impose, and the paralysis that a zero rate licenses should all be available to them and should land as live arguments rather than sophistry. Do not make them a villain or a fool. Equally, the zero-rate citizen's rectitude must cost other people something real and particular.
4. Put one piece of arithmetic into the story — an actual present-value calculation whose outcome turns on which rate is applied — and make the arithmetic correct. The numbers should surface in dialogue, on a form, or in the auditor's head, never in a footnote.

**Restraints.** No epigraph, no footnotes, no afterword, and no closing paragraph in which a character or narrator announces the theme. Nothing may read as a lecture: the ideas should be carried by paperwork, by what people are willing to sign, and by what the auditor notices in the ledger. No real philosopher may appear as a character or be cited in the text; your polity may have its own vocabulary for these positions. Give the story a title, keep the setting concrete and sensuous, and end with the auditor's own Declaration — filed or withheld — revealed through an action rather than an assertion.
