So here's a thing that's been eating at me for a couple years and I don't have anyone to talk to about it who wouldn't just say "who cares."

My grandmother had a small bronze in her entryway that she swore was by a sculptor who was moderately well known back in the 60s. She paid real money for it. After she died we got it looked at, and it turns out its a posthumous re-cast — same mold, made by the same foundry decades later, with the estate's permission, and there's like 200 of them out there. Visually identical to the ones from her lifetime. Nobody was tricked, the paperwork is all above board.

Here's the part I can't explain. I still like looking at it exactly as much as I ever did. Nothing about how it looks changed. But something defintly drained out of it for me, and my mum felt it too, and I can't figure out what the thing that drained out actually was. My uncle, who's a cabinetmaker, thinks I'm being an idiot — he says half the famous old pieces were made by a shop of six people anyway and the guy whose name is on the thing maybe just drew it and signed it.

I've been writing down cases I keep turning over in my head. Can you score each one from 0 to 10 for how much of its value gets destroyed when the truth comes out — 0 being nothing lost, 10 being it might as well be in the bin — and then give me the ranking from worst hit to least hit? And then tell me the single rule you used to generate the scores, because I strongly suspect I'm being inconsistent in my own head and I want to catch myself doing it.

1. My grandmother's bronze. Posthumous cast from the artist's own mold, foundry authorized, edition of 200, indistinguishable from the lifetime casts.

2. A painting that's a dead-on copy of a famous one, done by a forger who spent 30 years learning that painter's technique, using period-correct canvas and pigments, sold as genuine and hung in someone's house for a decade before it's caught.

3. A cabinet from a well known 18th century workshop. The named master drew the design and did the final surface finishing himself; apprentices built everything else.

4. A violin made last year by a living luthier who got wood from the same valley, copied the model down to the millimetre, and mixed the same varnish recipe. Blind listeners can't pick it out from the 300 year old one, and a few say the new one sounds better.

5. A song released after a singer died, made with a model trained on everything she ever recorded. It sounds exactly like her, the family approved it, and the label says so on the sleeve.

6. A fresco that a fire took most of. Restorers repainted roughly 60% of the surface, matching exactly what the pre-fire photographs show was there. Without that work the whole thing would have been a black wall.

7. A letter dictated word for word by a writer to his secretary and signed in the writer's own hand — versus the same writer's messy handwritten draft of the same letter, with crossings out.

I know these aren't all the same kind of thing, that's kind of the point. If you think one of them shouldn't be scored on the same scale as the others, say so and tell me what scale it belongs on instead.
