For twenty years I have watched the same argument stall in the same place: someone observes that the simple conditional analysis of dispositions dies on a finkish wire, everyone agrees, and then nothing further happens. I want to get past that point, because I am trying to write down a criterion that can actually be applied — hand it to two different engineers and get the same answer twice — for when a system in a reliability database genuinely has a disposition. Here are my verdicts on cases.

1. A high-voltage wire is live, and a concealed device would drain it dead the instant anything conductive touched it. I say it is live, even though anyone who touched it would be perfectly safe.
2. A glass ships in a custom foam cradle that guarantees it survives every drop it will in fact undergo. I say it is fragile.
3. A prop is solid plastic, but a studio rule guarantees it is swapped for a breakaway duplicate before anyone ever strikes it. I say it is not breakable, even though whatever strikes it will watch it shatter.
4. A hull is not watertight by construction; it is kept dry by a pump that is welded in, continuously monitored, and triple-redundant. I say the vessel is watertight.
5. A wire is dead, and a device would make it live the moment it is touched. I say it is not live — though I admit this verdict is mostly symmetry-driven rather than reasoned.
6. A toxin has a specific antidote, and I have taken it. I say the toxin is not poisonous to me and is poisonous to everyone else, and I cannot tell whether that is two dispositions or one disposition plus a fact about me.

My working principle has been "has the disposition iff it has the right intrinsic basis." It handles 1, 2, 3 and 5, but it is embarrassed by 4, where the pump is exactly the sort of extrinsic machinery that case 3 taught me to distrust; the difference appears to be only that the pump is bolted on rather than staffed. Cases 1 and 3 are the pair I most want explained: the test fails in one and I ascribe the disposition, the test succeeds in the other and I refuse.

Please give me the principle these verdicts add up to, as numbered tests applied in order, marking which test decides each case; say what the intrinsic-basis clause is actually doing — a criterion, or a placeholder for "not case 3"? — and what it is a placeholder for if it is one; state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot keep; and close with a seventh case of your own devising that you regard as genuinely hard for the principle you have just given. I am not after a survey of the literature. I want a procedure, or an argument that no non-circular procedure is available, with the point at which the circularity bites identified.
