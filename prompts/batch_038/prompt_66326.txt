ok so this has been bugging me since like third period bio. Our textbook starts the whole year with a list of the seven things every living thing supposedly does — moves, respires, responds to stimuli, grows, reproduces, excretes waste, takes in nutrition — and it works like a checklist, do all seven and your alive, miss some and your not. We spent a day sorting things into the two boxes and it got weird fast. Fire "grows" and spreads and burns up fuel. A crystal grows and kind of copies its own structure. A virus does nothing at all until it hijacks a cell. And a mule cant reproduce, but nobody wants to say a mule isn't alive. The book puts viruses in the not-alive box and we get graded on it, but my teacher also said the question doesn't really have a right answer, which feels like a cop-out considering it's on the test.

Then I went down a rabbit hole about how people actually search for life on other planets and it's basically the opposite method. NASA uses this one-liner, roughly "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution," and the real detection plan is more like: point a telescope at an exoplanet's atmosphere and look for gases that shouldnt be able to hang around together without something constantly remaking them, oxygen and methane at the same time. So they'd call a planet inhabited based on its chemistry being out of balance, and never once check whether anything there moves or excretes or takes in nutrition. Also I think the NASA line implies my dog isn't alive, since one dog on its own can't undergo evolution??

So what is each of these practices actually assuming about what "alive" means? Does the checklist only make sense if you think life is the kind of thing with necessary and sufficient conditions, or would it still work if life were just a fuzzy cluster of traits with no sharp edge — like, which parts does it strictly need vs. which parts just happen to fit? What views of life is each one quietly ruling out, like the old idea that living stuff has some extra ingredient in it? Are the two methods actually contradicting each other or answering different questions and I'm mashing them together? And if the astrobiology way of thinking won out, what would have to change in my class — would "is a virus alive" stop being a question anyone could put on a test at all?
