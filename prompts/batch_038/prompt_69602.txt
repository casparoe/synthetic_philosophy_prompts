I'm a TA for a second-year history and philosophy of science seminar and we've now spent three weeks on Lakatos two years running, and both times it ends the exact same way: nobody budges and we run out of session. One camp in the room thinks the methodology of scientific research programmes is a real third option between Popper and Kuhn with actual teeth in it — hard core, negative heuristic, protective belt, and the distinction between a theoretically progressive shift (the programme predicts something genuinely new) and an empirically progressive one (some of those new predictions get corroborated), which is supposed to let you call a programme degenerating without waiting for a single falsifying observation to do the work. The other camp thinks it's just Kuhn with better vocabulary. Their two moves: first, that no programme ever actually gets knocked out, because you can always retreat into the belt, blame an auxiliary, and say the good results are coming later, so the rules are compatible with literally any behavior a scientist could exhibit — which is the Feyerabend-style charge that a methodology that never forces you to give anything up isn't a methodology; and second, that "novel fact" is so elastic that any motivated person can score their favourite programme progressive. Lakatos's own answer, as that camp reads him, is that the rules were never meant to be a decision procedure for someone in the middle of a research programme — instant rationality is impossible, appraisal is retrospective, and the methodology earns its keep by organizing the historical record rather than by telling you to quit.

Last term someone in the group tried to settle it by running the progressive/degenerating test on two cases: the Newtonian programme's handling of Uranus's orbit, which ends with Neptune being found roughly where the perturbations implied, and the long stretch of Copernican astronomy where the system mostly absorbed anomalies instead of producing them. People came back with opposite scores for both, and when we tried to figure out why, it turned out they were disagreeing about things that weren't in Lakatos at all: what counts as novel versus smuggled in through the belt, how long a window of degeneration you're allowed before a programme counts as dead, and whether a single big corroborated prediction outweighs sixty years of retrofitting. So the fight looked less like a disagreement about Lakatos than about parameters everybody was importing silently. There's also a third smaller camp who think the whole thing is a category mistake — MSRP is a lens for writing history of science, and asking whether it's "right" or "testable" is like asking whether a pair of scissors is true. And one person keeps pushing a Laudan-ish line that we should drop the whole apparatus and just ask whether a tradition is solving problems effectively, which nobody else wants to take up because they think it makes rationality even mushier.

My working theory, which I'm happy to be told is wrong: the stalemate is mostly that "MSRP has no content" and "MSRP is a genuine alternative" are answers to different questions, and each side keeps scoring points on the question the other one isn't answering — the first camp is asking what a methodology must do for a scientist deciding whether to stay or switch, the second is asking what a methodology must do to make sense of a finished historical record, and the third is asking whether it's even in the business of appraising rather than narrating. So: is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive core here I'm explaining away? I want you to work through what each position actually commits to and say which parts of the impasse are verbal (turning on 'methodology', 'progressive', 'novel'), which parts are real but hidden-empirical (a question about the historical record that could in principle be settled if we could agree on the scoring rules, which is maybe the whole problem), which parts are a difference about standards of evidence — how many cases, and who gets to pick them — and which parts are just bedrock intuitions about what rational appraisal has to be that neither side can argue for. And then tell me what would actually count as progress in a room like this: a distinction someone hasn't drawn, a specified case with pre-registered counting rules, some formal criterion for progressiveness, a reframing of the question, or a good argument that no progress should be expected and why. Don't give me citations or a tour of the literature, I know roughly who says what — I want the diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement and reasons for it.
