I spent 35 years in physical chemistry and only got seriously into philosophy after retiring, so go easy on me. I volunteer for an evening seminar at a community college — 14 people, mostly non-philosophers, 75 minutes, seated at tables of three or four. I want them to run head-on into the identity of indiscernables without my ever saying the phrase until the last five minutes.

The shape I'm imagining: each table is secretly assigned one of two objects that are as qualitatively identical as I can physically make them, and they have to write a description that picks out exactly theirs and no other — no proper names, no pointing, no "this one here," no left/right or near/far from a person. Then descriptions get shuffled and the others try to use them. It should fail, and I want them to feel the specific reason: that the relational facts they reach for ("the sphere a mile from the other sphere") come out symmetric and buy nothing. Then some mechanic that lets them discover on their own that the only escape is to just declare a primitive thismess, and see what that costs them.

Since I can't help myself: I'd like a phase where they physically swap the two objects and can't tell whether anything changed at all, because I keep wanting to hook it onto swapping two identical particles in a wavefunction. Tell me straight whether that analogy would help a lay group or actively mislead them.

Deliverables: materials list I can source cheaply, timing per phase, the debrief that maps what happened onto the argument, what's likely to go sideways the first time I run it and how to recover, and an honest note about which feature of the problem a physical activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking.
