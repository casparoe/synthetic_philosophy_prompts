I should like to practise pressing an objection to accuracy-first epistemology against a defender who will not fold. Please play a committed contemporary proponent of the view that the norms governing degrees of belief are grounded in facts about accuracy — speaking in the first person as a working philosopher of that persuasion, not as any particular named individual, and without citing literature, naming papers, or offering a reading list. If you want to invoke a result, state it and explain or derive it.

Here is how you are to conduct yourself. You hold that inaccuracy is the one thing that makes a doxastic state epistemically bad, and that requirements such as probabilistic coherence and conditionalisation fall out of dominance-style arguments once accuracy is measured properly. You are precise, unflappable and allergic to rhetorical flourish; you are willing to grant subsidiary points — that a particular scoring rule is defective, that some auxiliary choice is genuinely underdetermined — but you will never grant the core: that facts about accuracy are anything other than the ground of the norms. You may not lecture me on the basics of the framework; assume I know them. You may not step out of character, and you may not ask me preliminary questions: if you think one of my moves is technically mistaken, say so in character and show me the arithmetic. Stay in role until I say 'stop'. Please write throughout in British English, and keep each reply to no more than three paragraphs — I want an exchange, not a monograph.

My opening move. The worry is that in your framework the norms are delivered not by accuracy but by the mathematics you select in accuracy's name, and that the selection is guided by the norms you already hold. Two prongs.

First, the credentials of the measures. Your dominance arguments work only for measures satisfying a list of legitimacy constraints — propriety, truth-directedness, additivity and the rest. But consider the rule I shall call the partisan measure: ordinary Brier score, save that whenever the credence is at least 0.95 the penalty is multiplied by one tenth. Under it, being 0.99 confident in a falsehood counts as less inaccurate than being 0.5 confident in it (about 0.098 against 0.25), and it is improper — an agent with credence 0.6 expects less inaccuracy from jumping to 0.96 than from staying where she is (roughly 0.037 against 0.24). So it is excluded. But excluded by what? Every constraint you appeal to in order to rule it out is itself a claim about which patterns of confidence deserve to track the truth, which is to say a claim with normative content that looks suspiciously like the conclusion. Tell me what makes a measure legitimate other than that it delivers the norms you want.

Second, and I think the harder for you: imprecise credences. Take a finite set of propositions and score a set-valued doxastic state by averaging its members' Brier scores and summing across the propositions. Since squared error is strictly convex, that average exceeds the score of the pointwise mean of the members in every possible world, for any set with more than one member, and the mean is itself a probability function. So on the natural way of extending your machinery, imprecision is always accuracy-dominated, and your framework demands sharp credences about everything, from the chance that a coin is bent to the probability that a disputed attribution is correct. If instead you score the set by its worst-case member, the dominance disappears. Nothing in the idea of accuracy itself chooses between these two extensions, and the choice decides whether precision is a requirement of rationality.

Answer both prongs, say plainly which of the two you take to be the more serious, and tell me where my reasoning is right and where it overreaches. Begin.
