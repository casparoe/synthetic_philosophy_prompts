I'm the lead curator for a permanent gallery on Earth-system change that opens next spring at a mid-sized science museum, and my design team has deadlocked over the very first thing a visitor will see. The plan is a twelve-metre timeline strip running along the entry corridor: a short run for the Holocene, then a vertical marker labelled with the start of the Anthropocene, then the rest of the strip. Our graphic designer needs a single year to set in type, and my advisory board is split four ways, with a fifth position nobody likes but which I think has to be scored anyway. One geologist insists the only defensible marker is the mid-twentieth century, because formal time units are defined by a globally synchronous physical signal rather than by a story about causes, and because that is what the international process actually converged on: bomb-test radionuclides, with plutonium from the early 1950s preserved in annually laminated lake sediments, were the basis of the golden-spike proposal put forward in 2023, and some proponents would push the marker a few years later to the 1964 peak of bomb-produced radiocarbon. One historian of colonialism argues that a 1950 start amputates four centuries of causal history and that the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide recorded in ice cores around 1610, following the catastrophic depopulation of the Americas and the reforestation of abandoned farmland, is both a genuinely global signal and the honest one. An environmental economist wants the late eighteenth century, the point Crutzen himself leaned toward, when Watt's steam engine began doing work at industrial scale and human energy use decoupled from annual solar income. An archaeologist points out that clearance and irrigated rice farming began moving atmospheric methane and carbon dioxide thousands of years earlier, which on that logic pushes the marker back into the early Holocene and dissolves the distinction between the two epochs altogether. The fifth position is that choosing a start date at all is a category error: what we are describing is a diachronous unfolding event with no boundary, and the stratigraphic establishment effectively sided with this, since in March 2024 the formal epoch proposal was voted down by a wide margin after about fifteen years of deliberation, chiefly because its record is too shallow and its start date too recent, with the rejection then confirmed by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences, which nonetheless concluded that the term will go on being used as a descriptor of human impact on the Earth system.

Here is what I need from you. First, score each of those five positions twice, on integer scales from 0 to 10, and put the results in a table. Axis A is stratigraphic workability: how well the candidate marker would function as a formal global boundary, given synchrony across independent archives, preservation in enough of them, detectability at the resolution required, and the depth and durability of the record. Axis B is causal and narrative fidelity: how much of the actual story of humanity becoming a planetary force the date preserves rather than amputates, and how legible it makes the agents and mechanisms involved, from forest clearance and colonial land use through fossil energy to the postwar consumption boom. Then tell me which positions invert their order between the two axes, and what those inversions diagnose: where the board is disagreeing about the empirical record, and where it is disagreeing about what a boundary is supposed to do. Third, pick a weight for Axis A relative to Axis B, state it as a number, defend the choice in two or three sentences, and use it to produce a composite ranking plus a tier list (S through C). Finally, commit to a print-ready recommendation: either name the single year that goes on the wall or explain the device that replaces it, and give me the fraction of the twelve-metre strip, as a decimal between 0 and 1, that should fall on the far side of the marker, saying explicitly whether you want the strip linear or logarithmic and what that choice does to the argument the gallery is making.

Two housekeeping notes. I am not after references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want your own assessment and the reasoning behind it, and false precision is welcome here, because forcing the trade-offs into numbers is precisely how I want the board's stalemate broken. If you think I have mischaracterised any of the five positions, say so briefly and score your corrected version instead of mine. And please do not end on a balanced non-answer: the designer has to print something, so give me a verdict, then give me the single strongest objection to that verdict and the one sentence of wall text you would use to answer it.
