I sit on the capital committee for a small coastal town, about 11,000 people, and I'm the one who keeps dragging long-run stuff onto the agenda, so this has been bugging me for a while. I want to work out what we should vote on next month, and more than that I want to know whether our last six years of votes add up to a position at all. Here they are, roughly in order:

- **Roofs.** We paid around 40% extra to put 40-year roofs on all three schools instead of 20-year ones. Unanimous, no real argument, nobody said the word "discount."
- **Power lines.** The engineers' own numbers said burying the lines pays for itself in avoided outage costs over something like 55 years. We voted it down 4-1. The reason said out loud was that they weren't going to make people pay now for something their grandkids recieve.
- **Seawall.** After the hurricane we approved $6 million for a seawall in about eleven minutes. It's rated for a hundred years of service, so it's a long-horizon project too, but it protects buildings that are standing now and voters who are standing now.
- **Trees.** We approved planting 400 street trees, which was cheap, and we've let maybe half of them die. When I raised it, the answer was that maintenance isn't a capital item.
- **Landfill closure.** We're legally required to close the old landfill in about 25 years. We put in exactly the required amount each year and no more, even though we're paying more interest on the bonds we issued than the closure fund earns, which as far as I can tell means front-loading it would definately cost us less in total. Nobody seemed to think that was worth discussing.
- **The waste facility.** A company offered us $300k a year for 30 years to put a lined industrial waste site on the edge of town. Their consultants said the chance of contamination reaching the aquifer was small but not zero, and that if it happened it'd happen 60 to 90 years out. We said no. What stuck with me afterwards: not one person argued that aquifer damage counts for less because it's far away. They argued it might happen and it would be very bad, full stop.

Now next month's item. A combined grant-and-town-money package to rebuild the stormwater system so it handles the rain we now get about once a decade, but sized for the conditions the models project for 2080. $4.2 million from us now. It prevents maybe $9 million in damage, and basically all of that lands after 2070 — on streets that haven't been built yet and people who are mostly not born yet. The committee is split in a way I find confusing, because both sides sound like they've actually thought about it. One member says our grandkids will be far richer than we are, so poorer people shouldn't be subsidizing richer people, and that means we shouldn't do it. Another says that's backwards: if the climate goes badly they'll be poorer than us and will need it more, plus we're the ones who made the problem, and anyway we can't know which way it goes so you should weight the bad scenario heavier.

What I want from you:

1. Which of the six old votes is the closest analog to this one, and what actually separates it from the others? I assumed it was the seawall, but the power lines keep feeling closer and I can't articulate why.
2. Write out the candidate rules our record is consistent with. I'm pretty sure there's more than one, and I suspect at least one of them implies the aquifer vote was right and the tree vote was wrong for reasons nobody in the room gave. If any of the six are flat-out inconsistent with each other, say which, and which one I should stop defending.
3. Tell me what to vote, and what we'd be committing ourselves to either way, in language I could use at a meeting without sounding like a textbook. If the choice turns on something we could go find out — how much richer or poorer the town is likely to be, how to handle not knowing — say what.
4. If there are proper names for these rules, give me the names with one plain sentence each, but I care more about the rules than the labels.
5. What should I put on a future agenda to find out which rule we're really running? Some proposal where the rules we might be using would split apart and we'd finally see which one we've been applying all along.
