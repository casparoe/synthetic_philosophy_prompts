For eleven years I have attended a monthly discussion group that meets in the back room of a pub in the town where I live. None of us has any academic training — I was a quantity surveyor until I retired — but one member, a woman called Hazel who spent thirty-odd years as a registrar of births and deaths, is the most rigorously consistent thinker I have ever met, and I write her positions down in a notebook because I lose the thread of an argument within a week. Her settled view is that death is never bad for the person who dies. She takes this from the old argument of Epicurus, that when death is present we are not, so there is nobody for it to be bad for, and she reinforces it with the point Lucretius makes about the eternity after our death being a mirror image of the eternity before it: nobody lies awake anguished over the centuries that elapsed before they were born, so why the fuss about the centuries that will follow. What interests me, though, is not the slogan but the pattern of her verdicts on particular cases, because I cannot work out which rule she is applying, and neither can the rest of the group.

Her verdicts so far, as I have them in the notebook. A healthy twenty-six-year-old killed instantly in a collision on the motorway, with work she loved and children she was planning to have: not bad for her, and Hazel insists that what people are really reacting to is how bad it is for her mother and her partner, which is a different question entirely. The same young woman, but told in January that she has perhaps six months, and spending them in pain and dread: those six months are bad for her, appallingly so, but the death itself, when it arrives, is not, since on Hazel's account the badness lies wholly in the having of experiences. A man of ninety-three who dies in his sleep, contented, with his affairs in order: not bad. The time before any of us existed: not bad for anyone. Then the case that divides the group. A stonemason who spent forty years restoring a parish church, prouder of that work than of anything else in his life, who wanted it still standing after him: he dies, and some thirty years later the restored fabric is gutted to make way for a car park, while the local paper names him as an instance of sentimental Victorian meddling. Hazel says that is bad for him, and says it flatly, and does not think she is contradicting anything above; her way of putting it is that it is not a matter of his being aware of anything, but of something he cared about while he was alive having been undone. Finally, a woman who dies at forty-seven having asked repeatedly and clearly for the cheapest disposal available, no service, no stone and no mention of her afterwards, whose wishes are followed to the letter and whose death distresses nobody, because nobody who loved her is left. Hazel says that is not bad for her.

The case coming up next month is one I devised myself to test her, and I would like your prediction before she gives hers. A man, call him Alec, kept a local history archive in a room above a shop — boxes of photographs, rate books, reels of tape on which people long dead can be heard talking — and said often that he wanted it to survive him. Two things complicate it. In the last two years of his life he had dementia, and throughout that period he told his niece over and over that the archive was rubbish and that he did not care what became of it; she believed he meant it, and he never returned to his old view, because by the end there was no old view left to return to. Then, four years after his death, a pipe bursts in the empty flat above and the room floods, and everything is destroyed. The landlord had had the pipework inspected and it passed, so nobody was careless and nobody meant him any harm. His niece died the year before the flood and nobody else alive had ever heard of the collection, so nobody is distressed. The following year a historical society in another county digitises a comparable collection from a town Alec never visited, so that the material his archive would have supplied is, in that sense, no longer missing from the world.

What I want is a prediction of Hazel's verdict on the flood, and the reasoning behind it. Please set out the candidate rules that would account for all six of her recorded verdicts at once — I can see at least three, something about the desires a person actually held, something about how they are remembered or regarded, and something about being wronged by other people, and I suspect these are not the only ones worth considering — and say for each whether it makes the flood bad for Alec or not. Then give me your best guess as to what she will actually say and why you prefer that reading of her over the others. I would also like to know whether the notebook is coherent at all, since half the group maintains that she simply contradicts herself between the first four cases and the stonemason, which she denies; if you think she is coherent, tell me what she would thereby be committed to without perhaps knowing it — what principle about harm, or about desire, or about how far a person's interests reach past their own death, she would be adopting by ruling one way rather than the other. Lastly, I have been toying with a further case to pin her down, namely a man who begs his executors to burn his letters and whose letters are published after his death to general acclaim, but I cannot tell whether that separates the rules or merely re-runs the stonemason. Please tell me whether it does the work, and if it does not, propose the case that would. I have read nothing on any of this, so if there are established names for these positions I should be glad of them, but please explain the reasoning in ordinary language rather than assuming I have come across it before.
