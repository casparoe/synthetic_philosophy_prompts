DRAFT: WHICH PARTS OF A LEGEND CAN BE TRUE?

Three cases are usually lined up together when people ask whether legend ever becomes history: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. The standard way of framing the comparison is evidential: Troy has archaeology, Arthur has almost nothing, the Exodus has Egyptian silence. I want to argue that this framing misdescribes the situation. The three cases do not differ primarily in how much evidence has survived. They differ in what kind of inference the surviving material can support, and once you see that, the question people actually want to ask — is the story true? — turns out to be malformed. Historicity is not a predicate of narratives. It is a predicate of the components of narratives.

Consider Troy. Schliemann's identification of Hisarlik with the city of the Iliad in the 1870s was, for a long time, treated as the beginning rather than the end of the question. What the subsequent century of work established is that the mound was occupied through the Late Bronze Age, that a substantial fortified citadel with a lower town beneath it stood there in the thirteenth century BCE, and that the phase conventionally labelled Troy VIIa ended in a destruction horizon around 1180 BCE with fire, collapsed masonry, sling bullets, and unburied bodies. Under Korfmann's directorship from the late 1980s, magnetometric survey suggested a lower town far larger than Blegen had assumed, and with it a city of real commercial and strategic consequence on the Dardanelles — a conclusion attacked in a public German controversy around the turn of the millennium by historians who thought the lower town was overstated and Troy's importance inflated. Meanwhile the Hittite archives, in a language unrelated to Greek and written for administrative purposes, contain a treaty of about 1280 BCE between the Great King Muwatalli II and one Alaksandu, ruler of Wilusa, a name most scholars identify with Ilios and a personal name plausibly cognate with Alexandros; other Hittite texts refer to a land called Ahhiyawa, often identified with the Achaeans, and to earlier trouble over Wilusa.

Now notice what this does and does not license. It licenses the claim that a large, well-placed Anatolian city was violently destroyed at roughly the right time, and that Hittite-era diplomacy in western Anatolia involved a place and a ruler whose names rhyme with the ones in the Greek tradition. It does not license the claim that Mycenaeans did it, still less that the destruction was the event the Iliad narrates. The poems were composed in the eighth or seventh century BCE, some four centuries later, and their world is visibly anachronistic — iron, cremation, and a political geography that fits the poet's own time as easily as the Bronze Age. The influential argument that the Catalogue of Ships preserves genuine Mycenaean geography is exactly that: an argument, and a contested one. What the Trojan case actually shows is that a place and a catastrophe can be historical while the story told about them remains fiction.

Arthur is usually said to be worse off, and he is, but for a reason that is rarely stated precisely. Gildas, writing in the sixth century, describes the siege of Badon as the great turning point of his own people's history and never names a commander on the British side. Bede in the eighth century is likewise silent. The first definite mention comes more than three centuries after the events, in the Historia Brittonum of about 830, which gives a list of twelve battles fought by a war-leader — not a king — and culminates at Badon. The tenth-century Welsh annals attach dates to Badon and to Camlann. A passing comparison in the Gododdin poetry, in which a warrior is praised by being said to fall short of Arthur, may be early or may be a later insertion, and its manuscript is thirteenth-century. Everything that makes Arthur recognisable — the court, the round table, the queen, the wizard, the kingship — is late, and much of it is Geoffrey of Monmouth in the 1130s. Since Dumville's attack in the 1970s the specialist consensus has hardened against historicity, and there is a further difficulty that no amount of source criticism can fix: the name itself may not be a name of a person at all, but a compound built on a word for bear, or a borrowing of the Latin gentilicium Artorius. In that case the tradition preserves a word, not a residue.

The Exodus looks at first like the mirror image of Arthur, because here the silence is Egyptian and monumental rather than literary. No Egyptian source records an Israelite sojourn, the plagues, or a pursuing force lost in the sea; and the Merneptah stele of about 1208 BCE already lists Israel among the defeated in Canaan, with the determinative for a people rather than for a town or a state, which sets an outer limit on how late any departure could be. Two things then pull in opposite directions. On one side, the narrative contains what look like genuine residues of contact with Egypt: the store cities are given as Pi-Ramesses and Pithom, and the very name Moses is Egyptian in form, matching the element found in Thutmose and Ramose, while the tradition attaches the deity to the southern wilderness and to a Midianite priest. There is also an Egyptian place-name list from the late fifteenth or fourteenth century BCE that appears to name a Shasu territory after a deity read as Yhw, which would put that name in the south long before any Israelite state. On the other side, the narrative fails at scale. Six hundred thousand men on foot, with dependents, implies a migrating population of perhaps two million moving through a desert whose oases and wells are known and have been surveyed, and arriving in a highland Canaan whose early Iron Age villages are continuous in pottery, architecture, and subsistence with the local Canaanite culture they emerge from. Excavation at Kadesh-barnea has produced a substantial Iron Age fortress and nothing of Late Bronze date.

Here I want to draw a distinction that I think does real work and that the Troy-Arthur-Exodus comparison otherwise obscures. Arguments from silence come in two kinds. A silence is recordable when the missing item is a document or inscription that a bureaucratic or monumental culture might reasonably have been expected to produce; such silences are weak, because states do not commemorate humiliation. A silence is consequential when the event, if it occurred as described, would have left physical traces irrespective of anyone's intention to record them; such silences are strong, because they cannot be explained by motivated forgetting. Egypt's failure to mention an exodus is a recordable silence and proves little. The absence of any demographic, settlement, or mortuary discontinuity in Canaan corresponding to the arrival of two million people is a consequential silence and proves a great deal. The Exodus case is not weakly evidenced because Egypt is quiet. It is weakly evidenced because the story's central quantitative claims are the kind that cannot go unrecorded.

Putting the three together suggests a test with three clauses. A component of a legendary narrative earns a claim to historicity when it is dysfunctional for the tradition that carries it — it serves no theological, dynastic, or dramatic purpose, or positively embarrasses the tellers; when it is corroborated through a medium that the tradition could not have contaminated, ideally in an unrelated language or in the ground; and when it is stable across the recoverable stratigraphy of the tradition rather than progressively elaborated. Badon is stable but uncorroborated. The destruction of Hisarlik is corroborated but tells us nothing about who destroyed it. The Egyptian names and the southern deity are corroborated in the ground and in foreign onomastics but do not support the migration they are embedded in. Each case satisfies some clauses and fails others, and the pattern of failure, not the quantity of evidence, is what makes the cases come out differently.

So the answer to the title question is that a legend becomes history piecemeal, at the level of names, places, catastrophes, and deities, and almost never at the level of plots. What I want to test in the discussion is whether that piecemeal conclusion is worth anything, or whether it is simply a way of agreeing with whatever the local specialist consensus already says.

The comments I am after, in five parts:

First, accuracy. Work through the historical claims one at a time — the destruction date and character of Troy VIIa, the Korfmann lower-town controversy, the Hittite treaty and the identifications of Wilusa, Alaksandu, and Ahhiyawa, the silence of Gildas and Bede on Arthur, the date and character of the Historia Brittonum's mention, the Merneptah stele and its determinative, the store cities and the onomastics of the name Moses, the southern deity in the Egyptian name list, the numbers in the wilderness narrative, and the state of the Canaanite highland settlement evidence. For each, mark it safe, doubtful, or wrong, and where you think I am wrong, say what you take the correct version to be and how confident you are. If you are reconstructing rather than recalling, say so explicitly rather than guessing at a title or a date.

Second, the framework. Tell me whether the three-clause test earns its place or is a relabelling of criteria that source criticism already has — multiple independent attestation, the criterion of embarrassment, the direction of tradition growth. In particular, say whether the distinction between recordable and consequential silence is a real distinction or whether it collapses into a claim about expected magnitudes of physical trace. If it survives, give me a case where the two come apart in the opposite direction from the Exodus, that is, where a recordable silence should have been treated as decisive.

Third, whether the argument is circular. I claim the three cases differ in kind of inference rather than in amount of evidence. Test whether that claim survives my own case analyses or whether I have assumed it, especially in the Arthur section, where I seem to be treating the absence of near-contemporary mention as decisive and then explaining why silence is not decisive.

Fourth, the best objections. State the strongest available defence of each of the three positions — a defender of a genuine Achaean war at Troy, a defender of a historical Arthur as a fifth- or sixth-century British commander, and a defender of a real, small-scale migration out of Egypt that later grew in the telling — and tell me which of the three does the most damage to my argument as written. Do not strawman any of them.

Fifth, structure and scope. Given the cut to roughly 800 words, tell me what to remove and what to protect, whether the closing claim that legends become history at the level of names and places rather than plots is a substantive thesis or a slogan, and whether I should add a fourth case to stress the framework.
