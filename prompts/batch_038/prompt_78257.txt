Been reading alignment stuff since the old Overcoming Bias days, and Goodhart's law is probably the most-repeated sentence in the whole community: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Last week it came up in a Discord and two people used it to argue for opposite conclusions inside the same hour, which broke my brain a little.

Person A: human approval is a proxy for what we actually value, so an approval-directed agent is a Goodhart machine waiting to happen — crank the optimization pressure up and it finds the reward-hacking corner of the proxy while the thing we care about tanks. Conclusion: you want agents aimed at the terminal thing, not at a stand-in for it.

Person B: any explicit goal you write down is *also* a proxy — a frozen crystallization of a messy, partly inarticulate value — and freezing it is exactly the setup the law warns about. Approval-directed agents are safer precisely because they keep tracking live human judgment instead of a specification nobody meant literally. Conclusion: aiming at the proxy is the mistake; aim at the judge.

Same slogan, opposite verdicts, nobody budged. My suspicion is they aren't even asserting the same claim and that the slogan is doing at least four different jobs. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be standing for — actually spell each one out with its quantifiers and its failure mechanism, not just give me labels — and for each say whether it's a near-tautology, a substantive empirical claim about a *shape* (how much optimization pressure before the proxy stops paying for itself?), flat-out false, or self-undermining? Then tell me per reading whether it supports A, B, or neither, and which one the people who repeat the slogan usually have in mind without knowing it.

Context that's been nagging me: the catchy version isn't Goodhart's own words, it's a later gloss. His was about UK monetary policy in the mid-70s and went "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." Which reads to me like a specifically *interventional* claim — the regularity collapses because the act of controlling changes the system that generated it — and I can't see why that would transfer to a fixed reward model optimizing a static proxy with nobody else in the loop. If that's right, does A's use of the slogan fail outright, or does it survive on some other reading? Related: is there a seperate reading where the failure needs other agents who can game the metric strategically, versus one that's pure selection-on-imperfect-correlation with no agents required at all? And is there any reading under which pointing at a proxy is strictly *better* than pointing at the target — something like "you cannot not Goodhart," because the target was never accessible to begin with? That last one feels like what B is really leaning on, but I can't tell whether it's a claim or just a shrug dressed up as one.

Menu of readings with verdicts, please, not an essay that picks a favorite and defends it. And flag anywhere you think my framing is smuggling in an assumption — especially if I've quietly assumed that the proxy-vs-target distinction is doing the same work in the RLHF case as it did in the monetary case.
