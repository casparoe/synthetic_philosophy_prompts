I've come around to Benatar's asymmetry — not the whole better-never-to-have-been package, just the asymmetry itself: the absence of pain is good even when nobody's around to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure isn't bad unless there's someone it deprives. From which I read off that bringing someone into existence always harms them, however well their life goes.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked, from "this kills the view" down to "awkward but survivable," and for each one, whether a serious antinatalist would concede it bites or wave it off — and what entitles them to. I'm less interested in "your premises clash with my intuitions" objections than in ones with some structural force: consequences the view is stuck with, cases it can't describe, or a result showing the asymmetry collapses into something its defenders explicitly reject (a person-affecting view, say, or a plain negative utilitarianism). Tell me where it stands on the question of whether it risks anything at all.

Second thing, and it's the one actually keeping me up. I have my own gloss on why the asymmetry is true: harm matters because there's somebody to lodge a complaint, and a world in which the sufferer is never created has no complaint in it even though it has the good of no suffering — whereas a world in which the happy person is never created has no complaint either, so nothing bad has happened. Asymmetry falls out of the complaint structure. But I now worry this shows the asymmetry is just an artifact of taking nonexistence as the baseline, and baselines are picked, not found; if I'd anchored on the happy person who exists I'd get a mirror-image asymmetry with the same complaint logic. Is that a genuine defeater for my view, or is it just a restatement of what the view already says? And if the asymmetry really is baseline-relative in that way, does it retain any bite — or have I qualified it into a position that can't lose, and if so is that actually a problem for a view of this kind?
