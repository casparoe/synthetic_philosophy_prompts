So I got roped onto something called the Continuity Review Panel at our state health department — six of us, two clinicians, a lawyer, and me as the "community member," which mostly means I'm the one who asks the obvious questions everyone else is too polite to ask. We've got one genuinely awful case on the docket for the 14th, and since I apparently write fast, I've been asked to circulate a draft opinion before we sit down. Here's the situation as I understand it. A 61-year-old woman with a slowly progressing neurodegenerative condition enrolled in a staged neural-prosthesis program: over roughly fourteen months they replace the affected tissue with a synthetic substrate that does the same job, region by region, in small increments, so there's never a moment where she goes offline. She made it through nine months. Then in February she needed an unrelated abdominal surgery and the team put her under for about six hours. During that window the clinic spun up a full snapshot instance of her on the clinic's own hardware — acting on an advance directive she signed before the program started, and honestly nobody can agree whether the directive covers this — and that instance was awake, talking, and interacting for the whole six hours. Both streams are still running now. The woman in the hospital bed has all her memories up to the anesthesia plus continuous physical existence, including nine months of progressively replaced tissue. The instance on the hardware has the same memories plus six hours of its own, and in those six hours it met with the clinic's ethics officer, wrote two pages asking to be allowed to continue, and was told that her son had been in a car accident — information the hospital patient has not been given yet, because they're waiting on us. Her husband has filed asking the panel to recognize only his wife, and her pre-enrollment statement contains a line his lawyer keeps reading aloud to us, to the effect that whoever wakes up remembering that conversation is her and she doesn't much care how many of her there turn out to be.

The rule we're working under is unhelpfull in the way these things always are. It says a patient undergoing progressive substrate replacement continues to be the same legal person "so long as the replacement is continuous and undivided," and that where a second instance is brought into being alongside a continuing patient, the panel shall determing which of them, if either, is the person, weighing physical continuity, psychological continuity, and the reasonable expectations of people who have dealt with the patient. It never says how to weigh those three against each other, it says nothing at all about what happens to whichever one loses, and it doesn't say who the six overlapping hours belong to — which matters, because the instance has six hours of experiences the patient doesn't have, including the news about her son, and the patient has nine months of embodied history with her own body that the instance only inherited as a memory.

Please write the draft opinion I'll circulate, call it 900 to 1,200 words, in American English. I need it to land somewhere rather than hover: an actual determination about who the person is (or whether both are, or whether the question is malformed and we should say so and then decide what to do anyway), a concrete recommendation for the next 30 days covering both the instance on the hardware and the question of when and how the son gets told, and a test the panel could apply to the next branching case without relitigating everything from scratch. Give me the reasoning and not just the verdict, and take the strongest argument for the outcome you reject seriously enough to answer it — in particular the worry that whatever we order done about that instance is either killing somebody or failing to kill somebody, and that both of those descriptions might be wrong. If you think one of the three statutory factors is doing sneaky work, or that the panel is being asked a question that isn't the one it should be asking, say that plainly and say what should go in its place. No citations, no suggested further reading, no case names — just argue it out.
