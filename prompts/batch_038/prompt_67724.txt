I've been reading a lot of mechanistic interpretability work lately and something about how the field describes itself has been bugging me. I want to figure out what the actual practice is committed to, as opposed to what people say they're doing.

Here's the practice. You train a sparse autoencoder on the activations at some layer of a big language model, it spits out a few thousand directions in activation space, and then you name them: this one is code bugs, this one is a particular famous bridge, this one is text where the speaker is flattering someone. You check the names by intervening — crank the direction up and the model starts shoehorning the bridge into every conversation, suppress it and the behaviour shifts the way you'd expect. And then the whole thing gets written up in a discovery register. Features are found, not made. The implicit picture is that the trained model already contains internal states corresponding to human-legible concepts, they were stacked on top of each other in superposition the whole time, and the autoencoder is a microscope that finally resolves them.

Now the part I can't square with that. The same people, sometimes in the same paper, will report matter-of-factly that if you crank the sparsity penatly or double the size of the dictionary you get a different inventory — features split, features merge, what was one thing at low resolution is three things at high resolution. Nobody treats this as alarming. It's framed as a resolution issue, like zooming in on a map. But if the features were really in there the way, say, elements are in a sample of ore, your choice of how hard to penalize density shouldn't change what's there. It's like doing chemistry where the care with which you set up your apparatus determines whether you have one substance or two.

Second thing, and this one might be my own confusion. The field seems to run two different tests for whether a feature really is X, and I don't think they're tests of the same claim. One is correlational: does it fire reliably when X shows up in the input. The other is causal: does manipulating it change what the model does. And everyone in this area knows those come apart — there's a long-standing, very well established complaint about classifiers trained on top of a network's hidden states, which is that being able to decode some property from a state (the grammatical subject of a sentence, whether a certain token is present) tells you the information is there in some sense but not that the network uses it for anything. Yet in the feature literature both results get offered as evidence for the same conclusion, often in adjacent paragraphs.

So what I want:

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Not the slogan — the actual commitments. What does it take for an internal state to have the content the field assigns to it, and is the right decomposition of the model into meaningful parts fixed by the model or partly by the analyst? Then sort the rival positions into ones this practice actively rules out and ones it's just silent about. The test I care most about: could a thoroughgoing instrumentalist — someone who thinks talk of the model representing anything is only ever shorthand for input-output dispositions, with no further fact underneath — go through the identical motions, train the identical autoencoders, publish the identical papers? If yes, then the discovery language isn't doing any work and the whole thing is philosophically underdetermined by the practice. If no, point me at the specific step in the workflow that only makes sense if you're a realist, because I haven't been able to find one.

Then the consistency question. Is there any single account of content under which the correlational test and the causal test are both evidence about the identity of a feature, and under which the hyperparameter sensitivity is genuinely a non-issue rather than a threat? Or does the practice need those to be two different questions that it's quietly sliding together?

And finally, if the field dropped the realism and became explicitly conventionalist about features — treating them as useful fictions indexed to a modelling choice rather than discoveries — what would actually change on the ground? My suspicion is: almost nothing, which is exactly what worries me. But tell me which reporting norms, which eval metrics, and which safety-relevant claims would have to be rewritten, and whether any of them would survive the rewrite.
