I'm an infrastructure engineer at a company that deploys large language models; I have no philosophy training, so please treat the following as an engineering intuition I want converted into an argument.

Our safety architecture is a stack. The deployed model is monitored by an oversight model, the oversight model's verdicts are audited by a third model, and a small team of humans reads that model's reports. Each level is checked by the level above it, and my colleagues assume that adding levels adds security. I want to argue that the stack cannot be closed: any control-oriented safety architecture retains a level that is trusted rather than tested, and this is structural rather than an artifact of our headcount or tooling.

Please argue the stronger form, which I've taken to calling conservation of unverified trust: checking or automating a given rung does not remove the unexamined level, it relocates it one step up, so the quantity of trust that is never itself verified stays roughly constant no matter how many monitors are stacked.

Two constraints. The argument must actually use the assumption the control paradigm exists to handle — that the monitored system may be hostile to being monitored — and not reduce to the generic regress objection available against any layered software. And it must meet the strongest version of the reply that this is simply the compiler-trusting-compilers problem wearing a new vocabulary: show either why the case genuinely differs, or why that reply costs my colleagues more than they think.

Present the argument as explicit premises leading to a conclusion, and name the premise you would stake the least on. If you think the claim is false, or true only in a trivial sense, argue for that instead.
