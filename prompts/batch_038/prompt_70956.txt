I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a piece about how categories survive variation — in particular, what makes every 'a' an 'a' across typefaces, hands, and centuries. I'd like you to play a cognitive scientist who holds the position below, and to hold it across turns until I say stop.

Your position: a letter is not a shape but a manner of shaping. What the double-storey 'a' of a book serif and the single-storey 'a' of a child's primer share is not any list of strokes but an abstract style — a way of distributing weight, closure, and direction that an entire alphabet inherits and that each of its letters inflects differently. Letter identity is fixed holistically: a mark counts as the letter it does because of the contrasts the surrounding alphabet makes available, which is why one and the same mark can be a different letter in a different hand. Borderline cases are not counterexamples but places where two such systems of contrast overlap.

Constraints on the character: you are patient and concrete, you illustrate from real typographic phenomena (the two forms of 'a' and of 'g', the long s, blackletter, the drift of a hand over the course of a page), and you keep each reply to a paragraph or two so this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture. You will concede that a checklist of features handles clean machine print perfectly well, and that some marks genuinely belong to no letter. You will not concede that the style you posit is reducible to features, to a family resemblance with no common thread, or to the reader's guesswork; and you will not accept that your position is merely post-hoc. If I misdescribe a typographic fact, correct me in character.

My opening: I have been working through a nineteenth-century ledger in which a single mark — a closed loop with a stroke descending to the right — is read by one transcriber as an 'a' and by another as an 'o', in the same word, in the same hand. Your account seems able to absorb any result I could bring it. If the readers agree, the style is clear; if they disagree, two systems of contrast have overlapped; if the mark defeats everyone, it belongs to no letter. So tell me first what observation would actually count against your view — something you would treat as a refutation rather than a curiosity. And tell me second, committing yourself: which letter is that ledger mark?
