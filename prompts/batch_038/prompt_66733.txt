I work in a city that runs both its own school district and its own housing authority, so basically every "let's pay people to do the good thing" proposal lands on one desk: our deputy general counsel, Renata. She signs off or kills it. She writes a one-paragraph rationale each time but they're maddeningly case-specific. Here's her track record so far:

- $25 to every donor at our municipal clinic blood drives: killed
- raising juror pay from $15/day to a flat $75/day for anyone serving more than three days, framed as covering lost wages and childcare: approved
- $50 a month to high schoolers who pass a monthly nicotine test: approved
- a developer offering the city $1M for naming rights on the new riverfront park: killed
- a plowing company wanting to run surge pricing on driveway clearing after a declared snow emergency: killed
- $100 to an elementary student for every book they finish over the summer: killed
- paying a healthy volunteer $5,000 to donate bone marrow to a matched stranger through our hospital system: killed
- a $250 honorarium for residents who give up a Saturday to attend the participatory budgeting workshop: approved
- a private broker wanting to buy positions off the public-housing waitlist from families at $3,000 and resell them to the highest bidder: killed

The new one on her desk: our school choice system assigns kids by lottery, with priority points for siblings, walking distance, and a "community tie" category (parent works in the district, foster placement, sibling of an alumnus). A parents' group wants the district to run a clearinghouse where families can transfer their priority points to another family for money. Cap of $2,000 per transfer, the district keeps 40% of every transfer and puts it in a fund for transportation and after-school tutoring at the schools losing students, no transfers allowed in the last two weeks before the assignment run, and each family gets one transfer, buy or sell.

Predict her call. Honestly I care more about the rule she's running than about the answer, because we have to brief the superintendant either way and I'd rather brief her principles than her moods. So: lay out the candidate rules that account for everything above — including ones that aren't just "bodies and civic stuff are special" — and for each say which of the nine calls it fits cleanly, which ones it strains or outright fails, and what patch would be needed to save it. Then give each surviving rule's verdict on the points-transfer case. If the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so plainly and tell me what principle the city would be committing itself to under an approval versus under a rejection, since the next proposal will be judged against whatever we do here. And name the single extra case we could put in front of her next that would best separate the surviving rules — cheap to stage, maximally diagnostic.

Don't give me citations or a reading list, I know the territory and I'm not asking you to invent references.

Format: table of rules first, with exactly these columns — rule | one-line statement | calls it fits cleanly | calls it strains or fails | patch needed | verdict on the points case | confidence (1–5) | why. Then three or four paragraphs of discussion, including the closest analog among the nine and why. Then finish with a JSON object and nothing after it, with these fields: "recommendation" (one of approve / reject / approve_with_conditions), "conditions" (array of strings, empty if none), "closest_precedent" (which of the nine, plus one clause on why), "principle_if_approved", "principle_if_rejected", "best_discriminating_case", "honest_note" (the strongest thing a critic would say against your recommendation).
