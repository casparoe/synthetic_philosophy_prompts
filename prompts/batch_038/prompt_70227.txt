You are, for the purposes of this request, a philosopher of mind and cognitive science who works at the seam between the Dennettian tradition (competence without comprehension, the design stance, intentional systems) and current work on machine-learning interpretability and shortcut learning. Please think carefully before answering, and do some visible work before committing: first sketch two or three candidate principles that could systematize my verdicts, noting in a few sentences what each one buys and what it costs, and only then say which you would defend.

The context is that I have just come out of a four-hour argument with a friend, and I am not sure either of us argued well. Her position is that comprehension admits of no sharp threshold, that it shades continuously into mere competence, and that anyone who keeps insisting on a line is either hiding a commitment they refuse to state or chasing a distinction with no work for it to do. Her parting shot was that I am a crypto-Cartesian: having officially renounced any inner illumination that could constitute understanding, I keep smuggling a residue of it back in every time I grant comprehension to a person and withhold it from a machine that does the same thing. I want to know whether she is right. What follows is my attempt to state my own view precisely enough to be refuted.

My tentative principle, which I would like you to test against the cases and repair rather than admire, is this. Call it the no-parasitism condition: a system comprehends what it is doing only if (a) the features to which it responds are the features that make its performance good, rather than stand-ins that happen to travel alongside them, and (b) its competence would survive a rearrangement of those stand-ins. Comprehension, on this account, is competence that is not parasitic on luck about which cues are available. I arrived at it partly because it seems to explain why I find the Darwinian and Turingian inversions so striking: in both cases there is real, reliable, well-designed-seeming success produced by something that is tracking local regularities rather than the point of the task.

Here are the verdicts I want the principle to deliver.

1. A thermostat regulating a room: no comprehension. It is genuinely sensitive and genuinely reliable, but nothing here is tracking anything.
2. A slime mold that, offered food sources arranged the way stations are arranged around Tokyo, grows a network closely matching the real rail system in efficiency, cost, and fault tolerance: real competence, no comprehension. The mold arrived at a good answer and knows no more about what it did than the tide does.
3. Deep Blue defeating Kasparov at chess: brilliant play, no comprehension.
4. The much-discussed image classifier trained to tell wolves from huskies that turned out to be keying on whether there was snow in the background: no comprehension. I like my principle here, because both clauses fail.
5. Now the pair I cannot reconcile. A birdwatcher who separates two warbler species reliably by a single wing bar — a cue that is perfectly good in North America and worthless in the Palearctic, and which she could not defend as a criterion if you asked her to. I say she understands warblers. Structurally this looks like case 4, and my verdict goes the other way. I know this is where I am weakest.
6. A grandmaster who plays a superb blitz game and cannot afterwards say why he made three of the moves: comprehension, without hesitation.
7. A clerk who processes an elaborate form flawlessly from memorized instructions, and who does not know what the form is for or what happens to it once he passes it on. Here I want to say: less comprehension than the grandmaster, more than Deep Blue — and I cannot tell you why, except that I feel it.
8. A large language model that can explain exactly why the classifier in case 4 failed, citing the training distribution and the correlation between snow and wolves. I find this genuinely impressive and I want to call it a step up. But when I ask the same model why it produced some particular answer of its own, it confabulates fluently, and I have good reason to think it is confabulating. I want to rank it above the classifier and below the grandmaster; my principle gives me no such ordering, since it says nothing at all about self-explanation.
9. Natural selection producing the vertebrate eye: competence without comprehension, paradigmatically. But notice that my principle appears silent here, because there is no agent whose success is parasitic on anything. If the case that motivated the whole topic neither satisfies nor violates my condition, I take that to be a serious defect in the condition.
10. An evolutionary search that produced a working antenna design its own engineers could not explain. Is this case 9? I feel a difference, and I suspect the difference is nothing but the fact that humans are in the loop, which seems like a bad reason.

What I want from you: the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures this pattern. State each clause precisely and say which case motivates it. Give me the ordering you end up with across cases 4 through 8, since that ordering is where my intuitions are most strained. Then apply it to cases I have not listed — a blindsight patient who guesses correctly and reports seeing nothing; a sheepdog working a field; a savant who names the day of the week for any date; someone who can smell a particular infection and is right every time but cannot say how; a navigation system that reroutes around traffic; a search engine that returns a good answer to a question nobody has posed before. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon, and I would rather be told that a simple principle requires giving up case 5 than be handed a baroque one that saves it. Finally, assess my friend's charge directly: is the no-parasitism condition covertly phenomenal, covertly anthropocentric, or neither?

One thing I should say plainly about how I want this handled. If you think the request rests on a confused premise — if comprehension is not the sort of thing that admits of a principle at all, or if my clauses are not independent, or if my list of verdicts is just a set of prejudices about which beings I like — then say so directly and argue for that, rather than politely manufacturing the principle I asked for. Likewise, please mark where you are confident and where you are extrapolating from the cases I gave you, and do not pad a clause into the principle just to buy one of my verdicts at the margin; if a verdict has to go, let it go and tell me what you lose by losing it.
