I sit on the committee that hands out our institute's annual prize — its both an honor (a medal plus the named lecture) and a decent chunk of money, and we've never formally separated the two. Here's our track record over the last dozen years, as best I can reconstruct it from the minutes and my memory of the arguments in the room:

- We gave it to a mathematician who'd ground away for eleven years at a problem everyone else had dropped. The result was solid, not spectacular, and nobody in the meeting claimed he was a genius; the case for him was basically that he'd stayed with it.
- One year we gave it to a famously effortless person — a result that looked like it came out of nowhere. The minutes record two rationales, one about the work itself and one about the invisible years before the apparent overnight success, and I dont think the committee ever settled which of the two it was endorsing.
- We turned down a nomination for someone who had put in truly enormous hours building behavioral targeting tech for advertisers. The recorded reason was that we're not in the business of rewarding application as such.
- We gave it to a biologist whose key finding came out of a contaminated sample, on the grounds that she'd then spent years turning the accident into something that stood up. Somebody's phrasing in the minutes was to the effect that the luck was in the finding and the work was in making it real.
- We split it once between the person whose name was on the famous paper and the technician whose unglamorous method made the paper possible — and in that case we split the honor but gave the money to only one of them. I remember that being contentious and the rationale never getting written down properly.
- Twice we've given the early-career version to someone whose absolute output was smaller but who had, in the words we used, done it from a much harder start.

Now this year's live nomination. She used a frontier AI model for the heavy generative lifting on a genuinely important paper: it suggested the key construction, drafted a lot of the intermediate work, and caught an error she'd otherwise have missed. She picked the problem herself, spent about a year checking and extending every line until she understood all of it, and the result is a real advance nobody else has. She also comes from a poor family with no academic background, but she's at one of the handful of institutions that can afford the good subscription and the compute, which most researchers in her field can't. Two of her peers got comparable results with pen and paper over considerably more hours.

What should we decide, if the aim is to be consistent with ourselves rather than with our gut feelings about AI? Specifically:

1. Lay out the candidate rules that fit all six precedents about equally well. I can think of three — reward costly effort, provided it's spent on ends worth pursuing; reward whatever is attributable to the agent once you subtract luck and starting advantage; reward the achievement but let the honor and the money track different things — and I suspect I'm missing at least one.
2. Say what each rule actually delivers on this nomination and exactly where they come apart. Note that our own precedents let us split honor from money, so "award it" may not be one decision but two, and different rules may split it differently.
3. Give me your best guess at what we should do, name which precedent is the closest analog, and say why it's closer than the ones that pull the other way.
4. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine this, say plainly which principle we'd be adopting by ruling one way rather than the other — i.e., what we'd be on the hook for the next time a case like this walks in.
5. Propose a further case, ideally one we could plausibly get a nomination for in the next few years, that would tell the candidate rules apart, and say what each rule predicts about it.

Commit to a recommendation with a rough sense of how confident you are rather than listing everything as equally defensible. Then tell me what would change your mind: which of the six precedents you're leaning on hardest and whether it deserves that weight, what somebody could say in the meeting that would flip your recommendation, and where you think your reconstruction of our implicit rule is most likely to be wrong.
