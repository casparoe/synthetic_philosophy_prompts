You are a philosopher of science who has sat through a great many hostile interdisciplinary seminars and knows precisely when a critique of a popular formal framework lands and when it backfires. Think carefully before writing: begin by working out what each subgroup in the room has at stake, since the same thesis draws quite different objections from people who fit models for a living and from people who think the modelling idiom is already a mistake.

Next month I present a paper at a cognitive science institute's research seminar. My thesis is that the free energy principle is best read as a claim about the boundary conditions of persistence — a system persists insofar as it avoids dispersing into states it cannot distinguish from one another — and not as a theory of mind. The argument turns on the fact that the blanket-relative partition into internal, sensory, and active states is fixed by where one draws the blanket rather than by the dynamics themselves; the same process therefore admits a reading on which there are no probabilistic beliefs inside the system at all. The inference talk, I claim, is a redescription that borrows its plausibility from the empirical success of the models built in the idiom. I conclude that philosophers who deploy the framework to settle questions about mental content are misusing it, and that its own defenders have quietly retreated to an instrumentalism they do not advertise.

The room will consist of computational neuroscientists and two mathematical psychiatrists who fit hierarchical models to behavioural and imaging data and regard them as productive instruments; one phenomenologist who thinks the entire vocabulary gets experience wrong from the outset; and several graduate students in philosophy of psychiatry who intend to build dissertations on aberrant precision. Nobody there works on mental content as such.

Prepare my question-period sheet. Give me the seven or eight objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased in the register of the subgroup that will raise it — the modeller will not put it the way the phenomenologist will. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in ninety seconds, a candid note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede outright. Then identify the one concession that, if I refuse it, costs me the room, and finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the least bad way to survive it. No references or reading lists; I know the literature and want the dialectic, not the citations.
