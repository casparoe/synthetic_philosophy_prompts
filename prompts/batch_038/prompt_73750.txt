I co-direct a three-week residential summer program in astrobiology for advanced undergraduates. This year's cohort is 24: roughly half are biology or chemistry majors with genuine bench experience, and the rest are philosophy, physics, and Earth-science students with none. I have two consecutive 90-minute sessions with the whole cohort in a single room with movable tables, and no wet-lab or computing facilities — everything must run on paper, cards, and a whiteboard.

The problem I want them to run into rather than be told is this: the standard criteria for life — self-sustaining chemistry capable of Darwinian evolution; the maintenance of a steep internal gradient against the environment at continuous metabolic cost; a self-producing, bounded individuality; an informational polymer with heredity — are usually presented as independent tests one applies to a candidate. In practice they are extrapolations from a single sample, they come apart exactly where it matters, and the verdict a group reaches is partly fixed by something they were not treating as evidence at all, namely the cost structure of being wrong.

Please design a two-session activity around the dossier below. I have fixed the dossier and do not want it changed in substance, but I do want it audited — see item 6.

Specimen 9-B. Recovered from a brine lens roughly four kilometres beneath an ice shell, at −18 °C, in cryobot meltwater; held in a sealed sample cell at in-situ temperature and pressure for 400 hours before analysis.

1. Not cellular. No microscopy reveals compartments, membranes, or discrete bodies. It is a continuous, faintly opalescent gel of about 2 mL whose boundary with the surrounding brine is a sharp chemical discontinuity rather than a physical wall.
2. Gradient maintenance. It holds its internal pH 1.8 units from the brine and its internal redox potential about 300 mV from equilibrium with the brine, stably, for the full 400 hours; both collapse within minutes if the cell is warmed above −5 °C. Maintenance is accompanied by continuous uptake of dissolved hydrogen and sulfate at roughly ninety times the rate measured in a sterile abiotic control cell.
3. Isotopes. Carbon and sulfur fractionation in the gel is large, directional, and reproducible across three subsamples; it lies outside the range produced by any known abiotic pathway at these temperatures and matches no known terrestrial biological pathway either.
4. Polymer. A linear polymer is present at about 1% of dry mass. Its backbone contains phosphorus but no identifiable sugar; its side groups resemble neither the canonical amino acids nor the canonical bases. Recovered fragments are non-random and non-repetitive but show no motifs, no reading frames, no complementarity, and no detectable ability to template their own synthesis.
5. Growth and fragmentation. The gel grows by accretion at its brine interface and, above about 2 mL, fragments along shear lines; fragments regrow. Daughters contain polymer, but their sequences bear no detectable relation to the parent's beyond what the shared brine would produce. Across six monitored fragmentation events, variation in growth rate, fractionation, and pH offset does not track lineage.
6. No catalysis. Homogenized or filtered, gradient maintenance stops within seconds and never resumes; the extract accelerates no reaction tested; nothing enzyme-like has been isolated.
7. Acclimatization. When the brine's hydrogen partial pressure is stepped down threefold, uptake kinetics shift within hours, and the shifted kinetics persist after the pressure is restored. The shift is reproducible in three independent samples, is not matched by the abiotic control, and is not accompanied by any detectable change in the polymer. No mechanism has been identified.

Mechanics I want built in:

- A measurement budget. Each group receives a fixed number of instrument-time units; every assay on the menu costs a stated amount and carries a one-line gloss of what it could possibly settle. Groups commit to an assay order before any result is released and cannot undo a purchase.
- Staged release. Results arrive in rounds. The dossier above is not handed over whole at the start — you decide what goes in the initial packet and what each assay buys.
- Assigned advocacy. Each participant is dealt one of the four criteria and must argue the verdict that criterion licenses on the data the group has actually bought, including participants who privately reject their criterion. Tell me how to keep this from collapsing into sincerity, into obvious bad faith, or into the philosophy majors announcing in minute four that the whole thing is a verbal dispute. I need a procedural block on that last move that does not feel like a gag.
- Committed verdicts. Individual written verdicts before group deliberation, a group recommendation after, and a second individual verdict later under a changed payoff structure with no new evidence.
- Two asymmetric scoring rules, revealed late and pulling in opposite directions. Under the first, a false positive is catastrophic (planetary-protection breach, mission cancelled, reputations destroyed) and a false negative is nearly free (the sample is re-analysed on Earth). Under the second, the reverse: a false negative means the discovery of the century is sterilized by the return-sample protocol and no one ever knows. Design them so the same data yields opposite majority verdicts and, importantly, so that the second rule retroactively changes which assays were worth buying. I want that second-order effect surfaced explicitly in the debrief.

Session two should hold terrestrial mirrors up to whatever the groups decided. For each of the four criteria, choose one real entity that nearly everyone in the room will insist is alive yet fails the criterion as applied to 9-B, and one that nearly everyone will insist is not alive yet passes it. The mule, the dry spore or tun-state tardigrade, the propagating crystal-defect population, the flame, the prion strain lineage, and the flow-reactor autocatalytic system are all candidates; tell me which mirror you are pairing with which criterion and why the pairing bites. Structure it so that participants apply their own session-one criterion to the mirrors and reach verdicts they find intolerable.

Deliverables:

1. Full rules, the assay menu with costs and glosses, the card and materials list, and a minute-by-minute timeline for both sessions.
2. The two scoring rules stated exactly, with the arithmetic that makes them diverge.
3. A debrief keyed to moments rather than to topics — the sentence I should say when group three burns its entire budget on sequencing, when a group refuses to commit, when someone notices that their verdict moved without the evidence moving. Include the point at which names finally enter: no participant should hear autopoiesis, the widely used NASA formulation, the shadow-biosphere proposal, or the argument (Carol Cleland's is the best-known version) that we should not expect to fix the extension of a term like life from a single sample and need a theory rather than a definition, until after the session-two mirrors have landed.
4. A candid list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with this cohort — bench-experienced students dominating, a group stalling with unspent budget, the contamination question swallowing session one, the role-play breaking — with the in-room fix for each.
5. Which feature of the real problem the activity cannot reproduce, and what I will have to supply myself.
6. An audit of my dossier. Which of the seven properties are jointly impossible, or would be recognized by a competent biogeochemist as a tell that the specimen is fabricated? Where is the absence of catalysis in tension with the observed acclimatization and the isotope effects? What minimal repairs would preserve the philosophically interesting features? Also tell me whether the contamination complication — a control swab of the sampling train returning low-copy terrestrial 16S, while the gel's own polymer fails to amplify under universal primers — is worth including, and if so where it should land.
7. An honest assessment of the central trick. Changing the scoring rule with no change in evidence risks teaching them that life is merely a decision threshold and that the question is really about institutional incentives. Say whether that risk is real, and how the debrief should defuse it without undercutting the experience of having had their own verdict move.
