"In the mid-1990s David Wolpert proved something disturbing. Average over every possible target function a learner might have to fit, and any two learning algorithms have exactly the same expected error. It makes no difference how clever your method is — deep nets, Bayesian inference, a random-guess oracle — averaged across the space of all possible problems they are indistinguishable. Macready extended the result to search and optimization: over all possible fitness landscapes, no optimizer beats blind guessing. The mechanism is that whatever advantage an algorithm picks up on one set of problems is paid for, dollar for dollar, by a corresponding loss on the rest, so the theorem is a literal conservation law. And this is at last the rigorous version of what Hume was groping toward. There can be no non-circular justification of induction, because the assumption that the future resembles the past is only one hypothesis among infinitely many, and the theorem shows that all of them tie. The practical upshot is uncomfortable but unavoidable. Any benchmark on which one method beats another is measuring nothing about the methods themselves, only about the particular slice of function space that benchmark happens to sample. So when deep networks dominate image classification, that is a fact about image datasets and not about deep learning; the success gives no warrant at all that the same architecture will handle the next problem you hand it. The whole business of inductive bias — the idea that some priors are simply better than others — turns out to be a confession that we picked a prior we happen to like. There is no such thing as a generally better learner, and there never can be."
