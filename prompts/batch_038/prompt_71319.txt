I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student, nominally in ethics, but I've spent the last two semesters sitting in on the formal epistemology reading group because they get the good room and somebody always brings pizza. Last month we decided we could wrap up the peer-disagreement debate in a single sitting, and I think we produced an argument that is valid, has premises none of us wants to deny, and has a conclusion none of us will sign. I've been staring at it for two weeks and I'm no closer. Reconstruction, cleaned up a bit:

(1) If two agents are Bayesian, start from a common prior, and it's common knowledge between them what each one's credence in p is, then those credences are equal. (Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result.)
(2) Rationality about degrees of belief is nothing over and above Bayesianism: coherent credences, updated by conditionalization. There's no further standard an agent could be meeting or failing to meet.
(3) Rational agents cannot differ in their priors. Priors are by definition what you believe before you've had any evidence; so no evidence can possibly bear on which prior is the right one; so a difference in priors is a difference with no rational ground whatever; and a person cannot be rationally permitted to believe something that has no rational ground at all.
(4) So: any two rational agents who commonly know each other's credence in p have the same credence in p.
(5) Hence mutually known, persistent disagreement shows that at least one party is irrational.
(6) My advisor and I have been fighting about the Sleeping Beauty case for four years — I half, she thirds. We've read each other's drafts, we can finish each other's arguments, and we've both announced our credences out loud in front of the whole group more than once, so whatever common knowledge requires, we've got it.
(7) So one of us is irrational. Same for every halfer/thirder pair that knows what the other thinks.
(8) If no two people who disagree can both be rational, then at most one person in the whole debate is rational: pick any two of them and at least one is out.
(9) Dozens of people have taken public stands on this question. I'm one of them, and I have no special reason to think I'm the survivor. So I should conclude that I'm the irrational one.
(10) But if I'm irrational, my reasoning is no guide to anything, including no guide to whether this arguement is sound. So if the argument is sound, I'm not in a position to accept it.

So it looks like the thing eats itself, which is exactly the kind of result that makes me suspect a bad step rather than that philosophy is over. But I genuinely can't find the step. Two notes so you don't waste your time on routes we've already walked. Someone in the group proposed that (6) fails because all we have is mutual knowledge to some high finite level rather than real common knowledge, and I think that's a non-starter: we can just keep making public announcements until it is common knowledge, and anyway the relaxations of the theorem I know about, the ones that let the priors be ε-close instead of identical, only shrink the disagreement — they don't kill it, and a gap of a sixth isn't ε-small for any ε worth the name. Also, for what it's worth, I'd much rather give up (10) than (2); (2) feels like the only thing keeping the word "rational" attached to something substantive. So tell me: which step do you reject, why did it look unobjectionable when we wrote it down (I want the mechanism, not just a label), and what does rejecting it actually cost — does it kill the whole Aumann-flavored move in the disagreement debate, or is there a weaker version of it that survives? And while you're in there, is (9) even licensed, or am I doing something illegitimate when I put myself into the sample like that becuase I happen to be one of the disputants?
