A major American survey-research organization defines Millennials as everyone born from 1981 through 1996 and Generation Z as everyone born from 1997 onward. Its note explaining that choice says two things that sit oddly together: "Generational cutoff points aren't an exact science," and they should be viewed primarily as tools for analysis — yet "their boundaries are not arbitrary," because most Millennials were old enough to register the attacks of September 11, 2001, while most of Generation Z has no memory of them. The same note points out that the Census Bureau has officially recognized only the Baby Boom. Downstream, however, the organization's charts draw a hard line at 1996, and its prose reports that Generation Z is more anxious, more diverse, and more digitally native than Millennials, as though someone born in December 1996 and someone born in January 1997 belonged to different kinds of people. Meanwhile demographers note that because cohort membership is simply period minus age, a linear age trend, a linear period trend, and a linear cohort trend are mathematically indistinguishable in survey data unless one of them is assumed away — and most of the evidence here is cross-sectional, so an apparent generation effect could be an age effect or a period effect wearing a generation's clothes.

What account of what generations are does this practice presuppose? I'd like you to work through several questions:

- Does fixing birth-year boundaries and then predicating properties of the bounded group require generations to be real, causally efficacious kinds, or can the whole practice run on something weaker — nominal kinds, analytic conveniences, or categories that the naming itself helps bring into being?
- Which rival accounts does the hard line at 1996 quietly rule out: that only birth cohorts of any arbitrarily chosen width are real, that nothing exists but individuals and their exposure histories (which vary continuously and unequally within a single birth year), or that the boundary is a matter of degree rather than a joint?
- Is the practice internally consistent, or does the methods note presuppose instrumentalism while the charts and the prose presuppose realism? If inconsistent, which half is doing the actual explanatory work?
- Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — that is, where would two or three incompatible ontologies license exactly the same published charts?
- If the organization adopted an exposure-based rather than a birth-year-based conception of cohorts, what would have to change in its output: the labels, the boundary years, the chart design, or the wording of its findings?

Throughout, please separate what is settled among demographers and social ontologists who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus. Write the answer in American English.
