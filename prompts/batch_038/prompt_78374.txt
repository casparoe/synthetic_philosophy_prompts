Last night a friend of mine — a former options trader who has spent the past two years immersed in the so-called ergodicity economics literature, and who argues on napkins — and I went until two in the morning over whether the Kelly criterion is a fact of arithmetic or a preference wearing a disguise, and I want the dispute written up properly by someone who will not simply adopt his side or mine. The case we were fighting over is this. You are offered a repeated gamble: each round you choose what fraction of your current wealth to stake; on a win, probability one half, the stake comes back plus fifty per cent of its value; on a loss the stake comes back less forty per cent of its value; whatever you do not stake sits in cash and earns nothing. Staking everything has a positive expected return of five per cent per round, and yet if you stake everything every round your wealth almost surely ends up near zero, since the typical path shrinks by roughly five per cent per round in the long-run geometric sense. Staking one quarter of your wealth each round is the fraction that maximizes the long-run growth rate, and it compounds at something like 0.6 per cent per round. My friend holds that this settles the question objectively. The twenty-five per cent figure is not anyone's taste; the expected-value maximizer who stakes everything is not merely venturesome but flatly mistaken, because the ensemble average his calculation appeals to is the average of nothing that ever happens to anybody — what happens to a single agent living forward through time is the time average, and multiplicative wealth dynamics are non-ergodic, so the two come apart. On his view the whole apparatus of utility functions over wealth is a confused detour, the growth rate is the only quantity entitled to be called the value of a strategy, and anyone who answers "it depends on your utility function" has misunderstood what kind of question is being asked.

I put four objections to him and he answered them with what seemed to me varying degrees of success, so I would like them treated rather than listed. First, the coincidence: maximizing the expected logarithm of terminal wealth is precisely what an expected-utility maximizer with logarithmic utility does, so my friend appears not to have abolished the utility function but to have nailed it to one particular shape and christened the result arithmetic. What, if anything, does the ergodicity argument contribute beyond that stipulation, and is there an independent reason to think the time average rather than the ensemble average is the normatively relevant one — particularly when, for a single agent deciding once, the ensemble of possible futures looks like the only thing there is to average over? Second, the horizon: asymptotic dominance is a claim about a limit, and I am not a limit; over any finite number of rounds there are fixed fractions that beat the growth-optimal one by the probability of finishing above a target I actually care about, and by other measures people plausibly care about, so I want to know whether the long run is doing real work here or merely rhetorical work. Third, the one-shot case: if the identical bet is offered exactly once and never again, there is no time average to take, and I could not get a straight answer out of him about what his criterion then prescribes or on what grounds. Fourth, absorbing states: actual wealth dynamics contain floors — a margin call, a bankruptcy, a pension that cannot be rebuilt — and a growth rate is defined only for an agent who survives to keep compounding, which makes the criterion look parasitic on an assumption about the absence of ruin that it never states aloud.

Please write an essay of roughly two thousand words on this controversy, pitched at a reader who is mathematically competent but not a specialist in decision theory: state the growth-rate criterion and the expected-utility criterion precisely enough that the exact relationship between them is unmistakable, give each side its strongest formulation rather than a straw man, and then reach and defend a definite verdict on whether geometric rationality is a genuine rival foundation for choice under risk or a repackaged preference. Do not close on a balanced note. I want to know which of us was right, or, if neither was, what the correct view is that both of us missed. The verdict must have consequences: tell me what fraction of my wealth to stake if the bet is offered once and never again, if it is offered every week for forty years, and if it is offered every week for forty years with a floor below which I am expelled from the game and can never return; and say explicitly whether those three prescriptions are generated by a single principle or by three different ones that happen to agree in one region. Where the objection I have just made to my friend is weaker than I think it is, say so and press on. Argue from the reasoning and the mathematics themselves — I want no literature review, no bibliography, no reading list, and no appeals to what some author has claimed; where a name is unavoidable, treat it as a label and not as authority.
