I am stuck on the central passage of the lecture-essay *Mind and Matter* and would like your help understanding what it is actually doing. Please think carefully, and before you evaluate anything, do a short brainstorming pass laying out the candidate readings you can see, so I can follow how you get to the one you defend.

Because I am working from my own notes rather than pasting the page, what follows is a sentence-by-sentence reconstruction of the argument, not his wording. Treat the reconstruction as the object of analysis, but tell me where you think it is likely to distort the original and what would turn on the difference.

(1) No one has ever had an experience that was not his or her own single experience; consciousness is given always in the singular, never in the plural.
(2) Everything we take ourselves to know about other minds is therefore not experience of other consciousnesses but an inference from the bodies and behaviour we do observe: we count bodies and posit one mind per body.
(3) That inference is not compulsory. Were one somehow to enter another person's experience, one would not find a second, alien consciousness there, but the same single I thinking, remembering and speaking from another body. The hypothesis of many subjects thus yields nothing experienceable that differs from the hypothesis of one subject appearing in many places, which he illustrates with analogies of the many-reflections-of-one-thing kind.
(4) The absence of memory links between my experiences and yours is no evidence of a difference of subject. Memory is only part of the record, and the record is accessible only from inside one appearance; an interruption of consciousness in sleep or anaesthesia does not divide me, since the same I resumes, so a lack of shared memories does not multiply me either.
(5) Hence the plurality of conscious beings is appearance only, and this, he says, is what the Vedanta teaches: the self in each of us is the one self of all things.
(6) It follows that the disappearance of a body is the disappearance of an appearance rather than the destruction of a subject, and that the ordinary fear of death rests on a confusion about individuation.
