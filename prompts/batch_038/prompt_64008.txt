I'm putting together a reader for a graduate seminar I'm teaching on the ethics of intervening in nature, and I've run into a problem that more argument isn't going to solve. By week four my students can recite the laissez-faire intuition, the reply that declining to intervene is itself a choice with foreseeable consequences, and the two standard worries — that we can't predict ecosystem-level outcomes well enough to act on them, and that we have no defensible way of aggregating welfare across species and generations. They recite all of it fluently and none of it moves them. I want one piece of fiction to hand out instead of another paper, and I'd like you to write it: roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words, self-contained, with no citations, no glossary, and no epigraph telling the reader what to think.

Here is the seed, which you're free to develop but not to abandon. A subantarctic research station, perhaps fifteen years from now, runs a small "welfare-inclusive" management program. Its central instrument is a ledger: a spreadsheet that attempts to estimate the aggregate welfare consequences of each candidate intervention, discounted across years and weighted across species. The decision on the table is whether to broadcast a slow-acting fertility-suppressing bait against the island's feral cats, which would end predation on the ground-nesting seabirds over a decade or so without the prolonged suffering that anticoagulant poison inflicts. The models predict that the rabbit population, released from cat predation, will multiply and strip the tussock; the ledger's largest term then stops being the birds and becomes the rabbits and, below them, the soil invertebrates. There is a documented precedent the older staff remember — an island where the cats were successfully eradicated and the rabbits and rodents took over and did severe damage to the vegetation, so that the managers had to come back for a second campaign against everything else. One modeller refuses to certify numbers she believes are meaningless; the station head has to sign anyway. Make the protagonist someone who sincerely regards predation at this scale as a moral emergency rather than background scenery, and let that conviction cost her something specific.

A few constraints on execution. I want the disagreement carried by what people do — with the spreadsheet, with the bait, with one another — not by set-piece debates; if you include an argument scene at all, let it go badly for whichever character is closer to being right. Don't resolve the question, and above all don't let the ending deliver a verdict by punishing one side of it. Please avoid the two stock scenes that this material always collapses into: the sentimental dying animal that does all the emotional work unaided, and the hard-eyed ecologist who intones that this is simply how nature operates. Do include one concrete moment in which a single assumption in the ledger — the conversion factor for invertebrate welfare — is altered, the total flips sign, and we watch what that does to a person's face and to the rest of her day. When you're done, append a brief note, not an essay: say which of the positions the characters occupy are live ones in the actual debate and where you've leaned on claims that are contested or underdetermined by the evidence; flag any place where the story lets a weak argument pass unchallenged for the sake of the plot; and tell me honestly whether you think the piece ends up smuggling in a verdict despite my instruction that it shouldn't. And if you judge that this brief can't produce good fiction — that a story organized around a welfare ledger and a management decision will inevitably become characters reciting positions at each other, or that my seeding already rigs the situation against the ecologists before the first sentence — please say so plainly and propose what you'd change, rather than writing something dutiful that doesn't work.
