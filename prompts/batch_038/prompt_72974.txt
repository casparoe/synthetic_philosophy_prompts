I am the part-time coordinator of a community currency covering a rural county of about sixty thousand people. The currency is a paper scrip pegged one to one to the national currency and redeemable at participating local businesses, and alongside it we run a mutual-credit ledger for businesses that trade with each other. I have no training in economics; I came to this job from running a food bank. Our governing board is nine volunteers: three small business owners, two retired teachers, a farmer, a town councillor, a nurse, and a pastor. We meet for ninety minutes once a quarter, and at each meeting I usually have two or three proposals to screen. A proposal typically asks for somewhere between two and fifteen thousand dollars in seed money, plus print costs, plus a few hundred hours of my staff time, and the reputational exposure matters more than the money: if a scheme collapses with people holding worthless paper, the program loses trust it took a decade to build. I can only make a recommendation; the board votes.

Over the past year I have been reading an eighteenth-century book by an Irish bishop — I believe it is called The Querist — written entirely as numbered questions about money. I have no academic background and I am aware I may be mangling it, so part of what I want from you is a check: tell me plainly where the ideas I list below are misstated, where I have imported something from the book's critics, or where the book does not support the use I am making of it. As I read it, the position comes to roughly this. Money is not wealth; wealth is a country's capacity to produce and circulate the things people actually need. Money is a ticket or counter whose entire worth lies in what it sets in motion — it works by getting people to work. A currency that sits idle has failed even if it holds its value perfectly, so the thing to design against is hoarding, not depreciation. On that basis it can be right to build a currency that costs something to hold, and to prefer credit backed by the real produce and property of a place over precious metal that can be exported or buried. And the question to put to any monetary arrangement is not whether it is sound in itself but whether it moves industry that would otherwise not move.

I want that turned into a screening procedure my board can run. Not an essay about the book, and not a general theory of local currency: a written procedure that nine non-specialists can work through in ninety minutes on a proposal they are seeing for the first time, ending either in a recommendation to fund, a recommendation to decline, or a named question the procedure cannot answer and that must go to a vote as a matter of values rather than facts. Please give me:

- the questions to ask, in order, and for each one what counts as a sufficient answer — specify what would satisfy the question and what would not, and which questions can be answered from the proposal text alone rather than requiring figures nobody will have;
- the points at which the procedure stops early with a no, rather than carrying a doomed proposal through the remaining steps;
- which of the distinctions above have to survive the simplification and which I can safely drop, and what is lost by dropping them;
- how the procedure handles the thing that worries me most. Many of our members are paid partly in scrip precisely because they cannot get regular work, and anything that makes the currency expensive to hold falls hardest on the people least able to spend it quickly. If the procedure tells me to design against hoarding, it may be telling me to design against the poor. I need to know whether that is a factual question the procedure can settle or a value question it must hand over to the board, and how the handover should be worded;
- the ways the procedure will systematically get it wrong: what a proposal can say in order to pass every question while still failing in practice, and what genuinely good proposals it will reject;
- a plain-language version of at most one page that could be handed to the board without any of the reasoning behind it.

Then run it out loud on three proposals we actually have in front of us.

(a) My own. A numbered, limited-edition series of scrip notes with artwork by local artists, sold at a modest premium, with two notes in each series deliberately scarce so that people collect them. The premium funds the program; the notes circulate at face value like any others. Two towns that tried something similar sold their collectible runs out quickly. I am attached to this idea and I expect your procedure to reject it. If it does, I want the rejection to follow from the reasoning above rather than from a general caution about novelty, and if the procedure says no for a reason that could be fixed by redrafting, tell me which feature to change and whether what remains is still worth doing.

(b) A mutual-credit line among eleven local businesses — a bakery, a miller, a printer, two farms, a hardware store, a haulage firm, a café, a bookkeeper, a brewery, a timber yard — with balances capped at plus or minus five thousand units, no interest, quarterly settlement, and a rule that any member running more than four thousand in credit has its cap reduced until it spends. It asks us for accounting software and forty hours of my time, and nothing else.

(c) A prepaid card issued in partnership with a regional bank, loadable by anyone, carrying a five percent town subsidy when spent inside the county, usable at every store in the county including two national chains, with no expiry and no fee. It asks for twenty-five thousand dollars.

Two things about how you present all of this. First, tell me how confident you are in what you claim: mark which parts follow straightforwardly from the position I described, which parts are judgment calls that another sensible person would make differently, and which parts rest on something you know about the book or about local currencies that I have no way of checking from where I sit. Second, single out the one point in your answer that you are least sure of, and tell me what would settle it — something I could observe in my own operation over the next two quarters to find out whether you are right.
