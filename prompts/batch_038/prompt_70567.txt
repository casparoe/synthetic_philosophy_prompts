I am the TA for a third-year aesthetics course and need to mark one answer from the final exam. Please give it a mark out of 25 and write point-by-point comments that can be returned to the student as they stand.

**Question (25 marks).** Tolstoy holds that something can be art and still be bad art. Explain how his account keeps apart what the lectures called the *gatekeeping* criterion and the *ranking* criterion, and say what work each of them does. Then apply that distinction to the following pair. (i) A conservatory-trained composer, paid by a festival, writes an oratorio about a famine she has not witnessed, working from press reports; it is performed to an audience of critics and patrons who are visibly moved. (ii) At a village funeral, a woman who has never composed anything sings a lament of her own making over her brother's body, and everyone present, including strangers, weeps with her. Which of these, if either, is art on Tolstoy's account, and which is good art? Finally, say on what ground Tolstoy denies Shakespeare the standing the tradition grants him, and whether that ground is the same one that condemns the oratorio.

**Student's answer.** Tolstoy rejects definitions of art built on beauty or pleasure, because they make art a matter of the audience's enjoyment rather than of what the work does. For him art is an activity in which one person deliberately passes on, by external signs, a feeling he has experienced, so that others catch it. The gatekeeping criterion is therefore infection: if nothing is transmitted there is no art, however skilled the making. The ranking criterion is the worth of the feeling transmitted, judged by the religious perception of the age, together with the degree of infection, which depends on individuality, clarity and sincerity.

Sincerity is the decisive condition, and it means the artist is not pretending: the feeling in the work must be one the artist really had while making it. This settles both cases. The composer never experienced the famine she depicts; she assembled reports and produced an effect her audience was trained to respond to. That is insincere, so the oratorio is not art at all, and Tolstoy's dismissal of it does not depend on any judgment about its content. The lament is art, because the woman felt what she sang. But its value is low: its feelings are crude, its means untrained, and it reaches a handful of people, whereas Tolstoy thinks the worth of a work rises with the number of people it infects.

The same account explains Shakespeare. Tolstoy denies him the standing the tradition gives him because the plays are inaccessible: they depend on allusion, on elevated diction and on the concerns of an aristocratic audience, and so cannot infect ordinary people. Since accessibility is what matters, Tolstoy can still praise writers whose works reach everyone whatever their subject. And because the two criteria come together on a single scale, a work with weak content can be made good art by sufficiently powerful infection; Tolstoy's moralism lowers the ceiling rather than setting a threshold.
