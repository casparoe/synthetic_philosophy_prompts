I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month's piece examines why mass agreement has become a less reliable guide to the truth than its volume implies. The column itself will be straight reportage. What I want from you is the satirical sidebar that will run alongside it.

The conceit: a fictional public body — call it the National Bureau of Settled Opinion, or something of similar register — has just published its annual report, and its balance sheet carries the nation's prevailing agreement as an intangible asset, measured and revalued each year. Write the independent auditor's report on those accounts, followed by five or six of the notes to the financial statements. Roughly 750 words, in the authentic voice and layout of the genre: opinion paragraph, basis for opinion, key audit matter, then numbered notes on accounting policies, impairment, related-party transactions, and events after the reporting date. Invent the audit firm, the engagement partner, and the bureau's officials. No real institutions, no living people, no citations or references to any actual literature.

The humour has to be entirely in the accounting, and the accounting has to be epistemically exact. Specifically, the piece must turn on the following, and must handle each through audit language alone rather than through anyone explaining it:

1. The key audit matter is whether some forty million recorded instances of public agreement constitute forty million independent observations, or substantially fewer. The firm's statistical specialist concludes that the population is sequentially dependent and weights it down accordingly; management disputes the adjustment and is quoted at length. The reader should come away understanding, without being told, that a long run of agreement can carry the evidential weight of a handful of genuinely fresh judgments, because each endorsement was formed partly in response to the endorsements already visible.

2. The recognition policy: an opinion is capitalised as settled once a threshold of visible prior agreement is crossed, after which the bureau ceases to test it against new primary information. Present the individual decision to stop testing as sound, even prudent — a reasonable person holding that evidence would do exactly the same — while the collective result is an asset recorded at a value no single holder would personally defend. Both halves of that must be true in the text; the satire lies in their sitting comfortably side by side.

3. Impairment. The carrying value is fragile in a distinctive way: a single new public event can reverse a long-standing consensus almost overnight, because nothing about the consensus was ever stress-tested. Include management's memo declining to recognise an impairment, give its reasoning in full, and let it be a genuinely defensible memo.

4. A related-party or round-tripping note: the same item of information reappearing repeatedly through restatement, syndication and echo, and being recognised as fresh support on each appearance.

5. One note must separate agreement produced because others' agreement is evidence from agreement produced because it pays — because standing alone carries career, funding or reputational consequences — and must show the auditor treating the two differently, with different procedures and different disclosure, because their accounting consequences differ.

6. Events after the reporting date, or a single dissenting non-executive director: one official formed a view from primary data, and was consequently treated as an anomalous transaction requiring explanation. The document should leave the reader unable to tell whether that outlier is the strongest evidence the asset has or a problem for the audit.

Tone is everything here. Deadpan throughout, no wink, no authorial aside, no closing line that steps outside the genre to signal the gag. A reader who has never encountered the relevant economics should still be able to see what has gone wrong; a specialist should be able to see that you understand precisely why it has gone wrong and find the piece fair rather than sloppy. Avoid the cheap targets: no joke about auditors being asleep at the wheel, no cartoonish fraud, no embezzlement subplot. The accounts should be prepared honestly, competently, and under standards that are internally coherent — the difficulty must lie in what those standards are structurally incapable of seeing.

End with the firm issuing an unmodified opinion, and let the final line be a wholly routine signature block.
