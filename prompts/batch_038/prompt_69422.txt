Okay so my friend Marcus and I spent roughly four hours yesterday arguing about whether it's rational to vote, and I'm still wound up about it, so I want an outside opinion before we do this again next week.

The setup we both accept: in any large election the chance your one ballot changes the result is vanishingly small, the cost of voting is real and positive (gas, an hour in line, a missed shift, childcare), and the instrumental payoff is that tiny probability times the benefit of your side winning. So the arithmetic says stay home. And yet a majority of eligible voters show up in presidential years, including plenty of people for whom the cost is genuinely high and the two candidates are genuinely close to interchangeable in their lives. One of us thinks that means large numbers of people are systematically irrational. The other thinks the arithmetic is being fed bad inputs. Neither budged, obviously.

Between us we came up with the following list of ways out. Some are famous, and two or three are Marcus's own inventions as far as I can tell. I want you to rate each one and sort them into tiers S through D by how well it actually disolves the paradox. Give each a score out of 10 on two separate axes, because I think they come apart: axis 1 is whether it explains why the particular people who vote are the people who vote, and axis 2 is whether it leaves voting looking rational rather than merely explaining it as an error. An answer can ace one and fail the other.

1. The duty/consumption term: the payoff isn't just p times B, there's an extra D for the act itself — doing your civic bit, the feeling of participating, the I-voted sticker — and D alone can cover the cost.
2. The expressive reading: voters aren't buying outcomes at all, they're expressing an allegiance, the way you wear a team jersey on a day your team can't possibly make the playoffs. The paradox only arises if you impose an instrumental frame the voters never adopted.
3. Minimax regret: if you stay home and your side loses by one, you'd feel far worse than the mild annoyance of having spent an hour at a polling place, so a regret-minimizer votes.
4. Marcus's correlation move: p is computed as if your choice were independent of everyone else's, which it isn't. Your reasons for voting — the news you read, your coworkers, the fact that you bothered to register — are shared causes that push similar people to the polls too. So the right question isn't "what happens if I alone defect," it's "what happens if the whole cluster of people I'm statistically tangled up with defects together," and that conditional probability is way bigger than 1/N.
5. Marcus's subscription move: democracy is a stock rather than a flow, it persists on a participation rate, so the relevant question isn't what my hour does at the margin but whether the practice survives if I free-ride. He admits this might just be Kantian universalization wearing a game theory costume.
6. Marcus's bookkeeping move: being pivotal covers both breaking an existing tie and creating one when your side was down by a single vote, and he claims the popular statements of the paradox only count one of those, so the p everyone quotes is off by a factor.
7. The rule-following answer: nobody runs this calculation. People vote because they're operating under a standing policy ("citizens vote") that's cheap to maintain and serves them well on the whole, and it's a mistake to demand optimization at the level of individual acts.
8. Bite the bullet: most voting is irrational, and the interesting question is why societies pay people in status for doing it — maybe because high turnout hands the winner legitimacy, which is worth more than all the wasted hours.
9. The premise is inflated: costs are lower than the toy model assumes (mail ballots, voting on the way to a shift you were driving to anyway) and the benefits of one candidate over another are bigger than centrists like to admit, so there's less of a puzzle here than the literature wants there to be.

Please structure it like this, in this order:

- **Bottom line**: three sentences, which tier the honest answer lives in and whether you side more with me or with Marcus.
- **The tier list**: a table with the item, its tier, its axis 1 score, its axis 2 score, and one sentence of justification per row.
- **Marcus's three moves** (~400 words): items 4, 5, and 6 specifically. Are they any good? Is he onto something that p is being calculated wrong, or is he just relabeling the problem? I want to know whether I have to stop making fun of him.
- **Steelman the other side** (~200 words): the strongest version of "they're just being irrational," stated so a smart person could hold it.
- **What would settle it** (~150 words): is there any observation that would actually discriminate between these answers, or do they all get retrofitted to the same turnout numbers by construction? If it's the latter, say so plainly.
