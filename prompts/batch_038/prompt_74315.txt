I sit on an interdisciplinary review committee that must decide this term whether our university should endow a center whose stated product is dated public forecasts of large-scale political instability: announcements to the effect that the coming decade will bring elevated risk of state breakdown in identified places, made in advance, on the record, and calibrated well enough that insurers, development agencies, and foreign ministries could rationally act on them. Two of the proposals before us come out of the quantitative-history tradition that traces long-run instability to demographic pressure, elite overproduction, and fiscal strain on the state, and that claims to find multi-century cycles of integration and disintegration in the record of agrarian empires and modern states alike. Our historians call these proposals unfalsifiable curve-fitting; the proposers reply that their critics are demanding a test no macrohistorical theory could ever survive. My assignment is to write the specification that will settle which side is asking for the impossible, and I want help drafting it.

So: what must a theory of long-run political instability be able to do in order to count as adequate *for this purpose* — that is, good enough to license a public, dated, actionable forecast? Give me the requirements list, each with the consideration or the concrete case behind it. The requirements should carry the weight of the document; any scorecard of existing programs belongs in a closing paragraph at most.

In assembling the list, take a position on each of the following, and tell me which are genuine desiderata and which are the prejudices of one camp dressed as standards:

1. Operationalization of the target. Instability is measured through sources — chronicles, newspapers, event databanks — whose density covaries with the very processes the theory invokes: more literate elites, more administrative capacity, more print. Is a requirement of theory-neutral measurement satisfiable in principle, and if it is not, what should occupy its place in the spec?

2. Non-triviality and resolution. A forecast of elevated risk of turmoil somewhere among thirty countries within a twenty-year window may be nearly certain on the base rate alone. Should the specification demand that a forecast beat the baseline available to someone who knew only the base rate and the recent past, and should it fix the spatial, temporal, and outcome-variable resolution at which a forecast must be issued to count as one? Note the worry that a sharpness requirement is itself loaded against cyclical theories, whose entire business is to deny that the base rate is stationary.

3. Mechanism versus skill. One colleague holds that a model earns the right to extrapolate only by being interpretable as a story about named social processes; another holds that this is nostalgia from an era before we had data, and that out-of-sample predictive skill is the whole of the matter. Whose requirement belongs, and on what grounds?

4. The phase-shift defense. When two of a program's variables fail to move together, the program can reply that they are dynamically coupled, that their peaks ought therefore to be offset in time, and that what deserves testing is the rates of change rather than the levels. This reply is available after a failed test as readily as before it. Is there a requirement that blocks the abuse without also blocking the legitimate dynamical point? Describe what such a requirement would have to look like.

5. Units, boundaries, and effective sample size. The theory's objects are states, but states are founded, partitioned, merged, and territorially rewritten, and our longest continuous series is a fraction of a single posited cycle. What must the spec require about the stability of the unit of analysis, about nonstationarity, and about how many effectively independent tests a research program can claim to have passed?

6. Reflexivity. Once the center publishes, its forecasts enter the system they describe. Officials may pre-empt; a successful pre-emption will present itself as a failed prediction. Must an adequate theory say something about this, or does it fall outside the specification altogether?

Then the part I most want. Identify any subset of these requirements — or of any you add — that cannot be jointly satisfied, given that there is only one world history, that the events of interest are rare, that the record thickens through time, and that a published forecast alters what it forecasts about. If such a subset exists, that is a finding about the proposals rather than a defect in the list, and I want it stated as a finding. Close by naming the single requirement that, once insisted upon, disqualifies the largest number of otherwise respectable candidates, and say whether you think insisting on it is fair.

Draft it as a document I could circulate to the committee: numbered requirements, a sentence or two of justification beneath each, and an explicit marking of which are non-negotiable and which are merely this camp's preferences. Do not supply references or a reading list — I have read the field, and it is the reasoning I need from you.
