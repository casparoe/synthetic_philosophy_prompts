I work on infrastructure at a mid-sized trading firm, and for the past year I have been maintaining an internal research harness that is essentially a prediction market populated entirely by language-model agents. The design has one quirk that I think matters philosophically, so let me describe it before I ask my question.

- Every agent is the same base model at the same weights, decoding greedily (temperature zero, fixed seed), so a given input always produces a given output.
- Every agent receives the same event description, the same historical data table, and the same resolution criteria.
- The only thing that varies between agents is a short persona preamble of three or four sentences, which we rotate deliberately: one agent is told to reason as a bearish commodity analyst, another as a skeptical actuary, another as a momentum-chasing retail trader, and so on. We do this because a market of genuinely identical agents never moves at all.
- Agents post probability estimates, then buy and sell binary contracts against each other, and the market clears at a price.

Here is the puzzle. The preambles contain no claims about the event, no data, and nothing I would obviously call evidence — they only tilt how an agent weighs what it is given. So I keep wanting to say these agents have the same evidence and the same computational machinery. Yet they produce a persistent eight-to-fifteen-point spread on identical events, they trade with each other continuously, and over months of backtests certain persona families make money off other persona families with annoying regularity. One of our quants says this is an engineered counterexample to the family of results that includes Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem: we have built agents who share everything relevant and still disagree, and the disagreement is precisely what generates the volume. I say he is cheating, that a preamble is a sentence in the context and therefore is information, so of course they disagree; the only real question is which of them is right about the underlying event. I am aware I have quietly dropped common knowledge of rationality from the list of conditions those results need, and I suspect that matters.

What I want from you is not a verdict on my quarrel with him but a number on the general claim underneath it. I have no formal philosophy training, so please correct my labels wherever I have got them wrong.

The claim, as precisely as I can state it:

C: For any two agents who are fully Bayesian-rational and who possess exactly the same relevant evidence, there is a unique rational credence in any given proposition. It follows that whenever two such agents persist in disagreeing, the disagreement must be attributable either to a difference in their evidence or to a failure of rationality on at least one side — never to a difference in rational starting points.

I understand that this is close to the assumption those theorems rest on, and that there is a standing argument, in economics and I think in epistemology too, over whether that assumption is a substantive claim about rational agents or a convenient idealization that merely defines the class of agents it then turns out to describe.

Give me your credence in C: an actual number between 0 and 1, not a survey of positions. My own is 0.55, mostly because I cannot see how to settle it. Our quant puts it at 0.85, on the grounds that anything else makes the word rational empty. A friend of mine who did a philosophy degree puts it at 0.3, and says the whole question turns on what counts as evidence — which I suspect is not the same thing he would say the question turns on if you asked him a week later. Tell me where the 0.85 and the 0.3 most likely diverge.
