I run infra for a mid-size SaaS company and I've spent way too much of this quarter trying to shave watts off our racks, which somehow turned into a Slack argument with a coworker who did a physics PhD. My claim was that since erasing a bit has to dump at least kT ln 2 of heat (the Landauer thing from 1961), there's a hard physical floor under how efficient any computer can get, and since our PUE is like 1.4 we're nowhere near that floor, so all the enthusiasm about reversible and adiabatic logic is a rounding error at best. He said my framing was naive because the Landauer limit is a lot more contested than the textbook version lets on, then pasted in a list of the different ways people have claimed to kill Maxwell's demon over the last century and said I should rank them before I say anything else about energy. I can't go chase down the actual papers, and I don't want a bibliography or a reading list out of you — just your reasoning and your judgement, argued in plain terms.

Here's his list, translated into my sloppy words (fix me where I've garbled them):

1. Szilard — the demon has to physically register a bit about each molecule, and the entropy bill is paid at the acquisition step, so a bit of information genuinely costs kT ln 2.
2. Brillouin — you can't see a molecule without bouncing something off it, and that physical signal dissipates at least as much as the demon stands to gain. The demon pays to look.
3. Landauer plus Bennett — measuring can actually be done for free if you're careful, because it can be done reversibly; the unavoidable cost lands on resetting the demon's memory to blank. This is the version everyone repeats.
4. Zurek — swap out Shannon entropy for algorithmic randomness, so the demon can't compress or predict its record better than the record's own complexity allows, and there's no free lunch left.
5. Sagawa and Ueda and the whole stochastic-thermodynamics crowd — with feedback in the loop the second law turns into an inequality with a mutual-information term, so you can pull out up to kT times the information gained on top of the free energy difference, and everything still balances once you count the controller.
6. Feynman's ratchet and pawl — no information theory at all, just the point that any one-way valve small enough to work on single molecules is itself being jostled by the same thermal noise it's trying to exploit, so it slips backwards as often as it catches.
7. Earman and Norton — none of the above actually works. Either you smuggle the second law into the derivation of Landauer's principle, which makes the "exorcism" circular, or you derive it from statistical mechanics with assumptions a cleverly designed demon can dodge. There was a follow-up with a great title about eating soup with a sieve or lotus-eaters or something.

What I want back: for each of the seven, a number from 0 to 100 on how well it actually disposes of the demon without quietly presupposing the second law it's supposed to be protecting, and a second number from 0 to 100 on how much it constrains real hardware like mine rather than just being a nice thought experiment. Then collapse those into a tier list, S down to C, with a sentence or two per item saying why it lands where it does and what specifically would have to go wrong for it to fail. Call out any pairs you think I've conflated or that get conflated constantly in the pop-science versions of this — my suspicion is 1 and 3 get mashed together all the time and that they're actually making opposite claims about where the cost lives, but tell me if I'm wrong. Say which single one of the seven you'd keep if you could only defend one at a conference, and which one you'd be embarassed to have bet money on. And then, at the end, just answer my original question straight: is kT ln 2 per erased bit a real engineering floor that someone building datacenters should have in the back of their head, or is it a curiosity that only shows up in single-molecule experiments?
