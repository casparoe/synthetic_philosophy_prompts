I'm six weeks into an MPhil essay for a philosophy of mind option and I've got myself into a proper state. The question is roughly whether phenomenal properties can do any explanatory work at all, and I keep bouncing between three camps: the Chalmers-ish dualists who concede that qualia are causally inert and then have to swallow the paradox of phenomenal judgement, the illusionists who say the whole business is a confusion we should talk ourselves out of, and the Russellian monists who make consciousness the intrinsic nature of the physical so it gets its causal work done for free. What's driving me mad is that each camp scores the others on a different scoreboard. The physicalists demand causal closure and no brute nomic danglers; the dualists demand that the explanatory gap be taken at face value and that Mary's ignorance be real ignorance rather than a new way of knowing something she already knew; the illusionists say that any view on which the fact that we're all sitting here talking about qualia is a cosmic coincidence is refuted by the fact that we're all sitting here talking about qualia. So before I commit to defending anything I want the spec. What does a theory of this subject actually have to deliver to count as adequate?

Fix the purpose like this: I want the desiderata for a theory good enough to underwrite an account of what introspective reports are and when they earn the name of knowledge — one that can tell me what's going on when someone says there's something it's like to see this particular red, and whether that utterance is evidence of anything beyond itself. The paradox of phenomenal judgement should be the central case, not a footnote. And there's a specific wrinkle I want the list to adjudicate. A mate of mine in the seminar floated what I've taken to calling the correlation-proxy reply: the report doesn't need to be caused by the quale itself, it's caused by the neural state, and the quale rides along reliably with that neural state in virtue of the psychophysical laws, so the report is true and non-accidentally true, just not by the route you'd have wanted. My instinct is that this pushes the luck up a level rather than removing it — the correlation itself becomes the unexplained brute fact, and we're back to a miracle with one extra step — but I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real objection or a physicalist reflex I picked up from the reading. Tell me whether there's a requirement that rules the manoeuvre out, which one it is, and whether that requirement is a real desideratum or a taste.

So: the requirements.
