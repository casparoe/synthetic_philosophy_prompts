I sit on the staff of a small private foundation that funds conceptual and methodological work in the human sciences. I trained as a statistician, but I no longer do research myself, and my job this cycle is to scope a narrow program on the interpretation of behavioral-genetic statistics. The board has approved enough money for exactly two three-year projects, and two constraints are fixed. First, we cannot fund new data collection of any kind: a project must be either purely analytical or a re-analysis of data that is already in existence and openly accessible. Second, each project must end with something a first-year graduate student in sociology, education, or public health could actually apply to their own paper without specialist supervision. The board is split on the premise of the whole program — two members are behavioral geneticists who think the conceptual objections to heritability estimates are overblown and mostly settled, one is a sociologist who thinks they are understated — so whatever I bring them has to be defensible to both camps rather than pitched at either.

The candidates I have shortlisted are these. (i) Individual-level attribution: whether it is ever coherent to say of one person that some fixed percentage of their trait value was caused by their genes, what precisely licenses or blocks the move from a variance partition to individual-level causal shares, and whether a counterfactual or causes-of-effects framework can deliver such statements without smuggling population-relative parameters back in. (ii) The environment-range problem: heritability is defined only relative to the distribution of environments actually present in the population studied, yet that distribution is almost never specified; is it even possible to characterize admissible environment-ranges well enough to compare estimates across populations, and if not, what follows for cross-national and cross-cohort claims? (iii) Genotype–environment correlation: if environments are partly selected in response to genotype, is the additive decomposition merely approximate, or is it mis-specified in a way that changes what the estimate measures? (iv) The within-group/between-group barrier: does the classic illustration of genetically equivalent seed lots sown in two differently fertilized fields really block inference from high within-group heritability to a genetic account of a between-group difference, or does it quietly presuppose that the two environments are matched in the relevant respect? (v) Malleability: the widely repeated claim that high heritability says nothing about how responsive a trait is to intervention — is that exactly right, or does it hold only under further conditions, and can one build a taxonomy of intervention types for which variance components genuinely are informative? (vi) The contested finding that the heritability of children's cognitive-test scores appears attenuated in families near or below the poverty line, together with the disputed attempts to replicate it: is the disagreement here conceptual, empirical, or both, and what should a non-specialist conclude today? (vii) The equal-environments assumption in twin designs: whether it is best treated as a testable empirical premise, a category mistake, or a modeling choice with known costs — and whether molecular-genetic methods have made the whole question uninteresting. (viii) The so-called missing-heritability gap: is the shortfall between twin-based and molecular-based estimates a conceptual puzzle or a measurement artifact, and would closing it change what heritability means? Please also consider candidates I have not listed if you think they belong above any of these.

One further thing to fold in, because it may be a mistake. I have been describing two things people mean when they invoke heritability in policy discussion: an interpretive notion, which is what the number licenses you to say about the causal provenance of variation in a particular population, and an interventional notion, which is what it tells you about how much of an outcome you could move by changing environments. My working hypothesis is that a large share of the published disputes are really disagreements about which notion is in play. A colleague objects that the interventional notion is not a distinct notion at all but a sloppy way of gesturing at genotype–environment interaction, and that dressing it up as a separate concept falsely implies that heritability has policy content. One of the two grants could plausibly be spent developing or refuting that distinction, so I would like your view on whether it earns its keep.

What I want back is a defended agenda, not a survey. Rank the candidates and tell me which two you would actually fund given the constraints above, with the dependency structure made explicit: which questions are upstream of others, so that answering one changes how the rest must be posed. State the criteria you are ranking by — magnitude of interpretive error prevented, tractability without new data, neglectedness, how many other questions hinge on the answer — and be candid about where those criteria pull against each other, since a project that prevents a great deal of confusion may be the least tractable of the lot. Identify one or two candidates you would refuse to fund, and say whether that is because they are already settled, because they are pseudo-problems, or because resolving them would leave practice unchanged. Finally, tell me what developments over the next two or three years would move your ranking, and what a successful end-state would concretely look like for your top pick, in the sense of the object a graduate student would hold in their hands at the end. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I am interested in the argument and in your judgment, and I would rather you flag the places where you are unsure than pad the case.
