I am a novelist. My third book is set in a mid-sized independent publishing house, Fenwick & Ordal, and one of its chapters reproduces in full an internal document: the revised house style guide issued by the head of copy, Margit Ordal, who in her sixtieth year has converted to the later Wittgenstein and has set about administering him.

Please write that style guide. Between 850 and 1,000 words, in numbered clauses with short headings, in the flat institutional diction of a copy desk, with cross-references between clauses and at least three indented marginal queries addressed to authors by name.

The document has to be a work of Wittgensteinian editorial theory rather than a list of puns, and it must arrive at its policies by the routes the philosophy actually travels. It should legislate, at minimum, on the following: that meaning is use, and consequently that the house's flagship series, The Anatomy of Ideas — twelve volumes, each contracted to open with a definitive account of a single concept, among them knowledge, justice, art, rule, number, person and game — is now in breach of a policy its own editor holds; that the backlist index must be rebuilt on family-resemblance principles, no headword definable, each placed only by overlapping similarities with its neighbours, the resulting index unusable and entirely principled; that a manuscript consisting of four words called out across a building site is a complete language and publishable as such; that an author's diary-notation, which he alone can interpret, must be rejected because there is no criterion of correctness for its use; that a cover showing an animal visible two ways cannot be settled by a vote of the sales conference, a change of aspect not being a change of opinion; that the marketing department plays a different game and can be translated but not contradicted.

One of the queries must turn on a copy editor who has conformed to the guide exactly for eleven years and then diverges, and on what fact about her past could constitute her having meant the rule she is now accused of breaking.

The target is a practice and a style of discourse, not a person: the conversion of a thesis about meaning into administrative procedure, and above all the reflex by which another language-game becomes an all-purpose immunity claim. Nothing may be winked at. Let the guide be wholly sincere, and let its founding error be structural — it issues rules in the name of a philosophy that claims to leave everything as it is, and a rule cannot say what makes its own application correct. Exactly one clause, unflagged as an exception, must give a straight definition of a term in flat contradiction of the guide's thesis; and exactly one passage must be accurate enough that a specialist would consider it the best thing in the document. Ordal may name her philosopher once, in a prefatory note; a house guide does not argue with the dead.

No epigraphs, no quotation from the texts, no section numbers, no bibliography: the philosophy must survive as house policy or not survive at all.
