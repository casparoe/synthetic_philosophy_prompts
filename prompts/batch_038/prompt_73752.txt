I run dispatch for a courier company with about forty vans in a mid-size city, and whatever I know about how models work I've picked up by watching ours get built, defended, and broken rather than from any coursework. Last month two of my staff got into a long argument that I couldn't settle, and I'd like you to work through it turn by turn and tell me who is right about what.

Our analyst, Ken, wrote a program that predicts how long a given run will take. It assumes the driver takes the shortest legal route, travels at exactly the posted speed limit the whole way, spends exactly four minutes at each stop, and is never delayed by traffic or weather. All of that is false and Ken knows it is false. The program nonetheless predicted our actual downtown delivery times within roughly four percent over a full year, and his position — he said he'd read something to that effect in Milton Friedman — is that the truth of the assumptions simply doesn't matter, that the only thing you may ask of a model is whether its predictions come out right, and that anyone insisting on realistic assumptions is asking for the impossible, since a perfectly faithful model of the city would just be the city.

Our senior dispatcher, Ana, said the model works for a reason Ken has never identified, and that the reason could lapse at any moment without warning. She pointed out that downtown our drivers in fact avoid the freeway because of the on-ramp queue, and the surface-street route they choose runs about ninety seconds longer than the freeway route the program picks; so the model is wrong about the route and wrong about the time in two directions that largely cancel. Ken answered that a cancellation you can measure is not luck but a fact about the district, and that if the errors offset across the whole range of cases he cares about, the model is as good as any model anyone has ever had. He added that what Ana really wants is a true theory, and that no one has ever possessed one of those either.

Ana then played what she took to be her winning card: eleven months earlier, when we added the hillside district, the very same program under-predicted run times by close to forty percent, and the fix came not from anyone studying prediction errors but from her saying that it was assuming the speed limit held on those grades, and nobody holds it going uphill. Ken said that episode proved his case instead of hers: the model had been tested inside its intended range, had been found inaccurate, and had been revised on the basis of accuracy — precisely the criterion he was defending. He further argued that an assumption by itself tells you nothing at all, because you cannot derive a prediction from an assumption without also fixing the conditions under which it is supposed to apply, so assumptions can never be assessed apart from predictions anyway. Ana replied that this turned his principle into an excuse: in every real case she has ever worked, the fastest route to a repair was to ask which simplification had stopped holding, and a doctrine that declares assumptions irrelevant while its practitioners spend their days reasoning about assumptions is not a theory of models but a way of avoiding an argument.

Where I'm stuck is that each of them seemed to win their own round. Please go through the exchange in order and say, at each point, whether the reply actually answers the objection it was given or merely restates the position in new words — and then tell me who comes out ahead on the general question Ken opened with, that the realism of assumptions is beside the point. I'd also like to know what neither of them raised that would have changed the shape of the argument, and whether there was some test we could have run on the downtown model that would have settled their dispute on its own terms, before the hillside district settled it for us by accident.
