I'm pressure-testing the epiphenomenalist's favorite escape from the paradox of phenomenal judgment and I want a purpose-built thought experiment for it, not the usual zombie or inverted-spectrum chestnuts.

The escape I mean: reports about qualia don't need qualia to cause anything, because the one physical brain state produces both the experience and the verbal judgment, so the match between them isn't a miracle, it's just a common cause. Call it the common-cause reply.

So: build me a single case, one paragraph, in which the subject's phenomenal judgments are accurate and the common-cause reply genuinely has nothing to grab onto. What the case is for: I want to know whether the paradox can still be made to bite once you grant the epiphenomenalist their best escape, so a scenario that just stipulates the escape fails would be cheating -- the failure has to fall out of the structure of the reporting mechanism.

Constraints:

- the accuracy has to be robust rather than a local fluke. It should survive the reporting pathway being swapped around (different output channel, different neural read-out, different modality, whatever you like), so the epiphenomenalist cant just point at one fixed physical chain that happens to line up with the experience in this one arrangement.
- nothing in the story may give qualia causal powers, and you may not dissolve the case by denying there are qualia, or by making the subject confused, inattentive, or vague about what they're going through.
- keep it physically plausible: present-day or near-present-day clinical/neuroscience setups are fine, no magic, no vats.
- the experience has to be a fine-grained one where the subject is making an actual discrimination (this shade vs. that shade, this bitterness vs. that bitterness). Global "yes there's something it's like to be me" reports are too easy for the common-cause story to absorb, so they don't count.

After the scenario, do the checking work:

1. Name the exact premise of the common-cause reply that your case breaks, and explain why it breaks it rather than just presenting the epiphenomenalist with a coincidence they can shrug at. If the honest answer is "they can shrug," say how expensive the shrug is and what it costs them elsewhere.
2. Spell out what an interactionist -- anyone who lets experience cause reports -- gets to say about the very same case that the epiphenomenalist can't say.
3. Give the strongest epiphenomenalist rebuttal you can find. There's more than one flavor available: redescribing what the report is really about, some non-causal account of how the judgment is justified, restricting the claim to coarse-grained contents, whatever. Then say honestly whether it rescues the view or just relocates the problem.
4. Tell me which single feature of your case is doing the work, and what the case collapses into if I delete that feature.

And if the constraints turn out not to be jointly satisfiable, dont quietly drop one -- tell me which you had to bend and why.
