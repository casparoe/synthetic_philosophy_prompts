I retired four years ago from a national time laboratory, where most of my working life was spent on the generation and comparison of atomic time scales. Only since then have I taken up philosophy in earnest, and I find the philosophy of time the most rewarding and simultaneously the most irritating branch of the subject, because it reasons at length about machinery I helped operate. I should like to work through one historical episode against one argument, and I would ask you to treat me as a colleague rather than a pupil: where my reconstruction of either the physics or the history is mistaken, say so directly rather than working around it.

The episode is the meeting of the Société française de philosophie in Paris on 6 April 1922, at which Einstein spoke on relativity and Bergson — who had been studying the theory closely — responded. Einstein's reply, as I understand it, drew a sharp line between physical time and psychological time and refused to grant any third, distinctively philosophical time, the remark usually quoted being "The time of the philosophers does not exist." Bergson's *Durée et simultanéité* appeared later that year. My reading of it, which I hold less confidently, is that he accepted the transformations and hence the plurality of times the theory yields, but denied that all of those times are equally real: for a consciousness situated in one system, only that system's time is lived, while the times attributed to other systems are in some sense fictitious or merely imaginary standpoints. And I believe he pressed the same point against the twin paradox, arguing that the retarded time of the travelling twin is a time no one actually lives through. If any of this is a distortion, correct it before proceeding.

The argument is the one that goes under the names of Rietdijk and Putnam: that if what is real for an observer is what stands in the simultaneity relation to that observer's present, and if special relativity makes simultaneity frame-relative, and if the reality relation is transitive, then the reality of a distant moving observer's "now" drags in events outside my own past light cone, and by iteration the whole of spacetime is equally real. Please set the argument out explicitly, premise by premise, and tell me which premise is load-bearing. Then run the 1922 exchange through it in both directions. Does Einstein's reply commit him to the argument, or could he have held his operational definition of distant simultaneity — the one built from exchanged light signals — as a thesis about meaning rather than about what exists, leaving the metaphysics untouched? Does Bergson's denial attack the first premise, the transitivity premise, or neither — is it an ancestor of Howard Stein's rejoinder, on which relativity licenses only an event's past light cone as what is already real, and therefore licenses no global present at all? Or is Bergson refusing the identification of the physicist's time with reality in the first place, in which case he is not answering the argument and the argument is not answering him?

Third, and this is where I want to be told if I am overreaching. In my old trade there were at least three distinct things called simultaneity, and the literature I have read collapses them. There is the coordinate simultaneity of an inertial frame in Minkowski spacetime, which is what the argument quantifies over. There is the local light-cone structure. And there is the realized global time scale, which is a stipulated, non-inertial, composite artifact: an ideal scale defined, as I recall the definition, as the proper time of a clock at rest on the rotating geoid; individual laboratory clocks corrected for gravitational redshift and motion before being admitted to the ensemble; that ensemble held against solar time by leap seconds announced by the International Earth Rotation Service; and the whole result published retrospectively in monthly circulars, so that the official planetary present is reconstructed some days after it has already passed. That third thing functions. It is a planet-wide equivalence class of events that no inertial frame licenses, and it nonetheless coordinates navigation, finance, and the timing of physics experiments. Does it constitute an objection to the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, a new twist on the old conventionality objection, or a category mistake — an administrative stipulation being mistaken for ontological privilege? If a working global present can be manufactured out of relativistically corrected clocks and a monthly least-squares fit, what work is the frame-relativity premise still doing? And does this third time hand Bergson anything after all — a time that is neither the physicist's nor the psychologist's, precisely the thing Einstein denied exists?

What I want at the end is a disciplined mapping: who and what plays which role, which features of the 1922 exchange the argument genuinely illuminates, which it misdescribes, and where it falls silent. Then a verdict, not a shrug: does the exchange confirm the argument, stand as an anomaly for it, or belong to a question the argument was not in a position to ask — and had it been asked, who would have had the better of it? Please also tell me where my laboratory habits are leading me astray. I would rather not have a reading list; I want the reasoning.
