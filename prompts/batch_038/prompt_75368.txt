I sit on the management committee of a small credit union in the north of England — around 4,000 members, run by volunteers with two part-time paid officers. I have no academic training of any kind; I was a shift supervisor for thirty-odd years, and I have been turning the following problem over in my own time for months, so please treat my framing as a layperson's.

We have a proposal before us for a pilot savings product, and it has split the committee. It would work like this. A member opens the account, chooses a fixed term (twelve, twenty-four or thirty-six months) and a monthly amount, and then signs a standing instruction telling us to refuse their own future requests to withdraw before the term ends, except where we judge there is genuine hardship. At the point of opening, the member also records a short statement in their own words about why they are locking the money away; if they later come in asking to break the agreement early, we would read or play that statement back to them before anything was processed, and we would contact one friend or relative they nominated at the outset to confirm the request is really theirs. The aim is not to stop anyone ever reaching their money, but to make it harder at the particular bad moments that members themselves predict: after a reduced-hours week, after a night out, when a brother asks for a loan.

The half of the committee in favour says this is precisely what a member-owned lender should do — help people hold to what they wanted when they were thinking clearly. The other half says we would be appointing ourselves to overrule an adult standing at our own counter asking for their own money, and that the person physically in front of us is the only one entitled to decide. A third worry, quieter but real, is that "hardship" would in practice be judged by staff who have an interest in keeping deposits where they are.

Nobody has yet answered what seems to me the crux: whether the member should be free to withdraw the standing instruction itself. If they can cancel it whenever they like, the whole thing binds nothing at the moment it is needed; if they cannot cancel it, we have taken away their exit entirely.

Please write the opinion I can table at next month's meeting. It should give a clear recommendation — proceed, proceed only with specified amendments, or decline — and set the reasoning out so that both halves of the committee can actually respond to it rather than feel overruled. It should propose a workable test for what counts as hardship, say who applies it and who reviews that decision, and settle the question about revoking the standing instruction with a concrete mechanism. Please also give the strongest argument on the side you come out against in its best form, and say what it would take to change your recommendation. There is no need to cite authorities or supply references; what we need is judgement we can act on.

Write in British English, in plain but formal prose of roughly 900 words, addressed to fellow volunteers rather than to lawyers, and list separately at the end any element of the scheme as described that you would insist on changing.
