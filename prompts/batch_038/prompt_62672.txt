I sit on the science committee of a mid-sized charitable funder that awards some thirty grants a year in the behavioural sciences, and I have been asked to circulate a paper ahead of next Thursday's meeting on a proposal that has split our two referees badly. The applicants want four years and three postdocs to test whether a bias towards inferring deliberate agency in ambiguous events — reading moving dots, rustling undergrowth, or chance patterns as the work of some intentional actor — is an adaptation for detecting predators and rivals, rather than a by-product of more general associative machinery. Their evidence is: the bias appears in all nineteen populations sampled; it is measurable in infants before much cultural input; twin work puts its heritability at roughly 0.4; an error-management argument to the effect that a false positive costs little whereas a false negative can be fatal; and, across fourteen of the nineteen populations, a positive correlation between bias strength and a reconstructed index of historical predation pressure. They also propose a laboratory manipulation. Referee One, a comparative biologist, recommends rejection: the fourteen-population correlation ignores shared ancestry and non-independence; universality, early emergence and heritability are precisely what one expects of any robust developmental by-product; the cost-asymmetry argument can be bent to justify a bias in either direction once the costs are assigned after the results are known; and because no non-adaptive hypothesis is specified in a form the design could distinguish, the project cannot fail. Referee Two, a behavioural ecologist, recommends funding: Referee One is not making a scientific objection but reciting a shibboleth descended from Gould and Lewontin; adaptation is a hypothesis like any other, the proposal includes a genuine manipulation, and the referees' own favoured alternative — a general-purpose learning mechanism — is itself an adaptationist claim about learning. She adds that requiring proof of adaptation in advance would have killed much of the field's best work, and that the spandrels critique has hardened into a device for policing a research programme one happens to dislike.

I spent most of last night arguing about this with a friend who reads philosophy of biology for pleasure and knows considerably more of it than I do. His position is that Referee One has done nothing but assert that an adaptive story could always have been told differently, which is true of any hypothesis in any science and therefore no objection at all; mine is that there is something real in the complaint — not that adaptation claims are untestable, which would oblige us to discard half the corpus, but that adaptation ought not to be the default and that the burden lies on the applicant to name the rival explanations the design is built to discriminate among. He replied that a demand phrased that way has no teeth, since any referee can always insist on more alternatives, and that I am looking for a way to sound even-handed while siding with Referee One. I am not convinced, but I could not put my finger on where the teeth are, and our committee's only relevant guidance is the single clause in the handbook requiring the panel to satisfy itself that "the central hypothesis is testable and that the evidence proposed is adequate to it". That clause is doing a great deal of work it was never drafted to do, and I would rather the memo I circulate did not simply reproduce the referees' stand-off.

Please draft the decision memo itself — fund, reject, or fund with conditions — in my voice, as a document of perhaps two pages that the panel can act on rather than admire. It needs a clear bottom line with a concrete course of action and timetable (if conditions, then conditions the applicants can actually meet within a funding cycle, and a stated route and deadline for resubmission); reasoning written so that both referees and the applicants could recognise their own objections and reply to them; and a short set of criteria the panel can apply consistently to the next such proposal, including some way of telling a legitimate methodological objection from an unacknowledged distaste for a research programme, since Referee Two's charge is the part of this I most need answered and I would rather it were engaged head-on than conceded in a closing paragraph. I would also like the memo to state explicitly whether it thinks the adaptationism controversy should be named in the letter to the applicants at all, given that our panel is not a philosophy seminar and that invoking a famous critique can be a way of winning an argument without making one. No bibliography and no reading list, please: nobody on this committee reads anything that is footnoted, and what I need is judgement we can defend on Thursday.
