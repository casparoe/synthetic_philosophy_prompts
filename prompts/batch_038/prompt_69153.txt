I teach Sidney Lumet's Twelve Angry Men in a social epistemology seminar and want to use it as a case study in the Condorcet jury theorem without letting students take the easy lesson. Write a reading of the film against the theorem's actual structure. Begin with the conditions: the theorem licenses confidence in a majority only given competence fixed above one-half and votes independent conditional on the state of the world. Take the first ballot — eleven for guilty, almost at once — and say precisely which condition it violates. I want the failure analyzed as correlation rather than as mere error, and I want your view on whether that correlation comes from the jurors' shared prejudices or from all twelve responding to the same body of evidence, since those are different ways of losing independence and the theorem treats them alike.

Then Juror 8's method: is he raising the competence of individual jurors, breaking the correlation among their votes, or doing something the framework cannot describe, since it takes competence as given in advance of any deliberation? Is there a revision of the theorem that can value what he does, or does the film dramatize a process whose entire point is that its inputs are endogenous to it?

Next, the awkward structural fact: the film's rule is unanimity, so no majority ever actually decides. Does that put the film outside the theorem's jurisdiction altogether, or does it change what the theorem is supposed to establish? Finally, the film never tells us whether the defendant is innocent; it establishes only that the case leaves reasonable doubt. Given that, is the film's moral about a jury's capacity to track the truth or about the threshold at which it should act — and what must the honest critic concede about where the jury-theorem lens stops applying? Close by saying what the film shows about collective judgment that a model of voters and their reliabilities cannot express.
