I am drafting a novel whose narrator is an economist on a government impact-assessment team, and in the ninth chapter she is cornered at a dinner party by a philosopher who believes he has refuted her profession outright. I want his case to be strong enough that a reader who knows this literature cannot accuse me of building her a straw opponent, but I also need to know precisely where it breaks, because she answers him in the next scene and I will not let her win by luck.

His argument, as I have drafted it:

(1) The only admissible evidence bearing on how well off someone is comes from that person's own ranking of the alternatives open to her; there is nothing further to consult.
(2) Such a ranking is ordinal. It records that she prefers A to B, never by how much, and any cardinal scale laid over it is an artifact of the measuring instrument rather than a feature of her.
(3) So no statement about the magnitude of anyone's gain or loss states anything real, and in particular there is no common unit in which her losses and my gains could be weighed against one another.
(4) Every cost–benefit analysis my narrator signs performs exactly that weighing, and every Kaldor–Hicks improvement she certifies is a disguised interpersonal comparison; therefore her whole body of work is meaningless.
(5) The argument also runs reflexively: I have no better access to the experience of the man I shall be in twenty years than to a stranger's, so I cannot compare my welfare now with my welfare then, and saving, training for a trade, and every other cross-time choice are irrational in the same way and for the same reason.
(6) But prudence is not irrational, and some policies really are better than others.
(7) Hence (1) is false: there must be further facts about people's welfare, over and above their own orderings, which it is possible in principle to come to know about others.

She would like to keep the Pareto principle, she would like to keep her job, and she would rather not concede that prudence rests on a different foundation from benevolence. Which step should she attack? Say why that step seemed unobjectionable when he read it aloud, what the exits at the other steps would cost her, and whether (7) — his own advertised conclusion — is the right target or a decoy he has sprung on himself.

End with a line beginning "Verdict:" naming the step she should attack, by number, with the reason in eight words or fewer.
