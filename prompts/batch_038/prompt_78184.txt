I run operations at a regional last-mile delivery outfit — about 200 drivers, and for the last year we've also been piloting a handful of autonomous sidewalk delivery bots plus two self-driving yard trucks that shuffle trailers around the depot overnight. Our risk manager keeps saying in meetings that the whole "responsibility gap" thing people wring their hands about is fake, because the machine is just a product, like a forklift or a tire or a coffee maker: if it hurts somebody the manufacturer eats it under product liability, so the buck doesn't vanish, it just travels up the supply chain. Our general counsel disagrees with her diagnosis but not her conclusion — he says no, it's more like an employee, the company is the employer, and there's an old legal idea where the master answers for what the servant does on the job, so you'd hold us liable the way you'd hold any courier company liable for its driver. Both of them land in the same place: stop worrying, somebody is always on the hook, the philosophers are just making work for themselves.

Can you audit both of those analogies for me? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly — in each one, what plays the role of the product, the defect, the manufacturer, the warning label; and what plays the role of the employer, the employee, the scope of employment, discipline — and then where each mapping actually breaks. Especially this: an employee can be instructed, trained, reprimanded, fired and shamed, and a product can be recalled and redesigned, but our trucks update themselves overnight across the whole fleet, and they're the sediment of a vendor, two subcontractors, a labeling outfit and several million miles of other people's driving. Which of those two handles fits that, if either? And I'd like you to separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion they're pushing from the ones that are just surface texture.

Here's the case we keep arguing about. One of the yard trucks, nobody in the cab, around 4am, hit a contractor who'd walked in through a gate that was supposed to be locked. From the logs, the software did exactly what it was built to do with the data it had. Nobody cut a corner — maintenance was current, the vendor met the published standard, our procedures were followed, the thing had been inspected that week. His family keeps asking one question, which is who did this, and every answer I have sounds like its answering a different question entirely. Which makes me suspect both my risk manager and my GC are running their analogies through the wrong pipe. The way I've been putting it to myself: treat responsibility like water poured into connected pipes, and everybody assumes it's conserved, it has to pool somewhere, so the whole debate is just about which container it ends up in. But maybe liability (who pays) and fault (who actually did something wrong) aren't one pipe, they're two, and the product analogy and the employee analogy both flow fine in the first one and go dead silent in the second. Does that hold up, or am I just renaming the problem? Tell me whether the no-gap conclusion survives once you split the two, and if neither analogy works, what would be a better one to argue with — I have to go back into that meeting and I'd rather not walk in holding only two bad metaphors.
