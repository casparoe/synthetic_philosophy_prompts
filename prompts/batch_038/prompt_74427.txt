I'm about four hundred pages into a novel whose second half is an impeachment — invented country, invented president, invented press — but three readers in a row have told me it reads like the winter of 1998 and I've stopped fighting it, so now I want to get the moral logic right rather than the procedure. What I care about isn't the constitutional stuff. It's the moment where the people doing the blaming turn out to be dirty too, and what that moment actually costs them. I want to write that scene honestly, and I've realized I don't have a clean grip on the theory underneath it, so I'm hoping you can do a real analysis for me and let me steal the structure.

Use the Clinton impeachment as the case. Since you can't look anything up, here are the facts I'll take responsibility for: the House Judiciary Committee ran an inquiry off the independent counsel's report, and in December 1998 the House adopted two articles, perjury before a grand jury and obstruction of justice, both growing out of sworn testimony in a sexual harassment suit and the president's denials about a relationship with a White House intern. The committee's chairman, a man in his seventies who had built a career as the moral voice of one side of the abortion debate, had acknowledged to reporters earlier that fall that he'd been unfaithful in his own marriage decades before and was no saint. The member tapped to be the next Speaker announced his resignation days after the House voted, having acknowledged his own extramarital affairs, and he'd been publicly urging the president to resign. At least two other members who voted to impeach were subsequently reported to have admitted affairs of their own. The votes fell mostly along party lines; five House Democrats voted for one of the perjury articles. The Senate acquitted in February 1999 — forty-five guilty votes on one article, fifty on the other, short of the sixty-seven needed — and the president stayed in office, was later held in civil contempt in the underlying case, and gave up his law license for five years.

Now run that through the standing-to-blame and hypocrisy literature, and run it in both directions. First, the mapping: who exactly was blaming whom about what — the lying, the relationship, the abuse of a subordinate, the obstruction — because those may not all come out the same way on a standing analysis, and I suspect that's where the interesting stuff is. Take the standard positions seriously and say what each one predicts about the chairman, about the Speaker-designate, and about the House as a body: the tu quoque-is-just-a-fallacy line, the line that blame is a form of moral address that misfires when the addresser isn't actually committed to the norm, the line that hypocrisy is a failure of integrity or self-consistency in the blamer rather than anything about the target's desert, and the line that your own wrongdoing entangles you so that your condemnation of another implicates you too. Then tell me which of these handles the two facts I keep going around in circles on: that the chairman publicly acknowledged his own fault before he kept going, and that the Speaker-designate acknowledged his and resigned over it. Does acknowledgment restore standing, or does it just swap one defect for a different one — and why would the identical admission produce opposite institutional consequences?

Here's the part I want you to push on, because it's my own idea and I can't tell if it's any good. I keep wanting to seperate standing you have from standing you hold in a role, and I've been thinking of a third thing, borrowed standing — the possibility that a committee or a legislature has standing no individual member has, and that members draw on it when they speak in its name, so that the body's clean hands can carry a dirty chair. Relatedly: is standing a seat you either occupy or don't, or a resource you spend down and can earn back? Test both of those against the case and tell me whether they survive contact with it, or whether they're just decoration. And be honest about where the whole apparatus gives out — this theory was built for one person blaming another, and impeachment is a political act with a constitutional job to do, so say plainly whether applying it here is an extension, a category mistake, or a sign the theory was never really about the cases people cite it for. End with what the case does to the theory overall: confirming, anomalous, or indifferent.
