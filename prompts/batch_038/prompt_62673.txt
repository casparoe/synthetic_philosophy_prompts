I sit on the Elections and Governance Commission of a national professional association of roughly 40,000 members, which elects a fifteen-member board in a single at-large contest under plurality block voting: each member may cast up to fifteen votes, and the fifteen highest totals win. For three consecutive cycles the slate coordinated by the association's largest caucus has taken all fifteen seats, while a rival slate drawing between 43 and 45 percent of the vote has taken none; turnout in those contests ran below 20 percent. The Commission must settle, before its June meeting, which rule to recommend for the bylaw amendment ballot this fall. Three proposals are formally before us:

(a) single transferable vote in the existing fifteen-member at-large district;
(b) fifteen single-member geographic districts under first-past-the-post;
(c) cumulative voting in five three-member districts, each member holding three votes that may be spread across candidates or piled onto one.

Three complications constrain the choice. Our bylaws reserve at least three seats for members within seven years of certification, and proposal (a) supplies no way to enforce that floor through the count itself; staff have suggested carving reserved seats out of the district, which counsel considers permissible but expects to be slow and internally contentious. Two years ago we absorbed a 4,000-member specialist society whose members are dispersed across every region and who have not won a seat since the merger. And the caucus that has been sweeping argues, persuasively to several commissioners, that proposal (b) would convert the board into fifteen regional delegates and cost the association its capacity to take a single position on regulatory questions.

Please draft the Commission's decision memo. It should commit to one proposal, or one proposal with a specified amendment, rather than ranking all three, and it should be actionable: what goes on the ballot, and in which cycle the new rule first operates. Deliver it as exactly five numbered items of two or three sentences each, with no preamble, no subheadings, and no signature block: (1) the recommendation and its effective cycle; (2) a diagnosis of the present rule stated in arithmetic the sweeping caucus would accept as fact; (3) why the runner-up proposal loses; (4) how the early-career floor and the merged-in society are to be handled under the recommended rule; (5) the opposing caucus's best argument given at full strength, together with whatever the memo concedes to it.
