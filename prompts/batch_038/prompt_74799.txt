I run a small research group in the philosophy of the life sciences which has spent the last two years sitting in on the genomics seminars and tumour boards of our teaching hospital, and I am now drafting the central section of a paper on what clinical oncology quietly assumes about evolutionary individuality. Consider three things that are done as a matter of routine in one institution, sometimes about one patient on one afternoon. First, a biopsy is sequenced in bulk; each variant is assigned an allele fraction; variants present in essentially every malignant cell are labelled clonal and those present in a minority are labelled subclonal, with a numerical threshold applied to the fraction; a branching genealogy of cell lineages is then reconstructed, with shared ancestors and ordered descent, and the report will say that a resistant subclone was selected by the first-line agent and subsequently expanded. Second, the same report, and the oncologist summarising it aloud, will speak of the neoplasm as a thing with properties of its own: aggressive, indolent, driven, of high or low fitness, likely or unlikely to progress — as though the tumour as a whole were the bearer of a fitness value, and as though that value were the sort of thing one could rank against the tumours of other patients. Third, selection appears again in two further registers: the hereditary-cancer service tracks the frequency of a predisposition allele across generations in pedigrees and populations, and the immunology service reports a neoantigen burden as a predictor of response to checkpoint blockade, which is to describe a second selection process conducted within the same tissue, this time by lymphocytes upon those cell lineages. Meanwhile the treatment norm against which all of this is judged — dose to the maximum tolerated level, then change agents when progression is detected — treats the neoplasm as an individual enemy to be eradicated, whereas the alternative protocol several of my clinical colleagues advocate, of dosing intermittently and below that maximum so that a population of treatment-sensitive cells survives and competitively suppresses the resistant ones, treats it instead as a community to be managed.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, not a survey of the levels-of-selection literature, so please keep your attention on what is actually done and on what has to be assumed for the doing to make sense. Begin with the first register: what does the practice of clonal and subclonal classification presuppose about the unit of selection — about what is being selected, what bears fitness, and what qualifies as a reproducer? Your account must say what makes a set of cells one lineage rather than a mere aggregate; what makes a difference heritable in a tissue whose genome is unstable, whose karyotype drifts, and much of whose variation is epigenetic, phenotypic and reversible; and what work a numerical cut-off on an allele fraction is doing inside a picture that otherwise speaks in the vocabulary of trees and common descent, where one imagines the threshold has no natural home. Then tell me which rival views the practice rules out, which it merely fails to entertain, and which it is neutral between: a strictly genic account on which the allele is the only genuine reproducer and cells are mere vehicles; the view that individuality is a matter of degree, so that a tumour is a low-fidelity, weakly individuated Darwinian population rather than a non-instance of one; the view that there are no units at all, only descriptions at different scales each capturing part of a single causal process; and the view that a neoplasm is best understood not as a failed organism but as a reversed transition in individuality, a soma that has slipped its developmental bottleneck. Identify precisely where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where two incompatible theories would license exactly the same trees, the same clonal labels, the same trial designs, and the same thresholds.

Thirdly, and this is the point on which my doctoral student and I have been arguing for some months: are these four registers consistent with one another, or does the institution hold commitments that cannot all be true at once without noticing that it does? I am not satisfied by the observation that different questions license different levels of description; I want a criterion for when that move is legitimate and when it is a fudge, and I want the criterion applied here, register by register, including to the case where the pathology report treats the tumour as a population of competing lineages while the trial protocol treats it as a single object with a single fitness, and the ethics of continuing a partly effective therapy is argued in terms of the patient's interests while the biology is described in terms of the cells'. Finally, tell me what would concretely have to change if the practice were rebuilt on either of two assumptions: that the patient, and not the lineage, the clone or the neoplasm, is the only genuine Darwinian individual in the room; or that nothing in the room is one. I mean change in the specific sense — the vocabulary permitted in a pathology report, the endpoints a trial is allowed to register, what the word relapse would come to denote, whether a subclonal fraction is reportable at all, and what justification adaptive dosing could still be given, since its rationale is explicitly ecological and therefore, I suspect, cannot survive the second assumption intact.
