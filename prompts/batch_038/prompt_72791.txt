I read a chapter in one of those fat pop self-improvement books (the name doesn't matter, it's the kind that lives on an airport shelf) that uses Franklin's moral perfection project as its centerpiece case study, and something about it bugged me without my being able to say where. Here's roughly how the chapter tells the story:

"Franklin, in his early twenties, drew up a list of thirteen virtues — Temperance, Silence, Order, and so forth — and built a chart to track them. The order of the list was basically arbitrary; he just wrote down the qualities he happened to want. Each day he scored himself on all thirteen at once, making a mark for every lapse, and the goal was to drive the total count down to zero, which makes him the first person to turn character into a metric. He also liked to call his own faults 'errata', printer's slang for a typo, and that tells you how lightly he took them: a misprint just gets corrected in the next edition. When the scheme failed, as he admitted it did, he explained why with a story about a man who had an axe ground until the whole thing came out speckled instead of uniformly bright, and who then decided he liked a speckled axe better. That is Franklin's actual conclusion about virtue: perfection is impossible, a spotless character would make you insufferable anyway, so aim to be well-rounded and moderately flawed. He dropped the project after that and never went back to it. And the scheme was purely secular — no God anywhere in it — because Franklin thought virtue paid off in this life only."

That's most of the chapter, and the last few lines are the moral the rest of the book hangs on.

My hunch, which I can't defend: the chapter reads the chart as an optimization device, a score to be minimized, when Franklin meant it as an attention device, and once you make that one correction a lot of the rest of the chapter's claims fall out with it. I also suspect it's promoting something Franklin mentions as a passing rationalization into his considered conclusion. Am I right about either of those, or am I just as confused as the book?

Could you go through it claim by claim and sort them into (a) accurate, (b) a fair-ish simplification that loses something but isn't wrong, and (c) actually wrong? For each of the wrong ones, say what Franklin's own version is and why the difference matters. Then tell me whether the closing lesson — perfection is impossible, so settle for well-rounded and flawed — survives once the errors are fixed, or whether it was only ever held up by them. And if there's anything in there that's right that I'm currently inclined to write off, flag it.
