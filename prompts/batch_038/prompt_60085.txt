I am a high school junior, and for the past year I have spent Saturday mornings helping out in a comparative cognition and robotics lab at the state university — mostly carrying things between benches and sitting in on the lab's reading group, which is run by a fourth-year graduate student named Marcus. The group has been working through the question of when it is legitimate to describe a machine as believing something, and Marcus has a habit of pronouncing on real systems as they come up. He always frames it as the same yes-or-no question: does this thing have beliefs literally, or only as a manner of speaking? I have written down six of his rulings so far, with the sentence or two he gave each time.

1. A conventional chess engine — hand-written evaluation function, alpha-beta search: **yes**. "There is a state in there that means the queen is on g5, and everything else the program does is downstream of it."
2. A bump-and-turn robot vacuum (bumper, cliff sensor, no map): **no**. "Nothing in it stands for the room."
3. A newer vacuum of the same brand that builds a map of the apartment and plans coverage routes: **yes**. "The map is about the apartment even when the apartment isn't in view."
4. A thermostat: **no**. "One state, one response, no room to get it wrong — and if it can't be wrong it isn't believing anything."
5. A network that learned to play from self-play with nothing about the game programmed in: **yes**. "Nobody told it what any of its features meant. It found out by winning and losing."
6. A text-only conversational model asked point-blank what it believes: **no**. "Nothing in its training ever checked whether it had the world right, so there is no fact of the matter about what its states are about."

In two weeks the lab is writing a panel for a science-center exhibit about a warehouse robot the undergrads built, and Marcus has been asked whether the panel may say, in plain language, that the robot believes the pallet is in aisle four. The robot is worth describing precisely because it is so unremarkable: the undergrads hand-coded a table of shelf locations, the table is updated deterministically from barcode scans, and there is no training, no reward signal, no search or optimization anywhere in the program, and no way for it to report on or check its own state. It does plan its route from that table, and it can be wrong — if someone moves a pallet without rescanning it, the robot drives confidently to the wrong aisle and idles there. Marcus has not ruled yet, and I would rather predict him than ask, since he says the exercise is better for me.

So, four things. First, lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with all six of his rulings — I expect there is more than one family of them — and say which of the six verdicts, if any, strains each candidate. Second, state what each candidate rule implies for the warehouse robot. Third, give your best guess at what Marcus will say about it and why, and then tell me separately whether his record actually licenses that answer; if it does not, identify which of the six earlier rulings he would have to abandon or reinterpret in order to stay consistent. Fourth, name one further case that would come apart under the two strongest candidate rules, and say what each rule predicts for it; you may use anything sitting around the lab — the slime mold that spans our mazes toward an oat flake, a line-following robot from the nineties still in a cabinet, a model that writes captions for photographs — or invent a system outright. Please do not include references, a bibliography, or a reading list: I cannot look anything up before the meeting, and what I need is the reasoning. End your answer with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by YES or NO, and then, in no more than twelve words, the rule you think Marcus is actually applying.
