I'm a postdoc in molecular evolution, and for the past two years I've been reading the discussion sections of comparative genomics and comparative physiology papers with a philosophy-of-biology text open beside me — I took a course on the subject as an undergraduate and never managed to let it go. Something keeps turning up in the primary literature for which I can't find a label, and it is not the familiar dispute between the historical account of function (a trait's function is what its ancestors were selected for) and the causal-role account (a trait's function is whatever it currently contributes to the capacities we happen to be explaining). I know that dispute, and I know the standard cases that motivate it: the δ-crystallins of the bird lens, which are lactate dehydrogenase homologues recruited into being dense, soluble, transparent packing material, and the antifreeze glycoproteins of Antarctic notothenioids, which were assembled out of a pancreatic digestive protease gene and now have nothing whatever to do with digestion. What I have started noticing is not the disagreement between the two accounts but a manner of writing that makes disagreement impossible.

The pattern is this. An author will write "the function of X is …" several times in a single paper, and each occurrence is silently backed by whichever account renders it true, with no indication of which sense is in play and no reconciliation offered anywhere. A methods section will justify calling a recruited lens protein functional by assaying whatever catalytic activity it retains — a move that borrows its warrant from descent, since what is being measured is a property the protein has in virtue of the enzyme family it belongs to — while the discussion two pages later announces that the protein's function is to raise the refractive index of the lens, which is pure causal role. Neither claim is false, and a pluralist will cheerfully say both are legitimate, that they answer different questions. That is exactly the trouble. When a reviewer objects on historical grounds (this expression domain is a few hundred generations old, nothing was ever selected for it), the author can reply that "function" was being used in the dispositional sense; when a different reviewer objects that the current contribution has never been measured and the variant may be a legacy hitchhiking on a linked site, the same author can reply that the trait's function is the established, selected one. The pluralism is licensed in advance by the literature, so the retreat never looks like an ad hoc maneuver anyone can point to. In practice the word has stopped carrying any risk: every particular use is individually defensible, and the collection of uses is answerable to nothing.

So does this have a name? I want the phenomenon rather than the label, which means I want the carving done explicitly — what exactly the thing is, what sits adjacent to it but is distinct, and where its edges fall. Several candidates I have already tried and think are near misses, and I would want to be told precisely how they are near misses: equivocation (both senses here are legitimate and publicly acknowledged, and the author would concede the ambiguity without embarrassment if asked, so nothing is being smuggled between meanings); an ad hoc rescue or an addition to the protective belt in the Lakatosian sense (nothing gets amended, the framework simply has two legs to stand on); the motte-and-bailey move as people usually describe it (there is no defensible core protecting an indefensible extension — both positions are defensible, and the vice is precisely that no usage is ever exposed); promiscuous realism or opportunistic pluralism as those get discussed for taxa and for the gene concept (close, but those concern whether the world carves up in one way, whereas this concerns a single word held to two criteria at once and therefore, effectively, to neither); and exaptation or spandrel, which name the biological situations rather than the way people write about them. If nothing in the literature actually fits, I would like you to coin something: give a definition with reasonably sharp conditions, tell me what would not count as an instance (an author who specifies the sense once and then holds to it throughout, say, or a case where the two accounts genuinely conflict and the author is forced to choose), and tell me whether you think this is a real epistemic vice or a benign division of linguistic labor that only looks like a vice to someone who reads papers instead of doing experiments. One practical note: I am at a marine station with intermittent connectivity and no access to my library, so please make the case inside your answer rather than pointing me toward references I cannot retrieve — I want your reconstruction of the territory, not a bibliography.
