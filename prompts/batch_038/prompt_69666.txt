Okay so I just spent three hours arguing with my friend about the Browning–Goldhagen fight — ordinary men versus willing executioners, why the guys in the police battalions did what they did — and neither of us buddged, but I've come away thinking we were arguing past each other in a way that's actually interesting, and I want to turn that into a proper research proposal. I'm applying for a term of funded research time, basically four months, no fieldwork, no new archival access, and they want about three pages.

Here's the half-formed idea. Both sides of that debate treat perpetrator conduct as *synchronic*. One side explains the shooting by what is true of the man at the moment — peer pressure, the commander's authority, the offer to step out that almost nobody takes up. The other explains it by what was true of the culture he'd been steeped in for decades. But notice that both are answering "why did he do it" with a state: a state of the situation, or a state of the person. Neither one really treats the explanandum as a trajectory — the claim that what predicts month six isn't the pressure in month six or the beliefs in his head, but what he did in month one, plus the fact that having done it he now has to keep his own story coherent. Call it the diachronic option. It isn't a third factor to line up beside situation and culture; it's the claim that both factors only bite through a sequence, and that a lot of the evidence the two camps trade looks different once you put a time index on it.

The concrete thing I keep coming back to: that famous moment where the commander offers his men the chance to step out and only a handful do gets used by both camps as a fixed data point — proof of obedience for one, proof of willingness for the other. But the diachronic view makes a prediction nobody checks. What happens to that fraction over the following months? If stepping out gets *easier* with repetition, that's numbing and habituation, which lands closer to the situational camp. If it gets *harder* even while the coercion stays equally intense, that's self-binding — the man has made the earlier acts his own and can no longer disown them without disowning himself — which lands closer to the cultural camp but for a reason that camp never actually gives. Those two curves are distinguishable in principle. That's the thing I want the proposal to be about.

So: write it. A precise question with a determinate answer; why the two standard readings don't settle it (and please don't just say it's more complicated than either); the approach, i.e. what I would actually be doing week by week for four months with no new archives; and why that approach rather than the obvious alternative, which is "get more perpetrator testimony and see." Be honest about what a result would even look like — a taxonomy of time-indexed explanatory claims? a set of discriminants applicable to material that already exists? a demonstration that one well-known exchange in the debate turns on an unnoticed ordering of the evidence? And state what would count as failure. I suspect one failure mode is that the diachronic business collapses into a repackaging of the situational account, and another is that the surviving testimony has no temporal resolution, so nothing can be ordered at all — in which case say so, and say what a smaller honest version of the project would be. Also flag where the scope is unrealistic for four months.

Don't bother with a bibliography or a lit review by citation, I'll add the references myself — talk about positions and arguments, not sources. And at the end be blunt with me: which of my moves above is the weakest, and what argument or piece of evidence would change your mind about the core claim? Like, what would convince you that sequencing adds nothing explanatorily and I should just go write the situational paper?
