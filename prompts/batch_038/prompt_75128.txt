I teach an 80-minute session in a second-year philosophy-of-probability class — 24 students, algebra and basic geometry only, no calculus and please don't sneak any in. I want an in-class activity where they run head-first into the failure of the principle of indifference using the chord-in-a-circle thing, but I don't want them handed the classic answers: they should generate them. So card-based recipes for "picking a chord at random" that all sound about equally arbitrary, some cheap physical way of actually making chords so groups get real frequency data to compare, and a token payoff scheme (no real money) that makes it sting when two groups who are both choosing "uniformly" end up with different numbers. They shouldn't hear the word Bertrand until the debrief.

The kicker at the end: I want every group forced to commit to one single probability for a bet against a hidden device of mine, so they can't just shrug that it's all relative. And the debrief needs to land the point that "pick a point uniformly" means different things depending on how you describe the circle, not just that there are several answers.

Two things I especially need. First, I was going to have them throw darts at a big paper circle, but a colleague says throwing drinking straws at it instead gives a genuinely different distribution, and I couldn't follow her argument — work out whether she's right and build that into the activity if she is. Second, tell me what will predictably go sideways the first time 24 people do this, and be straight with me about which part of the paradox the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand.
