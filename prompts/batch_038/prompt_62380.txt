I'm the batch release scientist at a mid-size generics plant — roughly 90 release decisions a month, and my signature is what lets a batch ship. Right now our SOP says you need a result at 95% confidence to call anything in-spec, and it's the same number whether the batch is a carton of antacid tablets or an IV antibiotic going to a neonatal unit. Everyone knows thats wrong, nobody's come up with a replacement. We also have a drafting rule that anything going to a regulator or into a dossier has to be worded so it would survive the most skeptical possible reader, which has turned our documents into walls of hedging, and people have stopped reading them.

We hired a philosophy consultant and she keeps saying we mash two different claims into one: that how much evidence you need before you're entitled to say you know something partly depends on what rides on it; and, separately, that the same sentence — we know this batch is within spec — can be true when I say it on the plant floor and false when an inspector says it in the same words about the same batch, with nobody's evidence having changed. I want that turned into something my team can run, not a lecture.

So: write a release-and-reporting procedure with two gates, one for whether I may act and one for what I may write down and to whom. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the gate closes with a no, and where the question is one of values so it gets handed to a named human. Constraints: it has to run in about 20 minutes per batch, the analysts using it have no philosophy and maybe one stats course, and two people sign off (me plus a QA lead).

Then run it on three cases: an excipient where we only hold the supplier's certificate of analysis because a skip-lot policy let us drop the in-house identity test, batch is a plain adult oral tablet; a neonatal IV antibiotic with one out-of-trend assay from a single analyst where a retest takes three days and the hospital is already rationing; and a shelf-life number extrapolated from six months of accelerated data going into a dossier for a market where stockrooms sit at 35°C for weeks.

Tell me which of her two ideas each gate is actually doing work for, which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat, what the procedure says when the evidence can never reach the bar the stakes demand but the batch has to ship anyway, and whether the strictest-audience drafting rule should survive — if not, what replaces it. Include one case where the answer is one the commercial team and I will both hate, and be straight about what the procedure flat out can't decide.
