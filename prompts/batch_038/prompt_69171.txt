I'm a software engineer, and I have no philosophy training of any kind, so please bear with the way I've set this out.

I sit on a small panel at my company that decides nominations for an annual "above and beyond" award. The panel is chaired by our principal engineer, whom I'll call Nadia, and her record is unusually consistent. I've been trying to work out what rule she is actually applying, because next week we decide a nomination I genuinely cannot predict, and I would rather vote consistently with our own record than on instinct. I should say that I know an award is not quite the same thing as the moral category: my partner, who does read philosophy, tells me the technical word for it is supererogatory. But Nadia treats the two as one question — she will only accept a nomination if she thinks the person was not required to do the thing — so I'll follow her framing.

Her six decisions to date:

Accepted: an engineer who spent three weekends, unpaid and unasked, rebuilding our alerting rules so that the whole team stopped being woken at 3 a.m.; there was no ticket for it.
Accepted: an engineer who gave a kidney to the young daughter of a colleague she had met perhaps twice.
Accepted: an engineer who turned down a better-paid job elsewhere and stayed a further fourteen months on a reduced salary, purely to keep an obsolete but safety-relevant system running for a handful of customers while we migrated them off it.
Rejected: an engineer who did that same alerting work inside normal hours, because it was the epic on their sprint.
Rejected: an engineer who gave up a weekend to cover the on-call shift of a colleague whose father had just died. Nadia's reason was that this is what being in a team means, and that the colleague was entitled to ask.
Rejected: an engineer who fixed his own service at 2 a.m. during an outage and then submitted himself. Nadia's reason was that he was paid to be there.

The new nomination. Ruth, a mid-level engineer, has for six years spent every Saturday morning pairing with new joiners from under-represented backgrounds and walking them through their first production change — roughly three hundred unpaid hours a year. Nobody asked her to start, there is no ticket, it appears nowhere in her role or her objectives, and it earns her no bonus. It has, however, become completely familiar: the induction plan quietly assumes it, new joiners are told about it in their first week, and when Ruth took four weeks off last year two managers complained to her line manager that onboarding had broken.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing remarks:

1. The single rule that best explains all six of Nadia's past verdicts.
2. A second, genuinely different rule that explains those six verdicts just as well.
3. What each of those two rules says about Ruth, and precisely where they come apart.
4. The vote I should cast if I want to be consistent with the panel's own record, together with which of the six is the closest analogue.
5. The principle the panel would be committing itself to by voting that way, and the one further nomination that would settle which rule Nadia is really running.

Please give me no reading list and no citations — I cannot follow them up at work, and I would much rather have the reasoning in plain terms.
