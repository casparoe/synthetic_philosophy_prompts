I draft and review commercial contracts for a living, and for some years now I have been reading philosophy of language in the evenings, mostly on my own. The two have collided in a way I cannot resolve, and I am writing to ask whether what I keep running into already has a name. I understand the classical analysis well enough to know that phrases of the form "the F" are treated as quantificational: to assert something of the F is to assert that exactly one thing is F, that it exists, and that it has some further property. I also know that most of the interesting objections concern what happens when one of those two conditions fails. My difficulty is that contract language seems to be built out of phrases that fail the uniqueness condition as a matter of routine, and yet behave nothing like the failure cases the literature is concerned with.

Three features of what I see, stated as concretely as I can. First, a clause will say that the Subcontractor shall indemnify the Buyer, or that the Deliverable shall conform to the Specification, and at the moment of signing there are, in the world at large, countless subcontractors and countless deliverables; nothing about the phrase picks out one thing. And yet nobody doubts that the sentence has a determinate truth condition, and when the clause is later litigated the dispute is never about whether the phrase succeeded in denoting. Second, very often no one has any particular individual in mind. The clause is drafted before anyone knows who will occupy the role, and it is meant to apply to whoever later occupies it — the parties' intention is not to pick out a person but to fix a position in a structure that someone may eventually fill. I am aware of a well-known distinction, drawn some decades ago, between using such a phrase with a particular individual in mind and using it without one, and I have read enough to suspect that neither horn is my case: there is no individual in mind, misdescribed or otherwise, and the phrase is nonetheless plainly not satisfied by just anything. Third, when nothing ever fills the role — no subcontractor is ever engaged — the clause does not strike me, or the parties, as false, nor as having said nothing; it simply never becomes live. But in adjacent situations, where the phrase fails because the thing it depends on was never produced (a Specification that was never attached, a Representative who was never appointed), the parties do argue, and they argue as though the question were about the world and their intentions rather than about the language.

So: is there an established name for this configuration, or a literature that treats it as a single thing? I am not after a label for its own sake, and I will not be satisfied with one. What I want is the carving. What exactly is the phenomenon, what are the nearest concepts in the literature on definite descriptions, how does what I have described differ from each of them, and which of them fits best — and is my third feature part of the same phenomenon as the first two, or have I bundled together two things that should be kept apart because the case where nothing fills the role is a failure case of a different kind altogether? If nothing established fits, say so plainly and propose a term, with a definition and with boundary cases marking where the phenomenon stops and where ordinary restriction, or the ordinary use of such a phrase with someone in mind, begins. Finally, an epistemic request: tell me where your identification is most likely to be wrong. Which of my three features would you want stated differently before you committed to an answer, what would a further case have to look like to force you to a different one, and if you find yourself tempted to make my phenomenon coextensive with an existing term — to stretch the term rather than concede that it does not cover what I have described — please flag that temptation as a suspicion instead of smoothing it over.
