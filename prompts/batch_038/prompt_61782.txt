I have been reconstructing what I take to be the sharpest objection to the Kripkean account of the necessary a posteriori, and the more carefully I formalize it, the more it looks as though it proves far more than its author needs. Before I trust any part of it, I want the diagnosis.

A distinction I will rely on. Consider:

(A) The sentence "Water is H₂O" expresses, in English, a metaphysically necessary truth.
(B) A competent speaker can know, without any empirical investigation, that if "Water is H₂O" is true at all, then it is true necessarily.

These are not the same claim, and I will try to keep them apart below.

The argument:

1. If t and t′ are rigid designators that designate the same thing, then the identity "t = t′" is metaphysically necessary.
2. That "water" picks out the same substance in every possible world is fixed by a rule of English, not by any discovery about the world.
3. Rules of one's own language are knowable a priori by anyone fully competent with it.
4. Hence (B): we know a priori that "Water is H₂O" is necessary if true.
5. The empirical work in discovering that water is H₂O consists entirely in establishing the antecedent — that the sentence is true.
6. But that antecedent is not contingent; by (1), it is necessary.
7. So the empirical work consists in establishing a necessary truth, and the modal status of that truth is already available a priori by (4).
8. Therefore our knowledge that water is necessarily H₂O is the product of an a priori modal rule plus an empirical input that adds no contingency.
9. The same schema runs through every theoretical identification: heat and mean molecular kinetic energy, gold and the element with atomic number 79, water and H₂O. In each case the modality is a priori and the content discovered is necessary.
10. Therefore nothing acquired a posteriori is contingent, and the necessity of whatever is acquired a posteriori is knowable a priori.
11. Therefore the category of the necessary a posteriori is empty — an artifact of confusing (A) with (B).
12. But we plainly do learn contingent things empirically: that it is raining, that the coin came up heads.
13. So (1), (2), or something in the region of (10) must go.

Two things trouble me. First, (12) collides with (11) only if (10) is read as a claim about all empirical knowledge, whereas (9) licenses it only for theoretical identifications; I can see that, but patching the scope does not obviously disarm the argument as applied to water itself, and I cannot tell whether what survives the patch is a serious objection or a triviality. Second, I am not confident that the singular case (Hesperus and Phosphorus) and the kind case (water and H₂O) behave alike at (2), and the argument treats them as interchangeable.

So: locate the step at which the argument first goes wrong, if it goes wrong anywhere. In particular, tell me whether the crucial move is (4)→(7) — whether knowing (B) really licenses the claim that the necessity of water's being H₂O is a priori available — or whether (2)→(3) is the weak joint, on the ground that a semantic rule cannot be fully grasped without knowing which substance it attaches the term to. Or say instead that the argument is valid and that one of (1)–(3) must be surrendered outright. Then price the options: what the Kripkean pays to block it, what a two-dimensionalist pays, and what someone who insists that rigidity must be understood as tracking the same underlying nature in every world, rather than merely constant designation, pays. I would rather keep (1) than any other premise in the argument; tell me exactly what keeping it costs me.
