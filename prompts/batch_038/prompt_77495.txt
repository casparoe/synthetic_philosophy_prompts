I am setting the final problem-set question for an upper-level seminar in decision theory and the philosophy of probability. I want one self-contained question on the two-envelope paradox that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar list of proposed resolutions. Build it on the following setup: the smaller amount S is drawn from the geometric family P_r(S = 2^k) = (1−r)r^k for k = 0, 1, 2, …, with 0 < r < 1; the player chooses an envelope at random, opens it, and observes X = 2^k. The question should have four parts worth 25 marks in total and be answerable in about 90 minutes without access to the literature:

(a) Derive the posterior probability that the player holds the smaller envelope, and the expected gain from switching, as functions of r and k; show that for k ≥ 1 the sign of that gain is fixed by r alone.
(b) Explain why the case k = 0 behaves differently, and why that does not by itself vindicate the naive fifty-fifty reasoning.
(c) Prove that E[S] is finite exactly when r < 1/2, and say what the coincidence of that boundary with the boundary from (a) shows about the two standard diagnoses: that the fallacy lies in holding the two hypotheses equiprobable whatever the observation, versus that it lies in priors with infinite or undefined expectation. Which diagnosis is doing the real work, and is there anything the first can explain that the second cannot?
(d) For r > 1/2, switching is favorable after every possible observation, yet before opening anything the two envelopes have the same distribution and the choice is ex ante indifferent. Say precisely what gives way — countable additivity, the law of total expectation, or the legitimacy of comparing infinite expectations — and whether a decision theory that recommends always switching is thereby refuted.

Please give me the question exactly as it should appear on the problem set, with the setup stated cleanly and a mark allocation per part; a model answer of roughly 900 words that carries out the algebra rather than describing it; and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific derivational and philosophical moves each must contain. In the marking scheme, also list the three errors I most expect and what each costs — among them, appealing to what the organizer could plausibly afford instead of fixing r; asserting the equiprobability of the two hypotheses after the observation without justification; and claiming that no proper prior licenses always switching, which part (c) shows is true only when the qualifier "with finite mean" is supplied.
