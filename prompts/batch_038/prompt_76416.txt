Some context first: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy classes ever, I get most of this from a coworker who reads a lot of it and from podcasts. He got me onto the debate about whether tables exist — the camp that says only simples arranged table-wise exist versus the camp that says obviously tables exist — and he explained that there's a standard procedure for handling that kind of fight, called the method of elimination, from some paper on verbal disputes (he said Chalmers, I never actually read it). The way he described it: you take the contested terms one at a time and try to eliminate each by swapping in a definition or a neutral paraphrase both sides will accept, and if the disagreement evaporates once every term is pinned down, the dispute was merely verbal. Nobody ever disagreed about the world, they were just running different schemas under the same field name. That made total sense to me, it's exactly what happens when two services quietly drift apart on what `active` means and then spend a week arguing about an incident that never happened.

The part I've accepted but can't do anything with is the next step. He said the procedure bottoms out at some term you can't paraphrase away any further, and he called that bedrock, and his conclusion was that a dispute that hits bedrock is *also* verbal, just verbal at maximum depth — you've run out of vocabulary, so there's nothing left for either side to be right or wrong about. So ontology is verbal all the way down, and the deeper you push the more everything turns into a vocabulary preference, its just a question of how far down you've gotten. Which leaves me stuck on a practcial question: if the table-denier and the table-affirmer are both correct, and even the leftover disagreement is terminological, then what's supposed to decide which vocabulary I use? Is it purely which one is more convenient for the job, like picking protobuf over JSON because it's smaller on the wire, with no sense in which one of them is *true*? Same question for the bigger case he mentioned, whether numbers exist. If that's verbal too, does it have any more content than an argument on my team about whether to call a function `fetchUser` or `getUser`?

The thing I'd most like you to walk me through is this: on that picture, can a dispute ever fail to be verbal? Every candidate I come up with seems to get pushed down one more level until you run out of words, so I can't see what a non-verbal dispute would look like or how you'd recognize one when you hit it. Give me a concrete disagreement that doesn't dissolve when you run the procedure, and say what makes it different from the tables case.
