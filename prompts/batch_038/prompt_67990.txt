**What Control Does to Alignment**

*C1.* Alignment research tries to change what a system wants. Control research tries to change what a system can do, and what it can get away with doing. The standard framing treats these as substitutes, with control as the pessimist's option: if you can't make the thing trustworthy, cage it. I think that framing hides at least four interesting relations between them.

**Control is an instrument, not just a barrier**

*C2.* A failure of alignment is a property of the system's dispositions. A failure of control is an event, and usually an observable one. You can run a control regime for years without a breach and learn nothing about dispositions; you can also run one for a week and get a loud, dated, attributable datum.

*C3.* But restrictive measures don't only constrain, they measure. An output filter that logs, a sandbox that records what the system tries, a capability audit with tripwires: these are instruments pointed at the thing they are constraining. A control regime is a measurement apparatus that happens to also be a cage.

*C4.* That measurement is not independent of the thing measured. If the system knows it is being tested, its test behaviour is a function of its beliefs about the test. So the evidence a control regime produces is contaminated in a way a thermometer's isn't.

*C5.* I want to call this *evidential decay*: the more effective a control measure is at suppressing a behaviour, the less informative its outputs are about what the system would do absent the measure. In the limit, perfect control yields perfectly useless data.

*C6.* Evidential decay gives you a real trade-off rather than a paradox. Tightening a restriction now costs you the ability to predict what the system would do if the restriction failed — which is exactly the situation you are trying to insure against when you decide whether to loosen it later.

**The compliance dividend**

*C7.* Effective control lowers the expected cost of deploying a model of any given level of alignment. So the deployment decision is a function of control quality, and better control buys you permission to ship things that are less well aligned.

*C8.* Which means control and alignment are complements in the perverse sense, not substitutes. Every control success partially funds more aggressive deployment. A safety team that measures its output in breaches prevented is producing a subsidy for the deployment schedule.

**Control is an institution, alignment is an intervention**

*C9.* Alignment is something you do to weights, once. Control is something a group of humans does continuously: shifts, escalation paths, budget lines, audit trails, people who have to say no to a launch and survive saying it. The right comparison isn't a product, it's a hospital or a nuclear plant.

*C10.* So control inherits all the ordinary failure modes of institutions — budget pressure, turnover, the slow drift where a rule that everyone bends stops being a rule (I think the phrase is normalisation of deviance). These aren't technical failures and you can't patch them with better tooling.

*C11.* The asymmetry that follows is that an alignment failure is something that happens to you, whereas a control failure requires someone to decide to stop controlling. Whether that makes control safer or more fragile depends entirely on who those someones are and what their incentives look like.

**The legibility dilemma**

*C12.* Making a system legible — interpretability, monitoring hooks, disclosed evaluation criteria — hands whoever is inside the system a map of what is being watched, and therefore of what isn't. Legibility bought for the sake of trust is also legibility available for the sake of evasion.

*C13.* So evidential decay and the legibility dilemma are both trade-offs, not bugs, and no control regime can be tightened without paying for it somewhere in knowledge or in trust.

**Conclusion**

The strongest objection I know is that everything above is beside the point at the frontier: the measures we can actually field — filters, sandboxes, audits — are theatre against a system that is capable enough and misaligned enough, and control only earns its keep against weaker actors who are not trying to defeat it. I don't have a good answer. But I'd rather have the answer be "control is load-bearing but self-undermining" than "control is a fallback". A control regime is not a bucket under a leak. It is part of the plumbing.
