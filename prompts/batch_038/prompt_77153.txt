**The Panel Problem: Hume's Standard Delivers Agreement Without Correctness**

**1.** Hume sets up his problem by an asymmetry. In matters of opinion and science, he observes, disagreement clusters at the level of general principles and tends to dissolve once terms are explicated, so that disputants discover they had been agreeing all along. In matters of taste the opposite holds: everyone deploys the same vocabulary of praise and blame, and yet agreement in the general terms conceals disagreement about every particular to which those terms are applied. From this he infers the need for a rule — it is, he says, natural to seek a standard of taste, "a rule, by which the various sentiments of men may be reconciled." His answer is famous and, I want to suggest, oddly easy to misread as an answer to a different question. The standard is not a rule for detecting beauty in objects. It is a specification of certain critics: where those who combine good sense with delicate sentiment, who have practiced the art and compared works extensively, and who have cleared themselves of prejudice, converge on a verdict, that convergence is itself the standard.

**2.** My proposal is that the account becomes legible, and its difficulty visible, when translated into the vocabulary of measurement theory. Hume's five conditions are conditions on raters rather than on the thing rated. Practice, comparison, delicacy, and the absence of prejudice are all ways of making a judge's outputs less noisy and less idiosyncratic; collectively they are a recipe for inter-rater reliability. Hume is then entitled to treat reliability as sufficient only because, on his view, aesthetic value is constituted by the responses of suitable responders — the verdict does not track merit, it makes it. That is a defensible position. It is not, however, the position his framing promises. He announced a rule by which conflicting sentiments are reconciled, and what he supplies is a rule for deciding whose sentiments were candidates for reconciliation in the first place.

**3.** Measurement theory separates what Hume runs together. Reliability is consistency: the same rater scores the same object alike on repeated occasions, and qualified raters score it alike. Validity is whether those scores track the property they purport to measure. Nothing gets you from the first to the second without an anchor — a criterion, an independently specified target. A scale that reads ten pounds heavy on every weighing is perfectly reliable and perfectly wrong; wine competition judging is the standing cautionary tale in the other direction, since even trained palates tasting blind turn out to be less consistent across repeated pours than their credentials imply. A panel can be maximally consistent and systematically mistaken, and consistency is not evidence against the mistake.

**4.** Hume appears to recognize the need for an anchor, and in one place he has one — but only for wine. He borrows from Cervantes the story of Sancho's kinsmen, who praise a hogshead with reservations about a faint taste of leather and a faint taste of iron, are ridiculed for their pretensions, and are vindicated when the cask is drained and a key on a leathern thong is found at the bottom. The delicacy of the tasters is confirmed by something in the wine, independent of anybody's judgment. That is precisely the criterion relation the aesthetic case lacks. There is no key at the bottom of the barrel. When the true judges converge on the inferiority of one poet and the sublimity of another, no independent fact vindicates the convergence — and Hume is not even in a position to say what such a fact would look like, since if there were one, the standard would be the fact rather than the judges.

**5.** So he reaches for a second, diachronic anchor: durability. A work still admired after centuries, and across cultures that do not share the prejudices of its first audience, has been tested in a way that a fashionable novelty has not. But durability is not independent of the panel; it is the panel iterated. Each generation's qualified critics re-approve what the previous generation's approved, and the persistence of approval is then offered as confirmation that the original approval was correct. The circularity is not the trivial kind — an iterated procedure can add information, as repeated sampling does — but the diachronic test inherits every bias of the synchronic one and amplifies it with a selection effect. What survives is what was copied, printed, taught, patronized, and legible to the institutions that decide what counts as a work at all. Durability therefore confounds aesthetic merit with institutional persistence, in the way that a longitudinal study with non-random attrition confounds the trait it measures with the trait that determines who drops out.

**6.** The obvious Humean reply is that the prejudice condition already does the work I claim cannot be done. Prejudice, on Hume's account, is not merely disagreement with the panel; it is the importation of a distorting standpoint — the critic who cannot enter the spirit of a work's own age and purposes, who condemns a pagan hero for lacking Christian humility, who refuses a genre its own aims. If that is right, then whether a judge is prejudiced is settled by a substantive condition about the relation between critic and work, not by conformity to consensus, and my charge of circularity in section 5 loses its force. My response is that the reply relocates the problem rather than solving it. "The standpoint appropriate to the work" is itself not given independently of good judges: we identify the appropriate standpoint by asking what a suitably delicate and practiced reader makes of the work, and we identify the suitably delicate reader by asking who adopts appropriate standpoints. Whether this is a vicious circle or a virtue-theoretic one I am prepared to live with, I cannot yet say.

**7.** What survives, I think, is less than Hume's framing promises and more than it looks like. His achievement is not a decision procedure for beauty but a calibration procedure for critics: an account of the dispositions one must cultivate in order to be a trustworthy responder. On that reading the standard of taste is not a rule for reconciling sentiments but a rule for producing people whose sentiments deserve to be reconciled. That leaves open the only interesting question, which is what makes a disposition the right one — and Hume's available answer seems to be that the right dispositions are the ones the right people have.
