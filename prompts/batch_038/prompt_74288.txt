I coordinate a study that collects verbal reports alongside behavioural and imaging data, and I have been asked to write the laboratory's standing protocol for handling the reports. Four graduate research assistants will use it to code roughly sixty transcripts a week, about twenty minutes each, and none of them has any training in philosophy of mind. The protocol must also satisfy two co-investigators who sit on opposite sides of the consciousness debate: one holds that phenomenal experience is real and that a subject is directly acquainted with their own, the other holds that experience talk is largely a theoretical fiction generated by cognitive processes that are themselves entirely describable from the outside. Our declared method is heterophenomenology in Dennett's sense — reports are treated as data from which the coder reconstructs the world as the subject takes it to be, with the question of whether that world contains what the subject says it contains left strictly open. My difficulty is that the method is normally stated as a set of slogans rather than as a workflow, and in pilot coding my assistants have drifted in both directions: some treat a sincere report as settled evidence that the subject had the experience described, while others quietly rewrite reports into claims about stimulus processing or about what the subject would do if asked. The coded record is what our paper's claims about awareness will rest on, and my principal investigator signs off on the protocol before submission.

Please write the protocol itself, as something a coder can keep beside them at a desk. It should contain:

1. The ordered steps from raw transcript to coded record, stating what each step may and may not put into the record, and why that order is forced — which steps depend on which.
2. Which distinctions must survive the simplification for non-philosophers and which can safely be dropped: report versus judgement versus mere disposition to respond; the subject's authority over what they said as against what it means; the subject's world as against the theorist's; sincerity as against accuracy.
3. A neutrality audit: a short list of code labels, phrasings, and inferential moves that betray the coder's own metaphysics, each paired with an acceptable replacement, on the standard that the same transcript must come out coded the same way whichever co-investigator is reading over the coder's shoulder.
4. Rules for conflict and confabulation: when two incompatible reports are both logged, when one is flagged as likely confabulation, when behavioural or neural data may override a report as against merely being recorded alongside it, and where the coder must stop and refer the case to me.
5. A check that the protocol has actually been followed, and something better than percentage agreement, since two coders can agree perfectly while both smuggling in the same metaphysics.
6. Honest marking of the steps that are judgement calls dressed as procedure, and a plain statement of what the protocol cannot do, written so that it can be handed to the co-investigators without embarrassment.

Then run the protocol end to end on three cases, showing the record actually produced at each stage rather than describing it: (a) two stationary lights of different colours flashed in quick succession a few degrees apart, with participants reporting one spot that moved across the screen and changed colour partway along its path; (b) a participant with a visual field defect who denies seeing anything in the affected region on trial after trial yet localises and identifies stimuli presented there well above chance; (c) a participant who, having been covertly shown an item other than the one they selected as the more attractive of two, gives a fluent and detailed account of why they chose the item now in front of them.

Add one further case of your own choosing on which following the protocol to the letter yields a result I will not welcome — for instance a case in which it leaves the study unable to say anything at all about whether an experience occurred, or in which the neutrality requirement and the demand for publishable data come apart. State which co-investigator will object and on what grounds.

Finally, address the standing objection that heterophenomenological neutrality is not on offer: that to treat a report as describing a world whose contents are held in abeyance, rather than as a subject's direct word on an experience they are in a position to know about, is already to have taken the deflationist side, since the realist denies that there is any such thing as bracketing the question. Say where in the protocol that objection bites, whether neutrality should be reinterpreted as a constraint on the coder's vocabulary rather than on the ontology of the coded record, and what I should tell each co-investigator.

Keep the philosophical justification separate from the operational steps, so that my assistants are not required to understand the debate in order to do the job, and close with a one-page card summarising the steps and the audit list.
