Historians and philosophers of history disagree about what inevitability could even mean. Two candidate analyses are worth taking seriously. On the probabilistic analysis, an event was inevitable if, given the antecedent conditions, the chance of its occurring was very close to one. On the analysis I want you to scrutinize — call it the decision-point analysis — an event was inevitable if and only if there is no identifiable choice made by an agent of the period such that a different choice would have prevented the event; inevitability is then a claim about the shape of a narrative rather than a measure over possibilities.

The decision-point analysis has real advantages: it explains why calling a war inevitable seems to slight the people who fought and lost it, and it sidesteps the notorious difficulty of assigning probabilities to events that happened only once. But it also looks permissive in the wrong direction, since for almost any outcome one can point to some decision that mattered, and it seems silent about cases in which the relevant agents had no alternatives at all — a famine produced by drought and existing transport networks, say — where we are most tempted to insist that the outcome really was inevitable.

Write an essay adjudicating between these two analyses of historical inevitability. Take the objections seriously on both sides, consider whether either analysis can be repaired (for example by relativizing inevitability to a specified class of decision points, or by treating the two analyses as answering to different purposes), and then defend a verdict: which account is better, or whether inevitability talk should be abandoned altogether. Do not settle for a non-committal conclusion or an even-handed summary of the dialectic.

Write in American English. At the end, state how confident you are in the verdict you have reached and single out the step in your argument you are least sure of.
