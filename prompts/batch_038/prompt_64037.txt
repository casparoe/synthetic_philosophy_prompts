I am a junior at a public high school, and for the creative-writing unit my teacher has agreed to let me propose my own subject, on the condition that the story engage a genuine intellectual dispute rather than a mood. My subject is a disagreement within the ethics of war about whether soldiers on the two sides stand on equal moral footing.

Write a short story of roughly 2,000 to 2,500 words that dramatizes that disagreement. The two positions it must hold in tension are these.

Position A: combatants are moral equals. What a soldier may do is settled by the rules both sides accept for how war is to be fought; the justice of the war belongs to those who began it, and the ordinary soldier is not its author. There is also a practical case for the convention — abandon it and no one surrenders, quarter is never given, and the victor ends up trying every private of the losing army as a murderer.

Position B: the convention is a comfortable fiction. Killing is justified by what it serves, so a man who kills in the service of an unjust cause does wrong even when he observes every restriction on how he fights, while his opponent's identical act is not merely permitted but demanded of him.

Ordinarily Position A borrows support from the fact that soldiers cannot reliably determine which side is in the right. I want that support removed. So build the story in a world where the question is settled publicly and early: a standing tribunal, whose method is transparent and which has been right often enough within living memory that both populations trust it, publishes a finding in the first weeks of the war stating plainly whose cause is just. Every soldier who enlists after that date enlists knowing. Do not name any real country, war, or institution; the conflict should be invented, and it should not read as an allegory for one particular real war.

To keep this from becoming two people arguing in a room, the story must observe these constraints:

- It is built from two scenes, one during the war and one after the armistice, joined by a single recurring document — a list of names, compiled for one purpose in the first scene and put to a very different purpose in the second.
- No character may state either position as a general thesis, and no one may use the phrases just war, moral equality, or war crime. The argument has to survive in what people do with orders, rations, a prisoner manifest, a handshake, or a refusal.
- At least one character must move between Position A and Position B, and the story must show concretely what the move costs him.
- Work in the consequence I find hardest: if Position B is correct, then the soldier on the just side who declines to fight is not merely a coward but someone withholding what is owed, and the reasoning that indicts his enemy also licenses harshness toward him.
- The ending must not decide the question. It should leave the reader feeling what is forfeited under each answer.

After the story, add an author's note of no more than 250 words identifying the three moments that carry Position A and the three that carry Position B, and stating what you take the story to imply about a war in which soldiers cannot claim ignorance of which side is right.
