I'm a backend engineer, and I sit on the five-person technical steering committee of a mid-sized open-source build system — roughly four hundred occasional contributors, a dozen regulars, a handful of very large corporate downstreams. I've been involved for about nine years, almost entirely on the storage and caching layers. I have no humanities training whatsoever, so I'd like the advice below to assume that.

Three years ago the project went through its worst episode. The lead maintainer of the scheduler subsystem reverted a large, popular, community-developed feature branch directly on the default branch, without review, on the grounds that it was destabilizing release candidates. The revert broke CI for several downstreams for most of a week. Within two days three contributors announced a fork; the mailing list and the chat server then produced about four months of argument that I'd rather not relitigate here; the fork drew away a meaningful share of contributors, never found funding, and is now largely inactive. The maintainer stepped down the following year, and this committee rewrote governance so that the protected subsystem requires two-person review. We've since agreed we should publish an official account of the episode in the documentation, linked from the contributor onboarding page, because newcomers keep rediscovering it and re-arguing it, and because a trade publication is preparing a retrospective and will publish its version whether or not we publish ours. Two drafts are in front of the committee, and they are not converging. The first, written by an infrastructure engineer, is a strictly chronological record sourced only to artifacts that still exist: merge and revert identifiers, meeting minutes, the public chat log, release notes. It deliberately contains no statement of anyone's motives and no assignment of fault. Their argument is that we cannot actually know why anyone did anything, that interpretation is what made the four months miserable, and that a document which takes a position hands the aggrieved side a target. They also note, to their credit, that our archive is lopsided — the fork coordinated in a private channel we have no access to, and the downstream users hit hardest were writing in a support tracker we no longer retain. The second draft, written by our community manager, is a narrative: it explains the dispute structurally, arguing that one person held unilateral merge authority over a subsystem everyone depended on and that the personal antagonism was a symptom rather than a cause; it says plainly that most participants acted in good faith under bad incentives; it identifies two specific decisions as mistakes; and it closes with the governance changes and what they're meant to prevent. Two committee members think this draft reads as a story in which the current governance arrangement comes out vindicated, that it turns identifiable living people into characters serving a plot, and that it quietly omits the three weeks during which this committee simply didn't respond, which is arguably what allowed the escalation. There's also a factual disagreement I can't resolve: the drafts order two events differently, specifically whether the fork was announced before or after the maintainer's second, much broader revert. The chat log covering that window was edited by a moderator, the timestamps are in mixed zones, and one relevant post was deleted. The single person who could settle the sequence has declined to be interviewed and has asked not to be named in anything we publish.

I described all this to a friend from a previous job who has a doctorate in history, half expecting her to say something practical about archival standards. Instead she said this is an argument historians have been having for a century and a half, and roughly outlined three positions. There was a nineteenth-century German, Ranke, who wanted to write the past as it actually happened and let the documents speak rather than the author. Then a British historian of the mid-twentieth century, who wrote a well-known short book asking what history is, and who thought the first position naive because the documents only speak when a historian has already decided which ones to call forward and in what order. Then an American literary critic who went further and argued that choosing to tell it as a story at all — with a shape, an escalation and a resolution, a genre — is a form imposed on the events rather than found in them, and that a bare chronicle which claims to have no shape has merely hidden its choices. I followed perhaps half of this. But the part that stuck with me is that our two drafts are each making a confident claim about what counts as sticking to the facts, and neither claim survives much scrutiny: the first draft's selection of which artifacts are even in the archive is itself a set of decisions, and the second draft's plot structure is doing interpretive work it never announces.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's committee call? I need a bottom line the committee can vote on: publish the first draft, publish the second, publish both in parallel with a framing note, publish neither and commission a third, or some other arrangement you think is better. Whichever you recommend, give the reasoning a committee of engineers can actually assess, state plainly what we should do about the ordering question we may never be able to settle, and make the strongest honest case for the option you're arguing against rather than a strawman of it. I'd also like you to address a disagreement underneath all of this, which is whether the document's job is to be a durable record of what happened or to change how contributors behave going forward; several of us suspect that once you settle that question, most of the rest follows, and I'd like to see whether you agree or think that's too tidy. Please keep it usable by people who will read it in a terminal window between builds: no bibliography, no footnotes, no citations, and don't assume any of us has read the historians my friend described — if you draw on them, do it in ordinary prose in your own words. Roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words. Finally, don't invent participants, quotes, or incidents beyond what I've given you, and don't try to guess which real project this is; the specifics you have are the specifics we have.
