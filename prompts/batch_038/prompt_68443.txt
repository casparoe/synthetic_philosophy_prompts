I work as a monetary economist in a central bank's research division — units of account, media of exchange, stores of value, seigniorage, relative prices, transactions costs, general equilibrium, and the welfare theorems are the vocabulary I actually think in. Our history-of-thought reading group spent two sessions on Aristotle's discussion of money and exchange: the treatment of proportionate reciprocity in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, where he sets out the four-term scheme with a builder, a shoemaker, a house, and a pair of shoes and says that just exchange is achieved by a kind of diagonal coupling of the terms, with money introduced as the middle term that measures everything because it conventionally represents demand; his remark that money is called by a name derived from custom because it exists not by nature but by agreement and can be altered and rendered useless at the users' will; the account in the first book of the Politics of how barter gave way to a money commodity chosen for being intrinsically useful and easy to carry, later stamped so that people would not have to weigh it out; and the distinction between household management and money-getting, with retail trade and lending at interest condemned as unnatural because this second kind of acquisition has no natural limit, whereas the first is bounded by the needs of the household. A colleague in the group insisted that this is really just a primitive transactions-cost theory of money — that Aristotle anticipated the double-coincidence-of-wants argument and got the basics right, and that the passages on natural and unnatural acquisition are moralizing with no economic content at all, so they can be deleted from the argument without loss. I suspect that verdict is too glib in both directions, but I cannot say precisely where, and I have no Greek and no access to the scholarly literature.

Please restate Aristotle's position as systematically as you can in the vocabulary I described above, taking each component in turn: the reciprocity scheme and its diagonal, the claim that money measures all things, the claim that demand is the real standard which holds exchange together and that money only represents it by convention, the origin story, and the natural/unnatural acquisition distinction together with the no-limit claim about money-getting. For each component I want the closest available translation — relative prices and exchange ratios, price systems with a numeraire, commodity money and its carrying costs, legal tender and fiat issuance, seigniorage and inflation, unbounded maximization versus satiated preferences — stated in the terms I would use in a paper, and then an explicit note on how much distortion the translation required. Where a modern concept is imported anachronistically, say so and say what Aristotle himself does and does not have: I specifically want to know whether he earns credit for a double-coincidence-of-wants argument or whether that credit is an artifact of the translation, and whether his statement that money can be altered and rendered useless at will is a theory of fiat money, a theory of debasement, a point about the revocability of a social convention, or none of these. Also attend to the place where he hesitates — his suggestion that things so different as a house and a pair of shoes cannot be made genuinely commensurable, and that demand holds them together only as a surrogate for a commensurability he declines to supply — because I think that hesitation is where the whole scheme becomes interesting rather than merely quaint.

Then give me the residue report. Which of his claims survive into my framework with their content intact; which can only be rendered by distorting them into something he would not have recognized; and which have no expression at all in the vocabulary of given preferences, budget constraints, and efficiency. On that last category I want a verdict rather than a shrug: for each untranslatable claim, is the failure a limitation of my framework — for instance, that economics has no way to represent sufficiency, a bounded end of acquisition, or the difference between use and accumulation, except by relabeling it as a preference — or is it a sign that the claim was never determinate enough to be true or false, so that my colleague is right to delete it? Finally, tell me whether anything in the residue has consequences that are still live: does the no-limit claim about money-getting amount to a substantive proposition about the dynamics of accumulation that a modern model could confirm or falsify, or is it purely a claim about the proper ends of a household that no model of mine can even state? Please write this as an analysis, not as a survey — I do not need a bibliography or a list of commentators, and I would rather you reason from the content of the passages as I have described them than hedge toward citations.
