I design the kernel and the termination checker for a dependent type theory in the Coq/Agda/Lean family. My working vocabulary is well-founded relations and accessibility predicates, structural recursion, inductive versus coinductive definitions, guardedness and productivity checks, universe hierarchies and predicativity, inhabitation, and the question of when an inhabitant of a dependent product exists given an inhabitant of each fiber. My philosophy is entirely amateur: I can state the outlines of the two cosmological arguments — the kalam version (whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause) and the principle-of-sufficient-reason version pressed by Leibniz and Clarke (each contingent thing has an explanation; the explanation of the whole collection cannot itself be just another contingent thing; therefore there is a necessary being) — but I suspect both are at bottom structural claims about grounding relations, and that is a thing I have precise machinery for. I would like to find out whether that suspicion is right.

So: restate each argument premise by premise and inference by inference in that vocabulary. For each premise, tell me whether it becomes a claim about well-foundedness, about termination or normalization, about the inductive/coinductive divide, about predicativity and universes, about the inhabitation of a dependent product, or about none of these — and how much of the argument's force actually survives the restatement. Address at least the following, and add anything I have overlooked.

1. The kalam proponent's ban on the "actual infinite" is cashed out as the claim that a beginningless series of past events would be a completed infinite totality, which is held to be impossible. Is that a well-foundedness requirement, or something else? Is an infinite past closer to an infinitely descending chain that a termination checker rejects, or to a non-well-founded structure of the Aczel kind — entirely coherent once you drop foundation as an axiom? Note that my framework cheerfully admits coinductive streams, which are completed infinite objects; so tell me what, if anything, distinguishes the past from a stream, and whether the potential/actual infinity distinction maps onto the inductive/coinductive distinction or onto something in my toolkit it does not map onto at all.

2. The principle of sufficient reason, read as a demand that every truth be derivable from the axioms, looks like a completeness postulate. If so, say what follows, including whether the demand is internally coherent for a system strong enough to talk about itself.

3. The necessary being functions as the terminus of explanation, and is sometimes described as self-explanatory or as existing in virtue of its own nature. In my terms that is a fixed point of the grounding relation — a derivation that licenses itself. My kernel rejects circular derivation outright, yet guarded self-reference is productive and legitimate elsewhere in the same framework. Say which of these two regimes the argument needs, and whether the difference between them is doing any work in the philosophical debate.

4. The complaint that an infinite chain of individually explained contingent facts still leaves the conjunction unexplained: is this a pointwise-versus-uniform distinction, a failure of dependent-product formation over an infinite family, or an equivocation between an indexed family and its total space? Give me your best formal reading and then say whether stating it precisely makes it true, false, or meaningless.

5. The final move, from a first cause to a free personal agent who chose to create: I expect no counterpart exists. Tell me whether that is a gap in my vocabulary or a sign that the move was never a structural claim.

Then the residue report. Separate what translated cleanly from what translated only under distortion from what cannot be expressed in my terms at all. In the middle category, flag every instance where the distortion came from a design decision of my proof assistant — I require well-foundedness, but that is a policy I adopted, not a metaphysical necessity — as opposed to a genuine feature of the argument, and do not smuggle my axioms in and present them as translation. For each item of residue, give a verdict: does it show the proof-theoretic vocabulary is impoverished, or that the premise was less clear than it looked in its original dress?

Close with the question I most want answered. If these really are structural claims about grounding, then stating them precisely should either vindicate or dissolve them: which of the two arguments, if either, is damaged by the translation, which is untouched by it, and is there a version of either that becomes stronger once it is stated as a claim about derivations? Assume I need no background, and give me no bibliography.
