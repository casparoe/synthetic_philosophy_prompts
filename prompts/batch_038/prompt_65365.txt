I cover the history and philosophy of science for a monthly magazine, and for our spring issue I would like to commission a work of short fiction to run opposite my feature rather than another essay. The feature concerns the current vogue for giving non-human forces a starring role in historical narrative — pathogens, monsoon failure, volcanic winters — and the older suspicion that this is environmental determinism in new clothes. I want the story to make that tension felt without ever stating it as a thesis. Assume a readership that is intelligent and scientifically literate but has no background in historiography.

The conceit is mine and is not drawn from any existing novel, series, or thought experiment, so please treat it as fresh material to be developed rather than as an allusion. Sometime this century, an international body called the Attribution Commission has been given a narrow but consequential power. When a member state argues that a past catastrophe was substantially the doing of a natural event rather than of human decisions, the Commission issues a binding finding that splits the outcome between the two: a fraction assigned to the event, the remainder to policy. Its findings govern debt relief, and because its wording is incorporated by treaty into national curricula, they also govern how the catastrophe may be described in school textbooks. A panel consists of three members — a climatologist, an epidemiologist, and a historian — and is advised by a standing officer with the title Advocate for the Non-Human Party, whose job is to represent the interests of the event itself, since the event cannot instruct counsel, cannot settle, and cannot be blamed.

The case in your story concerns a fictional island city devastated in a fictional decade by the ash winter of a fictional volcano, followed by a famine that the petitioners say was made survivable, and then unsurvivable, by the repeal of a grain-reserve ordinance some years earlier. The dispute that should drive the plot is technical and quiet: the petitioners want the counterfactual in which the eruption never occurred, while the state wants the counterfactual in which it occurred but the ordinance still stood. The two baselines yield wildly different fractions, and one character must explain in plain language why the data alone cannot settle which is legitimate.

A few requirements. Between 2,000 and 2,600 words, with a title. Tell it in close third person or first person from the perspective of the historian on the panel, and complicate her position by having her own earlier book entered as an exhibit by the petitioners, so that she is being asked to rule against her own account of the famine. Include at least two hearing-room scenes of dialogue, one of which turns on the baseline question. Give the Advocate real professional dignity and an argument the reader finds partly convincing. Let the determinism worry be carried by a concrete interest — who gains, in money or in exoneration, when the fraction rises — rather than by anyone lamenting reductionism. Invent everything: no real eruptions, plagues, treaties, cities, or historical figures, as I need to publish this without a fact-checker. Do not have any character discuss the underlying ideas in the vocabulary of philosophy; keep the concepts embedded in procedure, paperwork, and cross-examination. End with the finding issued and the historian obliged to sign or refuse, and close on a physical image rather than a resolving speech. No author's note, glossary, or list of sources — the magazine will run a sidebar for that.
