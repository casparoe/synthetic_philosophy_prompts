Our company's sustainability reading group just finished Jared Diamond's Collapse and the subtitle — "how societies choose to fail or succeed" — has become a catchphrase around here. Two people used it in opposite directions in the same meeting. One said it's basically hopeful: collapse is a choice, so we can choose otherwise. The other used it to assign blame, arguing that when a society went under, its leaders picked that outcome, full stop — which he then applied to a slide about our own supply chain risk.

I write incident postmortems for a living and the framing bugs me. No system I've ever debugged chose anything, and we're trained specifically not to hunt for the one bad decision. Also Diamond himself spends time on why societies don't perceive a problem in the first place, which seems to sit weirdly next to "choose".

Can you split "societies choose to fail" into the distinct precise claims it could be making — from whatever is trivially true of any outcome up to the strong version where a whole society counts as an agent with beliefs and preferences? For each one: what would have to be true, does it survive the actual archaeology (Tainter's diminishing-returns-on-complexity line, the Rapa Nui revision), and does it support either of my colleagues' uses. A plain list I can paste into the group chat is fine, and tell me which reading if any is the defensible one.
