For two terms I kept a running list of my intuitions about a family of neural-replacement scenarios, originally to bring to a reading group on personal identity that dissolved before we ever got to them, and the list has since acquired a life of its own. The standing assumption is a clinic that can substitute synthetic modules for individual neurons, each module functionally isomorphic to the neuron it displaces — same input–output profile, same contribution to the overall functional organization of the system — that can perform these substitutions at any rate and with or without anaesthesia, and that can equally scan a whole brain at once and instantiate the resulting organization somewhere else. Here is what I say about the cases.

1. One module a week for a year, the patient awake and going about his business: he survives, and he is the same person throughout; there is no moment at which a fact about death could arise.
2. The identical total replacement compressed into ninety seconds under general anaesthesia, the body oxygenated the whole time: he survives, and the rate makes no difference at all.
3. The same ninety-second procedure, except that each neuron is dissolved as it is copied rather than merely bypassed, so that the tissue in the skull never exceeds its original mass: he survives.
4. A whole-brain scan, a duplicate instantiated in a new body in the next room, and the original destroyed in the course of the scan: this is death. The person in the chair dies, and the one next door is a numerically distinct though qualitatively identical individual.
5. Scan and instantiate with the original left intact and merely preserved in suspension; the duplicate lives forty years and the original is then revived: I want to say the revived one is me and that the duplicate is also me, and I refuse to say that either ceased to exist — while conceding that they cannot both be identical to me.
6. Each replacement module is isomorphic not to the neuron it displaces but to that neuron as it would have been a decade hence, producing an imperceptible drift each week and, at the end of the year, a man noticeably more reckless and more easily bored than the one who walked in: he survives, but something has been lost that cases 1 to 3 do not lose, and I cannot say what.
7. Modules that reproduce the functional profile exactly but at one-tenth speed, so the whole system runs slow relative to the world: survival is unimpaired, and I hold that nothing determinate can be said about how the world looks to him — even though in another context I cheerfully say that doubling the clock doubles the amount of experience he has.
8. Two patients who exchange neurons one at a time over a month, so that each ends up running half the other's original tissue: each survives, and yet I also feel that at the midpoint there is a real sense in which there is only one person, spread across two skulls.
9. A wholly synthetic patient who has run on modules for years is gradually re-fitted with biological neurons grown from the tissue archived at his original scan, until he is once again entirely organic: he survives — though this looks like case 4 run backwards, and I called case 4 death.
10. Nightly snapshots are kept; a stroke erases the last decade; the ten-year-old snapshot is restored into fresh substrate: the restored person is me, minus ten years, and is more me than any stranger would be — but I would still rather wake in my own damaged body than be restored.

The clearest tension I can see is between 3 and 4: in both the original matter is destroyed, in both the resulting functional organization is what the scan or the interleaved copying specifies, and the only difference I can name is geometric — in 3 the destruction is spread over ninety seconds and interleaved with construction, in 4 it happens at once and the construction happens elsewhere. I find it very hard to credit interleaving with that much metaphysical work. My own tentative principle, which I will call the unbroken-realizer view, is that I persist just in case there is at every moment exactly one functioning realizer of my functional organization whose operation is causally continuous with my own prior operation, without interruption and without rival. It seems to deliver 1, 2, 3, 6, 7 and 9, and to rule 4 out as death; but as far as I can tell it misfires on 5, on 8, and especially on 10, where it should make the damaged body me and the restored snapshot nobody. Is there a principle, or a short competing set, that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? Please state each candidate precisely and clause by clause, saying which of my cases motivates each clause and what the clause does to the cases it was not written for; tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and why the ones it saves are worth the complexity; give me its verdicts on two or three scenarios I have not listed that probe its weak points; and tell me where that leaves case 3, the one I keep going back and forth on — whether interleaved destruction really is compatible with survival, or whether I have simply been squeamish about corpses in one direction and not the other.
