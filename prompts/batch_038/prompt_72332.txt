Ok so, context before the ask, because it explains why I'm nervous about this one. Before I started my PhD I spent two years as a field researcher on a campaign to get a community to abandon a marriage practice that basically everyone involved told me, privately and off the record, that they hated. My program director's entire theory of change was that it was a messaging problem — better posters, better framing, more "counter-narratives," more testimonials from ex-practitioners. I came to philosophy mostly to prove him wrong, and my dissertation is now about why the two standard options in the moral-revolutions literature both leave something out. Next Thursday I give the main talk at a workshop jointly run by our history department and our philosophy department, and the chapter I'm presenting is the one I care about most.

The chapter, roughly. The usual lineup is: (1) the arguments-did-it story — duelling, the Atlantic slave trade, footbinding all fell once good moral reasoning against them got widely enough disseminated, and the timing tracks the spread of pamphlets, novels, and sermons; and (2) the Appiah-style honor story — the practices fell when the honor scaffolding around them got rewired, so that refusing to participate stopped being a career-ending humiliation and started being a mark of standing. My claim is that these two share a hidden premise: that a reason against the practice, once it's been articulated, is *available* to the people it's addressed to, and that the only thing that varies is whether they accept it or what their honor code does with it. I want to argue for a third failure mode, which I'm calling audibility failure — the reason is on the books, it's even privately endorsed by lots of the people who keep doing the thing, but it cannot be deployed in public as a reason without the person deploying it eating a cost, and those costs are distributed very unevenly across the network. My three steps: (a) in all three cases the anti-practice arguments are on the record decades or sometimes a century before the practice actually moves, so "the argument arrived" can't be the explanans; (b) what changes at the moment of the revolution is not the argument's presence or its acceptance but who can say it out loud without being destroyed by it — the officer who wouldn't fight lost his regiment and his marriage prospects, the family that stopped binding feet lost the marriage market, and those costs fell on identifiable individuals, not on the group; (c) therefore the right unit of analysis is the distribution of defection costs, not belief states and not honor codes, and this makes a specific archival prediction — lots of private endorsement with almost no public uptake, followed by a short period where a handful of unusually cost-absorbing actors go public in a way that lowers the price of defection for everybody downstream of them.

The room is maybe eighteen people and it is not a philosophy room. Mostly British social and intellectual historians working the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — they will care about sources, periodization, and whether I'm reading a print culture as if it were a belief survey. Two historical sociologists who do event-history and quantitative work and who will think I'm being impressionistic about "networks" without having operationalized anything. On the philosophy side there's the organizer, who is a committed moral realist and thinks the best explanation of these changes is convergence on moral truth, and one grad student who will read every sentence I say through Foucault and discipline. Nobody has seen the draft except my advisor.
