I edit the fiction column of a small philosophy magazine that comes out four times a year, and this quarter's theme is the epistemology of sleep. We have run one too many stories about nested dreams and one too many about a protagonist who wakes to discover that the dream was the real life, so I would like a piece that instead gets underneath a dispute that is still genuinely live: whether there is anything it is like to be asleep at all, or whether what we call a dream is a narrative manufactured at or after the moment of waking. The argument runs from Norman Malcolm's claim that the past-tense dream report is not the past tense of any present-tense experience report, and that there is accordingly no such thing as experiencing a dream, through Dennett's paper Are Dreams Experiences? and its suggestion that the dream is a tale which inserts itself retrospectively into a memory system that kept no records while the sleeper slept, to the opposing camp that treats dream reports as genuine, if badly contaminated, first-person data about an actual conscious state. The standard counterexample wheeled in against Malcolm and Dennett is the lucid-dreaming literature, where a sleeper signals from inside the dream with pre-arranged eye movements that are then verified against the polygraph trace. I want the story to carry all of that tension without a single character ever articulating it.

Here is the seed I would like you to work from, though you may adjust the details. Set it in a sleep laboratory attached to a district general hospital in the last years before digital recording: paper rolls, grease pencil, hand-typed night sheets, a technician's stool beside the bed. The narrator is a technician, not a scientist and not a philosopher — someone whose entire job is transcribing, filing, and reconciling. Give the lab an invented piece of kit with its own paperwork: a bedside unit which, at the moment the technician presses a button on hearing the newly woken subject speak, prints a timestamped ninety-second digest of EEG, EOG and submental EMG onto the same sheet as the dictated report, so that every dream file carries both a transcript and a physiological ledger that can be checked against it. Let two case files drive the plot. In the first, a man dictates eleven minutes of long, vivid, spatially continuous dream narrative, and the ledger shows him quietly awake for the whole of it — alpha rhythm present, no REM period anywhere near the report. In the second, a woman is woken from a florid REM period and answers the lab's standard awakening question fluently with a denial: nothing at all, not even a blank; eleven minutes later, from the same unbroken REM period, she is woken again and produces a detailed account whose opening events include being woken once already and answering that there had been nothing. Neither file can be resolved from inside the record, and the closing beat should be the technician's pen held over the three boxes in the study's data column — experience occurred, no experience occurred, not determinable — with no verdict given.

Craft requirements: between 1,400 and 2,000 words, in a flat procedural register, so that the unease comes from paperwork, clock times, and the impossibility of settling the question, rather than from lush oneiric prose. Describe at most one dream in any detail, and describe it the way a transcript reads. No scene in which two characters debate the philosophy, no visiting professor, no naming Malcolm or Dennett anywhere in the story, no nested dreams, no wake-up-twist ending, no amnesia device. Include at least two points at which the technician does something a real lab does — scoring stages from the roll, running the awakening protocol, applying the cutoff for how long after waking a report still counts as a dream report — and get those right rather than gesturing at them. The dispute should be fully legible to a reader who knows it and the story should still land for one who does not. After the story, add a note of no more than four hundred words addressed to me as editor rather than to readers, in which you separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: what the polysomnographic and awakening-yield evidence licenses as consensus (that report rates differ sharply between REM and non-REM awakenings, with the latter shorter and more thought-like; that recall is unreliable and easily contaminated by suggestion and retelling; that verified in-dream signalling exists) as against what remains philosophically disputed (whether anything is experienced during non-lucid sleep at all; whether Malcolm's argument is a point about the grammar of dream reports or a substantive empirical claim; whether retrospective confabulation can do the work Dennett wants of it in the face of that signalling evidence). Flag anywhere the story leans on one side of a contested question as though it were agreed, and list what you have invented for the sake of the plot — the device, any protocol, any finding — since I would much rather know what is fabricated than have it smuggled in as fact. Do not append a reading list or citations, and do not put anything in quotation marks as though it were quoted from a real text.
