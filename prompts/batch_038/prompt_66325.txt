Some context. I'm a philosophy of history prof. Before I crossed over I trained as an economist and spent a few years doing macro modelling, so I'm genuinely fluent — not tourist-fluent — in the vocabulary of non-stationary time series: unit roots and drift, trend-stationarity versus difference-stationarity, permanent versus transitory shocks, the Beveridge–Nelson decomposition, structural breaks, hysteresis and path dependence, spurious regression, cointegration, Granger causality, ergodicity, the whole apparatus. I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the arc-of-history question — the slogan that goes back to the abolitionist pulpit in the 1850s and was picked up again in the civil rights movement a century later — and I want to test the philosophical versions of the thesis against the vocabulary I already think in, partly to see whether my old discipline is more honest than I remember.

Here's what I want translated. Not the pop version, but the four claims serious defenders actually make:

1. History has a direction: progress is a real long-run tendency, not merely the pattern our short and unrepresentative sample happens to display.
2. Regressions don't stick: the collapses into barbarism and the reversals of emancipation are real but transitory — the level comes back to the path.
3. Something is doing the bending: on the providential and Hegelian versions the tendency has a source and an end, whether that's Reason working through human passions or a moral governer of history.
4. The claim is practical as much as descriptive: holding it licenses hope and sustained effort for agents who are inside the process and can't see its whole trajectory.

Deliverable, in this order:

- For each of the four, the best honest translation into the econometric vocabulary — i.e. what the claim would have to be, precisely, to be a claim about a stochastic process at all.
- A verdict on each: translates cleanly / translates only with distortion (and which distortion) / no counterpart whatsoever.
- Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Take everything that didn't survive, group it, and for each group tell me whether it's a defect of the target vocabulary (it can't express something that's perfectly well-formed and apt for truth or falsity in the original) or evidence that the original claim was less clear than it looked — meaning the failure to translate is itself doing philosophical work, exposing a bit of the thesis that was never a claim. Be decisive about which is which; don't hand me a load of "it depends".

Two rules of the game. First, keep the translation honest and don't smuggle in my framework's assumptions while calling it translation.
