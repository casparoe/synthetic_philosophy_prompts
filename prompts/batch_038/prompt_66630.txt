I've been building analogy problems in a tiny made-up microdomain to stress-test a Copycat-style model I wrote myself (slipnet, workspace, codelets, temperature — the usual machinery, nothing fancy), and I've hit a case where I can't tell if I've found something interesting or just built a broken problem. I want your actual credence with the decomposition, not a shrug.

The domain: strings over a four-letter alphabet, a b c d, in that order. That's the entire stipulation. I deliberately never told the system wether it wraps around from d back to a, and the only relations available are successor/predecessor, position (leftmost/rightmost), and repeated-letter groups.

The problem: ab → bc, and now cd → ?

Two readings of the observed change are both live: replace the rightmost letter with its successor, or shift the whole string up one letter. On ab they coincide, which is exactly why I picked it. On cd they don't, and both of them also run into the fact that d has no stated successor. So you get a pile of candidate answers depending on which reading carries over and how the collision gets resolved: ca (rightmost-successor, alphabet wraps), cc (rightmost, successor slips to predecessor), da (shift-all, wraps), dd (shift-all, blocked letter freezes), bc (shift-all, direction flips), cd (rightmost, blocked letter freezes — i.e. no change at all).

I ran cd 2000 times. Distribution: ca 38%, cc 24%, da 19%, dd 8%, bc 5%, cd 3%, rest unparseable junk. Temperature behaves how you'd expect: pinned low it sharpens toward ca, cranked high it flattens out.

So: what's your credence that ca is the *correct* answer to this analogy — not the modal answer, correct? Decompose it for me. I'd expect terms like the chance the rightmost-successor reading is the one that should carry over, the chance the alphabet ought to be read as cyclic rather than as a line with a hard stop at d, the chance that a collision like this admits of a best resolution at all rather than just a distribution, and anything else you think belongs in there. A number and one line of justification per term, then how they combine into the headline figure.

Two more things. My collaborator puts this at 0.2 and thinks the whole exercise is confused, on the grounds that convergence in a stochastic system is no evidence of correctness; I'm at 0.65. Which of your terms is that gap most likely living in? And what would actually move your number — a different target string, an explicit stipulation about wrapping, the shape of the distribution changing, or some argument I havn't thought of?
