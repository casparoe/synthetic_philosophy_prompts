I am giving the keynote at a closed workshop on democratic innovation in about six weeks, and what I need is a realistic question-and-answer preparation sheet, not encouragement about the idea.

The audience will be roughly forty people. Most are empirical political scientists who study legislatures, elections, and representation, and several have personally designed, staffed, or evaluated citizens' assemblies. Two or three are deliberative democrats favorable to sortition but committed to the standard advisory model, in which a minipublic recommends and an elected body decides. One is a constitutional scholar whose work concerns counter-majoritarian institutions. One is a former legislative counsel who drafted statutes for a living. One is a practitioner at a consultancy that sells assembly design to governments. Nobody in the room is a lottocrat. The median participant holds that randomly selected bodies are a legitimate supplement to elections and a dangerous substitute for them.

The position I will defend is a design I call the negative chamber.

1. Composition. Four hundred citizens drawn by stratified lot from the electoral roll, serving staggered twenty-four-month terms in four annual cohorts of one hundred, so a quarter of the body turns over each year. No reappointment, ever.
2. Powers, exclusively negative. The chamber may annul any statute, regulation, budget line, or executive order within ninety days of enactment, on a two-thirds vote; it may compel a referendum; it may subpoena administrators and require an annual report on the state of the administration. It may not propose, draft, or amend anything, and it controls no money.
3. The argument. The pathology of career politics is not chiefly poor judgment but a structural incentive to add to the stock of law and to protect incumbency. Sortition removes reelection from the motivational set, which is precisely what term limits cannot do, since term limits churn who holds office while leaving the incentive intact. I further argue that positive legislative power for amateurs either demands deliberative resources nobody will fund or produces dependence on a professional secretariat, which reinstates the expertise capture the reform was meant to escape. Veto power is comparatively knowledge-light: judging whether a rule achieves its stated purpose and who bears its costs is a lighter epistemic demand than constructing a better rule. And a body that can only subtract generates far less rent to capture than one that can confer benefits.
4. Recruitment. Service is compulsory as in jury duty, with narrow incapacity exemptions, paid at the regional median wage plus childcare and travel, with statutory job protection. Non-response is handled not by chasing quotas but by successive draws from widening strata with individual-level post-stratification weighting.
5. Override. Any annulment is subject to a confirmatory referendum if the legislature re-enacts the measure by three-fifths.

I will concede on a slide that commitments 3 and 5 pull against each other, since an override that is cheap to trigger makes the veto cheap to ignore.

In the talk I will also invoke three precedents: the Irish Citizens' Assembly convened in 2016, ninety-nine randomly selected citizens plus a judicial chair, which examined the constitutional provision on abortion and produced recommendations the government was obliged to answer in parliament; Oregon's initiative review, permanent since 2010, in which panels of about two dozen randomly selected citizens produce a one-page statement for the voters' pamphlet; and the 2019 Ostbelgien arrangement, in which a standing council of twenty-four people drawn from previous assemblies sets the agenda for temporary ones. I will close with the Athenian council of five hundred: one-year terms, no member serving more than twice in a lifetime or more than once a decade, and a presiding officer replaced by lot every day. I mention these so that you can attack my use of the precedents as readily as the design.

What I want from you is a prep sheet. Specifically:

- The eight to ten questions I will actually get in this room, ordered by expected damage, that is, probability multiplied by severity, rather than by topic. Make the ordering explicit and tell me where the drop-off falls between merely irritating and genuinely destructive.
- Each question phrased the way this audience would phrase it, in the vocabulary of legislative studies, representation theory, and constitutional design, not in the vocabulary of reform-movement panels. Where a question embeds a technical premise about weighting, veto-player counting, principal-agent accountability, or status quo bias, state the premise and say whether I can grant it, dispute it, or must sidestep it.
- For each, a reply I can deliver aloud in about two minutes, what I should concede in my first sentence, and a frank note on where the reply is soft.
- Separate the objections that would be fatal to this design as specified from the objections to sortition in general, which I can absorb because the design already grants the point. Tell me which of my five commitments is doing the absorbing in each case.
- Name the single load-bearing empirical assumption, the one that if false collapses the negative-powers argument rather than merely complicating it, and tell me what I should say when someone asks how I know it.
- Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the answer I would have to give if it comes.
- Finally: if the room forces a trade, which of the five commitments should I surrender to save the others, and which must I never surrender?

Please give me no bibliography, no named studies, no named researchers, and nothing I would have to verify afterwards. Argue at the level of mechanisms, incentives, and institutional design, because I have to answer live with no notes in front of me.
