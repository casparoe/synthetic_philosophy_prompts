I teach a 12-person upper-level seminar on the philosophy of perception — once a week, 75 minutes, mixed group: about half have had a psych or neuroscience course, the rest are straight philosophy. We're three weeks into a unit on whether perception is cognitively penetrable and I've hit a wall. They've read the standard material, they can recite the encapsulation-vs-penetration dialectic on demand, and they've all been staring at ambiguous figures and that dress photo since they were teenagers. So whenever I try to demo anything, roughly half the room immediately says the exact thing I want them to have to discover for themselves: "fine, but that only shows the expectation changed what people *said* afterwards, not what they *saw*." Which is right, and which is the whole crux, and I'd rather they land on it by tripping over it in something they did than by hearing me assert it. So I want an activity where the group generates its own data — a within-participants taste-or-judgment thing with a counterballanced order assigned across the room so that when we tally the numbers on the whiteboard, the pooled average looks flat or even backwards while the subgroup pattern is obvious, and they have to explain that discrepancy to themselves before anyone is allowed to claim the phenomenon happened.

Give me the whole runnable package: rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and materials (assume two kinds of juice or wine, index cards, markers, a whiteboard, no working projector — nothing electronic, they record everything by hand). One student doesn't drink alcohol and one has a dairy issue, so build them in as real roles with real data-generating jobs, not as an afterthought or as observers. I also want a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto specific philosophical moves — including the epistemic payoff, i.e. what happens to the evidential weight of experience if experience is partly built out of what you already believe, and why that would matter for anyone leaning on how-things-look as independent support for a view. Please don't let me announce the target distinction at the start; ideally they hit it mid-activity and argue about it for ten minutes before I name anything.

And be straight with me about the failure modes. What predictably goes wrong the first time you run something like this — I'm already worrying about order effects bleeding into each other, one loud kid guessing the hypothesis aloud in minute three and contaminating the room, and the non-tasters slowly realizing they're furniture — and what do I actually say or do in the moment for each one. Most important: tell me which feature of this debate the activity simply cannot reproduce, the part I'm going to have to supply myself afterwards, and don't soften it. I'd much rather the thing over-claim nothing than be exciting. Open your plan with one sentence on what the whole exercise is for, and end the whole response with a line beginning "Verdict:" naming, in six words or fewer, the strongest claim this activity can actually license about penetration.
