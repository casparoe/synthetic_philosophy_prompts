I sit on a state commission that permits new additives for municipal water treatment, and Thursday we vote on a compound that's excellent at binding a known carcinogen — cheap, immediate, measurable benefit. The trouble: there's no assay for what we suspect it might do downstream, and if the suspicion is right the effect wouldn't show up in monitoring data for something like 25–30 years, by which point it'd be in every watershed in the region with no way to pull it back. Our statutes precaution clause cuts both ways and the panel is split — half read it as putting the burden on the applicant to demonstrate safety before a permit issues, half read it as only licensing measures that are proportionate and cost-effective, which they say a flat denial isn't. The usual adaptive-management answer (permit, monitor, revoke if something turns up) is no use here, precisely because there'd be nothing to see for three decades. And if we deny, the applicant says it'll supply the next state over, whose rules are looser, so the carcinogen still gets removed and the compound still gets released, just under worse oversight.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote: a recommendation, a decision rule the panel could actually reuse on the next compound that comes through, and an honest account of the strongest case for approval. Skip the overview of what the precautionary principle is, assume I've read it — I want the reasoning about this case. Last line should start "Recommendation:" followed by approve, approve-with-conditions, or deny.
