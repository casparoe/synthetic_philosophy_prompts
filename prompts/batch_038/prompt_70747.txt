I lead the evaluation function at a software company that licenses decision-support tools to two public-sector clients: a benefits agency that uses ours to triage claims and draft eligibility determinations, and a regional health authority that uses ours to prioritise referrals. Neither client will authorise a release for unsupervised operation until my team has filed a written finding on a question they phrase badly and we cannot avoid — whether the system understands what it is doing. We assess roughly twenty release candidates a year. Each assessment gets one assessor-day and is carried out by two people, one of whom has no technical background beyond an internal certification course; the two must be able to reach the same verdict on the same candidate without conferring. I sign the finding, the client's release board countersigns, and in a procurement challenge the finding is discoverable, so it may not overclaim. I have been told more than once by a client's lawyers that our findings read as though they were verdicts on whether the tool is conscious. They are not, and the rebuilt procedure should make that misreading impossible.

I want to rebuild the procedure around the idea of competence without comprehension: the thought that goes with Darwin's inversion of reasoning, in which well-adapted design arises with no designer and no foresight anywhere in the process, and with Turing's inversion, in which the formal steps of a calculation are executed correctly by something that need not grasp what it is doing; and that, crucially, comprehension is not an all-or-nothing property that switches on once the right competence is in place, but a layered and graded capacity assembled out of further competences, so that there are intermediate stations between blind reliable performance and full understanding. I want assessors to be able to run that thought without ever having met it.

Our current standard uses a three-rung ladder that I drafted, and I suspect it is wrong in a way I cannot name:

- Rung 1, operational competence. On the deployed task distribution, the outputs are ones a domain expert would accept, at or above the agreed accuracy threshold.
- Rung 2, interrogable comprehension. Asked to justify a particular output, the system produces a justification a domain expert would accept as a reason for that output, not merely as a plausible-sounding sentence.
- Rung 3, portable comprehension. Those justifications survive perturbation — paraphrase of the question, reordering of the case file, insertion of an irrelevant detail, restatement of the same case as a counterfactual or as a comparison with a near neighbour — without the justification or the decision changing in ways that betray that the justification was never doing any work.

Assess the ladder before you build on it. Does it preserve the distinction between competence and comprehension, or does it quietly redefine comprehension as having been selected on more data? Does it treat comprehension as the property of one component when the underlying view suggests it can be distributed across a system and its scaffolding? Does Rung 2 smuggle in the assumption that a subject which cannot articulate reasons lacks them? Add, merge, delete, or reword rungs as you judge necessary, and state for each change which distinction you were protecting and what you gave up.

Then deliver the following.

1. A one-page plain-language brief for assessors with no philosophy, stating what the idea claims and what it does not claim: it does not establish that the system lacks understanding, and it does not license inferring understanding from fluency either. Identify the single sentence in that brief most likely to be misread by a client's lawyer or by a journalist, and give your rewrite of it.

2. The procedure itself as a numbered sequence to be run in order, fitting on one printed sheet with checkboxes. For each step: the question to ask, what evidence counts as a sufficient answer, how that evidence is obtained within an assessor-day, the permitted verdicts, and whether the step is diagnostic (a matter of fact the assessors can settle) or a threshold judgment (a matter of risk appetite that belongs to the board and must be escalated rather than absorbed). Where the sequence branches, give the branch conditions explicitly. Where the sequence stops at a finding of no, say what the assessor writes in the finding.

3. A control condition: the same battery of interrogation items run on a competent human employee performing the same task. Justify including it, and address the obvious objection to it — that a criterion no human could pass is not measuring comprehension but merely detecting that the subject is a machine. State how much of the procedure survives if the human fails the same items, and what the finding should then say.

4. A rule for the scaffold. When the component that produces a justification is not the component that produced the decision, or when the understanding in play is supplied by documentation, a specification, and a reviewing human rather than by the model, does the finding attach to the model or to the assembled system? Say what must be true of the scaffold for it to inherit the credit, and what must be true for it not to.

5. Three worked runs of the finished procedure, each on a candidate described concretely enough that a reader can see the rungs being applied and can check your verdicts against your own steps:
   (a) a triage model at threshold accuracy whose justifications are individually impeccable, but which offers a different justification when the same case is re-presented with one irrelevant detail altered, while its decision is unchanged;
   (b) a legacy system of explicit rules and templates, auditable line by line, that passes every interrogation item and fails a class of cases a first-year caseworker handles easily;
   (c) a composite release in which a fast classifier makes the call and a separate language component is asked, afterwards, to explain it.
   At least one of these must yield a finding I will not like. Say which one you expect that to be, and commit to a verdict rather than presenting a menu of defensible readings; the board will ask whether the tool comprehends and I need an answer.

6. A closing section on limits, stating: what the procedure cannot establish — in particular, whether you accept or reject the framing that it can only license or withhold a warrant to treat the system's outputs as reasons, never settle whether there is comprehension in there; the failure modes most likely in our setting and which direction they err; what classes of system would pass the procedure while being, on the philosophical account, pure competence, and what classes would fail it while genuinely comprehending; and where the Darwinian and the Turingian inversion matter differently to an assessor — or, if you judge that the distinction between them buys the assessor nothing at all, say so and drop it rather than keeping it as decoration.

No citations, no bibliography, and no names of books, papers, or thinkers other than the two already mentioned above: my assessors will not look them up and I will not be able to check them. Everything must be self-contained and runnable from the sheet. Prefer the assessor's vocabulary to the philosopher's, and where a philosophical term is unavoidable, gloss it in the same sentence in which it appears.
