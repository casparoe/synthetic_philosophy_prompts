I'm a high school junior doing an independent study on Nietzsche's ideas about health, and I want to test those ideas against something he could never have encountered. In his late autobiographical writing he devotes a surprising amount of space to food, digestion, climate, and daily regimen, and he seems to treat getting the body's management right as prior to any question of morality or spirit; he also revised his own diet constantly and self-medicated heavily for pain and insomnia. Suppose he were alive today and learned about GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide, which many people take to lose weight by chemically reducing appetite rather than by exercising any restraint of their own. What would his verdict be? Please separate at least two cases: a patient prescribed the drug for obesity or diabetes, and a person of ordinary weight using it to get leaner. I'm most interested in whether, on his view, the objection would be to the drug itself or to the fact that it removes the appetite instead of training it, and whether his own lifelong resort to remedies gives him grounds to approve of it that a simple "shortcuts are decadent" reading would miss. Where his actual commitments underdetermine an answer, please say so rather than supplying a position he never had, and write the response in American English.
