My coworker is way more into this stuff than me and he wrote up a paragraph about a philosopher named Samuel Scheffler and his idea about why human extinction would be bad even apart from all the deaths it involves. I have zero academic background in any of this, I just turn it over in my head at night, so I honestly can't tell whether he's explaining the thing well or steamrolling it. Here's what he wrote:

"Scheffler argues that deep down we all believe in an afterlife, meaning we assume other people will still exist after we die, and that this assumption does more work in our lives than our own survival does. His test: imagine you learn the earth will be destroyed by an asteroid exactly thirty days after you die. Most people would be devastated, which proves that basically everything we care about — our work, our relationships, having kids — only matters because we assume humanity carries on. So he's shown that our real fear of death is just fear of the human race ending, and that we're alot more selfish than we admit, since the only reason we care about the future is that we want to be remembered in it. That's also why extinction is worse than a billion people dying today, and why we should be spending everything on stopping it."

Two things bug me and I can't tell if they're real problems or if I'm missing something. First, the direction feels backwards — I'd have thought his point was that being able to accept my own death depends on trusting that other people go on, not that my fear of dying is secretly a fear of all of us going. Second, when I picture the asteroid I notice at least three seperate reactions tangled up in me: the everything-will-be-pointless feeling, sadness for all the people who'd never get born, and plain not-wanting-to-die. His paragraph treats those as one thing. Does the argument need all three, or could it run on just one?
