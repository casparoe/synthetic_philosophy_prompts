Going through Einstein's 1949 Monthly Review essay on socialism with a small reading group I run out of my flat, and I'm properly stuck on one stretch of it. Here's the bit (with the sentences just before it, for context):

"For example, most of the major states of history owed their existence to conquest. The conquering peoples established themselves, legally and economically, as the privileged class of the conquered country. They seized for themselves a monopoly of the land ownership and appointed a priesthood from among their own ranks. The priests, in control of education, made the class division of society into a permanent institution and created a system of values by which the people were thenceforth, to a large extent unconsciously, guided in their social behavior.

But historic tradition is, so to speak, of yesterday; nowhere have we really overcome what Thorstein Veblen called "the predatory phase" of human development. The observable economic facts belong to that phase and even such laws as we can derive from them are not applicable to other phases. Since the real purpose of socialism is precisely to overcome and advance beyond the predatory phase of human development, economic science in its present state can throw little light on the socialist society of the future."

A few things I'd like help unpicking, roughly in order of how much they've been bugging me:

1. He's explicitly borrowing the phrase from Veblen. How much of Veblen goes across with it? As far as I understand it Veblen's stages are about ingrained habits of thought carried over from earlier conditions rather than a march of history with a next stop, and he's pretty sceptical that any stage gets cleanly superseded. If Einstein is loading that phrase with a directional, almost teleological sense — predatory phase now, socialism as the phase beyond — is he reading Veblen against the grain? And what breaks if he is?

2. There seem to be two different stories of how values get locked in here, sitting side by side: conquerors plus a priesthood controlling education, versus a phase of human development we've all simply never got out of. One is a story about particular groups seizing and entrenching power, the other about a general condition. Does he need both, or is the second doing quiet work that the first can't — namely explaining why even the oppressed go along with it?

3. The claim that observable economic facts all belong to the predatory phase, so laws derived from them don't apply to other phases. Is that just a point about the limits of induction, or is it something stronger — and doesn't it cut against him elsewhere in the essay, where he's quite confident about what central planning would and wouldn't deliver, including his warning later on that a planned economy is not automatically socialism and can come with the complete enslavement of the individual? If no law travels between phases, what's the standing of that warning?

4. Related: if we're still inside the predatory phase, what stops the post-capitalist order being predation with a planning committee attached? He says somewhere that ends get conceived by individuals with lofty ethical ideals and then taken up by others — which feels like a very thin mechanism to hang a phase transition on. Is that a weakness, or is it deliberately modest?

Don't feel you have to defend him. And please be straight with me about how confident you are in each of these readings — I'd especially like you to single out the one point you're least sure about, since I'll be the one having to answer for it on Tuesday.
