I'm a senior in a school philosophy seminar, and our term-long argument about whether patriotism is a virtue has gone nowhere, which puzzles me, since everyone has read the same handful of pieces. One group defends it in MacIntyre's way: loyalty to one's own community is not a bias that impartial morality corrects but the ground on which moral agency stands, and a patriot can criticize the country precisely because of that loyalty. The other group says patriotism is at best a dangerous indulgence and at worst epistemically corrupt, in the spirit of Keller's charge that the patriot believes their own country is good because it is theirs — a belief formed to protect a commitment rather than to track anything. Nobody has budged.

My hypothesis is that the two sides aren't running the same test at all: one is asking whether patriotism is a settled disposition that perfects an agent inside a tradition, the other is asking whether the beliefs patriotism generates are formed in a way we would accept from anyone else. If that's right, the impasse may be a matter of two different questions sharing the word 'virtue', and possibly also of an equivocation on what patriotism is loyalty to — a particular people and history, or a set of principles any nation could adopt.

Is that the real fault line, or is there a substantive moral disagreement that survives the diagnosis — or a hidden empirical question about what patriotic identification actually does to people, which both sides gesture at and neither treats as decisive? Give me the diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, tell me what would count as progress (a distinction, a change of question, some evidence) or argue that none should be expected, and say what would change your mind: which consideration would push you to a different diagnosis, and where your own reasoning is likeliest to break down.
