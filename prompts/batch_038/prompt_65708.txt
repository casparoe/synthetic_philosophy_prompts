I am working slowly through the fifteenth chapter of Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* in Aylmer Maude's 1898 English translation — the chapter in which he separates genuine art from its counterfeits — and I have run into a knot I cannot undo by myself. The stretch that has me stopped runs as follows:

> Art, in our society, has been so perverted that not only has bad art come to be considered good, but even the very perception of what art really is has been lost. In order to be able to speak about the art of our society, it is, therefore, first of all necessary to distinguish art from counterfeit art.
>
> There is one indubitable indication distinguishing real art from its counterfeit, namely, the infectiousness of art. If a man, without exercising effort and without altering his standpoint, on reading, hearing, or seeing another man's work, experiences a mental condition which unites him with that man and with other people who also partake of that work of art, then the object evoking that condition is a work of art. And however poetical, realistic, effectful, or interesting a work may be, it is not a work of art if it does not evoke that feeling (quite distinct from all other feelings) of joy, and of spiritual union with another (the author) and with others (those who are also infected by it).
>
> It is true that this indication is an internal one, and that there are people who have forgotten what the action of real art is, who expect something else from art (in our society the great majority are in this state), and that therefore such people may mistake for this aesthetic feeling the feeling of divertisement and a certain excitement which they receive from counterfeits of art. But though it is impossible to undeceive these people, just as it is impossible to convince a man suffering from "Daltonism" that green is not red, yet, for all that, this indication remains perfectly definite to those whose feeling for art is neither perverted nor atrophied, and it clearly distinguishes the feeling produced by art from all other feelings.
>
> The chief peculiarity of this feeling is that the receiver of a true artistic impression is so united to the artist that he feels as if the work were his own and not someone else's—as if what it expresses were just what he had long been wishing to express. A real work of art destroys, in the consciousness of the receiver, the separation between himself and the artist, nor that alone, but also between himself and all whose minds receive this work of art. In this freeing of our personality from its separation and isolation, in this uniting of it with others, lies the chief characteristic and the great attractive force of art.
>
> If a man is infected by the author's condition of soul, if he feels this emotion and this union with others, then the object which has effected this is art; but if there be no such infection, if there be not this union with the author and with others who are moved by the same work—then it is not art. And not only is infection a sure sign of art, but the degree of infectiousness is also the sole measure of excellence in art.
>
> The stronger the infection the better is the art, as art, speaking now apart from its subject-matter, i.e. not considering the quality of the feelings it transmits.
>
> And the degree of the infectiousness of art depends on three conditions:—
> (1) On the greater or lesser individuality of the feeling transmitted; (2) on the greater or lesser clearness with which the feeling is transmitted; (3) on the sincerity of the artist, i.e. on the greater or lesser force with which the artist himself feels the emotion he transmits.
>
> The more individual the feeling transmitted the more strongly does it act on the receiver; the more individual the state of soul into which he is transferred the more pleasure does the receiver obtain, and therefore the more readily and strongly does he join in it.
>
> The clearness of expression assists infection, because the receiver, who mingles in consciousness with the author, is the better satisfied the more clearly the feeling is transmitted, which, as it seems to him, he has long known and felt, and for which he has only now found expression.
>
> But most of all is the degree of infectiousness of art increased by the degree of sincerity in the artist. As soon as the spectator, hearer, or reader feels that the artist is infected by his own production, and writes, sings, or plays for himself and not merely to act on others, this mental condition of the artist infects the receiver; and, contrariwise, as soon as the spectator, reader, or hearer feels that the author is not writing, singing, or playing for his own satisfaction—does not himself feel what he wishes to express—but is doing it for him, the receiver, a resistance immediately springs up, and the most individual and the newest feelings and the cleverest technique not only fail to produce any infection but actually repel.
>
> I have mentioned three conditions of contagiousness in art, but they may all be summed up into one, the last, sincerity, i.e. that the artist should be impelled by an inner need to express his feeling. That condition includes the first; for if the artist is sincere he will express the feeling as he experienced it. And as each man is different from everyone else, his feeling will be individual for everyone else; and the more individual it is—the more the artist has drawn it from the depths of his nature—the more sympathetic and sincere will it be. And this same sincerity will impel the artist to find a clear expression of the feeling which he wishes to transmit.
>
> Therefore this third condition—sincerity—is the most important of the three. It is always complied with in peasant art, and this explains why such art always acts so powerfully; but it is a condition almost entirely absent from our upper-class art, which is continually produced by artists actuated by personal aims of covetousness or vanity.
>
> Such are the three conditions which divide art from its counterfeits, and which also decide the quality of every work of art apart from its subject-matter.
>
> The absence of any one of these conditions excludes a work from the category of art and relegates it to that of art's counterfeits.

Five difficulties have accumulated, and I cannot tell how far they are independent of one another.

**First, the reduction of the triad to one condition.** Tolstoy first says the degree of infectiousness depends on three conditions, then says they may all be summed up into the last one, sincerity, and offers a ground: sincerity "includes the first," because a sincere artist expresses the feeling as he experienced it, and since each man differs from every other, that feeling will be individual for everyone else. Two distinct moves seem compressed there — one about fidelity (expressing the feeling as experienced) and one about the metaphysics of feelings (each person's feeling being unique). Fidelity of expression does not yield individuality of content unless uniqueness is already assumed, and uniqueness of the person does not entail uniqueness of the feeling, since feelings are shared. The derivation of clearness looks weaker still: sincerity "will impel the artist to find a clear expression," which reads as a prediction about how sincere artists behave rather than as a conceptual containment. If neither of the other two conditions is genuinely contained in sincerity, is "most important" a claim about relative weight within an irreducible three-variable model rather than about reduction — and does anything in the wider argument turn on the stronger reading?

**Second, what "individuality" is supposed to name.** He glosses it as the individuality of the feeling transmitted, then says the receiver obtains more pleasure the more individual the state of soul into which he is transferred, and elsewhere ties individuality to the artist having drawn the feeling "from the depths of his nature." I can read this as novelty (a feeling no one has transmitted before), as particularity (the idiosyncratic inflection of a familiar feeling as this person had it on this occasion), or as depth of ownership. These come apart, and not harmlessly. A wholly novel feeling should be hard to recognize and therefore hard to catch, whereas the case he describes under clearness is the opposite: the receiver feels he "has long known and felt" the feeling and has only now found expression for it. If individuality means novelty, the first and second conditions pull against each other; if it means particularity, they collapse into one and the triad is really a dyad. Which reading does the chapter support, and does the answer bear on the first difficulty?

**Third, sincerity as both cause and evidence.** Sincerity is defined as the force with which the artist himself feels the emotion he transmits, and it is said to increase infectiousness more than anything else. Yet the receiver's access to it is described in the same currency: "As soon as the spectator, hearer, or reader feels that the artist is infected by his own production … this mental condition of the artist infects the receiver." So the sign of sincerity is itself an infection, while the criterion of art earlier in the chapter is likewise a feeling in the receiver. The theory then appears to say both that a work infects because the artist was sincere and that we know the artist was sincere because we are infected, which would leave sincerity explanatorily idle — a redescription of successful infection rather than a cause of it. Two escape routes occur to me and I cannot judge either: that feeling united with the author and feeling that the author means it are phenomenologically distinguishable, so sincerity is registered by a separate response that happens to produce the first; or that "depends on" throughout this passage is not doing causal work at all, and the three conditions are not mechanisms but aspects of what we attend to when we take a work to be genuine. Is either available to him, and what would each cost?

**Fourth, the Daltonism clause.** He concedes the indication is internal, and that those whose expectations of art have been spoiled cannot be undeceived, "just as it is impossible to convince a man suffering from 'Daltonism' that green is not red," while insisting the indication nonetheless "remains perfectly definite to those whose feeling for art is neither perverted nor atrophied." The analogy carries a great deal of weight and I suspect it cannot bear it. In the color case there are independent checks: normal observers agree, the defect has a physical explanation, and a dispute can in principle be settled by instrument rather than by the verdict of the qualified. In Tolstoy's case the only tribunal is the feeling of the qualified, and qualification is constituted by having the feeling. Is this simply an ordinary competence condition of a kind we accept elsewhere — a trained palate, a native speaker's grammaticality judgments, a clinician's ear — in which case the circle is benign because it is the standard circle of expertise? Or is it self-sealing, on the grounds that in each of those paradigm cases there is a way of being wrong that is independent of the expert's say-so and here there is not? I would particularly like to know whether the analogy is offered as an identity of epistemic structure or only as a similarity of incommunicability, since the two readings have very different consequences for him.

**Fifth, whether this account has room for bad art.** One measure is made to do two jobs: infection is a "sure sign of art," and the degree of infectiousness is the "sole measure of excellence in art," while the absence of any one condition "excludes a work from the category of art." Taken together these seem to leave only art, graded by strength of infection, and counterfeits, which are not art at all; the scale runs exclusively over successes, and the theory's only negative verdict is exclusion. Yet the chapter opens by complaining that bad art has come to be considered good, which suggests he thinks there is such a thing as bad art and not merely pseudo-art. Is the exclusionary reading right, and if so what becomes of that opening complaint? This matters because he twice brackets content — "speaking now apart from its subject-matter, i.e. not considering the quality of the feelings it transmits" — while introducing, elsewhere in the treatise, a second axis concerning the worthiness of the feeling transmitted. I want to understand how the bracket is meant to work: is subject-matter a separate criterion applied after the infectiousness scale is settled, or does it already modulate infectiousness, so that base feelings infect less readily and the two axes are not independent? If the latter, the "apart from subject-matter" clause is provisional in a way that quietly merges the two scales, and I cannot tell whether Tolstoy notices.

What I want is not a summary of his aesthetics — I have read the book — but a close reading of this passage that sorts my five difficulties into three piles: those that are genuine problems in the argument, those that are artifacts of my own inattentive reading or of the translation, and those that dissolve when the passage is set against what surrounds it in the chapter. Where a difficulty is genuine, say whether it can be repaired from inside his own framework, and what has to be given up if it cannot. Where you think I have misread a sentence, quote the sentence back at me and say what it is doing that I have missed. Please write your answer in British English.
