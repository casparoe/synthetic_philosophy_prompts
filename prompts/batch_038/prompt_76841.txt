For two summers now I've been volunteering on a little island restoration project — ground-nesting birds, rat traps, lots of clipboard work. The thing that got into my head has to do with the birds' alarm call. There used to be a snake on the island, the one pressure that call was presumably built around, and it's been gone for well over a century. But a couple of years back a different snake started turning up, hitching rides on the barge that brings in fuel and cement — a related species, not the same animal, and the birds go absolutely ballistic when they see one. They also go ballistic at a length of black irrigation hose left coiled by the water tank, and at a climbing vine with dark wiry stems that's spreading along the north ridge. I have watched a bird scream its head off at a hose for eleven minutes.

So I got into an argument with a friend of mine, who's doing a philosophy PhD, and it went nowhere but I can't stop thinking about it. He says the call means the old snake, full stop, because that's what it was selected for — which means every time a bird calls at a hose it's making a false claim about the world, and the fact that the hose-snake shows up and gets called at too is just luck, a coincidence of shape. I said that's insane: the birds that call at the barge snake survive and the birds that don't get eaten, so obviously what the call tracks now is something like "long thin thing on the ground," and the extinct-snake reading is the fiction. And then the woman who runs our captive-breeding program said something that actually stuck with me more than either position — the birds hatched in the pens have never seen a snake of any kind, their call goes off mostly at the vine and at the wire of the aviary, and she said maybe for those birds it doesn't mean anything anymore, it's just a noise with a trigger, a leftover. Three different answers about what the same sound means depending on which history you look at, and we're all supposedly talking about the same bird.

Could you write a short story that lives inside that problem? Something like 2,000–2,800 words. Invent whatever you need — the island, the birds, the names, don't try to reconstruct my situation, I'm not asking for a documentary. I'd like the plot to turn on a decision the project actually has to make: whether to run a playback program that trains pen-raised birds to fire the call at the real threat and ignore the hose and the vine. Whether that even makes sense — whether you'd be giving the call a new meaning, or just bolting a new habit onto a signal that still means what it always meant, or rescuing something that had gone empty — should be exactly what the characters disagree about without anyone putting it that way. Hard constraints: no lectures, no one explains a theory of meaning on the page, nobody name-drops philosophers or says the word "semantics," and no dialogue that's obviously a seminar in costume. The two main people should both be sharp and neither should win. Put in the hose scene if you like, I'd love that. And I want a reader to finish it feeling that there's a fact of the matter being fought over here, not just three people using one word three ways — that's the part I couldn't get across to my friend and I'm hoping a story can do it where arguing couldn't.
