I am drafting the framework chapter of a thesis on the KK principle, and my supervisor has insisted that I state up front what any candidate account of epistemic access must deliver before I begin adjudicating between them. I would like you to write that specification.

The domain is this: the principle that if one knows that p, one is in a position to know that one knows that p (the KK principle defended by Hintikka and rejected by Williamson), together with the wider family of luminosity theses — that being in a certain condition suffices for being in a position to know that one is in it — and the argument, originating in Knowledge and Its Limits, that no non-trivial condition is luminous.

What I want is the requirements list, not a survey of positions. For each requirement, please give:

1. The requirement itself, stated precisely.
2. The concrete case or consideration that motivates it. Vary the cases: I have encountered the slowly cooling cup of coffee many times, so draw instead on a child who reliably locates her own toy yet lacks the concept of knowledge; a subject whose self-monitoring is accurate about her moods but unreliable about her own beliefs; a body of evidence vague enough that rational confidence should be a range rather than a point; a condition whose obtaining partly depends on the subject's beliefs about whether it obtains; and whatever further cases you think do the work.
3. Whether the requirement constrains the target notion (knowledge), the access notion (being in a position to know), the logic in which the account is formulated, or the empirical picture of introspection it must be consistent with — including the findings on introspective error and confabulation that an access thesis either has to accommodate or explain away.

Where a requirement has a formal analogue, supply it: a frame condition, a constraint on the accessibility relation, a bound on how many iterations of imprecision a model can support, a restriction on which sentences may be substituted for p. I want to be able to check a proposed model against the list mechanically rather than by taste.

Then the part I most want to see. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp — for instance the safety-first camp's demand for a margin-for-error premise, the access internalist's demand that justification itself be accessible, the formal epistemologist's demand for closure under known implication, the introspectionist's demand that belief be transparent to the subject. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — say, luminosity for any condition that comes in degrees, together with a margin-for-error principle, together with the assumption that knowledge is all-or-nothing — set out the impossibility informally and identify which member of the set each camp gives up. That result matters more to me than anything else on the list. Also tell me which single requirement eliminates the most otherwise plausible accounts, and flag any requirement that turns out to be trivially satisfiable and therefore not doing real work.

Close with two brief sections. First, the demands that are routinely written into this debate but that you regard as prejudices rather than genuine desiderata, with a sentence on why each is mistaken. Second, how the list constrains the four strategies I will be evaluating: rejecting the margin premise, relocating luminosity from knowledge to confidence or credence, restricting KK to a domain or to a bounded number of iterations, and conceding the failure of KK while arguing that nothing worth having is lost.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have the literature. A numbered list with sub-headings is ideal, and a table is acceptable provided the motivating cases remain in full sentences.
