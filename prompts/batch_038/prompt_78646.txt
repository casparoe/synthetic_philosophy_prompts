My brother in law writes a small politics-and-ideas newsletter, a couple hundred subscribers, and last month's issue took a swing at Marx's theory of history. It went over really well at Thanksgiving and now he's expanding it into a longer piece and actually asked me for pushback, which is brave of him. I'm not a Marxist — if anything I sit to his right on most of this — but I have a nagging feeling he's knocking over a version of the view that nobody careful defends anymore, and I dont know enough to say where the joints are. Here's the core of the argument, roughly as he wrote it:

> "Marx's theory of history says the economy is the base and everything else — law, politics, religion, philosophy, art — is a superstructure sitting on top of it. He states it flatly: 'It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness.' The base is basically the technology a society has plus the way that technology gets put to work, so change the technology and the rest of the social order follows along behind it. That's why Marx could write that the hand mill gives you a society of feudal lords and the steam mill gives you a society of industrial capitalists. On this picture ideas are just reflections of class position, a mirror held up to the base with no causal power of their own — which is a problem Marx can't escape, because by his own account his ideas were products of his situation, not of the evidence: he never held a job, and his work was bankrolled for decades by a factory owner. And the theory is slippery in the other direction too. If some stretch of culture looks like it has nothing whatsoever to do with economics, the Marxist can always fall back on saying the economy is decisive in the last instance, which nobody could ever check. So historical materialism is either empty or false. Where it does risk a prediction — that socialism arrives once the productive forces burst through the shell of capitalism — history contradicted it: the revolutions happened in Russia and China, among the least industrially developed places on earth, not in England or Germany."

Can you go through that claim by claim and tell me what holds up, what's a fair if blunt simplification, and what's just wrong — separating what Marx himself wrote from what Engels, the Second International, and later Marxists did with it, since I suspect he's blending at least three different views into one target. Two things I'd especially like pinned down: the hand mill / steam mill line, because I have a vague memory it comes from an early polemical book and gets quoted wildly out of context; and the point about his ideas being caused by his class situation and Engels's factory money, because that's the bit that landed hardest at the table and I want to know whether it's actually an objection or a genetic fallacy dressed up as one. Then tell me whether the either-empty-or-false conclusion survives the corrections, and whether there's some weaker, more defensible version of the base/superstructure claim that a sharp person could still hold — because if there is, that's the version he should be arguing against instead. I want something I can forward to him, so no bibliography and no suggested readings, just the audit and the reasoning.
