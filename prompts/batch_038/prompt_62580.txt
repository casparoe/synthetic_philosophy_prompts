I am teaching the closing session of an undergraduate seminar on theories of freedom, and rather than spend a fourth week on the secondary literature I want to spend it on Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*, which the class has read. I would like a model reading I can circulate beforehand — literary criticism with a philosophical spine, not a study guide and not a plot summary — that asks what the novel contributes to the argument between three conceptions of liberty, and that is honest about where the novel refuses to cooperate with the theory.

Set up the reading against these three positions, which the students have already met:

- **Negative liberty** in Berlin's sense: freedom as the absence of deliberate interference by other people, measured roughly by how many doors are open to you and how little anyone obstructs the ones you choose. Berlin's accompanying worry is that the rival, "positive" conception — freedom as self-mastery, as the rule of one's higher or rational self over one's merely empirical desires — is hospitable to the move of forcing people to be free, since someone else can claim to know your real will better than you do.
- **Republican liberty** as non-domination: on Pettit's account, you are unfree not only when someone interferes but whenever someone holds a standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily in your choices — the well-behaved master's slave is unfree on every day the master happens to be kind. The test Pettit proposes is a social one: whether you can look others in the eye without fear or deference. His later formulation shifts the criterion from arbitrary interference to *alien control* — control over your choices that is not compelled to track your own interests and judgments, whether or not it ever issues in interference.
- **The structural gloss** Frank Lovett puts on this tradition: domination is a property of a relationship, and its degree is a function of how dependent you are on it, how unaccountable the other party's power is, and what it would cost you to leave.

I want the reading to work through a specific set of situations rather than generalities. Stevens's whole account of the two housemaids, Ruth and Sarah, dismissed in the mid-1930s at Lord Darlington's instruction during the period of the fascist-sympathizing friendship and the visit of the blackshirt leader; Miss Kenton's furious objection at their evening cocoa meeting, her threat to resign, and the fact that she never does; Stevens's insistence to her that it is not their place to judge such matters; and, as I recall it, Darlington later asking Stevens what became of the two women, having all but forgotten the order he gave. Then the international conference at the hall, with the French delegate and the blunt American senator who tells Darlington he is an amateur out of his depth, while Stevens's father lies dying upstairs and Stevens goes back down to serve. Then the village evening in Devon, where Harry Smith tells the company that dignity consists in having strong opinions about one's country and doing one's duty as a voter — a rival, democratic account of the very term Stevens has been defending all week. Then Mr. Farraday's American informality, which converts bantering into a job requirement, and the closing hours on the pier at Weymouth, where Stevens resolves to practise it. And finally the postwar years: the ruined reputation, the libel action, the dead godson, and Stevens's repeated evasions to strangers about whether he ever served Darlington at all.

The questions I want answered, in this order:

1. Which of the three conceptions actually names what has gone wrong in Stevens's life? He is remarkably uninterfered with — Darlington is indulgent, the household runs on habit, and for much of the novel nobody is stopping Stevens from doing anything. Make the negative-liberty and republican diagnoses compete explicitly, using Lovett's three factors where they bite, and say whether the novel's tragedy is one of closed doors or of doors held open at another man's pleasure.
2. Does Miss Kenton function as the control case? She objects, threatens the one exit available to her, and stays; her later life is, by her own account, a good one. Explain what a republican should say about her and about the difference between her position and Stevens's, and whether her threat — issued and then withdrawn — reduces or reveals her domination.
3. Harry Smith's account of dignity is the novel's only explicit statement of a democratic theory of freedom. Take it seriously as philosophy rather than as local colour: is it Pettit's non-domination in working-class idiom, is it Berlin's dangerous positive liberty, or is it something the two traditions both miss? Does the novel let Stevens answer it?
4. Turn the lens on the master. Darlington is used by his guests, steered by a clever woman with fascist commitments, and destroyed afterwards by a press he cannot answer; his own code of amateur honour makes him act on judgments he has no means of checking. Can a theory whose currency is a relationship between agents capture this, or does it need a notion of domination by an impersonal code? And where does that leave Stevens's professional ideal of dignity — the butler who is not shaken out of his role by anything — as a candidate for *self*-domination? Say plainly whether republicanism has the conceptual room to call Stevens's dignity a form of unfreedom, or whether it must treat his condition as merely unfortunate.
5. Where does the novel resist all of this? I want the reading to face the strongest objection to the republican frame: that it turns Stevens into a specimen of domination and thereby repeats the condescension of calling a contented person unfree, when what the novel seems to be tracking is self-deception, unexamined loyalty, and a failure of ordinary courage that has nothing distinctively to do with power over him. Consider whether his closing claim — that he trusted his employer's judgment, that he gave of his best, that the life was in some sense his own — is the collapse of the republican diagnosis or its confirmation.

Close by saying what the novel gives the liberty debate that argument alone does not: what it makes a reader able to see about domination, deference, or adaptive preference that a treatise on the concept could not deliver.

Practical constraints. Do not quote the novel: I cannot check quotations against a text, and the reading should turn on situations and structure rather than on wording, so paraphrase throughout. No page references, no bibliography, no further reading, and no list of scholarship on Ishiguro — assume neither you nor I can consult anything. If you are unsure whether a scene or a detail is in the novel, say so in a parenthetical rather than inventing it; the students have the book and will catch fabrication. Aim for an argument with a position, not a survey of possible positions.

Please use exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Three currencies of freedom** — roughly 200 words. Not a tutorial: state what each conception predicts about Stevens's case before the scenes are examined, so the later disagreement has stakes.
2. **The reading** — roughly 700 words, answering questions 1 through 3, organized by situation rather than by concept.
3. **The master's condition** — roughly 400 words, answering question 4.
4. **Where the novel refuses** — roughly 500 words, answering question 5, and conceding as much as is honestly due to the objection.
5. **What the novel adds** — roughly 250 words, ending with a single sentence stating which conception of liberty you judge the book to vindicate, and one sentence on what I should tell the class to look for when they reread the Weymouth chapters.
