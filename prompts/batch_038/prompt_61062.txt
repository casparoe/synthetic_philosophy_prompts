For eleven years I have been one of three volunteer administrators of a long-running persistent world: a text-and-blocks server with about four hundred regulars, many of whom joined when they were in secondary school and are now adults with children of their own. I manage infrastructure for a logistics firm by trade, and the only philosophy I have ever studied formally was a single evening course, so I write this as a layman who has been handed a job he is not equipped for. Last month a player of sixteen years used a duplication exploit to demolish a structure another player had spent four years building — a scale model, block by block, of the chapel in which her great-grandparents were married, sitting in a region of the map almost nobody visits. He sold the reclaimed materials and joked about it in a public channel. I banned him for ninety days, and his father, who also plays, has now written to me twice asking on what grounds, since nothing of value was destroyed and the chapel has since been rebuilt from our backups. Trying to answer him has made me realize that I have been adjudicating cases like this for a decade without knowing what my standard actually is.

Here is what I think, case by case. The chapel: a real harm, and a serious one — more serious, in my judgment, than the loss of a unique sword of comparable build time that changes hands on the grey market for real money, even though the sword is by our own rules and everyone's understanding the more valuable object. Yet when a player's account is stolen and the character is sold for around two hundred dollars on a third-party site, I think that is straightforward theft of property, the kind I would testify about in an actual court; the in-game act is the same as in the sword case, and only an out-of-game fact the thief neither knew nor could have seen from inside the server changes my verdict. A pyramid scheme run in our currency, which cannot be cashed out and which our written rules explicitly permit: not a wrong, or barely one, even though two players lost the equivalent of a year of evenings. A player who roleplayed a scene of sexual violence in a public channel within earshot of someone who did not want to read it: a real wrong done to that reader, though nothing was done *to* her; the identical scene, word for word, between two consenting adults in a private room: no wrong at all. A buggy script I wrote myself deleted a town-crier NPC that half the server had been talking to for nine years, and people were genuinely upset — I think no one was harmed and no one was wronged, but I also cannot say their upset was simply a mistake about the facts. A child's sandcastle kicked over by a stranger on a public build map, in a game whose design positively encourages that: a small real wrong; the identical act in a game advertised as being about griefing: no wrong at all, even though the victim in both worlds knew perfectly well where he was standing. And the case I keep turning over, the one the father's letters really turn on: since the chapel has been rebuilt from backups, block for block, in the same place, is the wrong merely diminished, or is the harm simply gone, leaving behind a bad character and a breach of rules rather than a loss? I have asserted both things, in the same email, to the same man.

The shorthand we admins use is something I will call the brick test, because I coined it and have never seen it anywhere: a thing counts as real if what was done to it would still have been a loss had it been made of brick and mortar, and if it is destroyable only because it happens to be made of data, then it is not real. It handles the chapel and the sandcastle. It makes the pyramid scheme a serious wrong, which I do not believe, since an evening of labour is an evening of labour whether or not it is recorded in a ledger nobody can cash. It is silent on the roleplay cases and on the NPC, where the material is identical and only the framing or the audience changes. It also cannot explain why the two-hundred-dollar character counts for more than the chapel.

So: can you give me the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that generates as many of these verdicts as possible — stated precisely enough that I could compress it into three lines of a code of conduct and hand it to two other people who will disagree with me? For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it in. Then tell me what it implies for cases I have not raised: our eventual shutdown of the server, deleting twenty years of builds, announced a year in advance; a memorial garden constructed inside the world for a player who died in 2019 and then vandalized; a player who paid a real-world artist four hundred dollars for a portrait of her character, which another player then deleted from the shared gallery; and an administrator — me, say — deleting a player's work as discipline for breaking an unrelated rule. Finally, be blunt about the cost. Which of my verdicts cannot be kept by any principle simple enough to administer, and which of the pairs that pull against each other — the stolen character against the demolished chapel, the public scene against the private one, the sandcastle in a game that invites it against the sandcastle in a game that does not — am I required to give up? I am not looking for a reading list or for names to look up; where a philosopher's idea is doing the work, give me the idea itself and the reasoning, not the attribution.
