**Draft: "The Politics Is in the Concrete"**

When engineers describe their work to the public, they almost always describe it as neutral. A bridge carries load; a reactor produces heat; a sorting algorithm ranks candidates by a stated criterion. Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (Daedalus, 1980) is customarily assigned as the classic rebuttal to this self-description, and it is customarily assigned as a specimen of technological determinism. This paper argues that the customary reading gets the conclusion right for the wrong reasons, and that once Winner's argument is reconstructed carefully it supports a stronger claim than he is willing to endorse: that after the design phase there is no politics of technology left to have.

Winner's central move is to identify two ways in which artifacts come to have political properties. The first is the case in which a specific technical arrangement settles a contested issue in the life of a community. His examples are the low overpasses on the Long Island parkways, whose clearances kept public buses, and so the poor and largely non-white riders who depended on them, away from Jones Beach; and the mechanical tomato harvester developed in California, which required fruit bred to be tough and to ripen all at once, and which favored large-scale processors while eliminating a great many farm jobs. The second is the case of inherently political technologies, systems whose very operation calls for a distinctive form of political organization. Winner's paradigm is the nuclear weapons complex, which cannot be administered in any way other than through a small, hierarchical, and closely guarded command structure. He also mentions decentralized energy technologies such as solar generation, which he thinks are compatible with a far more participatory political order.

On reflection, however, the two categories are not really two. Both are cases of a political decision made in advance by the people who build the thing and then hardened into matter. Whether the decision is about a clearance height, a breeding program, or a command structure, the artifact is political for the same reason: someone settled an argument by settling a design, and everyone downstream inherits the settlement. The distinction between the categories is at best a distinction of scale.

If that is right, the political life of a technology is front-loaded. The only genuinely political moment is its design; what follows is administration, and administration can be more or less efficient but cannot reopen a question the artifact has already closed. The practical corollary is that the regulation of technologies in use — safety rules, access mandates, non-discrimination requirements applied to systems already built — is largely futile, and that public attention should be concentrated entirely on ex ante design review, where the politics is actually decided.

Two loose ends. First, I am aware that some historians of technology have questioned the factual basis of the parkway story, suggesting that the clearances reflect engineering convention and cost rather than an exclusionary decision, and I do not know how much weight to give that challenge. Second, my own preferred illustration is not Winner's but a contemporary one: a municipal shift-scheduling system, licensed from a vendor and adjustable only by that vendor, which distributes the burden of irregular hours across a workforce in a way no one in the city council ever voted on. I want to say this is political in Winner's sense, but it sits awkwardly with the front-loading thesis of the previous paragraph, since here the politics seems to be maintained rather than decided.

Three requests, in order.

First, an accuracy audit of paragraphs two through four. Which of my characterizations would a careful reader of Winner accept, which are fair simplifications that cost nothing, and which are outright mistakes?
