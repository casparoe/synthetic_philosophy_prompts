Our Thursday group — six of us, mostly not philosophers, one guy who read a lot of economic history — got completely derailed last week into whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, and Ravi and Elena ended up in a standoff the rest of us couldnt break. Neither of them was being dumb, which is the problem. Roughly how it went:

1. Ravi opened with Bengal 1943. His line: Sen's contribution was showing that who starved didn't track aggregate food supply. The rice existed, but the groups paid in fixed cash wages — boatmen, transport workers, landless labourers — got priced out by wartime inflation while "priority classes" in war industries and the civil service were protected by a preferential food scheme. Add the denial policies (boats and rice stocks destroyed or removed along the coast against a Japanese invasion that never came) and the fact that the province never formally declared a famine, and you have named people making decisions with foreseeable lethal consequences. That's a crime, not weather.

2. Elena said "crime" is doing way too much work. Crime implies an agent who could see the victim and gambled with them anyway. The War Cabinet was rationing scarce shipping across a global war; nobody was choosing Bengali deaths. If every policy with foreseeable bad consequences is criminal, then every budget is criminal and the word stops picking anything out. And the cyclone, the tidal waves and the crop disease in 1942–43 were real, so nature dealt a bad hand — Ravi is arguing about how the hand was played, which is a different claim than the one he started with.

3. Ravi: that's the baseline, and the baseline is the whole issue. Elena was implicitly comparing Bengal to what a competent government would have done, but the real starting point is a population whose buffers — credit, land, access to Burmese rice imports — had been ground down for decades before anyone in London made a shipping decision in 1943. Set the baseline high enough and everything becomes mere negligence.

4. Elena: then the charge is unfalsifiable, which is her objection. If colonial history supplies the baseline, every death in a colony is attributable to the coloniser, "natural disaster" drops out of the vocabulary entirely, and there's no event left that nobody is culpable for.

5. Ravi offered what he called the legibility test, and this is the part I most want your verdict on: a famine is a political crime when the deaths registered in the state's own information channels and the state's first response was to the reporting rather than to the deaths — suppressing papers, not declaring, spending months arguing about price controls while the controls themselves drove rice into hoarding. Bengal passes. The Great Leap Forward passes harder, he said, because procurement was set from inflated local reports: corrupting the channel was the mechanism.

6. Elena's reply: legibility is scalar, which is exactly what she says sinks his view, and the test can't handle Ireland. There the reporting worked — inspectors, commissions, newspapers screaming — and roughly a million people still died behind a potato pathogen that no government on earth could have stopped. So his test condemns the one case where information flowed and excuses nothing where it didn't. She also said the export slogan is doing unearned work: what left Ireland wouldn't have fed everyone, so the difference-makers were single-crop dependence and slow relief, not theft. Then she added that Ravi's own framework struggles with the back half of Bengal, since a large share of the deaths came later, from disease, after the food crisis had eased — which is a story about relief and medicine, not entitlements.

7. Ravi's last move: fine, call it negligence instead of crime — but negligence scaled to capacity. Britain had spent the late nineteenth century building an elaborate famine apparatus across India, codes and relief works and the whole machinery, and then left it switched off in 1943. Switching off a machine you designed isn't the same as never having one. Elena said that's her baseline objection restated with better rhetoric.

They asked me to referee and I punted. So: go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, rating each one from clearly wrong to clearly right. In particular — does the legibility test survive the Ireland objection, or is Elena correct that it has no discriminating power? Is Ravi's switched-off-machinery move in 7 a genuine answer or just rhetoric? Is the 1944 disease point in 6 actually a counterexample to entitlement reasoning, or does it just relocate the argument? And tell me what both of them are missing, plus any empirical premise the whole dispute turns on that neither one actually established.
