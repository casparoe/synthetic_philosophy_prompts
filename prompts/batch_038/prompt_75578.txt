Please think carefully before answering: what I need here is adjudication, not exposition, and I would rather have a decisive judgment about each move than a balanced survey of the literature.

I audit a monthly philosophy reading group in my town — I am not a member, I work in freight logistics and simply enjoy being in the room. Last month the group got about twenty pages into an introduction to metaethics, after which two members, Nadia and Owen, began an argument about moral realism that outlasted the meeting and resumed at the coffee place next door. I took notes, and below I have compressed the exchange into six moves while preserving their order. I have tried not to sharpen either side, and neither of them is a pushover: Nadia is the anti-realist, Owen the realist, and I do not think anyone was arguing in bad faith.

1. **Nadia** opened by pointing at the two of them. Two reasonably informed people arguing in good faith for two hours without moving an inch; scale that up across the whole history of the subject. In chemistry, disagreement shrinks as information and technique accumulate, but in ethics the live questions are the same ones that were live in antiquity. If there were moral facts and we had any purchase on them, sustained reflection should have narrowed the field. It has not, so the leanest explanation is that there is nothing there to be right about, and what we are doing is voicing attitudes we have been trained to experience as discoveries.

2. **Owen** replied that her description of the data is simply false. Chattel slavery, the burning of heretics, the moral irrelevance of women, children, and nonhuman animals: each was defended by the best people of its day and each is now indefensible to us, and the change came mostly by argument rather than by conquest. That is convergence. But the deeper problem, he said, is that she is running an enthymeme — disagreement counts against realism only if you add the premise that a fact-governed domain must be one in which good-faith inquirers converge. He did not accept that premise and she had not argued for it. Ninety years on, excellent physicists still cannot agree what quantum theory says the world is like, and nobody infers from that there is no fact of the matter.

3. **Nadia** said the physics comparison fails because physics has a tether and ethics does not. A bad physical theory eventually issues a bad prediction and is killed by something outside the disputants' own sensibilities; in ethics the only thing that ever tells you your judgment is wrong is somebody else's judgment. So intractable disagreement means something different in the two cases. Worse for him, the convergence he is proud of proves nothing, because shared agreement is exactly what common descent predicts: our moral sentiments were shaped by selection for reciprocity, reputation management, and coalition loyalty, not for accuracy about a normative reality. Both the disagreement and the agreement are explicable without positing anything, and a posited entity that does no explanatory work is decoration.

4. **Owen** said that argument dissolves itself. What is the status of her premise that a belief-forming process shaped for fitness rather than truth is thereby untrustworthy? It is not an experimental result; it is a normative claim about which processes deserve credence, and it is a product of precisely the evolved, empirically untestable normative intuition she is impeaching. Applied consistently, her standard takes down logic, mathematics, and epistemology along with ethics, since none of those is causally tethered to experiment either. So either the debunking is universal — in which case she has argued for global skepticism rather than for anti-realism about morals in particular — or she owes an account of why morality alone is vulnerable. And she was ignoring a symmetry: the two of them disagree about metaethics just as stubbornly as anyone disagrees about ethics, so if intractable disagreement among informed, good-faith inquirers is her evidence, it is evidence that there is no fact about whether there are moral facts either.

5. **Nadia** said the symmetry charge assumes her view is a rival discovery, and it is not. She is not reporting an intuition about a normative reality; she is offering the leanest account of a natural phenomenon — human moralizing — and she holds it as provisionally as he likes. As for the boomerang, she would bite the bullet: she is an anti-realist about epistemic normativity too, which is more coherent than his position, since he wants to keep the epistemic facts and lose only the moral ones. And she knew which move he was saving — that moral facts are natural facts, like facts about health. But health is defined only relative to a goal: survival, functioning, the continuation of the organism. His realism needs the good to bind agents irrespective of their goals. So either he makes it goal-relative and concedes relativism, or he makes it categorical and non-natural and then explains how anyone could detect such a thing, and how the two of them could be disagreeing about one rather than merely making different noises.

6. **Owen** said that last challenge is the queerness objection with the serial numbers filed off, and it over-demands: it requires a detection mechanism for moral facts that we also lack for mathematical, modal, and epistemic facts, so if knowledge has to work that way to count, almost nothing counts. And her bullet-biting costs her the argument she began with. Her opening move depended on there being genuine, standing disagreement between people like them. But if a moral sentence states nothing fact-apt, then when he says a practice was wrong and she says it was not, they have not contradicted each other — they have made two compatible noises. She would have to relocate the disagreement into clashing attitudes or plans, and then what she has described is not disagreement about how things are, which is what her whole case rested on. She cannot have the argument from disagreement and no disagreements.

At that point it was late, they went around the loop a third time, and since I was the only person at the table who had not taken a side, they handed me the notes and asked me to referee. I would rather you did it.
