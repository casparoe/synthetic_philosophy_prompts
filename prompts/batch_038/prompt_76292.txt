Before I commit to a diagnosis of Moore's paradox, I want to know what I should be demanding of one. Write the desiderata for an account of why a sentence of the form "p, but I don't believe that p" is absurd when sincerely uttered in the first person present. Not a survey of the candidate diagnoses — the specification any of them would have to satisfy.

Fix the purpose first, because I expect it changes the list. I want an account strong enough to travel outside philosophy: specifically, one that could be applied to a system that reports on its own internal states — a language model, a monitoring agent — to decide whether such a system could produce a genuinely Moore-paradoxical self-report, or whether the phenomenon is reserved for creatures that have beliefs in the full sense. So mark, for each requirement you list, whether it presupposes introspective access, agency, or rational coherence, since those are precisely what is in question for such a system, and whether a requirement that presupposes them is thereby disqualified for my purpose or merely needs restating.

Then the requirements themselves, each with the case or consideration that motivates it and a judgment of whether it is non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp. Among the things the list must reckon with, saying in each instance whether it belongs or is a red herring: that the conjunction is not formally contradictory, so the absurdity cannot be located in inconsistency; the contrast with the same words used by a third party about me, which are unremarkable; the mirror form in which the negation falls on the world rather than on the belief ("It isn't raining, but I believe it is"), and whether a single diagnosis can cover both forms; the asymmetry across tense, where the past version ("It rained, but at the time I didn't believe it") seems innocuous and the future version does not; whether the absurdity survives when nothing is asserted to anyone at all — a diary entry, a soliloquy; the self-deceived or deluded speaker who apparently does manage to hold the pair of attitudes; and the subject whose only route to their own beliefs is the inferential route everyone else has, from behavior and testimony. Add any requirement I have overlooked, and add one I would be tempted to insist on that you think is a partisan demand dressed up as a constraint.

Finally, state which subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and what would have to be surrendered to keep the remainder; and name the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive diagnoses. If you then want to say in a line each how the familiar families of diagnosis fare against the list, do so, but keep the weight on the criteria.
