I am a novelist, and the long central section of my book in progress follows four friends who undertake a months-long, self-monitored experiment in daily drinking — an arrangement I cannot help seeing as a secularized version of what the Athenian Stranger proposes in Plato's Laws, where drinking parties conducted under a leader who stays sober are defended as a deliberate test and training of the soul, on the reasoning that one may expose a citizen to pleasure in order to cultivate resistance to it just as one might expose a child to fear in order to cultivate courage. I would like you to read Thomas Vinterberg's Another Round through that proposal. The friends begin with strict rules — a target blood-alcohol level held during working hours, nothing after eight in the evening, nothing at weekends — and the rules are precisely the element that decays; by the end one of them is dead at sea and Martin dances off a quay into the harbour. Take the Platonic frame seriously as an instrument rather than a label: does the film's experiment count as a failed version of the supervised one, failing because the supervisor is absent, or because it is conducted in private by equals who have each appointed themselves, or because it aims at pleasure and productivity rather than at virtue? And does Plato's vocabulary have any way at all of accommodating that final leap, or must it read the leap as the collapse of the argument the film has been making? I would rather you did not quote; I am after the reasoning. Then tell me honestly where the lens strains — what in the film refuses to be understood as a question about civic education, given that these are men drinking against boredom, grief, and their own mediocrity rather than against anything a legislator would recognize. Finally, if you can bear to advise: should my own chapter stage the missing supervisor explicitly, or does naming him turn the thing into a fable with a moral attached?
