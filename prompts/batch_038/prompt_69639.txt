Ok, so I've built an argument for my game theory problem set that I'm now fairly sure is broken, but everyone in my seminar group wants to save a different step and I cant work out who's right. It's supposed to show that rationality on its own — no equilibrium concepts, no appeals to what people actually do — pins down a unique prediction in every finite game.

(1) A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy, where the dominating thing is allowed to be a mixed strategy.
(2) A rational player never plays a weakly dominated strategy either: the alternative is never worse and is strictly better somewhere, so there's just no reason to prefer the dominated one.
(3) If (1) and (2) are common knowledge, each player can delete the others' dominated strategies, then their own, then the others' again, and so on.
(4) Deleting strictly dominated strategies is order-independent — the mixture that dominates s still dominates s after you've shrunk the opponent's strategy sets, and shrinking can only create new dominations, never destroy old ones. Since (2) puts weak dominance on the same footing as strict, the whole procedure is order-independent too and ends in one well-defined set.
(5) Whatever rationality rules out can't be rational play, so the survivors must contain everything a rational player could do under common knowledge of rationality — including all the Nash equilibria, since equilibrium play is rational play by definition.
(6) Hence: a unique, order-independent, equilibrium-respecting prediction, from rationality and nothing else.

Then two things happen to it. First, this game (player 1 rows, player 2 columns, payoffs listed as player 1 then player 2):

        L        R
U    (2,1)    (0,1)
D    (2,1)    (1,0)

Nothing is strictly dominated, so (1) by itself delivers no prediction at all here. But D weakly dominates U and L weakly dominates R. Delete U first and you get R knocked out next and land on (D,L) uniquely; delete R first and player 1's two rows tie, so both U and D survive. Two orders, two answers. Second: in a second-price auction with two bidders whose values are 10 and 6, bidding your value weakly dominates every other bid, so (2) wipes out every non-truthful bid — and (12, 6) is a Nash equilibrium (player 1 wins and pays 6, can't do better, and player 2 can't profitably outbid 12 for something worth 6). So weak-dominance deletion has eaten an equilibrium outright.

So (4), (5) and (6) can't all stand. Which step do you drop, and what does dropping it cost? I'd cheerfully sacrifice (6) — I don't need determinacy. What I don't want to give up is (2), because it's the only thing that gets me truthful bidding in the auction, and I think that's a genuine result rather than an artefact of how I set the argument up.

Three repairs have been suggested to me: that (2) is false as stated but true under full-support beliefs, in which case (3) is smuggling in an assumption I never declared; that (4) is a scope slide, because order-independence of the strict procedure doesn't transfer to the weak one even granting (2); and that I keep everything and move to lexicographic or "cautious" beliefs so weak dominance gets reinstated at each round. Are the first and third actually two different proposals or the same one twice? And for each of them tell me what I'm paying — specifically whether the auction result survives, and whether I'm still making a claim about rationality or have quietly switched to assuming a particular structure of higher-order beliefs about how players break near-ties.
