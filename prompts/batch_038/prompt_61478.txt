I am a second-year graduate student in political philosophy, and I have just spent a month reading David Lewis's *Convention* for a seminar on social ontology. Over the break I am also obliged to introduce a community screening of the 1971 film of *Fiddler on the Roof* at my hometown synagogue's film night, which is not an obvious pairing but is the one I have drawn. The reason it has ceased to feel arbitrary is embarrassingly local: my congregation is in the middle of an argument about whether to abandon a long-standing liturgical practice, and the argument has precisely the shape Lewis predicts. Nobody claims the incumbent practice is intrinsically better than the proposed one; nearly everyone concedes that the alternative would serve every purpose equally well; and yet nearly everyone also insists that changing it is not the sort of thing one may simply decide to do. I want to find out whether Anatevka can be read as a case study in Lewisian convention, or whether the film is really about a species of norm Lewis deliberately excludes.

So here is the reading I would like. Take Tevye's village seriously as a Lewisian population facing recurrent situations of a given type, and test the traditions recited in the opening number against his definition: near-universal conformity, mutual expectation of conformity, the preference for general conformity over conformity that is merely almost general, common knowledge of all of it, and the availability of an alternative regularity that would do as well. I suspect the arbitrariness condition is satisfied but the preference condition is not, since the villagers seem to conform less because unilateral deviation spoils a coordination payoff than because deviation invites communal sanction and cosmic penalty, and I suspect Yente the matchmaker is better described as a device for manufacturing salience in a matching problem than as a party to any convention; argue the point either way. Then I want the two set-pieces of norm change treated in the same terms. First, Tevye's soliloquy in which he argues himself, reason by reason and counter-reason by counter-reason, into accepting a poor tailor as a son-in-law: is that an agent discovering that a regularity is arbitrary and nevertheless concluding that he must go on conforming to it, which is exactly the psychology Lewis's account requires? Second, and more interesting, the invented dream in which Tevye stages a visitation from the dead to persuade his wife, since he plainly cannot change the practice by declaration or by giving Golde his actual reasons. Read that as an attempt to fabricate the common-knowledge state his own analysis tells him is missing, and tell me whether Lewis has the resources to describe what Tevye is doing when he lies about his ancestors.

Finally, the last act is what I most want handled, because it is where I think the film may have something to teach the theory. The expulsion and the scattering to America look to me like a convention that is not defeated by argument but dissolved because the recurrent situation stops recurring and the population disperses, and I want to know whether Lewis's static equilibrium analysis is structurally unable to register that, or whether the ending can be reconstructed inside his framework as a change in payoffs. Relatedly, the title image is a metaphor of precarious balance while Lewis's equilibrium is self-enforcing and robust; does the film trade on that ambiguity, and is the confusion a defect or the point? And please be an honest critic about the whole enterprise: say where the reading overreaches, where a fair-minded objector would insist that the show is sentimental about tradition in a way Lewis's rationalism is not, that Tevye's articulate self-examination is a librettist's imposition on a villager's psychology, or that the film is better evidence about a nostalgic American audience than about the world it depicts. I am not asking for quoted lyrics or dialogue or for any bibliography; describe the scenes and reason about their structure. Close by telling me what the film contributes to the philosophy of convention that argument alone does not, since I have twenty minutes at a lectern and I would like them to be worth the drive.
