I've been going back over Nietzsche's diet-and-health writing — the long stretch in Ecce Homo where he works through food, drink, climate and metabolism, plus the physiologcal arguments that do real work in Twilight of the Idols and the Genealogy — and there's a standing dispute I can't settle in my own head. Roughly: is the dietary material philosophically load-bearing, or is it symptom?

Camp A says load-bearing. On his account values and truth-claims are expressions of a bodily economy, so what you put in your stomach and what you allow yourself to believe are answers to the same question at different levels of description; you can't carve out the physiology without gutting the epistemology, and the health talk is where his naturalism is most literal rather than merely metaphorical.

Camp B says symptom. Here's a chronically ill man with migraines and a wrecked digestion, rationalising his regimen in the idiom of the popular health science of the 1880s — hostility to alcohol, suspicion of stimulants, praise of light meals and moderate living, the sort of thing you could lift out of any Victorian dietary reformer. Bracket it the way you'd bracket anyone's private fad and read the philosophy separately.

I want an essay that actually argues this out and comes down somewhere. To stop it turning into a survey of Nietzsche on the body, run it through two tests I've been kicking around. First, what I'll call the dietetic deduction problem: for each cluster of recommendations, can you genuinely get from the philosophy — will to power, décadence, the economy of the drives, whatever you take to be doing the work — to that specific advice, or does the advice arrive fully formed from somewhere else and the philosophy only ratifies it afterwards? Second, the the transmissibility test: does the argument generating the advice also constrain what he'd tell a reader with a different body, or is the only route from the metaphysics to the plate via his own symptoms? A cluster that fails both is symptom; one that passes both is doing philosophical work; and say what to make of the ones stuck in between, since I expect most of them will be.

Apply this to at least three concrete clusters — the anti-alcohol line, the heavy-meats-and-German-cooking line, and the climate-and-metabolism line — being specific about what each one costs him and what it earns him. Then do the downstream accounting. If most of it turns out to be symptom, what happens to the arguments that lean on bodily typology, for instance the priest in the Genealogy as a physiological type, or décadence as a bodily condition rather than a moral failing? Do they survive as claims about psychology that he happened to state in physiological language, or do they lose a premise? And on the other side, if the diet is load-bearing, what does he then owe us — a checkable physiology that is, on the whole, simply wrong — and does that infect the philosophy itself or only its applications?

One more thing worth pressing: he presents the regimen as the fruit of long self-observation, so treat the self-experiment clause as a live option rather than an aside. Is a first-person case study evidence for a general claim on his own perspectivist terms? If it is, doesn't that licence everyone's fad equally, which is a special problem for him because he wants to rank people and not merely describe them.

I don't want a balanced on-the-one-hand finish and I don't want a reading list at the end. Take a side, say which of the two tests did the deciding, and mark clearly where the best case for the other side genuinely bites — I'd much rather have a definite verdict I can push back against than a menu. Paraphrase rather than quoting, and don't give me chapter-and-verse references; I won't check them and you'd be guessing anyway.
