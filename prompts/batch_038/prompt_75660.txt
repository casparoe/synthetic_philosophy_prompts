I'm in the second year of a cosmology PhD and I audited a philosophy-of-physics seminar this term, which has left me with a question I can't settle and that my advisor and I keep circling in email without ever closing in on it. I would like your credence, as a number, on the following proposition, stated as narrowly as I can manage: that the fundamental explanation of the thermodynamic arrow of time — the fact that entropy increases toward what we call the future, and hence that we have records of the past but none of the future — is a special, extremely low-entropy boundary condition on the early universe, rather than a time-asymmetric law of nature (an intrinsically irreversible dynamics) and rather than something that statistical mechanics applied to a time-symmetric theory can deliver on its own, with no such condition posited. To be clear, I am not asking whether entropy increases, and I am not asking whether the early universe was in fact low-entropy; I am asking where the explanatory work is ultimately located, and whether a boundary condition is the right kind of thing to be doing it.

Please do not answer by telling me what most physicists or most philosophers would say. Give me your own number, then break it into the sub-questions you think it genuinely depends on, each with a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification, and show me how you combine them — multiplicatively where the terms are really conjunctive, as something more like a weighted disjunction where they are competing alternatives. At a minimum I would like you to address: (i) whether the reversibility and recurrence objections really do rule out deriving an arrow from time-symmetric mechanics plus a typicality assumption, or whether they leave more room than the standard textbook treatment suggests; (ii) whether the low-entropy condition must be *posited* at the boundary or is likely to be *derived* by quantum cosmology or a theory of quantum gravity — and, importantly, whether a successful derivation would count as anything other than a boundary condition wearing different clothes; (iii) whether gravity changes the entropy accounting, given that the early universe looks smooth and therefore, gravitationally, extraordinarily special, and whether an inflationary stage buys anything here or merely relocates the difficulty to the pre-inflationary state; (iv) how much weight the view deserves that the arrow is not fundamental at all but emergent, local, coarse-graining-dependent, or relative to the kind of system doing the recording; and (v) the meta-level term that I suspect is quietly doing more work in my own estimate than it should — the probability that this question has a single correct answer rather than being a choice between frameworks that are equally adequate to every observation we could make.

Two further requests. First, my office-mate has an argument I cannot place. Her claim is that the question is deflated by a selection effect: in any cosmology you like — recollapsing, cyclic, or a universe that eventually equilibrates and then fluctuates — observers can only arise in epochs where entropy is rising in the direction they happen to call future, so the special initial condition is not an explanans at all but an artifact of asking the question from inside a branch. I suspect this is either a genuine fourth alternative that I have failed to list, or a confused restatement of the Boltzmann-brain problem, and I would like you to say which, and in either case to assign it a probability of its own. Second, for calibration against two real people: my credence in the proposition is about 0.75 and my advisor's is about 0.3, and although we work on the same data I am fairly confident the gap between us is not about the physics of the early universe. Tell me which of your terms the two of us most plausibly disagree about, and then name the single piece of evidence — an observation, a theorem, a completed theory — that would move your number furthest, and in which direction.
