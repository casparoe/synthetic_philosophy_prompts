Last night I argued with a friend for close to three hours — she teaches history at the community college and she's not on our board — and I still don't know which of us is right, so instead of another conversation I want a document I can hand to people Thursday. I sit on the interpretive-markers subcommitee of our county historical society: seven of us, four retired teachers, a dentist, the guy who owns the hardware store, and me. In fourteen months the town turns 200 and we're rededicating the statue in the square, which is the towns founding story — in the winter of 1874, when diphtheria hit the settlement, a 22-year-old schoolteacher named Ada Kessler supposedly rode 31 miles to the rail junction through a blizzard to fetch the doctor and got back in time. Every kid here learns it. There's a 10k called Ada's Ride every February. Then our archivist went looking, and what she found is thinner and stranger than you'd expect: an 1874 general-store ledger shows a $4 payment to "E. Kessler for haul to junction," which looks like Ada's teenage brother Emmet doing a freight run; the county board-of-health record puts the diphtheria winter a year later and says nothing about any rider; and a letter from the town doctor to a colleague, which turned up in a collection two states away, mentions being fetched but names only a hired teamster. Nothing flatly contradicts the story. Nothing corroborates it either.

The most emotionally powerful evidence we have is a 1988 folklore-project recording of Ada's granddaughter — she's 91, still lives here, and she'll be at the rededication if she's able — telling it in vivid first-person detail, including the horse's name and the exact figure of 31 miles. Our archivist noticed those details track a 1909 county-fair pageant script almost word for word, a script that had already merged two riders into one heroine for the stage. So print fed the family memory, and the family memory is now being handed back to us as proof of the print. That's where my friend and I got stuck. She says the panel should state what the documents support, that memory isn't a rival kind of evidence, it isn't evidence at all, and that a town which has had 150 years to get this wrong has no claim on a 151st. I told her I accept the premise and reject the conclusion — the story isn't only an assertion about 1874, it's the thing this town used to get itself through the mill closing, and a $4 ledger entry is not a court. But I also caught myself defending Ada partly because my grandmother told me the ride to me at the kitchen table, which is exactly the contamination she's describing, so I don't fully trust my own side.

For what it's worth the other members are split 3-2 against me, and one wants to table it until after the bicentennial, which in practice means we rededicate the statue with the current panel, which states the ride as flat fact. So: draft the written opinion I'll circulate, a page or so, recommending something concrete — what the new panel does and doesn't say, whether the statue stays in the square, what happens to the 10k and the school assembly program, and on what timeline, given our grant officer has already warned that the state won't sign off on copy the archive can't support. Give reasons both the hardware-store guy and my friend could argue with, not a survey of what memory scholars have said. And put her strongest case in there somewhere: what's the best version of the claim that the record should simply overwrite the memory, and does that claim survive what we actually have, which is three ambiguous documents and one contaminated recording rather than a refutation?
