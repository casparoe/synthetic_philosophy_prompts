Our hospital's ethics committee has given two of us eighteen months, part-time, to draft the conceptual framework for a psychiatric advance directive program: documents in which patients with recurrent psychosis specify, while competent, what treatment is to be given later when they refuse it. The legal machinery already exists; what is missing is a defensible account of when the earlier self's instructions should override the later self's refusal — the Ulysses contract problem in its hardest setting. Which open conceptual questions should we spend that time on, and in what order? Candidates I have in mind: whether the later self's repudiation is a mere change of preference or a change in who is doing the deciding; the monitoring problem of who certifies that the triggering condition has arrived, given that the person best placed to judge is the one being overridden; the escape-hatch problem that any release clause seems to void the commitment while its absence risks catastrophic lock-in; and whether these are one problem or several that come apart in practice. Tell me which genuinely block the design of the directive, which are philosophically interesting but idle for our purposes, and which I have missed that matter more — and defend the ordering explicitly, by what each question depends on, by how far two non-specialists can realistically get in eighteen months, and by what would move a question up or down the list. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
