Every behavioral thing we're trained to score on a farm deck looks better when the animals have given up, and I've been unable to stop thinking about that for about two years now.

Quick context: no philosophy degree, no degree in anything relevant really. I do welfare inspections at trout farms and shrimp ponds for a tiny nonprofit (three of us, one van) and we're about to pitch a foundation for a nine-month project with a small budget and one part-time researcher. I need you to write the proposal — two pages max, and the person reading it is a sharp program officer, not an academic, so please no jargon wall.

The half-formed idea, from a lot of long drives between sites: acute misery is loud and chronic misery is quiet. Fish stop flashing, stop bolting at the net, stop competing for feed once the crowding and handling have been going on for weeks. A shrimp tank that's completely inert gets a good score from us. And then there's the other end of it — chilling shrimp in ice slurry before slaughter, which everybody likes because it looks calm, but calm and insensible are not the same word and I don't think we've ever really shown they're the same thing out here. Meanwhile the measures we treat as serious, like cortisol in fish or how fast they resume normal behavior, either need a lab we don't have or get read as "no sign of suffering" when they should maybe be read as "no response left to show."

So the question I want funded is roughly: what has to be true of a measure for it to be evidence about how an animal is doing, as opposed to evidence about how much behavioral capacity it has left? Is there a principled line between those, and does the whole indicator scheme in aquaculture cross it in a describable, fixable way?

What I need in the document:

- the question stated sharply enough that it could come out either way, not just a mood
- why the two camps we argue with — the "obviously they suffer, look at them" people and the "you're projecting, you can't know" people — both leave it open. Don't just name them, show the gap each one walks past.
- the method: what we'd actually do, month by month, given one part-time researcher and my farm access and no lab. And why that beats the obvious alternative, which is telling the foundation to just pay for more experiments and more sensors. If your honest read is that the conceptual work is worthless without new data, say that and reshape the project instead of padding it.
- what a result looks like. I want something an inspector can carry onto a deck in the rain and use to change a score, not a paper nobody opens.
- what would count as the project failing, phrased so I'd recognize it around month five rather than month nine
- a straight answer on whether nine months is realistic for this, and where you'd cut if it isn't

Two more things. Which animals this is about matters — I suspect the answer is different for trout than for shrimp and I'd rather the proposal admit that than pretend one framework covers both. And I have a hunch that psychology has a whole apparatus about whether a test measures the thing it claims to measure, and that some of it might transfer over. If you think that's a real move, build it in and explain the transfer in plain words; if it doesn't transfer, tell me why and don't force it.

No references please — whoever we hire can add those, and I definitely don't want invented ones sitting in a grant application.
