Ok, help me out with a piece of criticism. I'm a grad student running the last two weeks of an undergrad seminar on personal identity, and the Parfit day is going to be paired with Nolan's *The Prestige* (the film, not the Priest novel — I know they diverge, and I don't need you to litigate that unless it matters). Everyone and their brother does the obvious reading, so I want to deliberately steer away from it.

The obvious reading is Angier's machine. Tesla's device doesn't transport him, it makes a second Angier at a distance and leaves the original where he was, so every performance is a textbook branching case with one branch drowned in a tank under the stage, and Angier's own account of the cost — going in each night not knowing whether he'd be the one taking the bow or the one in the water — is basically the standard complaint that psychological continuity leaves out something that matters. That's fine and true, and I want at most a tight paragraph on it, because my students will find that take in the first ten minutes of googling and I don't want to spend the seminar on it.

What I actually want pressed on is Borden. His secret isn't duplication at all. It's that he and Fallon are twins who have manufactured a single public person between them since the start: one name, one career, one household, split down the middle, with whichever one isn't onstage spending the day in prosthetics playing a mute engineer. So on any given night the man in the box has half a set of memories, and the "Borden" the audience has been watching for twenty years isn't identical to either of them and never was. Their connectedness is maintained by a coded diary — i.e. by testimony, not by anything like the right kind of causal chain, which is exactly what Parfit needs and exactly what they don't have.

Some things I'd like you to actually argue about rather than gesture at:

1. Parfit's machinery is built for *prospective* survival — fission forward from one person. The Borden case looks like fission run backwards, or maybe not fission at all: two branches that were always there and one person assembled on top of them. Does his framework have any resources for this, or does it simply have nothing to say? And does it change the slogan that identity is not what matters — for Angier the loss of identity is a horror, for Borden it seems to be the achievement.

2. I've been calling this, for want of anything better, "composite survivorship," on the analogy of composite photograph. Is that a real distinction with work to do, or am I just decorating the fission case? If it's not real, tell me so and give me something better; if it is, tell me where its edges are, because I suspect it shades into ordinary cases (a married couple, a long-running firm, two people sharing one pseudonym) and I need to know whether that's a bug or a feature.

3. Does the film know it's staging two different puzzles? Cutter's opening account — pledge, turn, prestige, the prestige being the return of what was thought lost — frames both tricks as death-and-restoration, but only one of them literally is. Where does the film profit from that slide, and where would a careful viewer call it out?

4. The very first crime in the story is the knot that drowns Julia, and the film's own secret means nobody, including the man who tied it, can say which of the two did. Is that a case where indeterminate identity dissolves blame, or just relocates it? I think it's the most underdiscussed thing in the movie and I want to know whether you agree or think I'm overreading.

If the film is indicting shared identity rather than celebrating it, my whole session collapses and I'd rather find that out from you now. Say also, at the end, what the film adds to the Parfit debate that an argument laid out in premises couldn't — or say that it adds nothing and it's just an illustration, if that's your view.
