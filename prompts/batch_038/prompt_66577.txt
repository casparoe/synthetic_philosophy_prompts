I'm the coordinator of the human subjects office at a smallish university — it's me and one part-time staffer, neither of us lawyers — and we clear maybe thirty to forty low-risk behavioral protocols a month on the expedited track, mostly from grad students. We get about twenty-five minutes per protocol before it either goes out with fixes, gets flagged for the faculty committee, or gets held. The problem is that our checklist is a completeness checklist: are all the elements there, is the reading level roughly eighth grade, is there a signature line, is the contact info current. It tells us whether the form is finished. It tells us nothing about whether the consent is actually valid, and twice in the last year we've approved something that a committee member later tore apart in a way I couldn't answer, because I had no framework beyond the form.

So I want a triage procedure my team can actually run, and I want it built on the conceptual distinctions rather than on form hygiene — but with a decision about which distinctions survive the compression into twenty-five minutes and which get dropped, and why. Four things specifically. First, payment: I keep hearing that big payments to broke participants are coercive or unduly influential, and I keep hearing the opposite, that they're just good offers. I understand there's a baseline problem hiding in here — whether something counts as coercive depends on what you compare it against, and on whether the proposer had any obligation to improve the person's situation to begin with. I want the procedure to pick a baseline and defend picking it, or to tell me outright that the concept can't be operationalized and give me a substitute question. Second, deception and non-disclosure: which distortions or omissions actually void the consent versus which leave it intact, including the difference between lying about what will happen and simply not mentioning it, and including cases where the participant agrees up front to not being told everything, or where the form covers future unspecified reuse of their data by the lab and its collaborators. Third, capacity: I want it treated as relative to the particular decision and the particular moment, not as a global verdict on a person, and broken into the abilities that matter so a non-clinician can spot a failure without pretending to diagnose. Fourth, and this one is upstream of the other three: is consent a state of mind or a thing that gets said? Because if it's the first, my team is in the business of trying to infer what's in someone's head from a signature, which we can't do; if it's the second, we're in the business of fixing the signal. Take a position. Don't give me both-sides hedging on that one, it changes what the whole procedure is for.

Then write it as ordered questions with a stated sufficient answer to each, three exits — approve with named fixes, escalate to the committee because what's left is a values call rather than a fact, or stop — and a note on where the procedure will systematically misfire and which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure. No references, I don't need a bibliography, I need something a person with no philosophy training can run. Then work it on three live protocols: a study paying $400 for a three-hour session, recruited through a homeless shelter, with an extra $100 completion bonus that's forfeited if you leave early; a study where participants are told it's about memory but it's actually about how far they'll follow a confederate's instructions, with a full debrief at the end and the PI arguing that telling them the real topic in advance ruins the science; and a study enrolling people in their first days of opioid withdrawal inside a detox unit, where the unit's own director does the recruiting, and where the consent form asks them to agree to future unspecified reuse of the data. One of those is going to come out in a way our committee will hate and I already suspect which, so don't soften it. Finally: be committed where the material is contested and flag where you're genuinely unsure rather than papering over it, and if you think I'm asking for something that can't be done well — if twenty-five minutes of procedure on this is mostly theater, or if my framing of one of the four questions is confused in a way that would corrupt the output — say that plainly up front instead of politely building what I asked for.
