I'm in a historiography seminar this term — theory of history, mostly archives and power. We did Trouillot on the four moments where silence gets built into the production of history, then Stoler on reading along the archival grain, some Hartman, a bit of Spivak. I run the weekly discussion section for the undergrads and every year the exact same thing happens: somebody finds a gap in the sources and immidately says "the archive silenced them," somebody else says "no, you just haven't checked the right series," and then they talk past each other for forty minutes. I want a one-page handout that makes people settle it by looking at features of the documentary situation instead of by how they already feel about power.

So: a decision tree that starts from "you expected a record of X and there isn't one, or there is one that doesn't say what you wanted it to say" and ends at a diagnosis of which kind of absence you're actually holding. I've got a rough five-way cut rattling around that you should use as a starting point but feel free to sharpen, split, merge or reorder — Trouillot's four moments are roughly where I got it, but I think his "making of sources" covers at least two very different failures. My list: (1) nothing was ever written, the person or event never entered the record-making apparatus at all; (2) something was written and then destroyed, deaccessioned, withheld, censored or shipped out before independence — an act done *to* the records; (3) something was written and it survives, but the genre wouldn't let the person speak — inventories, court formularies, census columns, the clerk's own prose — so they're present as object and not as voice; (4) nothing could have been written, because the categories to describe the thing didn't yet exist for anybody, a sympathetic recorder included; (5) it was written and it does say it, but our questions and our training make it inaudible. For each leaf, give the methodological response it licenses (serial reading, prosopography, counting, speculative narration, flatly refusing to speculate, reading for the archivist's own anxieties, whatever fits) and give every leaf a name, inventing one if the literature hasn't got a handy term.

The branch questions are the whole point, so they have to be answerable from the evidence situation alone — what other series survive for this jurisdiction, who had custody of the file, what genre it's in, whether contemporaries had a word for the thing, whether parallel cases in the same box *do* speak — and nothing like "do you think archives are instruments of power?", since the handout is supposed to sort people by what they say about their documents, not by which label they'd pick. Then, at the bottom, a few sentences on: which pairs of leaves no question about the sources can actually separate and why; and which cells in the space come out empty — is anyone committed to a silence that's both category-impossible and somebody's fault, or to (3) and (5) being the same thing after all? For each empty spot say whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Format as an indented tree with node questions capped at two sentences each, whole thing under 750 words, and no bibliography or citations of any kind (don't give me page numbers I can't check). If you restructure my five-way split, add one line saying what you changed.
