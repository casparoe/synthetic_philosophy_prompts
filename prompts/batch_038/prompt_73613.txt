Teaching Kafka's In the Penal Colony in a jurisprudence seminar on legal validity, and I want to build the session around self-reference in law — the puzzle of whether an amendment clause can repeal itself, since the moment the new clause takes hold the old one no longer exists to authorize the change. Alf Ross turned this into a formal paradox, and the story reads to me like a dramatization of it. The Officer is the walking version: last authorized operator of a dead Commandant's procedure, who ends up feeding himself into the machine after erasing the soldier's sentence-text and substituting a two-word injunction to be just.

So: is his self-application the last valid act of the old order, a usurpation of it, or proof that an order can't outlive its final interpreter without consuming him? And where does that leave the new Commandant, who winds down the practice using procedures inheritted from the authority he's winding down, while the old man's tombstone in the teahouse floor still promises he'll rise again and lead his adherents back?

Give me criticism that's faithful in both directions — say where the story really is about validity and where it's about something else entirely (the sixth hour, the principle that guilt is never to be doubted), and where my framing just stops working. No need to force it; I'd rather have the honest negative result than a tidy allegory.
