Here's the question as it appeared on the paper:

"Question 3 (20 marks; part a 6, part b 6, part c 4, part d 4). On the Sunday before a five-day school week, the head teacher tells her class: there will be exactly one exam during the coming week, and on the morning of the exam you will not know in advance that it is to happen that day. The students reason as follows. If no exam has been held by Thursday evening, then on Friday morning the only day left is Friday, so they would know the exam is that day; so Friday is ruled out. But then if no exam has been held by Wednesday evening, Thursday is the only day left, so Thursday is ruled out too. Continuing in this way, no day is possible, and the teacher's announcement cannot be true. (a) Set out the students' reasoning as a valid argument, making the assumptions it needs explicit. (b) Say what you think is wrong with the reasoning, or what it really establishes. (c) Does the paradox depend on the announcement's being common knowledge among the students? Does it depend on any principle linking knowing with knowing that you know? (d) Suppose the teacher had instead said, on Tuesday evening: there will be an exam tomorrow, and tonight you do not know that there will be an exam tomorrow. Does this one-day version have the same problem, and what does your answer tell you about the five-day case?"

And here is the student's answer, in full:

"(a) The argument is a reductio on the assumption that the students know the announcement. Take the announcement to say two things: exactly one exam falls somewhere between Monday and Friday, and on the morning of the exam the students do not know that the exam is that day. Suppose that by Thursday evening no exam has been held. The students know this, they know the first conjunct, and they can put the two together, so the exam must be tomorrow; but then on Friday morning they would know the exam is that day, which contradicts the second conjunct. Hence there is no exam on Friday — and, importantly, the students are in a position to know that there is no exam on Friday. Now suppose no exam by Wednesday evening. Since Friday has been eliminated and they know it has been eliminated, Thursday is the only candidate left, so by the same reasoning they would know on Thursday morning that the exam is that day, contradicting the second conjunct again. So Thursday is out, and they know it. Repeating the step for Wednesday, Tuesday and Monday leaves no possible day, which contradicts the first conjunct. So the announcement cannot be true. The assumptions actually used are these: the students know the announcement; they know what day it is and whether an exam has already been held; their knowledge is closed under short obvious deductions from what they know; and knowledge they have on one day is still available to them on the next.

(b) I think the argument is valid and that people who go hunting for a fallacy in it are looking in the wrong place. What it shows is that the announcement cannot be true, not merely that the students cannot know it, because if it were true the students could deduce from it that it is false, and nothing true has that property. The announcement is self-undermining in the way the liar sentence is: it is a sentence that refers back to its own status. Tarski's theorem says that a language rich enough for arithmetic cannot define its own truth predicate, so sentences that purport to talk about their own truth have to be treated as lacking a truth value altogether, and I think the announcement should be handled the same way. Once you say that, the students' error is easy to state: they reasoned from something that is neither true nor false. There is also a simpler way to make the same point. The announcement has the form of a Moore-paradoxical sentence, 'there will be an exam and you do not know that there will be an exam', and Moore-paradoxical sentences cannot be coherently asserted, so the teacher never really asserted anything and there is nothing for the students to reason from. Either route gives the same result: the puzzle is not that surprises are impossible but that the announcement was never a genuine claim in the first place.

(c) On common knowledge, yes, I think the paradox depends on it. The exam is a public event and the second conjunct is about the class, so the induction goes through only if each student knows the announcement, and knows that the others know it, and knows that they know that they know it, and so on. That is an infinite hierarchy, and a teacher saying something once at the front of a room does not really deliver it. So the paradox never gets going in an actual classroom; it is a puzzle about an idealisation, which is part of why it feels unsatisfying. On the second question, the paradox also depends on the principle that if you know something you are in a position to know that you know it. If the students could know that the exam is not on Friday without knowing that they know it, the induction could not be stepped up to Thursday, and since that principle is very widely rejected — plenty of people know things without being in a position to know that they know them — rejecting it looks like the cleanest exit. The other assumptions I listed in (a), closure under obvious deduction and the persistence of knowledge, are hard to deny, so the trouble has to be located in this principle.

(d) The one-day version is worth taking seriously because there is no induction in it at all. On Tuesday evening the teacher says only that there will be an exam tomorrow and that the students do not know that tonight, so nothing about Fridays or Thursdays is involved. If the same trouble shows up here, and I think it does, since if the students believe the teacher then they know there is an exam tomorrow, which makes the second half of her announcement false, then the trouble cannot be blamed on the backward induction. That is a real lesson: the five-day case is just the one-day case stacked up, so whatever is going wrong is going wrong in a single step. Now, obviously a teacher can surprise a class in real life. If she says this and then gives the exam on Wednesday, the class will be astonished. But they will be astonished only because they had already talked themselves into believing that no exam was coming at all, which is a psychological accident rather than a counterexample. For students who take the announcement seriously, which is what the puzzle stipulates, the announcement still cannot be true."
