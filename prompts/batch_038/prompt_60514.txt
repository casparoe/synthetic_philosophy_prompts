I am an associate editor at a general philosophy journal, and roughly one submission a month argues that evolutionary explanations of our moral intuitions undercut those intuitions — some concluding that realism about one particular domain fails, others that morality as a whole is unjustified. Desk decisions are mine alone, referee goodwill is scarce, and I have about forty minutes per submission, working only from the abstract and the introduction. Write a triage procedure I can actually run in that time: an ordered set of gates, each with the question it puts to the paper, what counts as a pass, and whether a failure licenses a desk reject, a note back to the author, or a send-to-referees. It should convert whatever the epistemology of genealogical arguments really licenses into questions answerable on a first pass — how the target of the debunking is specified and how far the conclusion is permitted to travel beyond it; whether the argument needs a claim about why the belief was selected, or only that selection would have produced it whether or not it was true; what the author owes by way of an account of how a moral belief could have tracked the truth; and whether the premise doing the work is one the author's opponents can accept without abandoning their own view. Mark each gate where judgment cannot be eliminated, and say who or what settles the case if the procedure cannot.

Then run it on three abstracts. (1) A paper arguing that intuitions about the unfairness of free-riding are explained by reputation management in small-scale groups, and that realism about fairness therefore fails. (2) A paper arguing that because selection would have installed incest-avoidance intuitions whether or not incest were wrong, no moral belief is justified, and moral skepticism follows globally. (3) A paper arguing that nothing in our fitness history explains concern for the welfare of farmed animals, so intuitions in that domain are untouched by the debunking and stand vindicated by the absence of an adaptive story. I have defended something like the third line in print; tell me plainly if your procedure rejects it, and say where it is being unfair if it is. Close with what the procedure cannot decide, and the failure mode you expect it to have most often in practice.
