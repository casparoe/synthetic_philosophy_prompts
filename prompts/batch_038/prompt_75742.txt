Third-year PhD student in AI policy, currently on placement at a lab doing evals, and I've gotten into the habit of scoring every proposed safety measure by whether it carries an alignment tax — i.e. whether it leaves you with a less competitive model than a capability-maximizing rival would have. The problem is I've been keeping a list of my own verdicts and they don't hang together. Here's the list.

1. Refusal training on a narrow category (say, one specific synthesis route). Costs about 0.3% on a broad benchmark. Tax, small but real.
2. An RLHF/constitutional pass that improves instruction-following generally and happens to suppress some dangerous outputs along the way. Not a tax — a subsidy, negative tax.
3. Interpretability toolchain: two researchers pulled off capability work for a year, and the toolchain never ships in any product. Tax.
4. A pure capability side-project (a robotics demo) that eats the same two researcher-years. Not an alignment dividend. Nothing gets netted against anything.
5. A runtime monitor reading activations at deploy time, ~1% latency overhead, and across the whole eval suite it never fires on anything the unmonitored model wouldn't have refused anyway. Tax, pure.
6. Exactly the same monitor, same 1%, but it catches one bad output per million. Still a tax, and the same size — the benefit goes in a seperate column. I refuse to net.
7. Filtering a corpus out of the training data, where the filtering is expensive and the loss isn't recoverable. Big tax, permanent.
8. The same filter, but published as a community norm so everybody loses that data. Not really a tax — the frontier just moved.
9. A policy banning a class of architecture (models that rewrite their own weights during deployment). Costs nothing today, forecloses an option later. Tax, deferred, heavily discounted but not zero.
10. A kill-switch/rollback mechanism whose cost is only ever paid if the model gets widely and successfully deployed; if it flops commercially the switch is never used. Not a tax — you only pay if you already won.
11. An auditable logging requirement that slows inference, and whose cost likewise only bites at scale, only in the success worlds. Tax.
12. An eval regime requiring infrastructure only a frontier lab has: free for us, impossible for a small open team. I want to call this a tax but I also think its a tax on the wrong payer — it's a moat, and I'm not sure it belongs in the same ledger at all.

The tentative principle I've been running is: a measure carries an alignment tax iff a capability-maximizing rival, starting from the same position, ends up with a better model than you because you adopted it. Test that against the list and repair it or tell me to bin it. I already know 2 and 6 are a problem — the principle tells me to net everything into "better model," which handles 2 the way I want but partly offsets 6, and I explicitly dont want 6 offset. And 10 and 11 look identical under it, since both are costs that only materialize in success worlds, but I gave opposite verdicts; my best guess at the difference is that in 10 somebody has to choose to pull the switch whereas in 11 the cost is automatic, and I can't tell whether that's a real principle or just a story I like.

What I actually want: one account, or two or three rival accounts with their prices attached, that fixes the parameters I've been sloppy about — the baseline (rival? absolute frontier? my own do-nothing counterfactual?), the currency (benchmark points, expected value, probability of being ahead, option value?), whose balance sheet it goes on, gross vs net, and at what time. Then tell me which of the twelve verdicts each account reproduces and which it overturns, and for each overturned one say whether the account is wrong or I am. Then rule on two cases I haven't been able to settle: (a) a measure that makes the model markedly worse at persuasion and negotiation, which is exactly the capability the customers are paying for; and (b) a training-time compute cost where the compute would otherwise have gone into a run that was going to be scrapped for unrelated reasons anyway. And be explicit about what each clause is buying — I'd much rather give up two verdicts than adopt a six-clause principle whose only job is to save them.
