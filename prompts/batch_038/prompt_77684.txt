I'm a senior in a philosophy elective, and for our last unit we've been arguing about how sensation relates to value — specifically, whether the badness of pain is just a matter of how it feels. Last summer I broke my wrist skateboarding, and what has stuck with me isn't the injury but a strange split I noticed in the ambulance: I could describe the pain with real precision — where it was, its throbbing quality, roughly its intensity — while at the same time being completely consumed by hating it, as though the describing and the hating were two different people using the same mouth. My teacher mentioned that there's a genuine neurological condition in which that split runs the other way, pain asymbolia: damage to part of the insular region can leave a person's ability to locate and grade pain intact while removing what makes pain awful, so that patients report having pain and simply not being bothered by it, sometimes smiling through it. I want to write a short story built on that dissociation, but I don't want it to read like a medical case study or a cautionary tale about technology, so I've been working up a more specific situation and I'd like you to write the story itself.

The setting I have in mind is a residential rehabilitation facility in the Pacific Northwest for teenagers recovering from orthopedic surgeries and chronic joint conditions. The narrator is seventeen, female, with a degenerative hip that has already cost her a season of crew. The facility is running a reversible pharmacological trial that reproduces the dissociation on demand for a fixed window — the patients and staff call it the Quiet — and patients carry a laminated card on which they mark their pain from zero to ten three times a day. Please build the story around three things that I find harder to dramatize than they sound. First, the Quiet has to be shown working through the numbers: the narrator should keep grading her own pain honestly and accurately, including at moments when she is simultaneously doing something perfectly ordinary like eating lunch or arguing about a television show, and she should never once claim that the pain has stopped. Second, I want a detail that most treatments of this idea skip: her dread of remembered and anticipated pain survives the drug even though her present pain no longer bothers her, so she finds herself flinching at the thought of last year and at next month while remaining unmoved by what is happening to her right now — and I'd like her to notice the oddness of preferring her own memories to her own present. Third, the Quiet must cost her something that has nothing to do with her comfort: she stops protecting the healing site in a way that endangers the surgery she needs, and, more painfully for the story, she finds herself unmoved at a moment when someone else's pain used to move her — please make that second loss land on a specific person and a specific scene rather than as a general observation about empathy. Include one character who loves her and argues sincerely that she is better off now, that the pain she shrugs off was never the evil she took it for, and make that argument good enough that she is partly persuaded by it rather than dismissing it.

Constraints: first person, roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, and the clinical facts should stay in the background — no character may explain her condition, name a brain region, or deliver an exposition dump, and the narrator should never use words like suffering or agony about herself, since part of the point is that her vocabulary for her own state has thinned out. The story must end with a second dose of the Quiet available to her and the reader genuinely unable to predict which way she will go, and that openness has to come from the events rather than from a final paragraph of musing; please don't resolve it with a speech. Don't quote or cite any real researchers, papers, or patient reports, and don't add an author's note defending your choices. After the story, though, give me a separate coda of no more than 150 words answering two questions directly: what exactly did the Quiet take from her, and which account of pain's badness does the story put under pressure — the view that pain is bad because of how it feels, the view that it is bad because of what it disposes us to do, or the view that it is bad because of what it costs us in our relations with other people. If you think the story actually works against the account you'd have guessed I wanted, say so in the coda rather than smoothing it over.
