I've been trying to pin down what I actually believe about the "but my one purchase/my one vote/my one flight changes nothing" line of argument, and my verdicts are a mess. I'm writing something on this for a non-academic audience and I need to know what I think before I can say it. Here's the principle I reach for whenever I want to feel okay about myself: an act is wrong to the extent that it raises the expected amount of harm, and if it raises it by nothing worth mentioning, morality should leave it alone. Now test that against what I say when I'm not trying to feel okay about myself.

1. Buying a chicken breast at the grocery store: not wrong. I know what the industry is like and I know my purchase is part of the demand, but I cannot feel it.
2. Driving forty minutes to the airport to drop someone off instead of paying for a cab: not wrong, not even close.
3. But I've been told the store orders in batches, so that once in a while — maybe one purchase in tens of thousands — somebody's purchase is literally the one that tips the manager into placing an order for a few thousand more chicks. If I knew for sure mine was that one, I think it'd be wrong, and clearly wrong. Some versions of the arithmetic give this the same expected harm as (1). My verdict is not the same.
4. Going to the store specifically to be that person, out of spite at the industry: worse still, though the number of dead chicks is identical either way.
5. The case where a thousand people each turn a dial one notch on a machine wired to a stranger, and no single notch makes any difference he could notice, but all together they're torturing him. Turning the dial is wrong. I'm firm on this one.
6. Buying beer from a brewery I'm confident runs on exploitative labor, in a market where if I don't buy that six-pack someone else buys it that same afternoon: not wrong.
7. Everyone in my town recycles and I've quietly stopped because it's a hassle: wrong, and wrong in a way I can explain instantly — they're all eating the cost, I'm taking the benefit.
8. Emitting carbon along with everybody else, no shared scheme, nobody bearing a cost on my behalf: not wrong in that way at all.
9. Two long-haul flights to go to my best friend's funeral: fine. Two long-haul flights to set a personal round-the-world record: not fine, and I think the identical emissions are exactly the point.
10. Voting in a district decided by thirty points: I should vote, mildly, but skipping it isn't wrong either. Both halves feel stable and I can't get them to come out of one place.
11. Piling my plate at an all-you-can-eat buffet when the leftovers go straight into the trash regardless: wrong.
12. Buying carbon offsets for a flight I'm taking anyway, while privately not believing the offsets do anything: feels like it mostly cancels the flight. I know that's incoherent.

The one I keep going back and forth on: the chicken is already cooked and sitting on the table at somebody's house, and refusing it offends the host and saves nothing. Half the time eating it is obviously fine and refusing is a self-indulgent gesture; half the time refusing is the only thing consistent with 1 through 5.

So: is there a principle — or a small set of them — that gets these, or nearly? State it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and then run it on three cases I haven't listed, including at least one where it delivers a verdict I'd find counterintuitive, because I'd rather see that now than in draft. Then be straight with me about the damage: which of the above does it fail to save, and which would you tell me to give up? I especially want 5 versus 6 taken head-on, since those look structurally identical to me and I just can't feel any difference between them. No readings, no bibliography — the principle and the argument is what I want.

American English, please. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether there's a principle that fits, and roughly what it is — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no repeating my question back at me.
