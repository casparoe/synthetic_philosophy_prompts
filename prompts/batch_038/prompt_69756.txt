I convene a third-year philosophy of science module whose final four weeks are devoted to thought experiments, and I am now setting this year's two-hour written final. Students answer two questions out of five; each question is worth 25 marks and should attract roughly 850 words in about 45 minutes. The cohort has done the standard rehearsal: they have read the case for treating every thought experiment as a picturesque argument whose content comes entirely from premises the reasoner already accepts, and the rival "platonic" case for treating some of them as a non-empirical form of perception of relations among universals. Last year I asked them to apply that debate to Einstein's chase-the-light-beam case and got nineteen near-identical essays. I want this year's question built on a different example and driven into a corner where the labels stop doing the work.

The example is Stevin's wreath of spheres from the late sixteenth century: a closed loop of equally heavy, equally spaced beads draped over a prism whose two sloping faces share the same vertical height but have different lengths, with the lower, freely hanging segment of the loop cut away or ignored as symmetrically self-balancing. If the loop is not in equilibrium, it begins to turn; because the arrangement restores itself exactly after each step, the turning would never cease. Stevin treats endless turning as absurd, infers equilibrium, and then reads off the law of the inclined plane: the pull a body exerts along a slope bears to its full weight the ratio of the slope's height to its length. The crux I want examined is that the suppressed premise here is not an opponent-supplied empirical generalisation of the kind the falling-bodies case relies on. It is either a modal claim about what machines can do, or a symmetry claim about there being no reason to turn one way rather than the other, and those two candidates are not interchangeable: they differ in how they can be known, and they differ in what they license, since the symmetry claim alone seems to deliver equilibrium but not the quantitative ratio. Ask students whether Stevin's argument escapes the charge that thought experiments are just arguments in costume, or merely relocates the empirical commitment into a premise about perpetual motion, and make the question hinge on how the epistemic status of that premise bears on whether any genuinely new content has appeared. Some of my students are physics undergraduates who will be tempted to redo the statics instead of doing the epistemology; the marking scheme should make that cost them.

Please write the question as it should appear on the paper (self-contained enough that a candidate who remembers the setup but not the treatise can answer it, and not requiring any knowledge of publication dates or original-language titles), then a model answer of about 800 words, then a marking scheme out of 25 organised around five must-hit points with the marks each carries and, for each, a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates lose it. Finish with a short paragraph separating a first-class answer from a good-but-ordinary one, where the ordinary answer identifies the suppressed premise correctly but treats its being a priori or inductive as a matter of taste rather than showing what turns on the choice and saying what would count as a thought experiment whose conclusion outran its premises. Everything should paraphrase rather than quote the primary texts, since candidates sit closed-book, and leave the bibliography out entirely; I will attach the readings to the module handbook myself.
