Consider the common prior assumption as it enters Aumann's agreement theorem: two agents who share an antecedent probability distribution over a state space, and who come to commonly know one another's posterior beliefs about some event, cannot assign those posteriors different values. The assumption is ordinarily glossed descriptively — as the claim that the agents in question happen to share a prior, perhaps because of shared ancestry, shared evidence, shared culture, or shared design. On that gloss the theorem looks perilously inapplicable, since actual agents plainly do not share priors, and the whole apparatus appears to prove something about an idealization nobody inhabits.

I want you to argue for a different gloss, and for the consequences it carries. Specifically, argue for the following three claims:

1. The common prior is not a descriptive hypothesis about a population of agents at all, but a coherence constraint on a single agent's counterfactual reasoning across the states of one space — the requirement that an agent's credences in the various states, together with her conditional credences within them, be recoverable by conditioning on one fixed distribution.

2. Understood this way, the agreement theorem is properly a theorem about self-deliberation. It governs what an agent who takes her own reasoning to be reliable can make of her own anticipated future opinions, and of the opinions she attributes to others whom she models as running reasoning she endorses. Its subject matter is intra-personal and modal rather than sociological.

3. This reading rescues the theorem from the heterogeneity objection without weakening it. Nothing in the argument requires that real populations share priors, so the bare empirical fact that people disagree is not evidence against the theorem, and the theorem's status does not depend on the plausibility of a shared-origin story.

Build the case as rigorously as you can. State the formal setting you are working in — the state space, how each agent's information is represented, what a prior and a posterior are in that setting, and what common knowledge of posteriors amounts to — and identify precisely the step at which your reinterpretation of the prior does its work, so that a reader can see what the argument would look like if that step were removed.

Then confront the strongest objections available against you, rather than the weak versions: that the single-agent reading trivializes the theorem by collapsing its two protagonists into one; that a coherence constraint on counterfactual credences is either vacuous or else depends on substantive and contestable assumptions about which propositions are knowable independently of evidence; that the reinterpretation is ad hoc, adopted solely to immunize the theorem from a damaging counterexample; and that any genuinely diachronic or modal reading smuggles in claims about learning and belief revision that the static framework cannot support. For each, say whether you can answer it or whether you concede it, and explain why.

Close with an accounting of what your reading gives up. In particular: the applications people actually care about are interpersonal — what disagreement between two real agents licenses an outside observer to infer. State plainly whether those applications survive your reinterpretation intact, survive it in attenuated form, or are lost altogether, and if they are lost, whether that is a tolerable price for having a theorem that is true.
