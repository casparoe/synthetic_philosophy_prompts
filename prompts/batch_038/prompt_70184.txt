I'm teaching a small graduate seminar this term on formal guarantees for deployed agent behaviour — half complexity theory, half philosophy of risk — and I need one essay/problem question for the final. 60 minutes, 25 marks, closed book, nothing in front of them but the paper. It has to be unanswerable by reciting the usual worst-case-vs-average-case talking points: I want them actually manipulating quantifiers and noticing what a benchmark protocol does and doesn't license.

Here's the apparatus I want baked into the stem (tidy the notation up however you like, but keep the substance and the invented names):

- Our course defintion: a policy π is (ε, δ)-benign relative to a task distribution D just in case Pr_{τ~D}[ harm(π, τ) > ε ] ≤ δ.
- A candidate system, "Verity-4", was run through the Everyday Tasks Battery (ETB): 2,000 curated household and office tasks. The auditing firm's press release says the system "is safe: it behaves benignly with probability at least 98%."
- Two assumptions from week 5: the Coverage Assumption (D is stationary, and does not depend on π's own behaviour) and Bounded Harm (harm ≤ 1, always).
- Last year's student answer, to be quoted in the stem and then taken apart: "if δ is small enough, average-case and worst-case come apart by nothing worth caring about, since the failures live on a set of tiny measure, and you can ignore measure-zero sets."

The question should then have them do five things. (a) Write out with explicit quantifiers the claim the press release actually licenses and the claim everyone hears, and name three distinct gaps between them. (b) Show why one particular gap — Verity-4 shifting which tasks it ends up facing, so that D isn't fixed by nature — cannot be closed by running the ETB more times or making it bigger. (c) Say what extra assumptions you need to get from per-task guarantees to a guarantee uniform across all 2,000 tasks, and explain why the size of the battery is doing much less work than students assume. (d) Address the fact that the lab has now put dozens of candidate systems through the same ETB and retrains on whatever fails, so the 98% no longer means what it looks like it means — I want them to see that adaptive reuse of a fixed benchmark changes the interpretation of the number, without my naming any literature. (e) Say exactly what's wrong with last year's student: the measure-zero conflation, the fact that you don't know where the bad set is even if you know its size, and what happens when Bounded Harm fails.

Deliverables: the question as it would appear on the paper, with the apparatus above typeset into the stem; a model answer of roughly 750 words; and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, split the 25 marks across five must-hit moves, give the mark value of each, and for each add one line on how students typically botch it — e.g. writing the quantifiers in an order that quietly assumes the thing at issue, or reporting a union bound that runs over the wrong index set. Then a short paragraph on what separates an A from a B, described in terms of moves rather than vibes.

Keep it all self-contained: no citations, no suggested reading, no paper titles, no name-dropping real researchers or named theorems. The model answer can note in passing that the structure rhymes with average-case-versus-worst-case reasoning in other fields, but it can't lean on that, and any formal work has to use only notation the stem defines.
