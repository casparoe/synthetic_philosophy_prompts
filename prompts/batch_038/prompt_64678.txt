I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of social science and I'm writing the in-class final: one essay question, 90 minutes, 25 marks, on the gender-equality paradox — the finding that average sex differences in occupational and field-of-study preferences are *larger* in countries that score higher on standard measures of gender equality.

I want the question built around a stipulated argument rather than around the labels "nature" and "culture." Present students with this:

(1) If preferences for gendered occupations were chiefly produced by socialization into local norms, then as a society becomes more gender-equal, male and female preferences should converge.
(2) Across affluent democracies, the more gender-equal the society, the more these preferences diverge.
(3) Therefore these preferences are not chiefly produced by socialization into local norms, and the residual variation tracks sex itself.

The question must require students to (a) isolate the auxiliary assumptions without which (3) does not follow — at minimum, the assumption that the equality index is not simply tracking national wealth, and the assumption that both the index and the preference measure mean the same thing to a respondent in Oslo as to one in Ankara; (b) explain why, even granting the paradox, the conclusion could concern biology, or the affordability of acting on idiosyncratic preferences once material security is high, or the expectations that a reported "choice" already encodes; and (c) sketch one study design capable of discriminating "egalitarianism unmasks real differences" from "affluence lets small initial differences compound."

Please write the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of about 750 words, and a marking scheme listing the must-hit points, the marks each carries out of 25, and the characteristic error each is there to penalize — including the error of treating (3) as a clean modus tollens on socialization, and the error of supposing that rejecting (3) commits one to denying any sex-linked variation at all.
