My friend sits on the board of a climbing club, and they're rewriting how they vote on the access fund — four proposals this year, one winner. Two members, Dev and Marta, went at it during the last meeting and I was there taking notes. Here's roughly how it went:

Dev wants to switch from plurality to ranked-choice, on the grounds that it's the only method that's actually fair. Marta said "fair" is doing no work in that sentence: Arrow's theorem shows there's no ranked method that isn't either a dictatorship or violates something you'd also call fair, so picking between methods is aesthetic — they might as well draw straws, or just go with whichever one the treasurer understands. Dev said Arrow's theorem assumes independence of irrelevant alternatives, which is the axiom everyone agrees is bogus, so the theorem simply doesn't apply to them. Marta said dropping IIA doesn't get you out of the woods, it just relocates you: IIA is exactly what stops a hopeless third option from wrecking the result, and the methods that throw it out — Borda, say — are trivially gamed by running a spoiler, which is a real-world fairness problem and not a formal quibble. Dev said fine, then let people vote with scores or approvals, which captures intensity and steps outside Arrow altogether. Marta had three answers: Arrow is about social *rankings*, not about picking one winner, so "we escaped it" is a category error; scores smuggle in interpersonal utility comparisons, since an 8 from one member and an 8 from another are treated as the same amount of anything; and Gibbard-Satterthwaite says every non-dictatorial method with three or more options is manipulable anyway, so "strategy-resistant" is provably false. Dev said G-S only rules out methods where sincere voting is dominant, that score voting gets gamed far less in practice, and that "manipulable" isn't the same word as "unfair" — the real question is comparative, since every method is gameable to some degree. Dev closed by saying the theorem is about aggregating preferences people already walk in with, not about whether the board lands on a good decision, which is a seperate matter. Marta conceded that but kept her conclusion: since there's no principled way to pick, the board should stop hunting for the fair method, just deliberate, and choose something administrable. Dev said that doesn't follow — an impossibility result is a design constraint that tells you which trade-offs to make explicit, not a license to be indifferent.

They asked me to referee and I'm not qualified. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, who's right more often overall, and what both of them are missing? For each move give a verdict — right, wrong, or half-right — plus how confident you are in the technical claim underneath it. If you're fuzzy about the exact conditions of a theorem or what a result does and doesn't require, say so rather than bluffing; a hedged answer is much more useful to me than a confident wrong one. And if you think my framing is off — that these two aren't really disagreeing about the same thing, or that I've transcribed one of the moves as a strawman nobody would actually say — tell me that straight instead of grading it as posed.
