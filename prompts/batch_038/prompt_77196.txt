so I have to give the farewell speech at my advisor's retirement dinner in about three weeks and I'm pretty stuck on it. He taught strategy and the ethics of nuclear weapons for thirty-some years, and then maybe a decade ago he flipped completely — went from defending deterrence as a tragic necessity to arguing the whole enterprise is a form of mass hostage-taking that no amount of sincerity or good faith on your part makes permissible. Both crowds will be in the room: old colleagues and former students who went into goverment and still think the early work is the serious stuff, people he mentored after the turn, and his wife and three adult kids who basically only ever met version two and find version one pretty grim.

What he actually cared about across both periods was the intention problem. Deterrence only works if the retaliatory threat is real, meaning you would in fact do it; but doing it after the fact would be both pointless and monstrous; so what you have to somehow acquire is a genuine present intention to do something you'd be right never to do. He always insisted this isn't a trick you can pull off with self-deception or by hiring hotheads, because your adversary is also a person, and also reads people. His signiture seminar exercise was making each of us write out, by hand, in the first person, the order we'd have to sign, and then carry it in a wallet for the whole term. He'd say something like: if you can't picture yourself signing it you're not doing the job, and if you can picture it too easily you're not doing the job either.

Constraints, some from him and some from me:

- five minutes or so, out loud, one person at a lectern with a bad microphone
- no solution to the paradox, and no "in the end he chose peace" style landing. He'd get up and leave.
- it can't make either version of him look like a fraud, and it can't imply the flip was guilt or a breakdown or getting soft — his kids have specifically asked me about this
- the people from the field need to hear that I've actually got the argument right and not some movie version of it; the family need to not be bored or frightened
- he despises being thanked for his influence, so nothing about shoulders of giants, and no "he shaped how a generation thinks"

Write the speech for me. The thing I most want handled is the turn between the two halves of his career: I want it done inside the speech rather than stepped around, and I want whatever you say stayed constant across the flip to not collapse into following-the-argument-wherever-it-leads, which is exactly the platitude he'd expect. Also, if you can get the wallet exercise in there without it turning into a spooky story for the grandkids, do that too.
