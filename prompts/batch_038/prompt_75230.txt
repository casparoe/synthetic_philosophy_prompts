I have been invited to present at a one-day workshop on the structure of justification, and the part I am anxious about is not the paper but the discussion. I would like a preparation sheet for the question period, aimed at this room rather than at a general philosophy audience.

My thesis, in outline, is this. Foundationalism fails because a belief held without a reason is arbitrary, and labelling it basic names the difficulty instead of resolving it. Coherentism fails for a reason I think is less often pressed: it cannot account for increases in warrant, since a set of beliefs can be enlarged and made more internally systematic without becoming any better justified on the coherentist's own account. Infinitism, by contrast, is the only one of the three that explains why justification comes in degrees — the further down a chain of reasons a subject can go when pressed, the better justified the belief at the top, with no requirement that the chain ever be completed. Two of my commitments will attract most of the fire. First, I count only available reasons: considerations the subject could articulate on demand but is not presently entertaining, so that a chain can be unfinished and yet determinate in the warrant it confers. Second, and this is where I part company with the standard version of the view, I allow a reason to recur along the chain provided each recurrence is more specific than the last — the same consideration may be offered again at a finer grain of description. I am aware that this looks like circularity wearing a disguise, and I illustrate the whole picture with an analogy to a convergent infinite series, which I expect will be attacked.

The room will be about twenty-five people, mostly formal epistemologists who model justification probabilistically and are entirely comfortable discussing infinite products of conditional probabilities, limits, and whether such a product can converge to something non-zero. Two are committed foundationalists; one has argued for years that infinitism is merely coherentism with the circle unrolled into a line, and will not grant that they are distinct views. One is a coherentist who thinks the foundationalists and I are describing the same structure from opposite ends. The remainder are graduate students who know the trilemma from coursework but not the specialist literature, and one historian of philosophy who is likely to ask what the ancients actually held, which I am not equipped to answer.

Please give me the eight or nine objections I should expect from this audience, ranked by how much damage each would do to the paper if I answered it badly, and phrased in the vocabulary these people actually use — priors, conditionalisation, occurrent versus dispositional states, whether availability is a probability or a resource constraint, whether my series analogy requires a limit that my view cannot supply. For each, give me a reply I could deliver standing up in about two minutes, and then say honestly where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the opening minutes to defuse the predictable attacks, what I must refuse to concede on pain of collapsing into one of the rival views, which single reply has to land for the talk to count as a success, and the objection I am hoping nobody raises, together with an explanation of why my position is least defensible at that point. Please do not give me the standard sceptical objections, or the objection that nobody can complete an infinite task, since I have rehearsed answers to those and will not hear them here.

Write it as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter to a colleague, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, taking the objections one paragraph at a time in the order you have ranked them. Please write in British English throughout.
