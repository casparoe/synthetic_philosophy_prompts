I run the persistence unit every year and I've gone right off the ship itself — the planks version bores my students and honestly bores me. So here's a real one I fell into while helping my brother re-register a barn-find Sprite, and I want someone to think it through with me rather than just reciting the standard moves.

In the UK, if you rebuild an old car or assemble one from a pile of period components, the licensing agency (DVLA) has to decide whether it gets to keep the registration number of the original vehicle — which is what carries the age-related plate, the historic-vehicle tax class, the whole provenance — or whether it gets issued a Q-plate marking its age as indeterminate. And the decision is made by a points test on the original major components: chassis or monocoque bodyshell 5, front and rear suspension 2, both axles 2, transmission 2, steering assembly 2, engine 1. Eight points and you keep the car's identity, i.e. its number. Below that, you don't. And if I'm reading the guidance right, a brand-new replacement bodyshell supplied by the manufacturer scores the same 5 as the original one did.

What view of identity does a scoring rule like that presuppose? Walk me through it as if you were reconstructing an implicit theory from the artefacts alone. Identity here looks gradable, weighted, additive, and threshold-governed — and the standard objection is that identity is all-or-nothing and can't come in degrees at all. So is the agency in the business of asserting a metaphysics, and if so which one, or is it doing something else that merely looks like metaphysics from the outside? I keep going back and forth on this, partly because the Q-plate doesn't actually say "this is a different car"; it says "we can't tell how old this is." That's an evidential verdict, not an ontological one, which makes me suspect the whole thing is anti-fraud bookkeeping dressed as persistence. Tell me where I'm wrong.

Then: which rival accounts could license the practice equally well, so that the points table is underdetermined? And why is the chassis worth five times the engine, when the engine is what enthusiasts treat as the car's soul and what the market actually prices? Can any theory make sense of a newly manufactured bodyshell scoring full marks — that seems to kill every material-continuity reading in one move. And how does all this sit with the fact that the same legal system has no points test whatsoever for a company that has replaced every employee, director, and asset, or for a person who has replaced every cell? If the answer is "artefacts and persons are different kinds of thing," say what the difference is and whether the DVLA's rule is tracking it or just happening to fit.

Finally, tell me what the practise would have to look like if we swapped in a view on which the frame has no special claim — and pick a side on whether the agency is doing metaphysics badly or not doing metaphysics at all. No reading list, please; I know where the bodies are buried.
