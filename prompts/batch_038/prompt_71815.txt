I am putting together a chapter on the prehistory of what Hofstadter called superrationality, and I want your judgment on a pairing that I suspect is more than a decorative anachronism. Set the doctrine out as precisely as you can first: in a symmetric one-shot prisoner's dilemma, a superrational thinker reasons that whatever move she settles on, her counterpart will settle on the same move, because the counterpart is running the same kind of reasoning about the same problem; only the two symmetric outcomes are therefore live, and cooperation dominates. Note where the standard objections bite — that it illicitly treats the two decisions as one, that it smuggles a causal connection into what is only an evidential correlation, that the symmetry collapses the moment the players differ in their information or their reasoning styles — because I want to know whether these objections land differently inside a Spinozist metaphysics. Then take up the question I actually care about: would Spinoza have been a superrationalist? He is an attractive candidate for reasons that have nothing to do with game theory. Reason, on his account, is one and the same in everyone; insofar as people are led by reason they agree in nature and are most useful to one another, while insofar as they are driven by the passions they are contrary to one another; and the good that a rational person seeks for himself he seeks also for others. Part IV even argues that the free man never acts deceitfully but always in good faith, and the extended discussion attached to that thesis considers whether reason could ever dictate breaking faith and answers by asking what would follow if everyone reasoned the same way about the same case — which looks structurally like the superrational move. He is also a strict determinist who denies free will, which seems to give him an asset Hofstadter lacks: if reasoning is a natural process like any other, identical procedures under identical descriptions really do yield identical outputs, and no residue of freedom is available to break the symmetry.

Here is the case I want you to run him through, stated in his own vocabulary rather than ours. Two individuals, no sovereign above them, no shared power capable of compelling performance, no prospect of future encounters, and no reputation at stake. Each faces the ordinary cooperative and defective options; mutual cooperation is moderately costly to each and greatly beneficial to both, mutual defection leaves both worse off, and unilateral exploitation kills the one who is exploited. Now add the twist Spinoza could not have imagined: each of the two has been given a complete and reliable description of the other's decision procedure, and each knows that the other has been given a description of his own, so the symmetry of reasoning is not a hopeful assumption but a known fact about the world. What does Spinoza's free man do, and why? I want the answer teased apart into at least three possibilities and an assessment of which is most likely his: that he cooperates on something like the superrational inference; that he cooperates but the inference never gets a grip, because for someone actually governed by reason the other's preservation is already his own good and the situation was never a dilemma in the first place; or that he refuses to rely on the counterpart's rationality at all, since elsewhere Spinoza insists that in politics one must not count on people being rational, that right of nature extends exactly as far as power, and that agreements bind only while they remain useful — a realism that might make the description of the other's procedure irrelevant, because what matters is what the other's affects will do. Finally, extend the question to the many-person version: a symmetric collective enterprise, such as a shared defense or a common work, in which each individual's contribution looks causally negligible. Does the superrational inference scale for Spinoza, or is this precisely the point at which he hands the problem over to institutions, law, and the engineering of the affects?

Please structure the response as a reasoned ascription, and keep three registers visibly separate throughout: claims you think Spinoza's own texts support, extrapolations that are consistent with his system but that he never made, and points at which he would probably reject the framing itself — for instance the treatment of reason as a decision procedure one consults, or the treatment of virtue as instrumental to self-preservation rather than identical with the power one expresses. Where you invoke a Spinozist thesis, say which part of his work it comes from and how directly it bears on the case. Then, at the end, tell me how confident you are in the ascription you have made, on a rough scale, and single out the one claim about Spinoza you are least sure of — the place where you suspect you are reconstructing him into a contemporary debate he would not have recognized. Say what sort of finding, textual or argumentative, would most change your mind about that claim.
