I'm a fifth grade teacher by some weird accident of life also a member of our county's volunteer swiftwater rescue team — eleven people, two boats, one very loud group chat. I finished probation last spring. Ray has run the team for nineteen years and is handing it over in March because his hip is finally shot, and the county emergency management folks now want our dispatch/triage decisions written down as an actual protocol document, since the next crew reading it might be doing it at 3am in the rain and won't have Ray in the boat with them. Because I "have words" I got handed the job of drafting it. The problem is I've been on maybe nine callouts with him and I keep a notebook from debriefs, so I have a pretty good record of what he decided, but I cannot for the life of me tell you what rule he's running. Here's the track record as best I can reconstruct it:

- Ice storm, two calls at the same time, same distance, both in moving water: a solo paddler pinned against a culvert, and three people on a porch roof. Everyone hypothermic, nobody obviously worse off. Ray flipped a quarter for it in the parking lot. Coin said porch. Nobody argued and nobody thought it was strange.
- Two years ago: one unconscious woman in a flooded car versus five people standing on a highway median who were cold, soaked and swearing at us. He put everything into the car and left the five for the second unit. When I asked about it later he said he wasn't doing math, he was doing "who's going to be dead if we're wrong."
- A house collapse with twelve people in moving water versus one guy on a fence post, all equally reachable, all equally bad off. He went straight for the twelve, no coin, no hesitation. A rookie asked afterward whether they should have flipped for it. Ray said "maybe. I couldn't have." That was the whole answer.
- Winter tree-well call: one buried skier with a strong beacon signal and maybe twenty minutes, three people alive and digging in a tree well a couple hundred yards off. Ray split the team four and four instead of concentrating everyone on the beacon. All four came out. At debrief he said he split it because "I'm not the guy who picks which of those parties loses."
- Two victims in the same reach, both drowning: a 24 year old and a 78 year old with a bad heart. He refused to weigh it, sent one boat to each, and told the rookie who suggested the younger one was worth more "we don't do that. We don't rank people in the water."
- A call where the single victim turned out to be another team member's wife, with two teenagers further downriver who were both alive and hugging a tree. Boat went to the wife. The teens were fine when the second unit got there, but Ray said afterward that if they hadn't been he'd have made the same call and he wasn't going to apologize for it.
- Eight people tubing a closed section that flooded versus one motorist swept off a bridge by the same water. He went for the eight, and when someone muttered about them having it coming, he said he doesn't sort out whose fault it is while anybody's in the river.

Every one of those looks like arithmetic to me until I line it up next to the quarter in the parking lot, and then none of them look like arithmetic. The county template scenario I have to respond to is this: a levee overtops overnight and there are two locations and, in the window, only one boat crew and one helicopter slot. Location A is a school bus that went into a drainage ditch in near-freezing water with 26 high schoolers and a driver on it; the roof is holding, fire department can get warm air to them, and the realistic assessment is that nobody dies but if they aren't out in roughly three hours most of them loose limbs and a couple lose vision. Location B is a single man in a car in a deeper channel who goes under in about twenty five minutes and no other unit can reach him in time. Ray will be in surgery that week, so I need to do two things at once: write what the protocol says, and predict what he'll say when he reads it.

So — what are the two or three genuinely different rules that are consistent with most or all of those calls? State each one the way I could actually put it in a document, and tell me which of his decisions, if any, each rule strains on, because my gut says the quarter and the split team don't come from the same place as the twelve on the house collapse. Then tell me what each rule predicts for the bus scenario, and give me your best guess about Ray with the reasons you'd bet on it. And here's the part I keep going in circles on: some of these candidate rules might be impossible to tell apart by looking at what he does and only differ in what he'd say about somebody else's identical choice — whether flipping was allowed, whether the twelve were owed a reason, whether the coin was respect or cowardice. If that's true I want you to say so bluntly, because a protocol can only write down actions, not reasons, and if two rules generate the same actions in every case then for my purposes they're one rule. If there's a single scenario I could put to Ray in the parking lot before his surgery that would separate them, give me that scenario. And if the rules really do diverge on the bus, tell me what the team would be committing itself to by writing down each version — because whoever crew's that call next February has never met him.
