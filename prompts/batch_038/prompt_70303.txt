Ok so background: I'm not an academic, I run a small freight brokerage outside Cleveland and basically all my reading happens in cabs and hotel rooms, but I've been chewing on the ethics of climate change by myself for a couple of years now. A buddy of mine who writes a semi-popular energy newsletter sent me a podcast episode where the guest — an economist who does a lot of magazine writing — laid out what he called "the argument that ends the debate about future generations." I transcribed the chunk that got under my skin, more or less word for word:

> "Parfit's non-identity problem basically dissolves the whole question of what we owe people who haven't been born yet. Here's the mechanism. Which particular individuals come into existence depends on absurdly fine details of timing — who sleeps with whom, on which night, so which sperm meets which egg. Change anything at all over the next fifty years and you get a completely different cast of people a century out, and that includes the climate policies themselves: a serious decarbonization program rearranges everyone's lives enough that the people living in 2180 are not, person for person, the people who would have been living there had we done nothing. Now take the actual people in the hot world. Is their life worse than it would otherwise have been? There is no 'otherwise' — the only alternative for each of them is never having existed, and by stipulation their lives are still worth living. So we haven't made them worse off, and if you hold, as most of us do, that you can only wrong someone by making them worse off, then we haven't wronged them either. Parfit's own illustration is the energy choice: the cheaper, dirtier policy gives us a slightly better life now and leaves a degraded world a couple of centuries out, and a person-affecting morality literally cannot call that the wrong pick. He found that result intolerable and tried to escape by way of impersonal value — the world being worse overall rather than worse for anyone — but he couldn't get that to work either, because impersonal theories tip into the repugnant conclusion, where a colossal population living lives barely worth living beats a small glorious one. So the honest landing spot is that our duties run to people who are alive now or already on the way. The far future is a different-people puzzle, not a harm, and the economists arguing about discount rates are fighting over a number that isn't doing any moral work anyway."

I want an accuracy audit I can actually use, not a general essay on population ethics. Can you go through it claim by claim and sort each one into (a) straightforwardly right, (b) a fair simplification of something more qualified, or (c) wrong or a conflation? A few things I specifically want pinned down, because I can feel that they're doing work but I can't say what: whether "we haven't made them worse off," "we haven't harmed them," and "we haven't wronged them" are actually three separate claims in this literature or just one claim said three times, and if they're separate, which of the three the guest has actually earned by the end. Whether the baseline business holds up — is "never existing" even a legitimate thing to compare someone's welfare against, or is that a category error that the whole passage is resting on? Whether "a person-affecting morality literally cannot call that the wrong pick" is Parfit's view or a stronger claim than Parfit made, since I vaguely remember reading that he thought the dirty policy was still wrong and that the problem was supposed to embarrass a principle rather than excuse a policy. And whether the bit about him trying impersonal value and failing is right — is the repugnant conclusion a problem for all impersonal theories or just some, and does "I can't yet construct a satisfactory impersonal theory" really license "so we owe the far future nothing"?

Then tell me which premises are load-bearing in a way he doesn't advertise — I keep coming back to "by stipulation their lives are still worth living," which sounds like a technicality but seems to be exactly what a bad-enough climate trajectory puts in doubt, and I'd like to know whether the arguement flips if it fails. Also: does the final conclusion about duties only running to the living and the already-conceived follow from the parts that are true, or is it an extra step he smuggles in, and what does he have to add or drop to make it follow? What about people who are alive right now, including actual grandchildren — does any of the non-identity machinery even touch them? And finally, what's the strongest reply available to someone who wants to defend obligations to the far future without just denying the non-identity logic outright — I'd rather know the good counters than be told the whole thing is confused. I'm inclined to think we obviously owe something to people who haven't been born, but I don't want to be holding onto that just because it feels nicer, so please be blunt about where he's right and I'm wrong.
