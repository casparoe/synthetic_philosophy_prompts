I have read the futarchy literature since it consisted of a couple of blog posts and a draft paper, and I have grown tired of the two default moves that get made whenever Condorcet's name comes up in connection with it. The enthusiasts say he would have adored prediction markets because he was the first person to prove that aggregation beats individual judgment; the skeptics say he would have hated them because markets weight voices by wealth rather than counting them. Both are one-liners, and neither survives contact with what he actually wrote. I want a real reconstruction instead.

Suppose we could brief the Marquis de Condorcet — specifically the Condorcet of 1792–93, the mathematician turned legislator drafting a constitution, not the textbook name attached to a voting paradox — on governance by prediction markets. He is given the standard design: an elected body settles on a measure of national well-being, and speculative markets then determine which policies to adopt, under the slogan "vote on values, bet on beliefs." He is also given the standard objections: that values and beliefs may not be separable in the way the design requires; that such markets would be thin, slow, and vulnerable to manipulation by actors who profit from the decision itself; and that no comprehensive welfare metric may be definable at all. Assume he understands the mathematics immediately and does not need the finance explained to him twice. What is his considered verdict?

I would like you to work through the following pressure points, in roughly this order, and to reach a determination on each rather than merely noting the tension.

First, independence. The 1785 result concerns a binary question decided by majority rule, where each voter is independently correct with probability above one half, and the probability that the majority is correct rises toward certainty as the body grows. But in his political and revolutionary writings he develops a considerably richer notion of independence than the theorem's: independence of mind, yes, but also social independence — freedom from dependence on particular persons, patrons, and factions — and he treats independence less as a precondition for admitting people to participate than as something democratic practice is supposed to produce. Prediction markets look like the inverse on both counts. The price is precisely the signal that tells a trader what to think, and the weight of a voice is a function of capital, which is to say of one's standing in relations of economic dependence. Does this defeat his enthusiasm outright, or would he reply that aggregation still functions because the residual errors are uncorrelated even when the information is not? Take a position.

Second, the architecture of the aggregation. There is a result in market microstructure, from 1980, holding that a perfectly informationally efficient market is impossible: if prices fully reflected all available information, no one could profit from gathering it, so no one would, and the market would unravel. Efficiency therefore requires leaving rents for the informed, which means the epistemic work gets done by a relatively small number of well-capitalized specialists rather than by a great many small contributors. That inverts the structure of the jury theorem, which derives near-certainty from the multiplication of cheap, fallible, independent judgments. Ask Condorcet which of these two machines he regards as the better truth-tracker, and whether he would notice that they are not rival answers to the same question — one produces a probability that a binary verdict is correct, the other a point estimate of a continuous quantity. Does that difference matter to him, or is it a distinction without a difference from the standpoint of someone who thought moral and political questions could be made calculable?

Third, the values/beliefs split itself. Futarchy concedes the question of what makes a society good to ordinary politics and reserves only the empirical question for markets. Condorcet was not obviously a person who thought the normative question was beyond calculation. Would he accept the division of labor, or would he want the welfare metric subjected to the same treatment as everything else? Relatedly: the design requires the metric to be stable enough that market prices across time and policy are comparable, which is why the literature has had to bolt on veto mechanisms and agenda auctions. His own constitutional thinking was much more concerned with how a polity revises its fundamental arrangements over time. Would he regard a fixed, legislatively chosen welfare index as a species of constitutional rigidity he would reject?

Fourth, posterity. Prediction markets are liquid over horizons of months to perhaps a few years, and the futarchic literature treats this as a technical problem about discounting. The historical sketch of human progress that he wrote while in hiding, and which was published after his death, is addressed to generations not yet born and rests on the idea of an indefinite perfectibility. Trading requires a counterparty, a resolution date, and collateral; none of these can be supplied by people who do not exist. Is this for him a fatal structural objection — a form of government that cannot represent the unborn because the unborn cannot hold positions — or would he say that the same objection applies to any decision procedure whatsoever, voting included, and that futarchy is merely more honest about it?

Fifth, and finally, put him to a concrete decision. Here is a proposal: a national well-being index is set by the legislature for a thirty-year term, revisionable only by a supermajority; every policy proposal above a spending threshold must be evaluated by a decision market comparing expected index values with and without adoption; a market maker of last resort provides liquidity where trading is thin; and the elected assembly retains a veto it may exercise only by publishing its reasons. Would Condorcet sign this? If he would amend it, say which clause and what he would substitute. If he would refuse it, say what he would build instead, given the same instruments.

Some requirements on the answer:

- Give me a determinate verdict — endorse, endorse with named amendments, or reject — together with your confidence in it. A catalogue of cross-cutting considerations is not a verdict, and I can produce one myself.
- Distinguish, explicitly and as you go, between what you can anchor in positions Condorcet actually held and what is extrapolation from the general shape of his thought. Label the extrapolations.
- Handle the anachronism honestly. He has no concept of a derivative, a market maker, or an informationally efficient price. Say which of his existing concepts you are using to do the work in each case, and flag anywhere you suspect you are simply translating a modern idea into period costume.
- Assume I know the outline of his life and the standard history. Do not spend words on biography or on the Terror except where it bears on the argument.
- Around fifteen hundred words. No references, bibliography, or citation list; I will supply those myself.

Close with a short section on what would change your mind. Name the one or two considerations — a view Condorcet demonstrably held, an argument about how decision markets resolve in practice, a premise of the jury theorem read differently — that would move your verdict substantially or reverse it. Then say plainly where you think this reconstruction is weakest: at which point you are most likely to be projecting a contemporary rationalist's preferences onto an eighteenth-century mathematician, and what a competent historian would say to you about it.
