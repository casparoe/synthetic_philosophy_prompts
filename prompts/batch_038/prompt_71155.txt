Help me with a teaching example. I want a dialogue, maybe 700-900 words, between two equally sharp philosophers arguing about a proposed fix to Nozick's utility monster. The fix, which one of them is defending: stop comparing people by raw welfare levels altogether, since levels already assume a shared scale that may not exist, and instead score each person by where they sit within the range of welfare they're actually capable of. Someone at the top of a modest range then counts for more than a monster lounging at a comfortable midpoint of an enormous one — and the monster only gets a huge claim if it's also capable of enormous misery and is sitting near it.

Character A defends that. Character B thinks it's a disaster, and I want B pressing the real difficulties, not strawmen: there's no principled way to fix the endpoints of someone's range; capacities are open-ended and changable, so the view makes it better for you never to develop your potential, and worse for you the moment you do; and it can require preferring a world where everyone is mediocre but maxed out over a world where everyone is much happier but below their ceiling. A should hit back just as hard — that B's raw-level comparison is every bit as unmeasurable and quietly assumes the monster's own reports of bliss are trustworthy, that we normalize against a range constantly anyway (grading on a curve, pain scales anchored to a patient's own baseline, hedonic adaptation research), and that B's last complaint isn't a bug at all but the whole point of the view.

Nobody wins. Have them actually answer each other as the exchange develops instead of trading set speeches, and let at least one of them get genuinely shaken and revise part of their position halfway through.
