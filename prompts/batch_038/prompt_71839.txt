I'm helping run an interdepartmental reading group — mostly philosophy people, plus two economists who are quite smug about it — and we've just spent a fortnight on Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal. The discussion has got stuck in a rut I want to break: roughly half the room think the result is an artefact of the examples. Their line is that once you strip out the prudes, the lewd, the spiteful neighbours and the people who have strong opinions about what colour your house is painted, there's nothing left, because the paradox only bites when the profiles are populated with horrible or squeamish types who mind other people's private business. I want to put a case in front of them they can't wave away like that, and I want it tight enough that we can check the whole thing on the whiteboard in ten minutes.

So, build me a case to order. Two people, four social states, structured as one binary personal feature for each of them (A's feature and B's feature), with each person decisive over the pair of states that differ only in their own feature. Constraints:

1. Realistic and small-scale — an office, a flatshare, a family, a club, whatever — but no sci-fi, no organ allocation committees, no electoral systems.
2. The meddling must not be moralistic, prudish, religious or spiteful. No disapproval, no malice, no wanting the other to suffer. Give each of them a motive an ordinary, reasonably pleasant person could genuinely have: conformism, a snob's itch for distinction, fastidiousness about mismatch, nostalgia, a superstition, a personal project, that sort of thing. Say which motive you've used and why it's psychologically believable.
3. The two features have to be personal in the sense that actually matters here. Nothing about A's choice has any material effect on B or vice versa — no noise, no smell, no cost, no shared resource, no reputational spill-over. The only thing connecting them is that each happens to have preferences about the other's feature. This matters because I want to pre-empt the escape route where somebody says "well, then it isn't really his personal sphere, so make him waive the right".
4. Ban the usual suspects: no books read or unread, no clothing or paint colours, no underwear, nothing sexual, nothing drug-related.
5. It has to generate a genuine cycle in the social preference — not merely a consistent ordering whose top state somebody happens to hate, and not merely the old "the rights-respecting outcome is Pareto-inferior" punchline. And at least one link in the cycle has to come from Pareto rather than from somebody's right, so it's a properly Paretian-liberal cycle and not just a libertarian one.

Then show me the working rather than asserting it works: name the four states, give each person's full ranking over all four with a one-line reason for the shape of the ranking rather than a bare ordering, and go step by step through which pairwise social ranking is generated by which condition, tracing the cycle explicitly and flagging at each step whether the link came from L or from P. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph; the working can take as much room as it needs.

After that, three things. First, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work and what breaks if I remove it — in particular, does the cycle survive if both people have the same kind of motive (two conformists, or two snobs), and if not, what does that say about where the real difficulty sits? Second, tell me how someone who thinks of rights as side constraints on what may be done, rather than as inputs to a social-preference-generating machine, would say I've misdescribed the situation from the outset, and whether that reply actually rescues my group's "it's just nasty preferences" diagnosis or simply refuses to play the game. Third, and this is a seperate question we've been arguing about all week: do the two famous examples from the literature — the book one and the neighbours one — actually produce a cycle, or do they only produce the Pareto-inferior-outcome result? I have a suspicion people are conflating the two and I'd like to settle it.

British English throughout, please. And don't bother with references or a reading list, I've got the paper — I just want the case and the reasoning.
