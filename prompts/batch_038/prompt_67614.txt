I lead a small infrastructure team at a freight-logistics company, and for the last year I've been having a recurring argument with two friends over Sunday coffee about how a rational person should evaluate a risky bet. One of them holds that if a gamble is going to be repeated over your life, what matters is the long-run time-average growth rate of your wealth — the average per-round multiplier along the single trajectory you actually live — and that maximizing the expected value of a payoff across an imaginary ensemble of parallel yous is a category error, a claim he says follows from the mathematics of the process and not from any taste for risk-aversion. The other holds that this whole business is expected-utility theory wearing a costume: that the time-average growth rate of a multiplicatively evolving bankroll is nothing other than the expectation of the logarithm of the growth factor, so his opponent has simply assumed a logarithmic utility function and then announced that he derived it from dynamics. I find myself unable to adjudicate, because I can't produce a single clean example where the two criteria give different answers and it's obvious which criterion is being violated. I'd like you to build me that example, or the most honest substitute for it.

The specification: a concrete decision problem in an ordinary setting with ordinary money and hand-checkable numbers, in which an agent who maximizes expected utility with a logarithmic utility function and an agent who maximizes the asymptotic time-average growth rate of their wealth commit different amounts of money to the same opportunity. The decision should be a fraction of the bankroll to stake rather than a yes-or-no, so that each view outputs a number I can put side by side, and neither number should be the degenerate zero or all-in. Both agents have to be internally consistent: state explicitly what quantity each is tracking and over what horizon, and don't let the divergence arise from one of them carelessly applying their rule to a variable they don't actually care about. Hard constraints: no borrowing, no leverage, no insurance market or obliging counterparty on the other side of the bet, and no case where the entire divergence is manufactured by the minus infinity that total ruin contributes to a logarithm — my second friend has already warned me that any example resting on that is cheating. If after working through it you conclude that the two criteria cannot be made to come apart under constraints like these, say so plainly and give me the nearest thing instead: a case that looks like a genuine divergence to a careful reader but dissolves under a stated reinterpretation, with the dissolution worked out in full detail rather than asserted.

Either way I need the arithmetic and the analysis. Show the two calculations side by side, and add a third line for a risk-neutral expected-value maximizer so I can see where that agent lands relative to the other two. Identify the single structural feature of the case that drives any wedge between the log-utility agent and the growth-rate agent, and demonstrate that removing it collapses the case back into one where they agree. Tell me what the horizon is doing: whether the result survives at ten rounds, at one round, and in the limit, and whether the disagreement between my friends only becomes visible at some particular horizon. Then give each of them their strongest attack on the case you've built — specifically, what the "it's just logarithmic utility relabeled" person will say the apparent divergence really consists in, and what the "it's a fact about dynamics, not a preference" person will say about why his criterion is not a utility function at all and therefore cannot be reproduced by choosing one. Finish with your own verdict, in a few sentences I could read aloud at the table: is this dispute substantive or verbal, and if it's substantive, what observation would settle it?
