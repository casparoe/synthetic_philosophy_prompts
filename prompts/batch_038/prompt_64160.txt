My friend and I argued for most of last night about Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973), and I would like a considered arbitration rather than a summary of the usual positions.

The standard reading — the one every introductory ethics anthology gives — is that the story is a reductio of utilitarianism: a city whose joy is purchased with the torment of one locked-up child shows that aggregative reasoning licenses monstrosity, and those who walk away are the heroes who refuse the calculus. My friend's counter-reading is Buddhist, and it runs on two levels. First, she says the story is not about aggregation at all but about craving: the citizens' happiness is not well-being, because it is dependent, impermanent, and sustained by attachment to a condition they know to be contingent — so a Buddhist consequentialist scores Omelas far worse than a utilitarian does, and for reasons that have nothing to do with the child's line in the ledger. Second, she says the ones who walk away are failures rather than heroes, since the bodhisattva's vow is precisely to remain with the suffering being instead of seeking private release; walking away, on her account, is the arahant's exit dressed up as virtue.

My own position is a third one, and I want it tested rather than defended: that the utilitarian and Buddhist readings do not actually disagree about the child, or about aggregation, or about what one ought to do. They disagree about the welfare metric — about whether the citizens' joy is the sort of state that counts as well-being in the first place. On that view the story's real target is an assumption shared by William James, whose essay "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life" Le Guin credited as the tale's inspiration, and by the utilitarians he was attacking: that happiness is a quantity available for summation.

What I need from you:

1. Work through the story's particulars against each reading — the Festival of Summer, the citizens' knowledge of the child, the child's settled belief that its situation is permanent and that no help is coming, the narrator's remark about what would become of it if released, and the departures at dusk, singly and in silence. Say what each ethical framework does with each detail, and which framework has to strain to accommodate it.

2. Tell me where the story resists the Buddhist reading. Its narrator repeatedly invites the reader to invent details and insists the people of Omelas are neither simple nor naive, and Le Guin's own declared sympathies ran to the Tao Te Ching rather than to any Buddhist school. Is a Buddhist reading available on the page, or is it being imported by readers who want one?

3. Rule on my metric thesis. Is the dispute genuinely about what counts as well-being, or is my friend also making a substantive claim about the child that utilitarianism could not accept — and can Buddhist ethics be consequentialist in the sense the story requires, given that it denies the enduring self for whom consequences accumulate?

4. Close with what the story contributes that neither the ethics literature nor James's thought experiment delivers — something the narrative form does that an argument cannot.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words. Open with a two-sentence verdict on whose reading is better supported, then argue for it. Do not quote the story — I have it in front of me — so describe scenes instead, and please no bibliography or list of further sources.
