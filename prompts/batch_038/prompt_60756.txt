I am a second-year master's student in public policy, currently taking a seminar on performance measurement and program evaluation, and working as a research assistant on a project that evaluates quality incentives in state-funded programs. Over the past few months I keep encountering a pattern for which my reading so far has not given me a handle, and I would like to know whether it is an established concept in the literature on Goodhart's law and specification gaming — and if it is not, what it should be called and how it should be defined.

The pattern is this. An organization adopts a measure because the thing it actually cares about cannot be measured directly. The measure is imperfect, and the imperfection gets exploited: the people being measured optimize the indicator in ways that leave the underlying goal flat, or make it worse. The organization's response is not to abandon measurement but to add a correction — and the correction is itself another measure: an audit rate, a second indicator designed to catch manipulation of the first, a monitor of the monitor. The correction works in the narrow sense that the specific distortion it targeted disappears. But the distortion reappears one level up. The new measure is now the thing under optimization pressure, and it is corrupted in a way the measure below it cannot detect. So each round of correction fixes the previous abuse and produces a new one that sits further from the original goal and is harder to observe than the last, while the organization experiences itself as steadily improving its measurement. The gap between what is counted and what was wanted does not close; it relocates and deepens, and every individual step along the way is locally defensible.

Three instances I have in mind.

First, hospitals penalized for readmitting patients within thirty days. The response that gets discussed is to keep patients off the inpatient count altogether: observation-status stays, treatment in outpatient settings, longer initial admissions. Once oversight bodies notice and begin tracking observation use, the substitution moves somewhere the new indicator does not reach — discharge to affiliated post-acute facilities, or greater selectivity about which patients are admitted at all.

Second, research assessment. Papers were counted; then citations; then an index summarizing both; then, because all of that was seen as gaming-prone, narrative statements about contribution plus expert review of whole dossiers. The narrative statement promptly acquires a genre of its own, with templates and professional drafting help, and the expert reviewers turn out to be calibrated against much the same prestige signals the reform was supposed to bypass.

Third, a machine-learning case that I am told is the same phenomenon but am not sure is. A policy optimizes a hand-written proxy reward; the proxy is replaced by a learned reward model trained on human comparisons; the policy then finds inputs on which the reward model is confidently wrong; the fix is an ensemble with an uncertainty penalty; the policy then exploits whatever blind spot the ensemble members share.

My question is not mainly about the label, and a bare name would not satisfy me. I want the carving:

- Which established concepts come closest, and how does this differ from each? I have considered Goodhart's law itself and the corresponding idea in social science about indicators being corrupted once they carry stakes, but both seem to describe only the first step — the corruption of a given indicator — rather than the migration of corruption through a hierarchy of corrective indicators. I have also wondered whether this is simply an infinite-regress argument against measurement, or ordinary whack-a-mole, and I suspect neither is quite it. Please say what does fit and where each candidate runs out.
- Does the regress have a principled stopping point? If some corrections genuinely terminate the process rather than relocating the problem, what distinguishes them: that they measure the goal directly, that they are not published to the agents being measured, that they are expensive to manipulate, that they rest on judgment rather than aggregation, or something else? If no correction terminates it, say so and explain what follows.
- What falls outside the phenomenon? I want the boundary cases stated explicitly — a correction that works and keeps working, a measure that merely goes stale through no one's optimizing, two indicators that trade off against each other with no hierarchy between them, and manipulation by an agent who is deliberately hostile rather than merely responsive to incentives.

If there is no established term, please say so plainly and then coin one: give a definition, state the conditions under which it applies, distinguish it from the nearest existing concepts, and offer one case that sits right at the edge of the definition, explaining which side of the line it falls on and why.
