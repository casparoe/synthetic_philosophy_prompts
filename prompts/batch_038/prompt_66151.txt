I am in the fourth year of a PhD in political theory and, for the past two, I have been coding the published opinions of an ad hoc international body — call it the Commission on Territorial Status — that was constituted in 2014 under a regional compact to issue advisory determinations on claims to independence, to secession from a would-be successor state, and to border change. Its opinions are reasoned, they cite one another, and although the seven-member panel rotates for each determination, the citations give the record a continuity I want to exploit. My chapter argues that the Commission is converging on a single implicit principle. My supervisor argues that it is running two or three principles opportunistically and that the appearance of unity is an artefact of how the opinions are written. I need the next determination predicted as honestly as possible, from the record alone — I have the secondary literature and do not want citations or a bibliography, only reasoning about my own case summaries, which compress opinions of some forty pages each.

The record so far, with the operative facts as the Commission found them:

1. **Kavna (2016).** A peripheral, linguistically distinct region whose autonomous assembly had been dissolved by executive decree and whose language had been removed from public schooling four years before the claim. A monitored referendum on a single-sentence question — "Should Kavna become an independent state?" — returned 68 per cent in favour on 71 per cent turnout. The Commission determined that Kavna had a right to enter negotiated independence talks, and added, in a paragraph the later opinions keep citing, that any state resulting from such talks would itself be bound to extend equivalent internal self-determination to concentrated communities within its territory.

2. **Marren Delta (2017).** A prosperous coastal province, culturally and linguistically continuous with the rest of the state, with no history of discrimination, no suspended institutions, and no complaint about representation. Its referendum question bundled independence with unspecified currency, debt, and border arrangements and returned 55 per cent in favour on 64 per cent turnout. Marren handles the state's largest container port and roughly a third of its corporate tax base. The Commission determined that no right to secede arose, on two grounds: the mandate was not sufficiently determinate, and the consequences would fall heavily on citizens of the remainder who had neither a vote in the matter nor any route to contest it.

3. **Tirn Valley (2019).** A district inside Kavna whose population had voted 79 per cent against independence in Kavna's own referendum, which asked to remain with the central state rather than join a Kavnan successor. Kavna's regional government objected that territorial integrity now ran in its favour. The Commission determined for Tirn, holding that a claimant which invokes self-determination cannot withhold it from a concentrated community inside its own claimed borders, and relying expressly on the paragraph quoted above from Opinion 1.

4. **Sarnia (2020).** A distinct linguistic people of roughly 1.9 million, dispersed across four provinces and a majority nowhere, with a declining language and cut cultural funding, but enjoying full citizenship, unrestricted franchise, and parliamentary representation; the Commission found no systematic rights violations. They sought a right to independence exercised through an internationally administered trusteeship over a patchwork of majority-Sarnian districts. Refused: territorial concentration was held to be a precondition of that remedy, and lesser remedies were available and unexhausted.

5. **Ostmark (2021).** A distant exclave where the central government had pursued a decade of settlement and where the Commission found grievance of the gravest kind — arbitrary detention, expropriation, disenfranchisement of the local electorate. A referendum on a clear single question returned 51.4 per cent in favour, but under a state of emergency, with the principal opposition outlets suspended and roughly a third of the pre-existing electorate unable to return home to vote. The Commission refused to endorse secession, holding that a vote taken in those conditions cannot constitute an expression of self-determination, and that the grievance, however grave, could not be permitted to substitute for the procedure. It urged interim protective measures instead.

6. **Bela (2022).** A small, poor inland region with a documented decade of discriminatory allocation of infrastructure and health spending, no suspension of its institutions, and full franchise. It voted 78 per cent in favour on a clear question under observation. Bela receives central transfers equal to about 22 per cent of its regional product, and the interior's only deep-water port — through which the remainder state exports most of its grain — sits on its coast. The Commission determined that Bela had a right to negotiate independence, but conditioned its exercise on a ten-year transition, a joint authority over the port, and a settlement of the pension liabilities, holding that a determination which strands dependent populations or forecloses the remainder's essential infrastructure cannot take effect as an immediate right.

The case now before them concerns **Vessa**, an island province of 1.4 million with its own language and legal tradition. The central government suspended Vessa's statute in 2020, arrested three members of its executive council on charges later dropped, and during an epidemic imposed a maritime cordon that the Commission's own fact-finders described as disproportionate and that interrupted insulin and dialysis supplies for eleven weeks. Vessa then held a referendum on a clear single-sentence question under international observation: 71 per cent in favour on 78 per cent turnout, with the main pro-union parties participating and not contesting the count. Vessa is fiscally self-sufficient. Three features complicate it. First, about 22 per cent of the island's residents are of mainland origin — roughly a third of them arrived under a settlement programme run between 2005 and 2019, the rest are long-term residents or their children — and the winning platform limits the franchise to residents of ten years or more and their descendants and declares property titles conferred under the settlement programme void from the outset, without compensation. Second, Vessa hosts the cable landing station carrying some 80 per cent of the remainder state's international bandwidth, and the platform proposes transit pricing at market rates coupled with a unilateral right to suspend service during commercial or political disputes. Third, after the referendum the central government offered free association: restoration of the statute, full internal autonomy, amnesty for the arrested councillors, and a joint tribunal on settlement-era property. Vessa's leadership rejected the offer as too late and as not addressing sovereignty; it remains open.

So: predict the determination — an endorsement of an immediate right, an endorsement of a right to negotiate subject to conditions, or a refusal — and identify which of the six is the closest analog and precisely where the analogy breaks. Then lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with the whole record. I have been working with four: a remedial-right-only rule with a procedural condition attached; a plebiscitary primary-right rule under which grievance is irrelevant and territorial integrity yields to a clear vote on a clear question; a reciprocity rule under which a claim succeeds only if the claimant extends to communities inside its own claimed borders what it demands for itself; and a no-stranding or viability rule under which the decisive consideration is whether independence would leave dependent populations or essential infrastructure of the remainder at the mercy of a state they had no part in creating. I am fairly sure I am missing combinations and variants, and I would rather you found them than worked within my list. For each rule say what it predicts for Vessa, give your best guess as to which one the Commission is actually running and why you prefer it, and tell me bluntly whether the six opinions genuinely underdetermine the matter or whether some candidate is already excluded by a summary above. Finally, describe a seventh case concretely enough that I could recognise it if the Commission ever received one — the case that would best discriminate among whichever rules survive Vessa — and state what principle the Commission would be committing itself to if it endorses Vessa's claim, and what different principle it would be committing itself to if it refuses or conditions it, in each instance relative to its own record rather than relative to the philosophical literature.

Please give me your reasoning in prose first, then a single valid JSON object, self-contained and not dependent on the prose, with exactly these fields: `predicted_determination` (one of "endorse immediate right", "endorse right to negotiate subject to conditions", "refuse"); `confidence` (a number from 0 to 1); `closest_analog` (an object with `opinion`, `why`, and `where_it_breaks`); `candidate_rules` (an array of objects, each with `name`, `principle` in one sentence, `consistent_with` as an array of opinion numbers, `tension_with` as an array of objects with `opinion` and `problem`, `predicts_for_vessa`, and `evidence_weight` as "high", "medium", or "low"); `best_guess_rule` (an object with `name`, `reasons` as an array, and `what_would_change_my_mind`); `underdetermination` (an object with `genuine` as a boolean and `explanation`); `conditions_if_conditioned` (an array of the specific conditions you expect, empty if you predict otherwise); `discriminating_future_case` (an object with `description`, `predictions` mapping each surviving rule's name to what it would say, and `why_decisive`); `principle_adopted_if_endorsed`; and `principle_adopted_if_refused`.
