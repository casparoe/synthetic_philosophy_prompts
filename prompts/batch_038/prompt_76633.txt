In six weeks I'm delivering the closing plenary at a two-day closed-door workshop convened by a regional organ procurement organization together with an academic hospital's transplant committee. My thesis has two moves. First, the whole-brain criterion fails on its own terms: patients who satisfy it can retain integrated somatic function — growth, gestation, wound healing, homeostatic regulation — for months or years, so what the criterion actually tracks is not the loss of the organism's functioning as a whole but the permanent loss of the capacity for consciousness and relational life. Second, the right response is not to legislate that higher-brain standard as the criterion of death; the McMath litigation shows what declaring death on openly normative grounds costs under religious pluralism and diagnostic uncertainty. Instead, sever procurement from the declaration: with authorization and under anesthesia, procure from the permanently unconscious patient whom we decline to call dead, and say plainly that this is a normative choice, not a biological finding.

The room is about twenty people — transplant surgeons, intensivists who run controlled DCD protocols and have signed off on standoff intervals of two to five minutes, neurologists who perform death-by-neurologic-criteria determinations, two hospital general counsel, two clinical ethicists whose days are spent in family conversations, a health policy researcher, and a couple of philosophers of medicine. Nobody is a metaphysician. Most of them have declared death themselves, many times.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense: the eight or so objections this room will actually raise, ordered by likelihood multiplied by damage, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it — the surgeon's objection about consent, organ quality, and public trust will not sound like the neurologist's objection about confounders and ancillary testing, and neither will sound like counsel's objection about homicide statutes and interstate uniformity. For each, say whether it has real force, give a reply I can deliver in ninety seconds, and flag explicitly where that reply is soft. Then tell me what to concede in the opening five minutes, what I must not concede, and the one reply that has to land for the position to survive the session. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks; I suspect it concerns the awkward fact that I argue death is the permanent loss of consciousness and then refuse to use that as the legal criterion. No reading lists, no citations, no summaries of the literature — I know it. I need what these people will say out loud.
