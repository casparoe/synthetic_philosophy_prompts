I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on category formation, and the chapter of my dissertation on the letter-spirit problem — what makes every instance of a given letter an instance of that letter, across hands, weights, and centuries — has hit a wall that argument isn't getting me past. I'd like to approach it from the workshop side instead.

Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words on the following premise. A family type foundry has cast the same roman alphabet continuously for four hundred years. Its house *g* — the punchcutter's pride, the shop's signature — was renewed every time the steel wore out, but each new punch was cut by eye from a proof pulled from the type it replaced, never from any surviving original. Over the generations the ear has shortened, the lower bowl has opened, the axis has tilted a few degrees off vertical. Between any two adjacent generations the change is invisible; the earliest surviving proof and today's punch, laid side by side, are evidently different designs. An apprentice who has been set to copying the ledger discovers this and takes it to the master.

The story should turn on the question the ledger raises, which the master declines to answer directly: is the letter in the case downstairs still the house *g*? Give the master one answer and the apprentice the other, and make the disagreement substantive rather than temperamental — one of them treats the letter as the thing handed down the chain of cutters, the other as the shape — and let neither position be a strawman. Include a scene in which a printer who knows nothing of the ledger sets a page, admires the setting, and remarks that the *g* could have come from nowhere else; that scene must bear on the argument rather than decorate it.

Two constraints. First, no lectures: the philosophy should be carried by what the characters do with punches, proofs, molds, and each other. Second, do not resolve the story by announcing that the question was confused, that both were right in their own way, or that identity comes in degrees — unless you earn that with a concrete decision the apprentice has to make about the punch on the bench in front of her in the final scene. What I want to find out is which picture of what a letter is survives contact with an actual shop floor.
