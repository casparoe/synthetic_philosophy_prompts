I am in the final stages of a novel set in a mid-sized German university town, and one of its set pieces is a chapter built almost entirely around a piece of commercial ephemera that my protagonist picks up in the lobby of a conference hotel: a trifold brochure for a firm of moral consultants who will, for a fee, redraft a client's maxim until it survives Kant's universalizability test. I would like you to write that brochure.

The firm — please give it a sober, faintly Hanseatic name of your choosing, though I would prefer it not be an obvious pun — advertises three tiers of service, and I want those tiers to map precisely onto the machinery of the test rather than onto generic price levels:

- A basic tier for clients whose proposed maxim, once universalized, generates a contradiction in conception, and who therefore need the description narrowed.
- A middle tier for maxims that can be conceived as universal law but that no one could consistently will as such, because willing them would frustrate ends the client plainly has. This tier should be marketed as the harder sell, and priced accordingly.
- A premium tier offering a separate review under the formulation requiring that humanity be treated never merely as a means, together with associate membership in the firm's "kingdom of ends" client network, whose benefits should be described with the vagueness typical of membership schemes.

Beyond that, I would ask for the following:

1. The comedy must come from the apparatus being handled correctly, not from anachronism, not from mocking Kant as a person, and not from any character being stupid. A reader who has worked through the Groundwork should recognize the maxim-description problem, the distinction between strict and wide duties, and the charge of empty formalism being dramatized rather than announced. A reader who has never opened Kant should still follow it, because the brochure's own sales copy supplies enough context.

2. Include two anonymized case studies, each showing a client's maxim before and after the firm's work. In the first, successive redrafting produces a maxim so exquisitely specified — particular day, particular sum, particular sort of counterparty — that it universalizes effortlessly and uselessly; let the brochure celebrate this as a triumph. In the second, show a client whose maxim cannot be rescued at any level of generality, and let the firm's language grow perceptibly evasive as it bills them anyway.

3. Include one satisfied testimonial and one complaint, and let the complaint be more damaging to the firm than any external criticism would be, without the complainant intending it to be.

4. Somewhere in the small print, disclose the firm's own founding maxim — the principle on which its partners act in running the business. The reader should be able to see, unaided, that this maxim fails the very test the firm sells, since its efficacy depends on most people not doing what the firm does. Do not point this out; do not even gesture at it. The fine print should simply sit there.

5. Add a brief sidebar on services the firm declines to provide, which should quietly reveal where the firm thinks morality ends and prudence begins.

Length: roughly 700 to 800 words, laid out as actual brochure copy, with headings, a price list, bullets, and small print at the foot. The register should be that of professional-services marketing — reassuring, faintly legalistic, allergic to commitment. Keep it deadpan from first word to last: no winking asides, no authorial nudge, and nothing at the end that tells the reader how to take it.

One housekeeping request: please put no real philosopher's words inside quotation marks. Paraphrase as freely as you like, and let the firm's own slogans and boilerplate carry all the quoted language. Invented client names and initials are welcome.
