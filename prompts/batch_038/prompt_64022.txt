You are a philosopher who has taught Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation for many years and who regularly runs seminars in which students sort concrete historical projects into the essay's three modes of history. Please think this through carefully and work step by step rather than answering from a first impression.

Some context. I am taking a course on the Untimely Meditations. Our seminar leader runs a recurring exercise: she reads out a description of some actual or plausible historical undertaking and then delivers two verdicts on it — which mode of history it instantiates (monumental, antiquarian, critical), and whether it is, in the essay's own terms, a healthy instance that serves life or a diseased one that stifles it. She has never stated her rule, and the point of the exercise is supposed to be that we infer it. Here are the six verdicts so far, with the one-line gloss she gave after each:

1. A regional museum's permanent exhibition, "Twelve Who Built This Valley," with wall texts telling visitors what each figure's choices can teach them about their own ambitions. Monumental, healthy. Gloss: "A town that had stopped expecting anything of itself got back a horizon it could act inside."

2. A heritage society that has spent four decades transcribing the minute books of a dissolved drainage board, insisting that every entry matters equally because they are our records, with no attempt to make any of it usable by anyone. Antiquarian, diseased. Gloss: "They could no longer say what any of it was for, only that it was theirs."

3. A historian reconstructing, in technical detail, the legal instruments by which a state expropriated a community's land, so that present-day claimants can contest the transfer in court. Critical, healthy. Gloss: "People who were being told to be grateful got a document that let them stop."

4. A documentary that measures today's organizers against a famous revolutionary and closes by implying that contemporary efforts are trivial beside his; audiences report feeling that nothing they could do would matter. Monumental, diseased. Gloss: "It gave them a greatness they could only be ashamed to stand next to."

5. A survivors' association's truth-telling project about a boarding school that abused its pupils, which ends with the demolition of the building and the opening of a new school on the site. Critical, healthy. Gloss: "They cut the root, and then they planted something."

6. A graduate student's polemic dismantling the reputation of her department's founder, extended into a general demonstration that the whole literature of her field is contaminated by the same bad faith, after which she can read nothing without suspicion and has stopped writing. Critical, diseased. Gloss: "She freed herself from the tradition by destroying the ground she was standing on."

Next week's case, the seventh, is this. A town council has commissioned a single volume on its most famous son, a nineteenth-century engineer. The council's framing is that the book will be titled "The Man Who Built This Town" and given to every school as an example of what one person from here achieved. The archivist doing the work says she took the commission because her grandmother was among the labourers the engineer's firm brought in under indenture, and she wants the letters published so that people will stop telling her family to be grateful. Her method is to publish everything in the estate — every account book, laundry list and unanswered letter — with no selection and no commentary, on the principle that all of the town's records deserve to be kept. So far the council has withdrawn the funding, the manuscript sits in a drawer, the schools are using a celebratory biography written by someone else, and the archivist says she is relieved and has begun work on something of her own choosing.

Please do the following, in this order.

(a) Before committing to any prediction, brainstorm the candidate rules she could be running. I mean not just the three labels but the criterion she uses to assign a project to one of them — its subject matter, the need it answers in the people undertaking it, the stance it takes toward the past, some combination — and separately the criterion she uses to call an instance healthy or diseased. There may be several of each, and they need not pair up in the obvious combinations.

(b) For every candidate rule you judge genuinely consistent with all six verdicts, say what it predicts for the seventh case, both the mode and the health verdict. Be explicit about which candidates the six reports rule out and which report does the ruling out. Where a rule has to resolve a question the case description does not settle — for instance whose need counts, when the commissioners and the person doing the work need different things from it — flag that rather than resolving it silently.

(c) Give me your single best prediction of what she will say, and the reasons you prefer that rule to its rivals. I am especially interested in what her glosses tell you, as distinct from her labels: I suspect the glosses are carrying evidence the labels alone do not.

(d) Tell me which of the six earlier verdicts is doing the most work — which one, had it gone the other way, would most change the space of admissible rules.

(e) Design one further case that would discriminate between your two leading rules. Specify its subject matter, the need it answers and in whom, the stance it takes toward the past, and what becomes of the people involved afterward; then say what each of the two rules predicts for it.

(f) Finally, suppose she is running the rule you favour. Is there anything about the seventh case that she would be unable to say, or would have to say awkwardly? My own worry is that on one reading she is forced to call the volume critical even though the town will encounter it as monumental and the schools are in fact reading the monumental substitute, and I cannot tell whether that is a defect in her rule or a genuine feature of the case.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography, no reading list and no quotations — I cannot check any of them right now, and I would much rather you reasoned from the distinctions themselves than reconstructed the text; if you need to invoke something like the essay's account of a healthy mode degenerating into its characteristic excess, describe it in your own words. Second, if one of my six reports strikes you as not really available to any of the candidate rules, say so plainly instead of smoothing it over, and tell me what you think she actually said. Please mark clearly which parts of your answer are inference and which are guesswork.
