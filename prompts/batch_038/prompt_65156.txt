I've spent most of the last year arguing with a collaborator about gradual brain replacement — the standard setup, neuron by neuron swapped for a silicon equivalent that reproduces the original's input/output profile, done while the person is awake and talking so there's never a global interruption. I'm the one who thinks you survive it and stay conscious the whole way through. She thinks that somewhere in the process the person gets replaced by a functional imitator who inherits their memories and their first-person reports, and that calling this survival is just a refusal to notice what happened. We've agreed to actually write down a joint statement instead of starting another 3 a.m. text thread, and I want you to draft it for us, because we both get too rhetorical when we're annoyed.

What I need is the document a real adversarial collaboration would produce. First, a numbered list of claims we can both sign — and I want them substantive, not "we both agree the case is hard." Include things about what the description of the case does and doesn't fix, about what first-person reports from inside the process would and wouldn't show, about whether the fight is really about identity or about something else, about which intuitions are load-bearing for which side, about what a theory of the case has to be stable under. Then a disagreements table: the topic, my side's formulation in wording I'd accept, her side's in wording she'd accept, whether it's a genuine disagreement or a verbal one, and what would have to be settled to move it. Then the cruxes, and for each one a concrete case or argument we'd both accept as bearing on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance so we can be embarassed later. Anywhere you think we've been arguing about words instead of the world, say which part and why.

To keep this from collapsing into the generic Ship of Theseus recitation, run the collaboration over three variants of the protocol that we've actually been fighting about. Variant A, the archive: every swapped-out neuron is kept alive in a bath, dormant but viable, and the whole archive could in principle be reconnected at the end — does keeping the originals around, even switched off and disconnected, change who survives? Variant B, the lag: the replacements fire about 40 ms late relative to biological timing, but the delay is compensated elsewhere in the circuit so whole-brain behavior and functional organization are unchanged — does it matter that the compensation holds at one level of description and not another? Variant C, the schedule: same total replacement, but done as a spatially contiguous wave sweeping forward from the brainstem, versus a salt-and-pepper order that leaves every local circuit half-biological and half-silicon the whole time. For each variant I want the statement to say explicitly whether our two views give different verdicts or the same verdict, and where a difference would count as a crux rather than a tiebreaker we're just claiming. Don't give me a bibliography or a rundown of who said what — we know the literature — and please don't adjudicate. The point is a text we could both put our names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
