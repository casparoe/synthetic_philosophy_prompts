Next term I have eleven students — a mixture of final-year undergraduates and Masters students — in a philosophy of science seminar that meets for a hundred minutes a week around a single table, with a whiteboard, no laptops, and a departmental printer that will cope with a few pages of black-and-white handouts. We shall have finished three weeks on falsificationism and be turning to theory choice, and I want the session on closeness to truth to be a workshop rather than a paper discussion. What I am after is a game with real, if trivial, stakes in which the students rank competing claims about a hidden state of affairs, discover for themselves that a bold falsehood can be better placed than a timid truth, then reach for what looks like the obvious way of measuring that ranking — count what a claim gets right and deduct what it gets wrong — and watch their own scoring rule hand back a verdict they find intolerable. For my own part I know roughly where this is heading: Popper proposed essentially that subtraction as a measure in the 1960s, and it was shown not to work in the 1970s, but I do not want any of that said aloud until the students have built the measure and broken it with their own hands. My rough seed is a small world of four or five binary features whose actual values only I know, which the students interrogate by writing down candidate descriptions and by buying information from the banker with paper tokens; please improve on this as you see fit. The tokens should convert into something trivial but genuinely wanted — I am content for the winning team to nominate which paper we drop from next week's list, and chocolate may be involved — but no money should change hands.

The structural requirement I care about most is that nothing in the first hour says the words truthlikeness, verisimilitude, truth content or falsity content. The students should be told only that they must say which guess is nearer the mark, and they should hit the wall inside their own bookkeeping. Please design the toy world and the payoff scheme so that the wall is unavoidable for at least one pair of theories the group is likely to write down: two false descriptions which everyone in the room wants to rank, and which the ledger scores level. Tell me in advance which pair that is likely to be, and tell me what a student is most likely to say in the five seconds after they notice. I would also like one round in which the actual state of the world is withheld even at the scoring stage, so that the group has to decide what to do when nobody — not even the banker — can say who was closer; that round is meant to make them feel that whatever ordering they have been leaning on is not something they can compute from inside their own evidence. By the end they should be able to say why nearer the truth looked like a substantive ordering rather than a manner of speaking, why the natural way of measuring it fails, and why that failure does not obviously show the ordering was illusory all along.

What I need back is a complete, runnable package. First, the cover story and the participant-facing instructions, written so that they can be handed out without giving the point away. Second, a materials list stating exactly what is on each card or sheet. Third, the token economy and the full scoring rules, including how to handle ties, refusals to guess, and the student who writes down something logically empty in order never to be wrong. Fourth, a minute-by-minute timeline adding up to one hundred minutes. Fifth, the debrief, in the order I should run it, mapping particular moments of the game onto the distinctions it was built to expose, and giving me the questions to ask rather than the speeches to make. Beyond that, three things I shall want on the day. The predictable failure modes of a first running — where the rules will be misread, where a round will overrun, where a confident student will derail the exercise — and what to do about each. The repairs the group will propose once they hit the wall; I expect some version of averaging the right bits against the wrong bits, and I expect someone to declare the whole idea empty on the grounds that truth and falsity are the only real properties a claim can have. For each repair, tell me whether it should be tried out in a spare five minutes, conceded, or answered, and give me the answer. Finally, a frank closing paragraph on which features of the actual philosophical problem this game cannot reproduce, and which I shall therefore have to supply by talking afterwards. Please write the whole thing in British English.
