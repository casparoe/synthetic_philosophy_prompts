I'm a staff engineer at a small company that makes industrial control hardware, and for the past year I've been running an optional Friday lunch session where I try to get people reading actual philosophy — mostly because our whole job is reasoning about what a system *would* do under conditions that never occur in testing, and nobody here has any vocabulary for that. In two weeks I want to do dispositions: what it even means to say a bracket is fragile or a relay is disposed to close, and why the obvious way of cashing that out in counterfactuals falls apart. The room will be about a dozen: firmware people, two test techs, one very sharp intern who's read some philosophy on her own, and a product manager who walks out if things stay abstract for more than three minutes. Forty minutes, one whiteboard, no handouts.

Don't explain finks and masks to me — I've read the usual treatment and I roughly know what they are. What I want is the plan. The order the pieces have to go up on the board and what each one depends on (I suspect you can't get to masking until the idea of a *basis* is on the table, but I'm not sure the basis is worth the board time with this crowd, and if you think it is, tell me how to get it across without a metaphysics lecture). Which single example should carry the whole thing — ideally one physical artifact that can do the work for the naive conditional analysis, the fink case, and the mask case, so I'm not resetting the room three times — and what that example has to have and what it has to lack so it doesn't blow up on me. Because it has blown up on me before: I reached for a fuse and a bimetallic strip and half the room decided a fink was just a part wearing out and the other half decided a mask was a bad sensor, and I couldn't get them off either reading. So map the misconceptions this audience will bring or form — I'm bracing for 'the conditional analysis is fine, you just need more provisos', 'this is all just reliability statistics', and 'so a spec sheet is meaningless then' — and tell me exactly where in the sequence each one gets headed off instead of argued down at the end.

Two smaller things. A colleague insists there's a third kind of case beyond fink and mask, where the object isn't really disposed at all but something in the setup makes it behave as though it were, and he thinks leaving it out is malpractice — say whether that earns a slot in forty minutes with this crowd, and if it does, where it goes. And tell me what I should deliberately leave out (the reformed analyses, whether dispositions are reducible at all, that sort of thing) plus the honest way to say we're not going there without it sounding like a dodge. Last: give me one question I can put to the room at the end, preferably about a piece of hardware they've actually had to debug, that would tell me whether the fink/mask distinction really landed rather than them just nodding along until the pizza's gone.
