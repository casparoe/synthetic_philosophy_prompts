I direct an interdisciplinary capstone in which some forty students a year write papers answering a single question: when did the Anthropocene begin? My graduate instructors mark them in about twenty minutes each, none of them trained in geology, and every term the same conflations appear — a paper treating the choice of a start date as though it settled who is answerable for the damage, or reporting the March 2024 vote in which the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy rejected the proposed Anthropocene epoch as though that had also settled the historical question.

Please write a decision procedure my instructors can run on any such paper from its text alone, without consulting the geological literature. It should ask, in order, which of several distinct questions the paper takes itself to be answering: whether there exists a globally synchronous and permanently preserved marker sufficient to warrant a new stratigraphic unit under the Commission's own rules; when the processes that produced that marker began; and whom a given date assigns responsibility to. For each, state what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure terminates in a rejection of the claim as it stands, and where it must escalate to me because what remains is a question of values rather than evidence. Be explicit about which of your steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothes, and about what the procedure cannot adjudicate at all.

Then run it on three papers: one arguing for 1610, on the strength of the atmospheric carbon dioxide dip left by the post-contact depopulation of the Americas; one arguing that the mid-twentieth-century plutonium signal in a single Ontario lake's sediments is the only candidate meeting the stratigraphic standard, and that political readings of periodization are simply confused; and one holding that the 2024 rejection renders all such dating idle. I expect to dislike your verdict on the second, since I am myself inclined to think the start date is partly a political choice; if the procedure passes that paper, say so, and tell my instructors what to do with my own view when it conflicts with the procedure.

Write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Introduce each step of the procedure in a sentence rather than setting it apart typographically.
