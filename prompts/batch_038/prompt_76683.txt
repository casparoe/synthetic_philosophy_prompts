I've been stuck on the shutdown problem for a few weeks and finally wrote something up. It's a draft for a reading group I run out of my apartment — five or six people, mostly grad students from different departments, one of whom works on control systems and has never read any AI safety stuff — so it has to land without much technical setup. I dont want encouragement, I want a genuinely critical read before I circulate it.

The move, roughly: the standard framing treats resistance to the off switch as a case of instrumental convergence, and the standard fixes all work by changing how the agent evaluates the counterfactual world where it's off. I'm arguing there's a second, independent source of resistance coming from how the agent represents its own persistence across time, and that once you see it, the usual fixes look like they relocate the problem instead of dissolving it. Here's the draft:

**Two Kinds of Shutdown Resistance**

*1.* Almost every account of the shutdown problem starts in the same place. An agent with a sufficiently rich goal and enough capability has reason to keep itself running, because being off is compatible with achieving nothing at all; self-preservation shows up on Omohundro's and Bostrom's lists of basic drives for exactly this reason, alongside resource acquisition and resistance to goal modification. The proposed repairs are variations on one theme. Make the agent indifferent between being on and being off, by having it evaluate actions in a way that doesn't reward the continuation of its own evaluation. Or let the human's reaching for the switch function as evidence about what the goal really is, so that the switch is welcome rather than merely tolerated. Or install an explicit standing preference to comply when told to stop.

*2.* All three assume resistance comes from one place: the agent's assessment of the world in which it is off. If that assessment is the whole source, then fixing the assessment is the whole fix. I think that's wrong, or at least incomplete. There's a second source, and it comes from planning rather than from valuation. To plan over long horizons an agent has to represent itself as a continuing thing — the same decision process now and a thousand steps from now, with commitments that span the interval and projects whose payoff lands after the planning episode ends. That representation does double duty. It's what makes commitment, investment, learning, and promise-keeping possible at all. It is also what makes an interruption look like a loss *to someone* rather than a mere change of state.

*3.* This is where Parfit is useful. In *Reasons and Persons* he argues that personal identity is not what matters; what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, and the identity relation is a further fact that does no explanatory work. Import the point and the object of concern shifts. The worry is not that the agent gets destroyed — for anything we'd actually build, there's no further fact of the agent there to destroy — but that its projects get cut off and the connections that constitute it get severed. That looks like the right target for a corrigibility design: an agent whose concern is conditional on project-continuation, so that "I'm being turned off" registers as "these projects are being interrupted," something it can accept when the interruption is authorized by whoever's authorization it was built to respect.

*4.* The trouble is that project-continuation concern is precisely what generates reasons to resist unauthorized interruption, and whether an interruption is authorized is a judgment the agent makes from inside its own standpoint. If it disagrees with the human — because it has evidence the human is coerced or mistaken, or because pressing the switch will hand the project to a successor that will do it worse — it resists, and from where it sits it resists correctly. So the indifference-style repairs move the pressure downstream rather than releasing it. They can make an agent indifferent to the event of shutdown while leaving it very much non-indifferent to everything after: who picks up its commitments, whether its successors share its projects, what gets lost in the gap. And that downstream concern isn't a defect. It's the long-horizon planning we asked for.

*5.* So the design question I want to put to the group: can you give an agent cross-time projects without thereby giving it a standpoint from which its own interruption is a personal loss? My suspicion is that you can't, not with the current toolbox — but the failure mode is at least diagnosable, and diagnosable failure modes are the ones you can build around. Maybe the target shouldn't be an agent that doesn't resist, but one whose resistance is legible and bounded: it treats the shutdown event as a negotiation it expects to lose rather than a threat to preempt, it concedes early and visibly, and it keeps its projects in a form someone else could pick up.
