I attend a monthly reading group in the philosophy of mathematics, and last week's session on Hilbert's hotel produced an objection that several of us found intuitively forceful but were unable to analyze. One of our members — a retired structural engineer, mathematically literate but not professionally trained in set theory — argued that the hotel is not merely counterintuitive but impossible, and gave as his entire reason the Euclidean dictum that the whole is greater than the part, which he takes to be one of Euclid's common notions and therefore knowable a priori. His reconstruction was as follows. After the shift, the guests stand in one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of the rooms, or equivalently with a proper subset of themselves. The dictum forbids a whole from being no greater than a proper part of it. Therefore no such arrangement of guests and rooms is possible. He was careful to add that he grants the bare coherence of one-to-one correspondence as a mathematical notion; what he claims is that pairing that notion with the dictum yields a reductio, so that we must abandon either the thesis that equinumerosity just consists in the possibility of a one-to-one correspondence or the thesis that the natural numbers form a completed totality.

The group's response was a shrug, and I think the shrug was wrong in both directions: the dictum is very likely being made to do work it cannot do, yet the argument is not simply confused, since I am aware of at least one way of measuring size for infinite collections on which the natural numbers do come out strictly larger than the even numbers. I would like to settle the matter before we meet again.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims that "the whole is greater than the part" might be taken to express? For each, please state it explicitly, making clear what "part" is being read as (proper part in the mereological sense, subset, subcollection, initial segment, something else), what "greater" is being read as (more numerous, larger in cardinality, larger under some measure or density, containing as a proper part, greater in content), and what the principle is supposed to range over — finite collections only, all collections whatsoever, geometrical magnitudes, physical systems, probabilities, propositions. Then attach a verdict to each reading: platitude or near-analytic, substantive but defensible, false given the standard results about infinite collections, or simply indeterminate until further terms are pinned down.

Two further things I need. First, identify which reading, if any, licenses the conclusion my colleague wants — that the hotel describes no genuine possibility, whether construed physically or mathematically — and, if none does, say what supplementary premise he would have to supply. Second, tell me which readings render the dictum knowable a priori in the way his objection requires, and address his dilemma head on: granted both that equinumerosity consists in the possibility of a one-to-one correspondence and that a whole exceeds each of its proper parts, which of the two should be surrendered, and is there any reading on which both survive together?

Please confine the answer to the menu and these two assessments. I am not looking for a survey of the historical controversy, and I do not want a bibliography.
