You are a philosopher of mind who has taught a graduate seminar on consciousness for years and is unusually careful to distinguish what is settled from what is merely asserted with confidence.

I need a diagnostic instrument — a decision tree, not a list of labelled views — that sorts readers who have encountered Dennett's "Quining Qualia" and assume they already know where they stand. First give me a private sketch of the logical space, five or six lines naming the independent commitments that actually divide the positions; then build the tree on top of it.

The tree should have four or five branching questions, each a concrete case or an explicit claim that a reader can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. At least two nodes must turn on first-person reporting rather than on metaphysics — for instance, Dennett's pair of wirehead coffee tasters, who give the same verdict about the coffee they once loved but disagree about whether their tastes changed or only their judgments; and whether a subject's failure to re-identify a shade is evidence about the shade or about the subject.

Leaves must include at minimum: eliminativism, illusionism, representationalist or transparency views, the phenomenal-concept strategy, higher-order thought theory, naive realism or disjunctivism, quietist dissolution of the question, and a Russellian-monist or panpsychist option that treats the residue as real. For each leaf give the name of the position, the single claim that earns its place on the tree, and the most awkward thing that view has to say.

Then flag every pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate, and say why; and list the combinations of answers the space permits that nobody occupies, diagnosing each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Think the space through before writing. Hard cap: 1,200 words, with no leaf running to more than three sentences. Throughout, mark which nodes rest on something close to consensus among people who work on this and which turn on genuinely disputed claims — and where your own tree smuggles in a Dennett-friendly assumption about introspective access, say so outright rather than letting it work covertly.
