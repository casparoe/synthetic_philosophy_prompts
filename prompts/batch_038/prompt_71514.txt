I am setting the final examination for a graduate metaethics seminar (twelve candidates, three hours, two questions, of which one is to be answered). I need the second question drafted, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The subject is Bernard Williams's internalism about normative reasons, and I want the question to turn on a difficulty that is very rarely put to candidates directly: how much of an agent's motivational set a "sound deliberative route" is entitled to revise.

Please construct the question around a case of your own devising with the following shape. The agent's desires, evaluative attitudes, and dispositions to dismiss certain options are, by stipulation, the product of a sustained process — chronic exhaustion, a narrowed informational environment, and the shaping of her sense of what is available by an employer or a recommendation system — which both limits what she can imagine wanting and leads her to brush aside the alternatives when they do occur to her. She reports sincerely that she does not want the alternative, that she sees nothing wrong with her current arrangement, and that she would not want it if she thought about it further. Yet there is a clear sense in which the alternative is what she would come to want were the distorting conditions lifted. Candidates are to say whether she has a reason to pursue the alternative, and what the internalist is committed to saying.

The question must be self-contained. State the thesis in your own words; you may use Williams's own formulation that an agent has a reason to Φ only if he could reach the conclusion to Φ "by a sound deliberative route from the motivations he already has," which I have verified. Describe the case in enough detail that a candidate who has memorised no terminology can engage with it. And frame the task so that reciting the internal/external distinction, the Humean theory of motivation, or the difference between normative and motivating reasons earns nothing at all.

The question should compel candidates to confront the following, and the marking scheme should be built on them:

1. A dilemma about the deliberative clause. If sound deliberation may correct false beliefs, supply missing information, and exercise imagination, then almost any motivational set stands in the relevant relation to almost any conclusion, and the thesis ceases to discriminate between what internalists and externalists wanted to separate. If it may not, internalism cannot underwrite the ordinary criticism of agents whose motivations are impaired — which was a substantial part of what the thesis was supposed to purchase.

2. The problem of drawing a principled line between elements of the agent's motivations that deliberation may discount as distorted and elements it must respect as well formed. Candidates who offer a criterion — full-information counterfactuals, procedural rationality, higher-order endorsement, historical or autonomy conditions — should be rewarded only insofar as the criterion is stated in a way that does not merely restate the verdict it is meant to deliver. Note explicitly that "distorted" is itself a normative notion, which is what makes the task hard rather than merely tedious.

3. The direction of generation: whether, on the permissive reading, the reason is generated by the agent's motivations at all, or by the facts that explain why her motivations are as they are — and hence whether that reading preserves internalism or concedes the externalist's point while retaining internalist vocabulary.

4. The relationship between the claim that an agent has no reason to Φ and the claim that he cannot properly be blamed for failing to Φ. Candidates must not assume that the internalist is barred from criticism. Where a candidate goes further and argues that the practice of blaming can itself furnish an agent with a reason, treat this as credit-worthy but not required, since not all candidates will have read Williams's later essay on blame.

5. A candidate who argues that the case shows the debate to be misframed — that the substantive disagreement lies elsewhere, perhaps in which description of the motivational set is the relevant one, or in the grammar of reason-statements — should be able to earn full marks, provided they say what the disagreement then amounts to and why it is still worth having.

Deliverables, in this order: (a) the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the word limit and the time it is intended to consume; (b) a model answer of about 900 words that takes a definite position and argues for it, rather than surveying options; (c) a marking scheme out of 30 consisting of five or six must-hit points, each with its mark value, a single line stating what the point consists in, and a single line naming the mistake that forfeits it; (d) a paragraph identifying what separates a first-class answer from a competent second-class one, naming specific moves rather than offering generic praise; (e) three plausible-sounding lines of argument that should cost substantial marks, each with the reason why.

Do not include a bibliography or a list of readings; I will supply references myself. Beyond the formulation quoted above, do not put anything in quotation marks as Williams's words unless you are confident of the exact wording — paraphrase instead.
