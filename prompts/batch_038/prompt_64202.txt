I cover neuroscience and AI for a general-interest magazine and I keep landing on a practice nobody involved ever stops to justify. Play the role of a philosopher of mind who's been hired to audit a clinical specialty — think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the candidate views before you commit to one.

The practice: anesthesiologists routinely put people under using chemically unrelated drugs — propofol, which works mostly on GABA-A receptors; ketamine, an NMDA blocker; volatile ethers like sevoflurane; even xenon, an inert noble gas with no ordinary receptor pharmacology at all. All of them count as interchangeable routes to one and the same thing, unconsciousness. The endpoint is defined behaviorally: no response to a voice or a sholder shake, no movement, no memory afterward. Many of them also run an EEG-derived monitor that collapses everything into a single number from 0 to 100 supposed to read off "depth" — even though the field knows that number goes unreliable for ketamine and nitrous oxide and can look light while the patient is nowhere near awake. And the same specialty accepts that a paralyzed, motionless patient is sometimes aware.

So what theory of mind does all this presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account: what has to be true about mental states for a noble gas and a GABA drug to deliver the same one, and for a single scalar to be a sensible target? Is the practice committed to functionalism and multiple realizability, or to something weaker — a loose cluster concept, or just a control target with no metaphysics attached at all? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Are the three parts (interchangeable drugs, one number, behavioral endpoint) actually consistent with each other given the paralysis cases? And flag where it's underdetermined, where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same thing. End with what would have to change in the OR if the field adopted a substrate-specific, neural-correlate view instead.
