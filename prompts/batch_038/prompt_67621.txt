My brother and his wife both teach philosophy of mind at a small college in the upper Midwest, and on Saturday I am giving the toast at their reception. I am the older brother; I teach high-school chemistry and took exactly one seminar in philosophy of mind fifteen years ago, which is to say I know the vocabulary imperfectly but I do know it. Before they let me stand up, they attached a condition, half as a joke and half not: no propositional-attitude verbs about people. Not "Tom believes Marta is extraordinary," not "you two want the same things," not "we all hope," not "she has always wished for," not "they intend," not "look at how much he loves her" if love is being used as a state tucked inside somebody. My brother has held his position since he was a graduate student and has never softened it: folk psychology is a theory in very bad shape, a pre-scientific account of the mind that cannot handle sleep, learning, memory, or mental illness, that has generated no research program worth the name, and whose fate will be the fate of caloric and phlogiston and the vital force rather than reduction to neuroscience. On his view a sentence like "Tom wants to grow old with Marta" is not roughly true or true in a useful way; it is not true, because nothing in the world answers to the wanting. Our grandmother, who is eighty-eight and has been married sixty-four years, told me on the telephone that what she most wants to hear from me on Saturday is that these two believe in each other.

Marta's position is different and harder to state, and the toast has to hold both of them without refereeing. She does not think folk psychology describes anything, either, but she does not think that is a loss or a reason to go quiet about it; she treats the whole vocabulary as an instrument whose terms need not pick out anything for its sentences to be worth saying, and she suspects that the dispute between them is partly about what would even count as settling it, since my brother has to be able to say something about his own denial. She has also been known to press the worry that if you take the attitudes away you have taken the persons away, and that a wedding between two people who do not exist is a strange thing to attend. The room will be about ninety people: our mother, who is devout and will be listening for whether anyone thanks God; our grandmother; roughly a dozen of their graduate students; and Marta's department chair, who works on the philosophy of psychology, sits at the head table, and will notice within ten seconds if I caricature either position or fumble the argument. Note also that they wrote their own vows, so they said the forbidden things to one another in front of all ninety people an hour before I speak and got away with it, because the vows were theirs and the condition is attached only to my toast. And my brother ends every phone call to our mother with "I love you," has done so for twenty years, and does not appear to experience this as a contradiction; I would like the toast to be permitted to notice that without explaining it away.

Please write the toast. Four to five minutes spoken, so about 650 words, and I would rather it come in under than over. It must be deliverable by me: a chemistry teacher talking to his family, no term I would have to stop and define, though I would like exactly one analogy drawn from my own subject, because caloric and phlogiston are the only route I have to letting a room of non-philosophers feel what it is like to think a very familiar way of talking has nothing answering to it. It should get two real laughs, neither of them at my brother's or Marta's expense, and neither of them depending on the family being treated as people who are not following along. No irony, no air quotes, no wink aimed at the head table: if the running gag is that Tom does not actually love his wife, I cannot give the toast. Within those limits it still has to do the thing an ordinary toast does — the moment where you say why these two in particular belong together — and it has to do it out of materials my brother's ontology permits: dispositions, habits, the accumulated record of what they have actually done, what two nervous systems do when they are in a room together, sixty-four years of my grandmother's marriage treated as evidence of something or other. It must end on a line our grandmother could repeat at lunch on Sunday without feeling she was made a fool of. After the toast, add no more than 150 words telling me where the constraint bit hardest and what you substituted for the banned vocabulary, and flag any sentence in which you think you have quietly conceded my brother a piece of realism he would disown.
