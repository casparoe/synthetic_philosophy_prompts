i run the listener-mail segment for a tiny philosophy-of-religion podcast — two of us, no budget, an episode every other week. One recurring bit is "send us your theodicy," which started as a joke but people actually write in, six to ten a month, anything from four paragraphs to a two-thousand-word essay. Right now I read each one and go on gut feel, which is how I aired two that turned out to be the free will defense with the serial numbers filed off, and rejected one that a guest later told me was genuinely interesting.

So: build me a triage procedure I can run on a submission in about fifteen minutes, by myself, with no philosophy degree. I read widely but I cant write or check a formal argument, so every step has to be a plain question with a plain test for what counts as an answer that passes — don't assume I know the jargon. Ground it in what the literature on evil actually says about when an answer works: Rowe's inductive version and what would and wouldn't block it, the split between a defense and a theodicy, Wykstra's point that you're only entitled to the "no reason appears to me" premise if you'd expect a reason to appear to you were there one, Draper's move of comparing theism against a hypothesis of indifference rather than against the bare claim that gratuitous evil exists, Adams on evils that wreck a person's ability to make their own life make sense, and skeptical theism plus the costs it gets charged with.

Shape it as: ordered questions, what a pass looks like at each one, where the procedure just stops at no, and which steps have to go to our staff philosopher because theyre values calls rather than something I can check. At minimum it has to handle whether the submission is a defense or a theodicy, whether it generalizes past the writer's own story, whether it explains why that particular suffering couldnt have been prevented more cheaply, and what it says about the distribution — animals, children, sheer quantity. And give me a short list of rejection reason codes I can paste into a reply, so the person recieves something back from being turned down.

One house rule, non-negotiable: we don't air anything a grieving listener could plausibly hear as "your person's death paid for something worth it." I already suspect this eats most of what the literature counts as successful, so tell me straight what survives it instead of papering over the collision.

Then run it on three actual submissions:

1. Six hundred words from a man whose daughter died of leukemia at nine. Argues the year of treatment made his family capable of a seriousness and a kind of love they would otherwise have spent their lives avoiding, and that God's business isn't comfortable lives but formed ones. Best prose we've had all year, and I'll say up front that this is the one I want to air.
2. Twelve hundred words, semi-technical. God can't guarantee outcomes without turning every choice into a puppet show, so creatures get genuine causal weight, and a world with genuine causal weight has to let the consequences land. Concedes it doesn't cover earthquakes and cancer directly, but says the same freedom explains why the response to them is ours to make.
3. Three hundred words. A toddler can't grasp why a parent permits a needle; the gap between us and God is incomparably larger, so the fact that no reason appears to us carries no information at all. Ends on a line about trust.

Tell me which one gets the slot, and if the procedure says no to number one, show me exactly which step kills it and whether that step is doing real work or just encoding my house rule.

Last thing: where does this procedure misfire? Which kind of submission does it wrongly kill, which does it wave through, and what's the one distinction from the literature you had to blunt or drop to make the whole thing fit in fifteen minutes.
