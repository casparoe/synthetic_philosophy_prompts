I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student and this term I'm co-running a little "argument clinic" for the first-years — people bring in one page with the central argument of their draft on it and we poke at it for 45 minutes. Embarrassingly large share of what gets brought in is some version of a transcendental argument: the author wants to say that some commitment (to the external world, to other minds, to a norm, to the value of their own agency) is a condition of the possibility of something the antagonist can't give up, so the antagonist is self-stultifying. Sometimes that's a real argument. A lot of the time it's a conditional with a modal word bolted on so it sounds deep, or a regress argument, or just "denying this would be expensive for you" wearing the costume.

What I want is a one-page triage procedure the first-years can run on their own draft in maybe 20 minutes before they come to the session. Not a history lecture — a sequence of questions to ask of the argument in order, where for each one you say what a passing answer looks like, what a fail looks like, and whether a fail means stop-with-a-verdict (here's what the argument actually is instead) or hand it to the group because the question is about how demanding we want our label to be rather than about the shape of the argument. Ground it in what people actually mean by the term — the Kantian conditions-of-possibility idea, the anti-sceptical use and Strawson's and Putnam's versions of it, the performative-contradiction stuff from discourse ethics, the constitutivist versions that run through agency and practical identity — and build in whatever Stroud's objection forces on you, including the modest/ambitious split. But the point is that it has to be usable by someone who's read none of that, so keep the name-dropping to a minimum and put the work in the questions.

Then run it on three sketchs, all of which I've compressed and possibly mangled, so treat them as given rather than trying to reconstruct a real paper:

A. To deliberate at all is to take one's choices to be based on reasons. Taking one's choices to be based on reasons commits one to there being reasons. So anyone who deliberates is committed to there being reasons, and the global error theorist about reasons cannot both deliberate and deny this.

B. If I were a brain in a vat in the way the sceptic describes, my word "vat" could not pick out vats. So the sceptic's hypothesis, as I would express it, does not say what she needs it to say. Hence that particular global hypothesis is not coherently statable by me, and the sceptic loses her best case.

C. This one is mine, so be blunt. Any disagreement that is genuinely a disagreement — as opposed to two people talking past each other — presupposes that the parties share enough standards for one of them to be answerable to the other. A relativist who says no standards are shared across frameworks cannot present her view as correcting mine, since correcting requires exactly the sharing she denies. Therefore there are standards shared across frameworks.

For each of the three, show the sheet being run: which question it passes or fails, at what point the procedure stops, and what the verdict is in the sheet's own vocabulary. C is the one I think works, so if your procedure kills it I want to know clearly whether that's a defect in the argument or an artefact of where you drew the line between passing and failing — and I want the artefact possibility taken seriously rather than waved at. Also tell me what the procedure can't do at all (I assume it can't tell anyone whether the claimed enabling condition is actually true, only whether the argument has the right shape — but say what else falls outside), and flag every step where the sheet is really a judgment call in a procedural costume, because if I hand this out and someone spots that, the whole thing loses authority in the room.

One more thing before you build it: if you think the request rests on something confused, say so plainly instead of giving me a polished one-pager that quietly begs the question. I have a nagging worry that "transcendental argument" isn't one form with a checkable shape at all — that it's a family of moves sharing a rhetorical register — and that any triage sheet will mostly sort arguments by how closely they resemble the examples the sheet was built from. If that's right, I'd rather hear it flat out, plus whatever narrower version of the task is worth doing instead. Same goes if you think the honest sheet would have so many "ask the group" escape hatches that it stops being a procedure and becomes a list of topics.
