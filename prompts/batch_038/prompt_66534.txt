I am marking take-home short answers for a final-year seminar in the philosophy of language; the unit was externalism about meaning and mental content. Below are the question as set and one candidate's response. I need a grade and the comments that go with it.

**Question (25 points).**

Putnam's Twin Earth case is standardly used to argue that the meanings of a speaker's words are not fixed by what is inside that speaker's head. Consider the following variant, which we will call Level Earth — "level" because nothing in the case is supposed to turn on a hierarchy of authority among its speakers.

Level Earth is just like Putnam's Twin Earth in every intrinsic respect. Its inhabitants are molecule-for-molecule duplicates of Earth's inhabitants; they associate the word "water" with exactly the stereotype we associate with it; and its lakes, rivers, and rain are made of a liquid that is macroscopically indistinguishable from ours but whose composition is XYZ rather than H2O. To this we add one stipulation, which is social rather than psychological. On Level Earth the word "water" is nobody's special province: there is no natural science, no craft or trade that deals in discriminating liquids, and no group whose verdicts about which substances are water the rest of the community defers to. Every speaker applies the term entirely on her own authority, and no one's use is answerable to anyone else's. Grant, for the sake of the case, that this arrangement is compatible with the inhabitants' being intrinsic duplicates of us; what matters is which facts about the situation fix reference.

(a) [8 points] Does Putnam's argument still get a grip on Level Earth? That is, does "water" in a Level Earth speaker's mouth refer to XYZ, refer to H2O, or fail to refer at all? Say what, on Putnam's account, fixes the reference in a case like this one.

(b) [8 points] Given your answer to (a), what work is the division of linguistic labour doing in Putnam's argument, and what work is it not doing?

(c) [9 points] A classmate objects to your answer to (a) as follows: "If what a speaker's word refers to is fixed by something outside her head, then the Level Earth speakers do not know what they are talking about, and they cannot later find out that they were wrong. When chemists eventually arrive on Level Earth, they will not be discovering anything about water; they will simply be changing the subject." Evaluate this objection.

**Candidate's response.**

(a) Yes, the argument still gets a grip: "water" on Level Earth refers to XYZ. Putnam sets the original case in 1750 precisely so that the intuition cannot depend on anyone's knowing the chemistry. Neither Oscar nor Twin Oscar knows the composition of the liquid he calls water, and their stereotypes are the same, yet their words pick out different kinds. So what fixes reference cannot be anything in their heads. On Putnam's account the fixing is indexical: "water" means roughly whatever bears the same-liquid relation to the stuff around here, and on Level Earth the stuff around here is XYZ. The absence of chemists therefore changes nothing, since reference was already fixed by the liquid the speakers were interacting with. Worth adding that "water is wet" comes out true in the mouths of both Oscar and Twin Oscar; they are not disagreeing, they are saying different things.

(b) It follows that the division of linguistic labour does no semantic work in the argument. It is a separate hypothesis about how a community distributes the job of tracking an extension once that extension has been fixed: most speakers rely on specialists to tell them whether a given sample is gold or fool's gold, but that reliance is not what makes "gold" mean gold. What the Level Earth case brings out is that Putnam's externalism is at bottom a thesis about the social character of meaning. What determines what my words mean is not my individual psychology but my standing in a shared public practice, so meaning is something a community holds collectively rather than something any single speaker possesses. A speaker wholly cut off from such a practice, who acquired "water" privately and had no one to defer to, would have no determinate extension for the term at all.

(c) The classmate is half right. Right: the Level Earth speakers do not know the essence of water, and on Putnam's view they need not; their competence consists in mastery of the stereotype, which Putnam counts as one component of meaning, so they know part of what their word means but not the part that fixes its reference. Wrong, I think, is the sceptical framing: they are not systematically confused, and their ordinary beliefs are true of XYZ exactly as ours are true of H2O. But the second half of the objection does seem to land. If reference is fixed from outside the head and the speakers have no privileged access to what fixes it, then when chemistry arrives there is no independent fact for it to uncover: the composition that gets settled on is the composition their community accepts, and the extension is fixed accordingly. So the new chemists do not discover what "water" was always picking out; they settle what it picks out, and the classmate is right that this looks more like changing the subject than like correcting an error. Putnam can resist this only by insisting that the pre-scientific term was already indexed to the local liquid, but that simply restates the difficulty, since what is wanted is an account of why the index holds onto XYZ rather than onto whatever the community later agrees upon.
