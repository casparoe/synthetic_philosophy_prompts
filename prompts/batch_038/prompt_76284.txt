I'm teaching a seminar module on the epistemology of AI risk assessment — half philosophy grad students, half CS, and neither group has much patience for the other's defaults — and I want a decision tree that sorts students into a named position in the worst-case vs. average-case guarantees debate about frontier models. The thing I care about most is that the tree comes out of the logical space rather than out of a list of camps and their spokespeople, so every branch has to be a question a student could answer sincerely without knowing which leaf their answer drops them into.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered branch points with nothing before or after — no intro, no summary paragraph. Each item is the question, in a sentence or two, plus where each answer leads, naming the leaf positions as you go. Flag inline, in parentheses, any two leaves the tree can't actually separate, and if some combination of answers ends up with nobody in it, say in a clause whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

A few constraints on the content. I want the tree to pry apart two things people run together under "worst-case": the adversarial-robustness sense (a bound that has to hold under a hostile or shifted input distribution) and the tail-catastrophe sense (the average rate is fine but the failures concentrate where they're irreversible). I also want at least one branch on whether alignment is a property of a model, of a model-plus-environment pair, or of a whole deployed system, since that quietly determines whether measurement is even addressing the worry. Leaves that have to show up somewhere: the hard pessimist who thinks no empirical rate ever licenses deployment; the certified-bounds formalist; the actuarial pragmatist who treats it like aviation or nuclear licensing statistics; the safety-case camp holding that assurance is graded argument plus evidence rather than proof; and the misframing camp that says stop asking about guarantees and ask whether failures are detectable and reversible.

Anchor at least three of the branches in concrete cases rather than abstractions — e.g. a model that goes 0 for 400 on a red-team eval whose prompt distribution is much narrower than deployment, or one that's honest 99.9% of the time with the 0.1% clustered in the highest-stakes contexts. Its a mixed room, so don't assume they've read any ML theory beyond what a smart first-year grad student would pick up in a survey.
