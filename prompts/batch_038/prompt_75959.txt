I'm writing the methods chapter of a dissertation in political economy whose central claim is that a country's regulatory architecture was locked in by a contingent decision decades earlier and would not have converged on today's arrangement by itself. Before I defend that claim, I want to know what standard of adequacy it has to meet. Write the desiderata for a theory of path dependence — what such a theory must deliver in order to license claims of the form "the same outcome would not have arrived anyway." For each requirement, give the consideration or test case behind it, and separate what is genuinely non-negotiable from what is the standing prejudice of a particular camp (the increasing-returns people, the replay-the-tape contingenty people).

At a minimum, address: how the theory distinguishes real lock-in from mere persistence or high switching costs; what it owes us on the individuation of outcomes, since at fine enough grain every history is unique and at coarse enough grain everything converges; whether it must locate a bifurcation point, and whether it can identify one ex ante rather than by hindsight; what counts as a feasible alternative at the time, as against a merely technically available one; whether the locked-in outcome must be worse than the road not taken, or whether path-dependent optimality is allowed; and how the theory handles the standards and institutions that did get displaced. Finally, tell me which subset of these requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single one eliminates the most familiar versions of the theory.
