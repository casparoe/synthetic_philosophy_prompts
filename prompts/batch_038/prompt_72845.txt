I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Libet's experiments, and I would like a second opinion, because I suspect he was more right than he was entitled to be and I could not say where. He is well-read, and I do not think he was caricaturing anything. His summary ran roughly as follows:

'Libet sat people in front of a clock whose dot swept around the face faster than a second hand and told them to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it, noting the position of the dot at the moment they first became aware of the urge to move; the muscle recording gave time zero. What he found, consistently, was that the readiness potential — the slow negative shift over the motor areas that precedes self-initiated movement — began roughly half a second before the movement, whereas the reported urge came only about 200 milliseconds before it. So the decision had already been made, unconsciously, some 300 milliseconds before the person experienced making it. The conscious urge is not the cause of the action; it is a late report on a process that is already running. Libet thought he could rescue free will by saying that consciousness still has the final 200 milliseconds in which to veto the act, but that does not help: a veto would itself have to be initiated by the same unconscious machinery, so the move just shifts the problem back a step. And the result is not about wrist flexions in particular. It is a clean demonstration of something true of intentional action generally: what we call deciding is a narrative assembled after the fact.'

Three things bothered me without my being able to name them. First, he slid constantly between the claim that the brain had begun preparing a movement and the claim that the brain had decided, and I could not tell whether that was loose phrasing or the load-bearing step of the argument. Second, I do not know how much weight the reported clock positions can bear, given that the subject is dividing attention between the dot and her own inner state. Third, the veto reply did not seem to me as easily dispatched as he made it sound, though I could not say why.

For what it is worth, I went into the evening inclined to agree with his conclusion and quite unwilling to defend anything like libertarian free will; what I came away doubting is not the conclusion but whether this experiment earns it.

So: please audit the summary claim by claim, distinguishing what a specialist in this literature would endorse without qualification, what is a simplification that quietly changes the argument, and what is simply mistaken. Then tell me whether his two conclusions — that the veto move fails, and that the finding generalizes beyond the experimental task — survive once the summary is corrected. I am not asking you to track down the original papers or to supply references; I want your assessment of the reasoning, and where a step depends on a background assumption about what free will would have to be for the experiment to threaten it, please flag the assumption explicitly rather than letting it pass as data.
