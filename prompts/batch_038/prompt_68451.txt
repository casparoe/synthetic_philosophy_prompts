Write a short story of 1,500–2,500 words about a mountain-valley colony that has been split for three generations by a quarrel over two names. Develop it as you like, but the following is given and should not be substituted out:

- The founding survey party baptized the massif at the valley's head *Meridian*, fixing the name by a description — the first peak visible from the north ridge when the season's fog lifts. Miners working the massif's far flank beneath a permanent cloud bank baptized their hill *Kettle* some sixty years later and never suspected it was the same body of rock.
- The story begins after an orbital survey has settled the identity beyond dispute. Treat that identity as something learned the hard way from evidence, and yet, once learned, as something that could not have failed to hold.
- An elder who has built his life around the quarrel insists that Meridian might not have been Kettle — that the valley got lucky, that it could all have come out otherwise. Show through scene and dialogue alone what is confused in this and what he is really reaching for: the contingency sits in the descriptions that fixed the two names, not in the identity those descriptions fixed.
- One practical stake must hinge on the distinction and resolve against everyone's expectation. The charter grants the founders' heirs "the Meridian watershed" and grants the miners' guild the workings beneath "the peak that shadows the chapel at solstice." One clause survives the discovery untouched; the other does not; and the reason has to do with how each term was fixed rather than with anyone's desert.

No lectures and no exposition dumps about semantics. A reader who knows the literature should recognize the argument being enacted; a reader who doesn't should simply find the old men's quarrel heartbreaking.

After the story, add a short paragraph saying how confident you are in the philosophy as you have rendered it, where you have taken liberties — in particular whether charter language is really best read as rigidly designating, and whether the elder's confusion is genuinely modal or merely metalinguistic — and single out the one point you are least sure of.
