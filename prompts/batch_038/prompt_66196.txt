I'm sitting in on a first year seminar about how we can know anything about the past, and two analogies keep getting thrown at each other until I've lost track of what either one can actually carry.

The reader starts with the old Rankean picture: the historian's job is to show what the past was actually like, and the way you do that is get yourself out of the way, so the good historian is a mirror or a camera and the past leaves its print on the plate. Carr attacks exactly that -- history is a process of interaction between the historian and his facts, the facts of the past only become historical facts because somebody selects them, and the historian is a man of his own age before he's a man of any other -- and the guy running the seminar keeps pressing an image for it: the historian is a fisherman, and what he hauls up depends on the mesh of his net and which bit of water he chooses to fish in. Then White goes further and says the finished history is a verbal artifact with a plot imposed on the material -- romance, tragedy, comedy, satire -- shaped by tropes, which is roughly what a novelist does with her material.

The analogy I actually want audited comes from my roommate, who's a lit PhD student and has read more White than I ever will. She says historians are basically novelists: both face an undifferentiated mass of stuff, both pick a plot and a vantage point and decide whose head we spend time in, and both end up producing a story that makes sense of the material rather than copying it. And she uses it for a strong conclusion -- since the narrative is made rather than found, there's no non-question-begging way to prefer the standard account of, say, the origins of the First World War to some determined revisionist retelling, they're both just stories, and calling one of them objective is only a move for shutting down rivals. When I pushed back she said I was still holding the camera.

Can you run the audit properly? Give me the mapping -- what in the historian's trade plays the role of the novelist's raw material, plot, characters, drafts, publisher, reader -- and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually bite on her conclusion and the ones thats just local colour. I especially want to know what the novelist side quietly imports and how much of it transfers: the licence to invent, the absence of any obligation to a referent, success measured by coherence and interest instead of by getting it right, an author who is the entire source of the work. Then: does the mirror/camera analogy do any better, or is she right that its dead -- and where does the fisherman land? Is that a modest point about selection a Rankean could swallow, or does it already concede her conclusion?

If neither one will hold the weight, tell me what a better analogy would be and what your substitute can and can't do. I have a vague feeling mapmaking is promising, since a projection is a genuine choice with genuine distortions and the map still isn't fiction, but I can't tell whether that just relocates the problem.

One more thing: be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are live fights among people who actually work on it. I'm aware the flattening move I'm putting in my roommate's mouth is close to what White's defenders insist he's been misread as, and I don't want you telling me what White's position was with a confidence you don't have. Where specialists disagree about what his view implies, give me the shape of the disagreement rather than quietly picking a side.
