I sit on the clinical ethics consultation service at a teaching hospital and am preparing a write-up of a recent case for our quarterly teaching conference. I want an audit of an analogy that has become load-bearing in our internal discussion, because I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear and I cannot yet say precisely where.

The case, stripped of identifiers. A woman signed an advance directive at 68 specifying that if she ever reached a condition of advanced dementia — unable to recognize family, unable to sustain a conversation, dependent for all basic care — she did not want antibiotics for intercurrent infections, did not want transfer to a hospital, and did not want artificial nutrition or hydration, and that she wanted comfort measures only. She re-read and re-dated the document at 74, two years before her diagnosis. She is now 82, in a nursing facility, and in substantially the condition the document describes. She has a urinary tract infection with low-grade fever. She is not distressed: she eats with apparent pleasure, hums, holds a caregiver's hand, and smiled twice during the consult. She resists no care that is offered and displays no visible preference about continuing to live. Asked a simple yes-or-no question about whether she wanted to go home, she said yes, then lost the thread. Her daughter is the healthcare proxy and wants the directive honored.

Our attending, who has thought about these cases considerably longer than I have, frames the whole matter as a Ulysses contract — the standard precommitment picture. His argument, as near as I can render it without quoting him: Odysseus had himself lashed to the mast while he was still himself, and instructed the crew not to release him however urgently he pleaded, and to bind him faster if he begged. A directive is the same instrument. The ropes exist precisely so that they hold when the later self is in no position to judge; a crew that unties him because the sea looks calm has defeated the entire point of the arrangement. He then adds two further claims that I find harder to answer than the first. (i) The patient's present cheerfulness is no evidence against the directive, because a man lashed to a mast may look perfectly at ease until the island comes into view. (ii) The caregivers' reluctance — the fact that following the directive feels wrong to us at the bedside — is itself confirmation that the device is functioning, since the whole purpose of precommitment is to bind people against their own and others' present-tense sympathies. On his telling, the crew's job is not to renegotiate.

I would like to know how far the analogy actually carries all of this. Please write in American English, and organize the response under the following headings, in this order:

**The Argument Under Audit** (about 120 words). Restate the attending's reasoning as a set of distinct claims — the core analogy plus claims (i) and (ii) — so that we can see which of them is doing the work. If you think any of the three is separable from the others, say so here.

**Correspondence Table** (a table, roughly ten rows). Map the source onto the target element by element: Ulysses, the crew, the mast, the ropes, the wax in the crew's ears, the Sirens, the order not to release him, the instruction to bind him faster, the duration of the passage, the island as a discrete and foreseeable event, and Ulysses's own state of mind before and after. For each row, note in a final column whether the mapping is faithful, strained, or absent in the dementia case.

**Load-Bearing Disanalogies** (about 500 words). Only those mismatches that bear on the intended conclusion — that the directive should be honored against this patient's present interests and her present expressions. For each, say what it costs the argument. I expect at least one of them to concern whether the later self in the Odyssey case and the later self in this one are in the same relation to the earlier self, and at least one to concern the fact that the ropes in the story are physical while a directive must be read and applied by someone with discretion of their own.

**Disanalogies That Do Not Matter** (about 150 words). The obvious objections a critic would raise that are superficial and can be set aside, with a sentence on why each is a non-issue. I want this section because our conference audience will raise several of them and I would like to concede them quickly.

**What the Analogy Quietly Imports** (about 250 words). The premises about identity over time, about who counts as the beneficiary of the decision, about the character of the threatened harm, and about the standing of the people who must carry the order out, that the story supplies for free and that the clinical case has to earn. Be explicit about whether the analogy presupposes a view of personal identity that a reasonable person could reject.

**Verdict on the Three Claims** (about 350 words). For the core analogy, and separately for claims (i) and (ii), state whether it survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If any of them survives only in a weakened form, give the weakened form explicitly. I am especially interested in whether (ii) — that our discomfort is evidence the device is working — can be defended at all, or whether it is unfalsifiable as stated.

**A Better Comparison** (about 200 words). If some other comparison would carry the part of the attending's conclusion that does survive — something that preserves authority-by-succession while leaving room for the successor's own standing and for changed circumstances — propose it, give the mapping in brief, and say what it costs to switch. If nothing better is available, say that and explain why.

Please do not supply references or a bibliography; I want the analysis, not the literature. Aim for roughly 1,500 words in total.
