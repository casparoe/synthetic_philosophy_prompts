Write a dialogue of roughly 1,500 to 1,800 words between two epistemologists who have just finished co-teaching a graduate seminar on external-world scepticism and have stayed behind in the empty seminar room, with the argument still on the whiteboard, to settle a disagreement that the seminar failed to resolve. Give them names of your own choosing.

They agree on every preliminary. They agree that Moore's proof consists in holding up one hand, then the other, and concluding that at least two things of the kind he calls external objects exist; that the inference is valid; that the premises are distinct from the conclusion; that the conclusion is true; and that Moore was right to insist that one can know something without being able to prove it. Their disagreement concerns only what such a proof can accomplish for someone who doubts the conclusion.

The first, a conservative about perceptual warrant, holds that a perceptual belief is warranted only in the context of a prior, non-evidential entitlement to take for granted that one's faculties are reliable and that one is not dreaming or a handless brain in a vat. Because the sceptic withholds that entitlement, reasoning from the premise cannot confer warrant on the conclusion: transmission fails, and the proof amounts at most to a reminder of what Moore was already entitled to assume.

The second, a liberal (or dogmatist), holds that an experience as of a hand confers immediate, non-inferential warrant on the belief that here is a hand, that no such prior entitlement is needed, and that the warrant transmits normally through the valid inference — so Moore proves his conclusion in the ordinary sense. She concedes that the proof will not move a sceptic who actually doubts, and insists that this dialectical failure is an unremarkable fact about argument, not a symptom of anything wrong with the warrant.

Requirements for the exchange:

- Neither position may be a strawman. Give each its strongest available form, and have each press the other on the cost their view is known for: the conservative must explain why an entitlement resting on no evidence is anything other than arbitrary, and how such an entitlement can be lost and regained; the liberal must explain why immediate warrant does not license bootstrapping, whereby a subject iterates her own perceptual beliefs into knowing that her vision is reliable. Each must answer, and each must also give up something real.
- No character may rest a claim on its intuitiveness. If one does, the other should say so and demand an argument, and the demand must be met rather than deflected.
- Any use of the charge of begging the question must be disambiguated on the spot: is what is at issue that the warrant for the premise depends on the conclusion, that the argument presupposes what it is meant to establish, or merely that it is dialectically improper to deploy the argument against a doubter?
- Midway, introduce the following case, which neither has discussed, and have both work through it explicitly. Before a lecture, an announcer whom the audience has every reason to trust says that, as part of a demonstration, some of those present have been given a drug producing brief episodes of vivid visual and tactile experience of objects in near space; that these episodes are by design indistinguishable from veridical perception; and that no one will be told who has been dosed or when an episode occurs. A member of the audience then looks down and has an experience as of her own two hands. Have each character answer in order: (i) whether she has warrant for the belief that here are two hands; (ii) whether she may reason from that belief to the conclusion that the drug is not presently affecting her; (iii) whether the answer to (i) would differ if the announcement had come only after she formed the belief. Then make them compare notes. If the two views deliver the same verdicts on all three, one of them must say so plainly and the dialogue must then locate a question on which the views genuinely diverge; if they diverge, they must identify the precise structural commitment responsible.
- Moore's own two admissions — that he cannot prove his premises, and that knowledge does not require proof — must each be claimed by one character as support and resisted by the other, with reasons.
- End with two sentences written jointly on the whiteboard: one that both would sign, stating what they now agree the proof cannot do, and one that they cannot both sign, stating as exactly as they can manage the single claim on which their disagreement turns.

Paraphrase Moore and everyone else throughout: do not place any real philosopher's words inside quotation marks, and do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review by name. Terminology for the positions themselves — conservatism, liberalism or dogmatism, entitlement, transmission failure, hinge proposition — may be used freely.
