I'm the unpaid and very reluctant secretary of a community association in Sheffield — about forty-five of us, a mix of people who've been involved for twenty years and people like me who drifted in when the kids started at the after-school club in the hall. We run three things: the hall, a twice-weekly food bank, and a community garden on a council lease. Almost all of our deciding happens in one of three places and the three places don't agree with each other — a monthly meeting (nine to fourteen people usually turn up out of forty-five), a WhatsApp group that literally everyone is in, and a steering group of seven that technically signs off on any spending. In March it all blew up. Someone floated in the WhatsApp group the idea of taking on a council contract to run a winter night shelter out of the hall, about twelve people got very enthusiastic, nobody said no, and three weeks later our chair was quoted in the local paper saying we'd commited to it. I didn't see the thread until afterwards, and nor did maybe fifteen others, and two people who did see it now say they were against it the whole time but didn't want to be the one who dampened it. Meanwhile three of the enthusiasts have gone quiet, the food bank volunteers are furious because it's the same kitchen, and my partner thinks I should resign.

What I want isn't advice about this particular mess, it's a reusable procedure — one side of A4, something a facilitator can actually run in ten minutes in a room — that tells us when a bunch of us has genuinely decided or committed to something *jointly*, as against when we've just got a load of individuals who each happen to want the same thing or each intend to pitch in. I've read a bit around this since March and it turns out there's a real disagreement underneath: some people think joint commitment is built out of each member's interlocking plans and their responsiveness to each other, so you look for the meshing; others think a group can end up committed in a way that isn't the sum of anyone's attitudes at all, and that once you're in you can't just unilaterally climb out without wronging the others. Use whatever of that actually changes what we do on the ground — but no lecture, no reading list, no references, I'm not citing anything to anyone, I just need something the steering group of seven can adopt next Tuesday that works for people with no philosophy training. It should say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops and hands the thing back to the whole membership because it's a question of values rather than facts, and how long each step is supposed to take.

There are five situations it has to cope with, because all five keep recurring: (a) the WhatsApp one above — what would have to be true for a thread with a dozen keen people and no dissent to be a group decision, and does it matter that the thread is where everyone is but isn't where everyone is *looking*; (b) our consensus habit of standing aside, where someone lets a proposal pass without backing it — is a standing-aside member inside the commitment, i.e. can they be rebuked six weeks later for not helping, or is standing aside precisely the device the procedure should protect them with; (c) turnover — the garden group made a five-year promise to the council in 2021 and most of the people who made it have left, so does a commitment outlive the members who entered it, and if so in virtue of what; (d) the case, which I think is called the discursive dilemma, where the group ends up committed to a conclusion that no individual member actually holds because different people sign up to different premises; and (e) when one person can just walk away — a food bank volunteer wants to drop her Tuesday shift in week four of a six-week rota she signed up for, and I honestly cannot tell whether that's her business or a wrong to the rest of us. Run the procedure on three of these, and make sure at least one of the three comes out with an answer I won't like; I suspect the night shelter is going to come out saying we really are committed and that I'm bound by it even though I never saw the messages, in which case I want that argued straight rather than fudged for my benefit. Then finish by telling me which distinctions you had to drop or blunt to get it into ten minutes, and where this procedure is most likely to misfire in practice.
