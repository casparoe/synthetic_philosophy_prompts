Read The Remains of the Day for me as a Grice problem, and tell me honestly whether I'm onto something or just being clever at a novel's expense.

Some context so you know who you're dealing with. I have zero philosophy training. I've worked the front desk at a small hotel for twelve years, which means my entire job is hearing people say one thing and mean another — "the room is fine" is never a report about a room — and a few years back I went looking for a name for that and fell down a rabbit hole on conversational implicature. Grice, the cooperative principle, the maxims, what is said versus what is implicated, and that little taxonomy of ways you can fail to fulfil a maxim (flouting, exploiting, opting out, some third thing about a clash — I can never keep them straight). Here's the idea I can't shake. Everyone calls Stevens an unreliable narrator, and I think that's right but it skips the mechanism. Stevens isn't lying, or barely. He's actually extremely scrupulous about what is said — he polices the literal content of his sentences harder than anyone else in the book. What he does instead, over and over, is generate an implicature and then retract it before it can reach anybody, including himself. Grice's whole point about cancellability is that a conversational implicature can be defeated by adding something like "I don't mean to suggest that..." without contradicting what you said. Stevens lives inside that clause. He appends the disclaimer to everything, in his own head, in real time. For want of a better term I've been calling it cancelling on yourself. The places I keep thinking of: Miss Kenton bringing flowers into his room and him turning it into a matter of household propriety; her catching him with the romance novel and his explanation about improving his command of English; the cocoa evenings and how he narrates them as a scheduling arrangement; the evening she tells him she's engaged and what he does with that; the fact that he goes on calling her Miss Kenton for decades, which she eventually points out about his letters; his father dying upstairs during the conference and the story he tells himself about why that was the right thing. And Lord Darlington's orders about the two Jewish maids, where Stevens reports the order accurately and declines to report anything about his own view of it.

So, what I want:

2. Grice's account of speaker meaning runs on the speaker intending the hearer to recognise the intention. What happens to that when the speaker is also working to keep the intention out of his own view? Is Stevens still meaning things on Grice's account, or has the novel found a case the account wasn't built for? Related: if he's blocking recognition in every actual hearer, is the reader the only audience left, and does that make the implicature the novel's rather than his?

I find that detail unbearable. Is failing to get a joke a failure on one particular maxim, or is Stevens doing something more drastic — quietly opting out of the whole cooperative principle while continuing to look cooperative?
