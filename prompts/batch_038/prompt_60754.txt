I spent forty years designing experiments, and I have come to philosophy rather late with the same instinct: ask what a rule is for, and how one would know it had been broken. Reading about the norms of assertion, I keep meeting the claim that the knowledge rule is *constitutive* of assertion rather than merely regulative of it — analogous, we are told, to the rules of a game. Taken at face value, this seems to me to dissolve the debate before it has begun. If an utterance that falls short of knowledge is not strictly an assertion at all, in the way that a vector field failing to generate a symplectic flow is not strictly a Hamiltonian one, then the truth norm and the justification norm cannot be rivals to the knowledge norm; they would simply be describing some other practice, and choosing among the three would be a quarrel over words rather than over the governance of a single, independently identifiable act. Why, then, do philosophers persist in treating them as competing substantive norms? And is there anything left for the constitutive reading to explain, or does it render the knowledge rule true by stipulation — and hence, to my way of thinking, untestable?
