I spent thirty-eight years doing laser spectroscopy, retired two years ago, and took up philosophy of mind partly to keep the portion of my head that used to worry about measurement from going soft. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not an opinion, so please work as a professional philosopher of mind does — someone who has argued about this material with colleagues and has a view about where the arguments actually bite, not someone summarising a debate for a general readership. Think carefully before answering; the structure of the disagreement matters more to me than its outcome.

The dispute in question is illusionism about phenomenal consciousness against the realist camp. Illusionists — Dennett, Frankish and Rey are the names I keep meeting — hold that phenomenal properties as ordinarily conceived, that is intrinsic, private, non-functional qualities that constitute what-it-is-likeness, do not exist, and that we come to believe they do through an introspective process that systematically misdescribes the real, functionally characterisable states it reports on. The realist rejoinder, which I take to be the standard one rather than any particular author's, is that the position is not coherent: an illusion of experience would itself have to be an experience, so the illusionist either concedes the datum or explains away the very reports her argument needs, and the view thus collapses into eliminativism or quietly reinstates what it denied.

What puzzles me is that both sides appear to accept the same first-order facts. Same reports, same psychophysics, same neuroscience. Neither camp accuses the other of misreading the data, and yet nobody moves an inch. I have three hypotheses about why, and I would like you to test them rather than merely catalogue them.

One, calibration. In my own field a great many apparently intractable quarrels turn out to be quarrels about whether an apparatus is a measuring device or an artefact generator. On this reading the realists treat introspection as a calibrated instrument whose readouts are the data to be explained, while the illusionists treat it as an apparatus with a known systematic bias whose "phenomenal" output is exactly what a biased readout looks like from the inside. If that is the structure, the dispute ought to be decidable by third-person calibration work — confidence ratings against discrimination thresholds, change blindness, blindsight, the lability of report. Ask whether it in fact is, and whether the existence of such results on both sides refutes the hypothesis or merely shows that the calibration concept does not transfer.

Two, grain. Whether a feature is "there" can depend on the resolution at which you describe it. In spectroscopy, one broad band versus several resolved lines is not settled by nature alone but by the instrument and by the question being asked, and both descriptions are true at their own grain. If the consciousness dispute has this shape, then the illusionist and the realist are not contradicting each other so much as refusing each other's level of description, and the argument is about which grain is authoritative.

Three, stipulated reference. The term is introduced by stipulation, and the two camps then use the stipulation differently: the realist accepts it, denies anything physical could satisfy it, and concludes that the stipulation has misdescribed physics rather than that nothing satisfies it; the illusionist accepts it and denies anything satisfies it. If so, both are right about the concept and the live question is whether to keep it — a practical matter of conceptual engineering dressed as a metaphysical one.

There is a fourth observation I cannot place. The objection that an illusion of experience is itself an experience seems to gain its force only if "seems" in the premise is already being read as phenomenal, which is a reading the illusionist has explicitly refused. That would make the impasse something else again: each side's central argument valid under its own semantics and question-begging under the other's. I do not know whether that is a sharp point or a confusion wearing my own vocabulary, and I would like you to tell me which.
