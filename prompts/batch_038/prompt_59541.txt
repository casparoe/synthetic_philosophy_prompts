The conceptual issue I want explained is this. Everyone agrees that the proverbial horseshoe nail — the trivial component whose absence cascades into a lost battle and then a lost kingdom — is an image of historical contingency. Everyone also agrees that chaotic systems exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions. These two ideas are routinely run together, and I believe they are not the same notion at all. Please explain, as precisely as ordinary prose permits, how they come apart in both directions:

(a) How a process can be maximally sensitive in the technical dynamical sense — microscopic differences in state growing exponentially, so that fine-grained prediction beyond a short horizon is impossible — while remaining entirely robust with respect to every macro-outcome a historian would bother to record.

(b) How a process can exhibit no amplification whatever, no exponential divergence, no positive Lyapunov exponent — and yet have its macro-outcome flipped by a perturbation so small that a physicist would treat it as negligible.

In the course of the explanation, address at least the following:

1. Sensitive dependence is defined relative to a metric on the state space and to a coarse-graining of that space, neither of which the dynamics itself supplies. Explain why "how chaotic is history?" is consequently not a well-posed question until one has fixed (i) the class of admissible perturbations, (ii) the macro-description under which outcomes are compared, and (iii) the time horizon. I have in mind something like: exponential amplification of microscopic differences; the crossing of a threshold or discontinuity, where nothing is amplified but a switch is thrown; and the breaking of a coordination or tie, where a system that would otherwise have settled into one of several available arrangements is nudged into a different one. 3. Distinguish predictive underdetermination (an epistemic fact about what can be forecast) from counterfactual dependence (a modal fact about which nearby possibilities differ in outcome).
