Consider the debate over the hole argument in the philosophy of spacetime physics. Einstein formulated a version of it in 1913, it lay dormant for decades, and its modern revival by Earman, Stachel, and Norton in the 1980s produced an enormous literature and, so far as I can tell, no agreement. Here is how the field looks to me from the outside.

Every party accepts the same mathematics: general relativity is formulated on a manifold of events carrying a metric and matter fields; the field equations are invariant under diffeomorphisms; and a diffeomorphism that is the identity everywhere outside some matter-free region H yields a mathematically distinct model that agrees with the original on every diffeomorphism-invariant quantity and on every possible observation. What they disagree about is what follows from that. Broadly:

- One camp treats diffeomorphic models as related by a gauge symmetry — they represent one and the same physical situation, so there is no failure of determinism; the price is that the argument does show manifold substantivalism, the thesis that spacetime points are individuals with identities of their own prior to and independent of the fields, to be untenable.
- A second camp accepts that equivalence and keeps the points, holding that possible worlds related by a diffeomorphism are one world differently described; on this view the argument's conclusion requires haecceitism, the further thesis that two worlds can differ solely in which point plays which role, and that thesis can simply be refused.
- A third camp thinks the argument never gets off the ground: it depends on an unargued reading of models on which a diffeomorphism pairs the points of one model with the points of a different one, whereas if the manifold points are the physically real entities and diffeomorphisms relate a model to itself, there is no second solution and no indeterminism to explain away.
- A fourth camp concludes that what is real is the invariant geometric and causal structure rather than the points, so the argument is not a refutation of realism about spacetime but a clarification of which part of the formalism carries physical content.
- Running through all of this is a further dispute about method: whether a general criterion of theoretical equivalence — isomorphism up to diffeomorphism, or some categorical equivalence between theories — can identify the surplus structure once and for all, or whether no purely formal or model-theoretic criterion can decide what counts as gauge, so that only physical considerations such as verifiability and determinism can do the work.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than assumed, is that nobody in this debate disagrees about any fact of differential geometry, about what general relativity says, or about what is observable. The disagreement is entirely about a prior question in semantics: what the domain of a model is for, and what it takes for two models to be about the same world. The camps use "represents the same physical situation" with different criteria, one keyed to empirical indistinguishability and the other to the identity of individuals, and they therefore cannot contradict each other. On this picture no further theorem about diffeomorphism-invariant quantities could settle the matter, and the appearance of a substantive metaphysical dispute about spacetime is an artifact of running together two questions — whether general relativity is deterministic, and whether spacetime points have identity independent of the fields.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than an adjudication of it. Is my hypothesis correct? Is the dispute verbal, or substantive but about standards of theory-choice rather than about the world, or a case of two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work — for instance, whether a generally covariant theory possesses enough diffeomorphism-invariant observables to localize events, which is where this debate touches the problem of time in canonical quantum gravity? Or is the hole argument simply a hard question rather than an ill-posed one? Does it matter at all to the philosophical dispute that some historians hold the modern reconstruction to misrepresent Einstein's 1913 reasoning?

Support your diagnosis by setting out what each of these positions is actually committed to and identifying the precise point at which their commitments diverge, rather than by surveying the literature. Then state what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction to be drawn, a formal result to be proved, an experiment, a reformulation of the question, or a reason to expect none.
