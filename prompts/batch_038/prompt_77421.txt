I am revising the first sections of a paper on the bootstrapping problem, and I would be grateful for a critical read of the draft below. It is meant to stand on its own, and I have deliberately left out the citation apparatus at this stage, so please do not send me references or a reading list; what I need is a judgment on the argument itself. Here is the draft.

The familiar case is this. A driver has no antecedent reason to think her fuel gauge is accurate. She glances at it, it reads F, and she comes to know that the tank is full — or so a reliabilist must say, since the gauge is in fact reliable and her belief is formed by a process that tracks the state of the tank. She then notes that the gauge read F and that the tank was indeed full, so the gauge read correctly on this occasion. Repeating the procedure over a hundred tankfuls, she accumulates a hundred such successes and concludes that the gauge is a reliable indicator of fuel level. Vogel called this bootstrapping and deployed it against reliabilism. Cohen pressed the more general point: the trouble is not peculiar to reliabilism, since any view that permits basic knowledge — knowledge that p obtained from a source without prior knowledge that the source is reliable — together with the closure of knowledge under known entailment, licenses the reasoning. The standard exits are to deny closure, to deny that basic knowledge exists, or to deny that warrant transmits across the step in question.

My proposal is that the diagnosis should be structural rather than substantive, and that the right place to look is an asymmetry that the standard framing obscures. Run the inference in the other direction. Suppose the driver already knows that the gauge is reliable; she reads F and concludes that the tank is full. Nobody objects to that. Both directions are applications of closure to a premise that is, by hypothesis, known. So the defect cannot lie in closure, and it cannot lie in the epistemic status of the first-order premise, which is the same in both cases. The difference must lie in the relation between the content of the conclusion and whatever it is that makes the premise warranted in the first place.

Now, what warrants the premise is not itself a premise the driver possesses. She does not reason from the gauge's reliability to the reading's accuracy; she simply takes the reading at face value. The warrant rides on a connection — the covariation between gauge states and tank states — that functions as an enabler of the inference rather than as an input to it. In the acceptable direction, the conclusion is a consequence of the content of what she knows. In the objectionable direction, the conclusion is a generalization of the enabling connection itself. That suggests the following principle, which I will call the Generalization Constraint: warrant transmits from a premise to conclusions that follow from the premise's content, but it does not transmit to a conclusion that generalizes over the connection on which the warrant for the premise depends. Bootstrapping, on this account, is transmission failure under generalization. It is not a species of circularity, and it does not require us to say that the driver has assumed anything she has not established.

This has two explanatory advantages. First, it captures the intuition that the reasoning is not question-begging and yet is still defective, which is precisely the combination that makes the case hard. The driver never premises the reliability of the gauge; she generalizes over the enabler her warrant silently rides on, and generalizing over an enabler is not the same as assuming it. Second, it makes contact with the wider family of cases in which an ordinary perceptual warrant seems unable to support a conclusion that is entailed by what it supports, including the hinge-style cases in which a warrant for a claim about one's immediate surroundings is said to fail to transmit to a claim about the external world. Those conclusions, too, are best read as generalizations over the enabler — that one's faculties put one in touch with a mind-independent environment — rather than as mere entailments of the first-order content. I flag this second claim as the part of the account I am least sure of, since the entailment relation in such cases is obvious and the generalizing relation is not, and I am not certain the two can be kept apart.

The immediate objection is that the constraint condemns far too much. Enumerative induction looks like exactly the same shape of inference: I accumulate observations and generalize, and my warrant for each observation rides on the same enabling connection I am now generalizing over. If that is bootstrapping, all induction is bootstrapping. My reply is that the two generalizations differ in their objects. Ordinary induction generalizes over the contents of the observations — the world's regularities, the sun's rising, the copper's conducting. It leaves the instrument-to-world mapping fixed and says something about the domain the mapping reaches. Bootstrapping generalizes over the mapping itself. Induction is therefore untouched by the constraint, and the constraint earns its keep by drawing a line that we independently want drawn.

A corollary is worth stating because it is strong. If the constraint holds, no amount of accumulation from a single source can yield knowledge of that source's reliability, however many readings one amasses and however uniform they are. This is not yet skepticism, because it is a claim about the shape of self-support rather than about the extent of our knowledge, and it predicts exactly where reliability knowledge must come from: a second, independently individuated source. We check the fuel gauge against the dipstick, the wristwatch against the time signal, the reading against the tactile measurement. It also fits how such knowledge is acquired developmentally, since a child learns that her eyes are trustworthy by comparing what she sees with what she grasps, not by tallying her visual successes.

I see three problems with all of this, and I would rather raise them myself than have them discovered. The corollary depends on the two sources being genuinely independent, and it is not obvious that sight and touch are independent in the required sense; if both draw on a common apparatus, cross-modal comparison may be bootstrapping with an extra step, in which case the corollary threatens to collapse into the global skepticism it was meant to avoid. The account also needs a criterion for what makes a source independently individuated, and if individuation turns on our descriptions rather than on the world, the epistemology will inherit whatever arbitrariness the taxonomy contains. Finally, it is not clear to me that the Generalization Constraint is a substantive thesis rather than a redescription of transmission failure in a new vocabulary, with the word generalization doing nothing but marking the cases we had already identified as bad.

The payoff I am hoping for concerns the skeptic's demand. The Cartesian challenge asks us to establish, in advance of trusting perception, that perception is reliable. On my account that demand is not merely unsatisfiable but malformed in a determinate way: reliability of perception as a whole is a generalization over the enabler for which no second source is available from inside the practice, so no possible evidence bears on it. The correct response to the skeptic would then be not to attempt to show that we can bootstrap after all, but to deny that the target of the challenge is a genuine empirical hypothesis about our faculties.
