I cover neuroscience and the philosophy of mind for a national magazine, and once a year I set and mark the take-home for a module in a graduate program for science writers, in which students must convert a live philosophical dispute into something a news desk could act on. This year's assignment read:

News desks periodically receive studies pitched as evidence that conscious decisions do not cause actions — readiness-potential experiments in which neural activity begins before the subject reports having decided, or transcranial magnetic stimulation that reportedly removes the reported intention while leaving the movement intact. Explain Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and then say what such a study could and could not contribute to that question, writing for an editor who has to decide whether the pitch stands up.

One student submitted the following:

"Kim's argument starts from causal closure: every physical event is determined by prior physical events, so if my raising my arm has a complete neural cause there is no causal work left over for anything else. It then adds exclusion: no effect has more than one sufficient cause unless we are willing to say it is systematically overdetermined, which Kim rules out, since two independent sufficient causes for every single action would be an implausible coincidence. Nonreductive physicalists hold that mental properties supervene on neural properties without being identical with them, which makes the conscious intention a distinct sufficient cause competing with its own neural realizer, and Kim's conclusion is therefore that conscious intentions are epiphenomenal. The studies in the assignment bear directly on this, because they test it: a readiness potential that begins before the subject reports deciding shows the neural cause was already in place, and a TMS pulse that removes the reported intention while the movement continues shows the intention was not needed. But they cannot settle the dispute, because a neural cause that precedes or accompanies an action is perfectly compatible with the intention also causing it, and exclusion is a metaphysical principle rather than a hypothesis any experiment could falsify. An editor should treat such pitches as stories about the timing of awareness, not about mental causation."

Please mark it. Reply with a grade out of 100 followed by exactly five numbered comments of one or two sentences each, the grade at the head of the first comment, with no introduction or conclusion; each comment should name a specific claim in the answer and say what you would tell the student about it.
