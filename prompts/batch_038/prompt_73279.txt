I am auditing a graduate seminar on the neuroscience of agency this term, and after last week's session on the Libet paradigm two of my classmates — Owen, who is in the psychology department and works on motor control, and Nadia, a philosophy student writing on moral responsibility — got into a long argument in the hallway that I was unable to referee. I have to write the seminar's response paper on this material and I would rather get the argument right than repeat the received line, so I am reconstructing their exchange as faithfully as I can and asking you to settle it.

Owen opened with the standard reading: in the original experiment the readiness potential begins roughly 550 milliseconds before the finger movement, whereas subjects asked to watch a rotating clock face and report when they first became aware of the urge to move place that awareness only about 200 milliseconds before the movement, so the brain has committed some 350 milliseconds before consciousness arrives, which makes the feeling of initiating an action a story told after the fact. Nadia's first reply was that the half-second figure is an artifact of how the potential is measured: it is an average aligned to movement onset, and if subthreshold neural activity drifts noisily toward a movement threshold, then averaging backward from the crossings that happened to trigger a movement will manufacture a slow ramp long before anything decisive occurs, so the crossing itself may sit much closer to movement onset than the averaged trace implies. Owen conceded the modeling point but said it changes nothing that matters: even on that picture the crossing is driven by unconscious drift, and the only positive evidence Nadia has for conscious initiation is the clock report, which is itself demonstrably shaky, since the reported time of awareness shifts when a tone is played shortly after the movement and the rotating-dot method is hardly a neutral readout of an inner event. Nadia then accused him of applying a double standard: he had just admitted the neural measure cannot be trusted to date an unconscious event, yet he was using the unreliability of the introspective measure to date the absence of a conscious one, so if neither instrument can tell him when anything happened, his conclusion is assumed rather than supported. She added that Libet himself refused to draw Owen's conclusion and invoked a veto — free won't — in the last hundred to two hundred milliseconds, and that later imaging work on intentional abstention identified a distinct region recruited when people deliberately decline to act, which on her reading shows consciousness doing genuine causal work in the control of action. Owen's final move was that the veto is not in the same evidential boat at all, because withholding can be measured without asking anyone to date a private event: cue a subject to cancel and you can measure cancellation rates and their neural correlates directly, whereas the awareness report depends entirely on introspective timing. He closed by saying that even if the veto is real, a will restricted to cancellation is not the freedom people think they have when they say they chose to raise their hand.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who is right at each point — the opening statement of the result, the averaging objection, Owen's concession-and-redirect, the double-standards charge together with the veto argument, and the closing reply about the difference between timing a private event and measuring cancellation? For each, say whether the move is sound, partly sound, or mistaken and what specifically makes it so, flagging any place where one of them is trading on a description of the experiment that is not quite accurate. Then tell me what neither of them raised that a referee should have brought into the room, and end with your bottom line on whether this family of evidence licenses the conclusion Owen wanted. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a considered letter: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bolded phrases, and no tables, working through the five moves in the order I gave them, with roughly a paragraph each, and saving the omissions and the overall verdict for the final two paragraphs.
