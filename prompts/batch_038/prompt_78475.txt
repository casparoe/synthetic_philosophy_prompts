I've recently joined a long-term acoustic study of a resident bottlenose dolphin community: decades of recordings, a well-mapped catalogue of signature whistles and burst-pulse sounds, and no bilingual informant. Several of my colleagues insist that Davidson's programme of radical interpretation supplies the right theoretical frame for what we are doing. Before I read further into it, I would like to know what to demand of such a theory.

Write the desiderata: what any account of how an interpreter moves from observed behaviour in circumstances to determinate meanings must deliver, with the case or argument motivating each item. For every requirement, say explicitly whether it is constitutive of there being meaning at all or merely a constraint on our method of uncovering it — because that distinction decides whether a failed decoding shows the animals lack language or shows our procedure is deficient. At minimum, address: charity as something applicable without already knowing the truth of the sentences being interpreted; the attributability of systematic error and of merely local irrationality; the underdetermination left by inscrutability of reference; and compositionality plus finite statability. Flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list is itself a result, that is what I most want to see — and identify which single requirement eliminates the most candidates.

Then briefly: how Davidson's own account, Lewis's version with a naturalness constraint, and Dennett's interpretationism fare against it.

Please be candid about the standing of the list itself: how confident you are that these are the right requirements, which one you would most expect a careful reader to dismiss as an artifact of my engineering interests rather than a genuine philosophical demand, and which single claim you are least sure of.
