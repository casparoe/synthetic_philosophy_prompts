I am in my fourth year of a philosophy PhD, working on the ethics of harm, and this spring I am teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar whose middle unit is the doctrine of doing and allowing. For the week where we move from the classic cases into harder structural ones, I want to screen the ferry sequence from Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight, partly because nearly every student in the room has already seen the film and partly because the sequence has an unusually clean architecture for our purposes. As I recall the setup: the Joker has rigged two evacuation ferries leaving Gotham with explosives, has handed the passengers of each boat the detonator for the other boat's bomb, and has announced that unless one boat blows up the other by midnight he will destroy both. One ferry carries ordinary civilian evacuees, the other carries prisoners and their guards, so the two boatloads are neither equal in number nor equal in social standing. On the civilian boat the passengers hold a vote, a majority endorses detonating the other ferry, a well-dressed man volunteers to do it, and then finds he cannot; on the prisoner boat a large inmate takes the detonator from the guards, walks to the window, and throws it into the river. The deadline arrives, neither boat has acted, and the Joker is stopped before he can finish the job. If any of those details are off, correct me and work from the film as you know it.

What I want is a reading of that sequence — and, only if it genuinely bears on the argument, of the film's final confrontation, in which Dent dies and Batman accepts public blame for it — in terms of the doing/allowing distinction, taking the philosophical apparatus seriously rather than using it as decoration. Several specific questions matter to me. First, does the sequence actually present a doing-versus-allowing choice, or does the Joker's threat quietly collapse the contrast, so that refraining from pressing the detonator is itself a way of bringing about the deaths of everyone on both boats through somebody else's agency? Second, if the contrast survives, what does the sequence suggest about the difference between allowing a person's wrongful killing and merely failing to prevent a death that no one is causing — is the presence of an identifiable wrongdoer doing any moral work in what the passengers feel? Third, what happens to the distinction under the vote on the civilian boat: does collective authorization convert an individual doing into a distributed omission, and if so is that a moral improvement, a moral evasion, or simply a fact about how the sequence is shot? Fourth, is the prisoner's disposal of the detonator an omission at all, or a highly agential refusal that functions as a commitment device binding everyone on board — and does it matter that he is the one character with the least standing to be believed? I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of Quinn's framework or of Foot's negative and positive duties, and do not bend the doctrine to make the sequence tidier than it is. Where the sequence resists the reading, or where an honest critic has to concede that Nolan is using a moral dilemma as a set piece rather than advancing a position about it, please say so plainly, and say what in the filmmaking — the cross-cutting, the treatment of the two boatloads, the way the decision is withheld rather than argued — makes you think so.

Let me also put my own hypothesis on the table so you have something to push against. I have come to suspect that the sequence gets its rhetorical force precisely from the fact that the doing/allowing distinction does no justificatory work in it, and yet the passengers experience it as though it did: what is dramatized is the phenomenology of the distinction, not its logic, and the film is uninterested in telling us which of them is tracking the truth. Tell me whether you think the film endorses that reading, undercuts it, or is simply indifferent to it, and tell me where you disagree with me if you do. Finally, I want to know whether the sequence gets something right about coerced choice — about the way a threat from a third party can restructure an agent's options so that the morally significant question is no longer doing or allowing but something else entirely — that the standard treatments, which mostly argue over whether the distinction is a distinction without a moral difference, tend to miss. Please close by identifying two or three specific moments you would actually put in front of undergraduates and the question you would attach to each. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I know that literature well, and what I need is your interpretation.
