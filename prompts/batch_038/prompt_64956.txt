I do reliability tooling for a mid-size cloud provider, and I've been handed a task I'm not sure is coherent: write the spec for an engine that, given the telemetry of an incident, outputs an actual causal attribution — including attributions to things that *didn't* happen. The reason is a March event. A rack overheated at 03:10; the operator on shift had an unacknowledged thermal alarm sitting from 02:55; and the second cooling pump, which would have held the temperature on its own, had been down for maintenance since 22:00 with a ticket nobody ever escalated. Legal asked us to name the cause. Every account I read either names far too many things or none, and I've now been told to pick the semantics before we build anything. So before I choose, I want the requirements list: what an account of causation by omission has to be able to deliver to serve as the semantics underneath something like this. Not an essay on the debate — the spec, with the case or consideration behind each item.

Two constraints from our situation that you should treat as fixed, because I suspect they do most of the work. First, the engine only ever sees machine data: sensor logs, alarm records, shift rosters, maintenance tickets, config history. No access to anybody's beliefs, knowledge, or intentions, ever. Second, the output goes on an internal dashboard that non-lawyers read and that sometimes gets handed over in a dispute, so an attribution has to be auditable — it can't depend on a fact the engine has no way to observe, and it has to be reproducible when the same trace is fed in again. Third, minor but real: the dashboard has a timestamp field for each named cause.

Build the list around these three, they're the ones that keep breaking things:

- **The two-shift rack.** As described above. Either the missed ack or the missed escalation alone would have prevented the overheat. But so would a hundred other non-events: the vendor's remote watchdog didn't fire, a hurricane somewhere else didn't take out the grid, an architect five years ago didn't specify N+2 redundancy, I didn't personally fly to that datacenter and press the button. We need something determinate and not obviously arbitrary, and we need it to stay determinate when I re-run the trace with three more sleepy operators added, or thirty.
- **Two teams, one check.** Our compliance rule says two separate groups must each run a pre-deploy validation. Both skipped it. Either validation on its own would have caught the bad config that later caused a six-hour outage. Counterfactually, neither group's skipping made any difference — and yet we're expected to name both, not neither. Adjacent problem: there was also a third group that *could* have run the check but was never assigned it and had no reason to. That one we're expected to leave out. Whatever separates those two is a requirement, not a nicety.
- **The dutyless bystander.** A contractor from a completely unrelated company had read-only access to the same console, saw the alarm, understood what it meant, could have acked it in one click, and didn't. Variant: a person in another country with no access at all who could not have known anything about it. Our intuition is that neither is a cause of the overheating, but on a purely structural reading both are just omissions that made a difference. And remember the constraint: the engine can't see what either of them knew.

For each requirement, say why it's a requirement and whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or a preference of one camp — a colleague insists that causation must be backed by an actual physical process and therefore that omissions are never causes at all, and I'd like to know whether that's a real constraint on the spec or just a flag he's planting. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the largest number of otherwise respectable accounts. And the part I most want: if some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given the constraints above, say so flatly and say which one I should give up, because a spec that can't be met is worse than one that's knowingly incomplete. Finish with a couple of sentences each on how the main families — process or conserved-quantity views, counterfactual/interventionist views, norm-relativist views, and the structural-equation accounts of actual causation — do against the list. No references or reading list, I can't chase those anyway, I just need the criteria.
