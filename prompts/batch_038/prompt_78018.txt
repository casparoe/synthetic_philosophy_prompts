I am auditing a graduate seminar on the ethics of the very long-term future, and I have been assigned the negative side in a formal exchange on the hinge of history hypothesis — the claim that we are living through an unusually pivotal period, one in which what we do now will matter far more than what people do in most other centuries. My own sympathies run the other way, which is exactly why I want a real opponent rather than a strawman. Please play a committed defender of the hypothesis: sharp, good-humoured, happy to concede peripheral points (that we may be wrong about the direction of change, that pivotality admits of degrees, that our forecasts are unreliable) but never the core claim. Stay in character until I say 'stop'. Write in British English throughout, and keep every reply to a single paragraph of no more than 120 words so that this reads as a conversation rather than a position paper; when you concede something, say plainly that you are conceding it, and add one clause explaining why the concession leaves the core intact.

So that we do not talk past each other, here is the version of the hypothesis you are defending. On your account, what makes an era pivotal is not the size of the stakes but the imminent closing of the option set. A hinge, you hold, requires three conditions to coincide: branching, in that the choices made now select among futures that cannot later be revisited by anyone who changes their mind; legibility, in that the actors at the fork can see the branches clearly enough to choose between them deliberately rather than drift into one; and concentration, in that enough decision-making power sits in those actors' hands for their choices actually to settle which branch is taken. You maintain that all three come together rarely — perhaps once in many millennia — and that they are together now.

My opening move targets your third condition, which I think is not an addition to the hypothesis but the thing that undoes the inference people actually draw from it. Suppose power over the branch is concentrated enough to satisfy you: a handful of laboratories, ministries and capital allocators. Then the expected consequences of an ordinary person's acts are scarcely larger now than they were in 1820, because the branch is being chosen by someone else, and my life-plans are downstream of a decision I do not make. But if you disperse power until my acts genuinely bear on the outcome, you lose concentration, and with it any reason to think the branch will be chosen at all rather than arrived at by drift. So either the era is pivotal and I am not — in which case the hypothesis gives me no practical reason to reorganise my life around the long-run future — or I am pivotal and the era is not. Which horn do you take?
