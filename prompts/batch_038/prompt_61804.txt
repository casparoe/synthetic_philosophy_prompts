Two of our working-group members have spent a year arguing past each other about online misinformation, and I want the document their dispute should have produced: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them.

The two parties are these.

**Party A — the Exposure View.** The relevant pathology is the *epistemic bubble*: a structure that excludes other voices largely by omission, because users sort themselves, follow their own kind, and click on what they already like. Algorithmic ranking matters less than people assume; on a large-sample analysis of how users interact with socially shared news, individual choice limited exposure to cross-cutting content more strongly than the ranking did. Therefore the remedy is cheap, unavoidable contact with opposing claims, plus media literacy: widen what reaches the user and let ordinary epistemic faculties do the rest.

**Party B — the Discrediting View.** The relevant pathology is the *echo chamber* proper: a structure that actively teaches members to distrust outside sources — mainstream outlets, experts, fact-checkers, the other party's motives. Bubbles are broken by exposure; chambers are not, because exposure arrives already flagged as hostile testimony, and can even be metabolized as further proof of the outside world's bias. Therefore exposure-based remedies are at best inert and at worst counterproductive; the target must be the trust relations themselves — source transparency, accountability of intermediaries, and slow repair of institutional credibility.

Both accept the following empirical findings as data, and the joint statement must treat them as jointly owned, not as one side's ammunition: (i) in a randomized field study, Republicans assigned to follow a liberal Twitter bot expressed substantially more conservative attitudes afterward, while Democrats assigned to follow a conservative bot showed slight liberal increases that did not reach significance; (ii) users over 65 shared nearly seven times as many articles from fake-news domains as the youngest cohort, an effect that persisted after controlling for partisanship and ideology; (iii) corrective information reduces belief in misinformation on average, and dramatic "backfire" effects have proven hard to replicate.

Please produce the statement with the following components.

1. **Numbered agreed claims.** What both can sign — about what a bubble and a chamber are, about which of the three findings above each view must explain and how, about what a platform can and cannot measure, about which policy instruments are non-starters for both. Flag any claim that is only a superficial agreement resting on an ambiguity, and say so in the entry.

2. **A disagreements table.** Each row: the precise formulation of the disagreement, stated in wording that each side would accept as a fair statement of *its own* position; whether the disagreement is about mechanism, magnitude, or remedy; and what each side thinks the other is illicitly assuming.

3. **A crux register.** Identify four to six sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. For each crux, give a concrete test or case both accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction in advance — direction of effect and, where it makes sense, the threshold below which the party concedes. I want the cruxes to include at least one built around a three-arm platform experiment both are asked to pre-commit to: Arm 1 receives feed-level diversity injection; Arm 2 receives *epistemic friction* on sharing (a brief delay plus a panel showing the source's ownership, correction history, and prior reliability rating); Arm 3 receives both; with a passive control. Outcomes: sharing of claims later rated false, self-reported trust in mainstream outlets, and affective polarization toward the out-party. Write each party's predicted ordering of the arms on each outcome, and name the result that would force each to abandon its view rather than reinterpret it.

4. **The verbal-dispute section.** Say which parts of the year-long argument were about words — for instance, whether "echo chamber" as used by the public, by journalists, and by social epistemologists picks out the same phenomenon, and whether "the algorithm did this" is even a candidate explanation given finding (i). Where you conclude a dispute was terminological, show the two readings and what each party was actually claiming.

5. **A closing paragraph on residual disagreement.** What genuinely survives the collaboration, stated so narrowly that no third party could mistake it for a broader dispute, and what each party commits to stop saying.

Aim for 1,100 to 1,500 words. Keep the tone of a document both would put their names to: no adjudicating who was right, no hedged summaries of the literature, and no invented studies — where you need an empirical claim beyond the three above, describe the kind of evidence required rather than asserting that it exists.
