I teach a third-year normative ethics seminar of sixteen students in a single 110-minute session, in a room with one long table and movable chairs. They have worked through total utilitarianism in its Sidgwickian form and have read the opening chapter of Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), but the separateness-of-persons objection has reached them only as a one-line aside, and when I lectured it last year the response was polite note-taking and blank stares at the follow-up essay question. I would like a runnable in-class activity — plus debrief — in which they collide with the objection themselves before anyone names it: no jargon, no framing lecture, and the labels (including the Rawlsian charge that utilitarianism extends to society the principles of choice appropriate to a single rational individual and thereby fails to take the distinction between persons seriously, and Nozick's counterpoint from *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) that there is no social entity with a good of its own that can be sacrificed for that good) appear only at the end. Budget constraints are real but modest: no money and nothing tied to their grades, since I want the stakes felt rather than coercive — a shared bag of chocolates, control over how the final ten minutes of class are spent, or something of that order is my ceiling.

The asymmetry I want them to feel is between intrapersonal and interpersonal pooling. Round A: one student plays the successive temporal stages of a single life and must decide whether an early stage absorbs a cost that a later stage repays with interest; nearly everyone judges this obviously rational, because it is just prudence. Round B: identical arithmetic and an identical grand total, except that the stages are now distributed across several students who share one ledger, none of them able to opt out. Round C: the same final allocation is reachable, but through separate ledgers where every transfer requires the transferee's consent. The debrief should then force them to say what changed between A and B, given that the numbers did not move, and what changed between B and C, given that the outcome may not have moved either — and to nominate, from what they actually experienced, what the single subject in Round A possessed that the pooled group in Round B lacked (shared memory and anticipation, one stream of experience, one deliberating agent, identification with the later stage), with each candidate answer then pressed for its own problems. Here is my worry, and the reason I am asking rather than improvising: Round B risks collapsing into a demonstration about coercion, since it looks like Round C with the right of refusal withdrawn, and I fear my students will credit their discomfort to being overruled rather than to being aggregated, so that the session ends up vindicating a libertarian point about consent while the intended target — that the calculus treats a plurality of lives as though they were one experiencing subject — slips past unnoticed. Please build the mechanics so that coercion cannot be the explanation (for instance, unanimous advance agreement to pool, so that what differs from Round C is the structure of the ledger and not the presence of force), and tell me honestly whether that is achievable at all or whether the objection simply cannot be staged this cleanly.

What I need back: the full rules and a materials list (index-card chits or dried beans are fine); a conversion scheme with concrete numbers for a table of sixteen such that the totals in A, B, and C are literally the same and the only difference is structural; assigned roles, including what to do with any student left idle in a given round, since a bored participant is worse than a confused one; a minute-by-minute timeline; the debrief as an ordered list of questions, each mapped onto the specific moment in play that was supposed to generate it and onto the corresponding move in the Rawls and Nozick arguments (paraphrase only — I will supply the quotations and page references myself); the two or three failures I should expect on a first run and how to recover from each without breaking the fiction, where I would guess at one student with an economics background attempting to open a futures market in Round C and at least one student in Round B flatly refusing to act as someone else's stage; and a candid closing paragraph on which feature of the objection the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that it is precisely the unity the objection invokes: because the game cannot hand the pooled team a single stream of experience, the students in Round B will feel their own separateness throughout, which may make the objection seem either trivially true or question-begging rather than forceful. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and what a facilitator can say in the room to salvage the point once the game has, inevitably, failed to deliver it.
