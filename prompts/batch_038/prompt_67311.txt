I've been logging my gut calls on when an indicative conditional is okay to say out loud, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Take "assertable" in the ordinary sense: I could say it to someone without misleading them, given what I know.

The rule I've been testing myself against is: you may assert "if A then C" just when the probability of C given A is high on your evidence, and how likely A itself is doesn't matter.

Cases, with my verdicts.

1. Maya may or may not have caught the 7:15. I'd put P(she caught it) at about 0.2 and P(she's here by nine | she caught it) at about 0.95. "If Maya caught the 7:15 she'll be here by nine" — completely fine to say, and I feel no pull at all to believe she caught it.

2. Our printer jams maybe one run in ten, and when it jams it's the paper tray about three times in ten (rest is toner, damp paper, whatever). So "either the printer won't jam on this run or the tray is misaligned" is ~0.93 likely and I'd bet on it happily. But "if the printer jams on this run, the tray is misaligned" I would not say. Nearly the same information, opposite verdicts, and the thing I'd bet on is the more probable of the two.

3. "If the meeting's still on tonight I'll be there" — said by me, after I've read the cancellation email. Feels like a joke or a small con, not an assertion. Worse, my rule doesn't even get a grip here, since I've given the antecedent probability zero and you can't conditionalise on that.

4. Sam: P(he's at the party) = 0.9, P(he's having a good time | he's there) = 0.93. "If Sam's at the party he's having a good time" comes out assertable, and I guess its fine, but it feels strictly worse than case 1, which has almost the same conditional probability and a far less likely antecedent. I'd rather just say Sam's having a good time. My rule can't see any difference between 1 and 4.

5. The one that actually bothers me. Alice and Ben are both forecasting whether a front stalls over the valley and triggers a flood warning. Alice has been staring at moisture fields off one model; on her evidence P(warning | stalls) ≈ 0.95. Ben has been watching a ridge build offshore off a different model; on his evidence P(no warning | stalls) ≈ 0.9. I think Alice is entitled to say "if the front stalls over the valley they'll issue the warning" and Ben is entitled to say "if the front stalls they won't." I also think those can't both be true, and I don't want to say truth is just whatever falls out of the speaker's evidence — one of them is getting the weather wrong. But I can't say which, and I don't think either of them has misspoken or is being reckless.

6. "If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy then someone else did" strikes me as perfectly good, and the counterfactual version, if he hadn't, someone else would have, strikes me as false. My rule handles the first and simply doesn't apply to the second, which feels like a dodge rather than an explanation.

So: is there a principle that gets these, or nearly all of them? State it — or the two or three rival versions — precisely, say which case is doing the work for each clause, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it has to overrule. I'd rather give up two or three verdicts than end up with something that just patches each case individually. And there are two things I've left undecided where I want the principle's call: (a) what "it's not the case that if the front stalls they'll issue the warning" actually says — to my ear that's much stronger than "the conditional probability isn't high," it sounds like a claim that the front stalls and no warning goes out; and (b) nested ones, like "if the front stalls, then if the warning goes out, the bridges get closed." Does my rule just apply twice, and if so am I committed to that being equivalent to "if the front stalls and the warning goes out, the bridges get closed"? Because if I am, I suspect I'm already standing in the territory of Lewis's result that probabilities of conditionals can't track conditional probabilities across a whole language without the language collapsing into something trivial — and I'd like to know whether cases 1 through 6 push me there on their own, or whether that only bites once you start embedding conditionals inside conditionals.
