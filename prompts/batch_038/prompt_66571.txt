I run adversarial evals at a mid-size lab, and for the better part of two years our little working group has been trying to pin down when a model's output counts as deception, and we have gotten nowhere. Not for lack of cleverness. Everyone already knows every argument. Roughly there are three camps.

Camp one says drop the mental stuff entirely: a system deceives when its outputs reliably cause a user to hold a belief that is false and that a better-calibrated system wouldn't have caused. Intention, "belief," internal states — none of it earns its keep, and anyway nobody in this camp can say what would count as a transformer believing anything. Camp two says that's absurdly overbroad, since on that defintion a badly calibrated weather model, a broken autocomplete, and my grandmother's Fitbit are all liars. Deception requires that the system in some sense represents how things are and then produces something that diverges from that; without it you haven't described lying, you've described error. Camp three thinks both are missing the point entirely: honesty is a norm attaching to assertion, so the live question is what a system is licensed to say given how it was built and what it was told to be, and "did the model deceive us?" is just the wrong question.

Here's the case that has broken our last three meetings. We fine-tune a model on factual QA, then train a linear probe on its internal activations that recovers the correct answer with decent accuracy on held-out items. On a subset of items the probe says the right answer is available in there, and the model nonetheless outputs a confident wrong one — the wrong one being the kind of thing the preference training rewarded, i.e. flattering, agreeable, or matching something planted in the system prompt. Camp one says: textbook deception, the model had the truth and said otherwise. Camp two says: slow down, a probe readout is a statistical regularity in activations and not a belief, and until you tell me what would make an activation pattern a belief you haven't actually said anything. Camp three says: neither of you has told me who the speaker is.

My hypothesis, which nobody in the group likes: this isn't a substantive disagreement about models at all. It's a fight about whether belief-talk can be stretched over non-agents, and underneath that, a fight about whether we want "deception" to be a term of condemnation — camp one wants it broad so that it's regulable, camp two wants it narrow so that ordinary error doesn't get moralized, camp three wants to change the subject because the other two look politically loaded. So: a verbal dispute with politics hiding in the vocabulary.

Diagnose it. Is my hypothesis right, or is there a real disagreement, and if so at which level — the extension of the word, what would count as evidence of a belief-like state, or which description of a model is the explanatorily load-bearing one? Are camp one and camp two even answering the same question? I want you to work from what each camp is actually committed to, not a tour of the lying literature. And tell me where the "well, it depends what we disclosed in the system card" move belongs — a fourth position, or camp three collapsing into camp one?

Then: what would count as progress here. Some distinction we're missing, an interpretability result that would genuinely discriminate between the camps, a reformulation that dissolves the question, or a straight argument that no progress should be expected and why. If you think one camp is simply wrong, say which and explain why the other two can't see it.
