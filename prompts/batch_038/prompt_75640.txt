I chair a standing names-and-commemorations panel at a mid-sized public university: six volunteers (two faculty, two students, an alumnus, a retired secondary-school history teacher, and me, an administrator in the provost's office — no philosophers), eight to ten cases a year, one four-hour meeting and a two-page published statement of reasons per case, and a recommendation that goes to the president, who signs it and almost always follows it. Roughly half the cases have the same shape: someone proposes that a building, a professorship, or a lecture series be stripped of the name of a long-dead benefactor because of what he did, and a defender answers that we may not judge him by our standards — he was a man of his time. We have no way to assess that answer. Last year we accepted it wholesale in one case and dismissed it out of hand in another, and two members resigned in protest. What I want is a decision procedure my panel could actually run from the page: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the standards-of-his-time reply may not even be raised, the points where the question is factual and must be referred to our consulting historian (with a specification of which facts to demand from him), and the points where the question is one of values and must be handed back to the panel to vote. It has to be written for a lay reader with no philosophical vocabulary, and it has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure.

A philosopher friend sketched some distinctions she thinks the procedure must preserve, and I would like you to tell me which of them survive that kind of simplification and which should be dropped: that whether an act was wrong and whether its agent was blameworthy come apart; that "everyone believed it" and "everyone did it" are different claims and that only one of them can possibly exculpate; that the standards-of-his-time reply may concern what the person could have known or what he could have been expected to do given what he did know, and that these two fail in different cases; that ignorance a person cultivated, or had a material interest in preserving, is not a defence but an aggravation; that explaining conduct is not excusing it; and — the one she says we have been getting wrong for years — that our question is not whether the man deserves blame but whether we should go on honouring him, honour being something the living confer for reasons of their own. Tell me which of these does real work at a table of six non-specialists and which collapses into another once you have to write two pages of reasons. I also want the procedure to force us to name a comparison class before it asks whether anyone met the standards of it, since one and the same life looks different measured against his own profession, against the dissenters in his own city, or against the authors he quoted approvingly; tell us how to choose a class without simply picking the one that yields the answer we already want.

Then run it on three cases. The first is ours, and the file is full: a teaching-hospital wing named for Amos Hollis (1831–1904), a merchant who, in a country that had abolished slavery decades earlier, built a fortune brokering cotton and sugar produced by enslaved and coerced labour abroad; the ledgers and correspondence we hold show him negotiating directly with suppliers who described their labour force in plain terms, and one letter of his calls a supplier's practice a traffic in human flesh that he will not defend, only price; in 1874 a boycott campaign run by Quakers in his own city named his firm publicly and cost him at least one contract, and a competitor switched suppliers and said so in the papers; he also endowed the wing in question, paid above-market wages at home, and built a school for his workers' children, and his descendants have offered a further gift conditioned on the name's retention. I expect this result to be one I will not like — Hollis endowed the department I work in — so give it to me straight. The second is a residence hall named for Jefferson, where our file assumes what his published and private writings make plain, that he described slavery as an evil and predicted retribution for it, that he freed very few of the people he enslaved, and that manumission in his jurisdiction carried legal conditions and financial costs; treat the Hemings finding as available to us but contested. The third is a lecture series named for Churchill, where the relevant material is the Bengal famine of 1943 and his long-standing views on India and empire, which were contested in his own time in Parliament, in the press, and inside his own party. For each, give the verdict the procedure actually produces, the questions our file cannot answer, and the step at which the reasoning passes from facts to values. Two last things. Tell us what the procedure should do when it cannot be run at all: our Columbus case (a plaza and a mural) turns on contemporaneous protest that our historian says the record genuinely disputes, and I need a stated default rather than a shrug. And decide whether repentance is a step in the procedure, a consideration bearing on honour rather than blame, or a confusion — one member argues that a man who later repudiated his own conduct, and he has in mind the Dominican friar who spent decades denouncing the treatment of the indigenous peoples of the Spanish Caribbean and who retracted his earlier acceptance of African slavery, is a different case from a man who never flinched. Close with the limitations: I suspect the procedure can tell us whether the standards-of-his-time reply has been earned, but not whether to keep a name, and I would rather state that to the panel now than have them discover it in year three.
