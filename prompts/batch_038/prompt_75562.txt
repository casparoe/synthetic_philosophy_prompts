I have a probability PhD and spent twelve years as a quant — filtrations, martingales, changes of measure are my native language — and I have never read a page of epistemology. Someone has just explained to me a principle attributed to van Fraassen, apparently called Reflection: if you are now certain that at some later time you will assign credence p to a hypothesis H, then your present credence in H, conditional on your later assigning it p, ought to be p itself. Before I read further, I want it rendered in my own terms as literally as you can manage. Is the credence process of an agent who updates by conditionalization simply a Doob martingale with respect to the filtration generated by her own beliefs, and is Reflection then nothing over and above the tower property written in prose? Restate the principle and its standard justification that way, and then give me the residue report: which parts carry over exactly, which carry over only after you have quietly bent a definition, and which have no expression at all in stochastic-process language.

Please engage with three specific suspicions. First, that the counterexamples philosophers raise — an agent who expects to forget, to be drugged, to be talked into something — are just information loss, i.e. the filtration fails to be monotone, and are therefore uninteresting. Second, that a real agent chooses what to investigate and when to stop updating, so her filtration is endogenous to her actions and partly self-fulfilling, and I cannot see how the framework represents that. Third, that revising the measure itself rather than conditioning on new information — a drift change of the Girsanov kind — is a perfectly coherent operation in my vocabulary, whereas Reflection appears to forbid it outright. For each, tell me whether I have located a genuine limit of the probabilistic gloss, or whether the philosophers are smuggling in a normative claim about self-trust and epistemic authority that conditional expectation simply cannot encode. Do not flatter the analogy: if Reflection turns out to be trivial once translated, say so plainly, and tell me what work, if any, it is still doing.
