I am in my final year of high school and, for an independent research project that I will present to my school's philosophy society in the spring, I have taken up a position about the aggregation theorem usually credited to Harsanyi. I would like it stress-tested before I put it in writing. I can follow the mathematics of the proof, but I have not been trained to notice where representation theorems are commonly over-read, which I suspect is where my trouble lies.

My position has three parts.

First, the theorem itself: if each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the expected-utility axioms, if social preference over the same lotteries satisfies them as well, and if society is indifferent between any two lotteries to which every individual is indifferent, then social preference must admit a representation that is a weighted sum of individual expected-utility representations, with positive weights.

Second, and this is the part I actually care about, the philosophical upshot is negative rather than positive. The theorem does not so much establish utilitarianism as disqualify its main distributive rivals. A view on which society ought to be averse to risk in the distribution of welfare, as distinct from risk in its level, is not merely a competing set of values; it fails a constraint on rational social choice under risk. Prioritarianism and egalitarianism, on my reading, are in that position.

Third, the standard deflection does not work. Defenders of those views reply that the theorem concerns only representations of preference over lotteries and says nothing about welfare. But distributive questions just are questions about how to choose under risk when people's prospects differ, which is precisely the theorem's domain; so retreating behind the distinction between preference and welfare looks like changing the subject rather than answering the argument.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one I want to know what would have to be true for it to bite. In particular:

- The individual utility functions are each unique only up to positive affine transformation, so the weights are not invariant until a common zero and unit have been fixed. Does the theorem therefore deliver interpersonal comparability, or does it presuppose it? If it presupposes it, how much of my second claim survives?
- Suppose individuals' preferences over risk systematically violate independence, through ambiguity aversion for instance. Does that make my first claim false, or merely inapplicable, and is there a difference that matters here?
- One dose of a medicine, two patients who need it equally. The theorem appears to leave society indifferent between a fair coin toss and simply favoring the first patient. Can a defender absorb this, and what would have to be true for the absorption to succeed rather than to look like a bullet bitten?
- Does my third claim actually defeat the preference-versus-welfare reply, or does it smuggle in a premise about the relation between the two?
- What does my position commit me to that its rivals do not, including any separate argument I need for equal weights, and anything about populations large enough that the weights stop being well behaved?

Finally, I want an honest verdict on two things. Has the position been insulated by qualification to the point where every objection turns out to be a rejection of one of the axioms — and if so, is that a defect in a result of this kind, or simply what a representation theorem is supposed to look like? And which of the items above would a committed defender of my view concede as genuinely counting against them, as distinct from what critics merely allege? For what it is worth, I would find a demonstration that the conclusion can fail with all my premises intact far more persuasive than any intuition about coin tosses, which I suspect can always be re-described.
