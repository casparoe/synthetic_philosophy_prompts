I've been having a bet on football and sitting in card games for going on fifteen years now, and somewhere along the line I lost the ability to think about probability except as a price you'd pay. I never studied any of this, I've just read bits and pieces and thought about it a lot on long coach journeys, so what I've ended up with is a list of things I believe rather than anything you could call a system. I want to know whether the list hangs together, because I've got a feeling it doesn't but I can't see where the crack is, and I definitely can't tell you which bit I'd be willing to lose.

Here's what I hold. (1) Having a degree of belief in something just is having prices you'd bet at — I don't think there's anything else in there, no private feeling of confidence doing the work. In practice I quote two prices, one I'd buy at and one I'd sell at, and a mate at the table keeps telling me that means I don't really have degrees of belief at all, which I don't accept but can't argue against properly. (2) If your prices break the usual rules — don't add up across the possibilities that cover everything, your price for "this or that" out of line with the two separately, that kind of thing — then somebody can build you a bundle of bets you'd each happily take which leaves you poorer whichever way it lands. I call that getting bled. (3) Getting bled isn't evidence of irrational belief and it isn't a symptom of it either, it's the very same thing described from the outside. (4) I should take any bet that's worth taking at my own prices and turn down any that isn't, and what's at stake or how big my bankroll is doesn't come into it — only the odds and my prices. (5) That said, there are bets I'd turn down whatever the odds, namely any bet that could wipe out my whole bankroll, because I only get the one and I want to still be playing next week. (6) If I'm certain of something I should be certain of everything that follows from it — not being certain of the consequences is just a polite way of admitting I wasn't certain of the premise. (7) But obviously I can't actually work out all the consequences of what I believe, and I don't think that makes me irrational; if you offered me a bet on some gnarly claim I hadn't noticed was equivalent to something I'm sure of, I'd price it wrong and I wouldn't lose sleep. (8) A bookie who knows the result and I don't can take my money all day and that shows nothing wrong with my beliefs, it just shows he knows something I don't. (9) And yet I do think a group can go wrong in the way I've been describing: if the five of us each price things perfectly sensibly on our own, but the club's combined book bleeds us, then somebody's got it wrong. (10) I change my prices only by taking in something new and re-pricing everything according to how well each possibility would have predicted that new thing — I don't think sitting and thinking harder about what you already knew is allowed to move you.

So go through it as an audit, not an explanation of the general terrain. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do — I'd bet at least one of these is fine and I'm being paranoid about it. Rank the real problems by how bad they are rather than giving me a flat list where everything sounds equally urgent, and for each one say what the cheapest thing to give up would be and what I'd be losing by giving it up. If I've worded something badly, tell me what you think I actually mean before you judge it. Two last things: I'd like you to say how confident you are in the main claims you're making and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure about and why, and please write the whole thing in British English.
