A neuroscientist colleague of mine has been defending a deflationary position in a reading group, and he leans heavily on the theatre metaphor with which global workspace theory is standardly introduced. His argument runs roughly as follows. The metaphor says that consciousness is a theatre: unconscious specialist processors work backstage, a spotlight of attention picks out one item at a time, and whatever is lit is broadcast to the whole audience — the rest of the brain — which is what makes it available for report, reasoning, and voluntary control. My colleague says that once one notices that the spotlight and the broadcast are not decorative imagery but descriptions of an actual neural mechanism, the metaphor has been discharged: the only thing left to name is availability, and the further posit of phenomenal consciousness — a "what it is like" over and above access — is simply the residue of an image that has not yet been cashed out. He reinforces the point with material from his own clinical work in anaesthesia. There, he notes, patients are sometimes responsive without being recollective: in the isolated forearm procedure a tourniquet keeps one arm perfused so that a person who is otherwise paralysed can squeeze on command, yet a fair number of such responders afterwards report nothing at all about the intraoperative period. For him this is the theatre with the house lights down but the machinery idling — no broadcast, hence no report, hence, on his view, nothing it is like either. He concludes that anaesthetists should not trouble themselves over undetected intraoperative experience, since a state that cannot be globally broadcast cannot be an experience; and that Block's distinction between access-consciousness and phenomenal consciousness is a philosopher's artefact produced by taking a metaphor literally for too long.

I find this convincing while he is saying it and hollow afterwards, and I cannot tell which of my two reactions to trust. Could you audit the analogy for me?

Specifically, I would like: an explicit correspondence table stating what plays the theatre, the stage, the spotlight, the backstage processors, the audience, and the lights-down condition — and, above all, what plays the role of the spectator or director who watches the show, together with a judgement on whether that slot has a legitimate occupant once the metaphor is mechanised or whether it smuggles in a homunculus; the disanalogies, separated into those that actually bear on the conclusion he wants from those that are merely picturesque; an account of what the theatre image quietly imports that the target lacks — for example, that a performance is a performance for someone, that the spotlight falls on items already determinate before they are lit, and that the theatre persists whether or not anything is on the stage — plus any further imports you think matter; a diagnosis of whether "the metaphor has been discharged by the mechanism" is a legitimate move, or whether it slides from the claim that broadcasting explains availability to the claim that broadcasting is all there is, and if the slide is real, exactly where it happens; and an assessment of the anaesthesia case, asking whether responsiveness without later recall licenses his inference, what evidence would license it, and how the no-report paradigm literature and the overflow dispute bear on the question. If the theatre cannot carry this weight, say what analogy — if any — would be a better instrument for the view he is defending, and whether that better analogy would leave his clinical conclusion standing or pull the rug out from under it.

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the main claims of your audit, and single out the point at which you are least sure of yourself — in particular, if you think the spectator question is what decides the matter, say how much of your verdict rests on it.
