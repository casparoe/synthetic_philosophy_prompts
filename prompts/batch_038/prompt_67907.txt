I sit on the science advisory board of the consortium that operates the Kestrel Array, and in eleven days the board votes on the most consequential proposal it has ever received. Four months ago the Array recorded a narrowband signal at 8.4 GHz from the direction of a system roughly sixty light-years away: eleven-second bursts carrying a drift rate consistent with a rotating emitter, detected on nine passes, matching nothing in our own interference catalogue. The proposal asks the board to authorize a single deliberate reply — a structured, high-power transmission aimed at that system during a fourteen-month window opening next March, after which, on the group's modelling, the geometry will not support a transmission of comparable strength for about thirty years. Our spectrum regulator has already cleared the frequency, so the only question left is ours. The consortium's charter, however, was written for a world in which we merely listened. Article 4(2) permits an intentional transmission where the expected benefit to humanity is positive and the probability of grave harm is negligible; Article 4(3) forbids the board to rest a determination on scenarios that are in principle incapable of confirmation. Two members have told me privately that their institutions will withdraw if a reply is authorized, which would end the Array's funding.

The board is split three to two, and the arguments have hardened into positions neither side now examines. Those who would authorize point out that a century of our own broadcast and radar leakage has already made this planet detectable across a wide volume, so a reply changes degree rather than kind; that a civilization sixty light-years away capable of producing the signal we received is itself some evidence that technological societies can persist rather than destroy themselves; and that declining is not a neutral act, since it forfeits irreversibly, and on behalf of everyone, the one thing the Array was built to look for. Those who would decline answer that leakage and a targeted, structured, high-power beacon are not the same object at interstellar range, that the harm in question is unrecoverable and would be imposed without the consent of anyone living or unborn, and that a nine-pass, single-instrument detection is nowhere near confirmed. Last night I argued about this until two in the morning with a friend who licenses reactors for a living, and he pressed a point I have not been able to answer: if the board applies Article 4(3) honestly, the scenario its cautious members invoke is inadmissible, since it cannot be confirmed in principle, and once that evidence is excluded the decision collapses into a comparison of epistemic benefits against institutional costs — which would make our charter not a safeguard but a loaded die in favour of transmitting. He also thinks both camps are dressing an unquantifiable judgement in the vocabulary of probability, and that what the board actually owes everyone is an explicit default rule stating which kind of mistake it prefers to make.

Could you draft the memorandum I will circulate before the vote — about a thousand words, addressed to the board as a body? I need it to take a position rather than survey one: authorize, decline, or authorize subject to conditions, with a timetable the transmitting group could actually work to. It should say how the board ought to read the word negligible in Article 4(2), and how to reconcile that clause with Article 4(3), since whichever way we resolve that tension will govern every future proposal. It should state what would count as sufficient independent confirmation of the signal before anything leaves the dish, and what evidence arriving between now and the vote would change the recommendation. And it should give the losing side's strongest consideration a fair statement — one the members who hold it would recognize as their own rather than a caricature — including an answer to my friend's objection that our own charter has already decided this question against us. Please write for members who have read nothing on the subject and who share no technical background; I want the reasoning itself, not references to other people's work.
