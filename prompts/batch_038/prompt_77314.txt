I chair the panel of a small foundation that funds theoretical work on mind, language, and culture. Each cycle we receive roughly forty proposals from people trained in quite different fields, and our nine panelists split predictably: half accuse the rest of insisting that everything be explained by evolution or physics, half accuse the first of proposing to fund explanations whose central mechanism nobody has ever located. I want a screening procedure, grounded in Dennett's crane/skyhook distinction and its mirror image, greedy reductionism, that a non-specialist panelist can run on an unfamiliar proposal in twenty-five minutes and summarize in one paragraph for the file.

Please give the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, and how to distinguish a proposal that genuinely posits a skyhook from one that merely posits a mechanism this panelist has not encountered. Include how to catch the opposite failure — a proposal that dissolves the explanandum rather than explaining it — and where the procedure must stop and either hand the call to a specialist or record the panelists' disagreement as a clash of background commitments rather than a defect in the work. Flag explicitly which steps are judgment wearing a procedural costume, and say what the procedure cannot detect.

Then run it on three case summaries you compose: a proposal to explain the origin of human moral norms by appeal to a dedicated faculty for detecting objective normative facts; a proposal arguing that pain's felt character is a post-hoc confabulation with no causal role; and a proposal explaining the emergence of writing as an exaptation of an existing object-recognition system, with specific testable predictions. At least one of the three should come out in a way I would rather not publish.
