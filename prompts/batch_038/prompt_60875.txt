I'm in the second year of a type design MA and I've been reading around Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project, the one where the goal was a program that, given a few letters drawn by some designer, could invent the rest of the alphabet in that designer's style, with everything living on a small fixed grid. The claim that stuck with me is the negative one: there is supposedly no single correct 'a' sitting there to be found, and what a letter is turns out to be something more like a weighted cloud of acceptable ways to make it, plus a style that determines which of those ways belong together. I keep going back and forth on whether that's a real insight or just a fancy way of saying you'll know it when you see it, and I've decided I'd rather think about it through a story than through another paper, so I'm outsourcing.

Write me a short story, somewhere around 1,800 to 2,500 words, that treats "what makes every 'a' an 'a'" as a plot problem instead of a topic of conversation. Here's a setting seed you can use, bend, or toss: a state bureau that certifies the official letterforms used on road signs and in school primers, working after whatever-it-is that made the old punches unrecoverable. A designer submits a complete alphabet. Twenty-five of the letters pass with no argument at all. The 'a' comes back to the committee six times, and each time it's a slightly different object — first a two-story 'a' with the bowl broken open at the join, then the same idea with the stem leaning, then something that is arguably an 'o' with a leg, then a form the designer insists is 'a' and that nobody on the committee can say why it isn't. The sixth form is the sixth form because it passed through hands and machines and fax drums on the way, not because six people held six opinions; copying being lossy should be load-bearing in the story, so if the forms get redrawn by a different clerk each round, or traced, or sent as a degraded scan, do that. Close third person on the person whose actual job is to say yes or no. Dry, procedural, funny in the way bureaucratic stories are funny.

Two hard constraints. First, nobody lectures: no character utters the word essence, nobody name-checks Plato or family resemblance or a philosopher of any stripe, and no dialogue that could be lifted into a seminar handout — the thinking has to be carried by objects, forms, procedures, and what people do with them. Second, give me at least three concrete visual descriptions of glyph forms, specific enough that a reader could actually draw the thing from your words alone; hand-waving about "shapes" and "curves" is exactly what I'm trying to get away from, and this is where I think most attempts at this story will fail.

On the ideas: let at least two incompatible positions be genuinely present and genuinely attractive — that 'a' is just a slot in a system which any shape whatever could fill by decree, and that there's a real structural core (a closed counter, a stroke meeting the baseline, whatever you pick) without which the thing stops being an 'a' no matter what a committee votes, and, if you can work it in, that the right answer is a spread of forms rather than a form. I don't want the story to adjudicate between them in an essay voice or resolve them with a sudden understanding in the last paragraph, but I do want it to cost something: by the end one of those positions should be noticeably more expensive for somebody than the others. Give me an ending that's a decision rather than a realization — someone signs something, or refuses to. And please no dream sequences, no machine that turns out to be conscious, no reveal that the letter was a person all along, and no twist where it was the designer who was the real 'a'. Small thing, but I'd also like at least one moment where a character who is not the protagonist turns out to have been right about the forms for reasons that have nothing to do with typography, since that seems true of how these things actually go.
