I teach a PhD seminar in formal epistemology whose second half is devoted to probabilistic updating beyond strict conditionalization. I am now setting the three-hour, closed-book final (50 marks in total) and I need one question worth 25 marks on the Judy Benjamin problem — the case Bas van Fraassen devised to show that coming to learn a conditional probability is not something Jeffrey conditionalization can handle unaided. Please write the question as it will appear on the paper, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The exam text must be fully self-contained: state the case and every numerical stipulation in the question itself, so that nothing depends on students recalling the literature. Build the question around the following material.

The case. Judy has been dropped at night into a territory divided into a northern half and a southern half, each subdivided into two sectors: NE, NW, SE, SW. Her prior is uniform over the four sectors, so P(N) = P(S) = 1/2. A headquarters radio operator whom she takes to be perfectly reliable transmits: "If you are in the north, then you are in the north-east sector, three chances in four." That is, Judy acquires the constraint P(NE | N) = 3/4 and nothing else.

Why this is a problem. Jeffrey conditionalization as standardly formulated takes as input new unconditional probabilities for the cells of a partition and propagates them via Q(A) = Σᵢ Q(A | Bᵢ) P(Bᵢ). A constraint on a conditional probability supplies no such input: every Q satisfying Q(NE | N) = 3/4 is compatible with the rule.

Four candidate resolutions, which I want students to discriminate between rather than merely list:

(A) Keep the north/south marginal at its prior: Q(N) = 1/2, hence Q(NE) = 3/8, Q(NW) = 1/8, Q(SE) = Q(SW) = 1/4. Rationale: the message bears only on the distribution within the north.
(B) Maximize Shannon entropy subject to the constraint: Q(NE) ≈ 0.3505, Q(NW) ≈ 0.1168, Q(SE) = Q(SW) ≈ 0.2663, so Q(N) ≈ 0.467. Because the prior is uniform, this coincides with minimizing reverse relative entropy Σ Q ln(Q/P) from the prior.
(C) Minimize forward relative entropy Σ P ln(P/Q), equivalently maximize Σ P ln Q, subject to the constraint: Q(NE) = 0.6, Q(NW) = 0.2, Q(SE) = Q(SW) = 0.1, so Q(N) = 0.8.
(D) Read the transmission as the material conditional N ⊃ NE and conditionalize on it strictly: Q(N) = 1/3.

The question should not be answerable by observing that Jeffrey's rule needs unconditional inputs. I want it to require, across clearly mark-weighted parts:

(i) a precise statement of which inputs are missing and why no choice of partition in the standard rule yields the constraint;
(ii) explicit verification by computation of at least two of the four figures above, together with an account of the principle each of (A)–(C) embodies and of why the constraint alone cannot arbitrate between them;
(iii) the crux: whether the case shows that conditionalization must be supplemented by a substantive normative principle, or that we have mis-described Judy's evidence. Here students must construct a generative model of the headquarters transmission — a prior over which message HQ sends given where Judy is, or over how HQ selects which sector to mention — and show whether strict conditionalization on the proposition "HQ sent this message" reproduces (A), (B), (D), or none of them;
(iv) a short-answer item pressing the point that "P(NE | N) = 3/4" is not obviously a proposition about the world at all, but a claim about how Judy's own credences are to stand, and asking what would have to be true for acquiring it to count as evidence in the ordinary sense.

Deliverables:

1. The exam question, with parts and mark allocations summing to 25, and an indication of the time each part should take (about 75 minutes overall).
2. A model answer of roughly 900 words that derives the figures rather than asserting them. If any figure given above is incorrect, do not silently substitute your own: flag the discrepancy, show the correct computation, and build the marking scheme around the corrected numbers.
3. A marking scheme listing the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the marks each carries; then, separately, five errors that should cost marks, each with the deduction and a one-line diagnosis of the confusion it reveals — for instance applying Jeffrey's rule with the partition {N, S}, treating the constraint as unconditional information about NE, or presenting maximum entropy as a neutral default rather than as one substantive choice among others.
4. Two sentences, suitable for the examiners' meeting, on what most sharply separates a first from a strong second-class answer.

Please include no reading list, bibliography, or citations: students have no sources in the exam room, and the question must be answerable entirely from what is printed on the paper.
