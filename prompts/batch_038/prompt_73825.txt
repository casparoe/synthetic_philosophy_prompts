I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, mostly philosophy-of-mind stuff dressed up so people read it on a train. I'm working on a piece about the extended mind thesis — the Clark/Chalmers move where the notebook on the table counts as part of the cognitive system — and about how the "cognitive bloat" critics answer it. My editor wants the "this debate is way older than 1998" angle, so I asked two academics I interview regularly what the real ur-text is, and both of them, independently, pointed me at Dewey's 1896 reflex arc paper in the Psychological Review. Fine, so I pulled up the actual thing instead of quoting the secondary summary like a coward, and its a slog. Dense, compressed, full of terms he never bothers to define. I dont want to run a nut graf built on this that some Dewey person then tears apart in a letter, so I need help reading one stretch of it properly.

Context: the running example is a child reaching for a candle flame (Dewey credits James for it). Here's the part I'm stuck on, roughly the middle of the paper:

"In other words, the real beginning is with the act of seeing; it is looking, and not a sensation of light. The sensory quale gives the value of the act, just as the movement furnishes its mechanism and control, but both sensation and movement lie inside, not outside the act.

Now if this act, the seeing stimulates another act, the reaching, it is because both of these acts fall within a larger coördination; because seeing and grasping have been so often bound together to reinforce each other, to help each other out, that each may be considered practically a subordinate member of a bigger coördination. More specifically, the ability of the hand to do its work will depend, either directly or indirectly, upon its control, as well as its stimulation, by the act of vision. If the sight did not inhibit as well as excite the reaching, the latter would be purely indeterminate, it would be for anything or nothing, not for the particular object seen. [...] The eye must be kept upon the candle if the arm is to do its work; let it wander and the arm takes up another task. In other words, we now have an enlarged and transformed coördination; the act is seeing no less than before, but it is now seeing-for-reaching purposes. There is still a sensori-motor circuit, one with more content or value, not a substitution of a motor response for a sensory stimulus."

A little later he adds: "More technically stated, the so-called response is not merely to the stimulus; it is into it."

So, several things I can't pin down. What is doing the work in "the sensory quale gives the value of the act" — is "value" here something like worth or normativity, or just "the specific character this act has," so the sentence is descriptive rather than evaluative? And when he says the circuit now has "more content or value," is he claiming the seeing has literally become a different process once the reaching is folded in, or just redescribing the same events at a coarser grain? Relatedly, what is his "single concrete whole" — a mental state, a functional relation, a stretch of behavior? When he calls it "the fundamental psychical unity" a couple paragraphs earlier, does "psychical" for Dewey mean anything like "phenomenally conscious," or is it just his word for "the unit psychology has to explain"? And the "into it" line: into it how? That phrase has been quoted at me three times and nobody has ever actually unpacked it.

Here's the part that matters most for my piece, and the part where I suspect I may be fooling myself. My working thesis is that Dewey already makes the parity move: for him the stimulus isn't a fixed environmental input that the organism then responds to — it gets constituted by what the organism is busy doing — so the boundary of the psychological unit is drawn around the coördination rather than around the skull, which is exactly the shape of the extended mind argument a hundred years early. A grad student I talked to says that's the classic equivocation: Dewey's coördinations are still sensori-motor circuits inside one body, so nothing he says licenses running the circuit out into the world, and citing him against the bloat objection buys you zero. Which of us has it right? If there's a third option — Dewey doing something neither of us has described — I'd rather hear that.
