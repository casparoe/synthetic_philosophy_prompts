I'm in a reading group that meets in someone's kitchen every other Thursday, and last week two of the regulars had a fight that I genuinely couldnt adjudicate, so I'm handing it to you. Quick context so the moves make sense: over a couple of months we'd all talked ourselves into pooling money for a projector to watch films after the discussion portion. Everyone chipped in except Sam, who was traveling for work and just sort of never got asked directly. When it arrived and someone texted him for his share, he said no — not because he was being cheap about the money, but because he claimed there was never any group decision for him to be bound by. That set off Maya and Dev, who argued about it for an hour while the rest of us ate chips.

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it, roughly in order: (1) Dev backed Sam on the philosophy: nobody ever formed an intention with the content "we buy a projector," because what each of us had was conditional and personal — I'll chip in if the others do — and a pile of matching conditionals is just an aggregate, not a joint commitment, so there was nothing for Sam to opt out of. (2) Maya said that misses what makes it ours: each person's intention is partly constituted by the others', I intended it *because* they were intending it and we all knew that, and on top of that the group plainly behaved like an agent — it deliberated, compared a projector against a bigger screen, weighed costs, and settled. Dev said settling isn't agency, a market settles prices. (3) Maya then offered her own criterion, which she said she'd made up on the spot and calls the objection test: a group has an intention only if, once some course is on the table as the group's, at least one member is disposed to object *in the group's name* if another member tries to drop or break it, and the rest treat that objection as a legitimate complaint rather than meddling. She said we pass — when Sam balked, three people objected on the group's behalf and even Sam conceded the complaint was fair in principle — and a crowd converging on an exit fails, because nobody ever objects in the crowd's name when someone peels off. (4) Dev had two replies: the test is question-begging, since "in the group's name" already assumes a group with standing to be spoken for, which is the whole thing in dispute; and it also gets the facts backwards, because Sam himself later objected in the group's name when someone floated selling the projector on eBay, which on Maya's test counts as evidence of a group intention Sam insists he never had. He added that conflict-avoidant groups would fail the test despite clearly acting together. (5) Maya said the first charge fails because the test is observational, not semantic — you don't need to know what a group is to notice who objects to what and how the objection lands, and "in the group's name" is just shorthand for the objector not citing a personal stake; and she conceded the conflict-aversion case but argued the test is a sufficient-condition detector, not a definition, so silence is weak evidence while objection is strong. Somewhere in the middle Theo interrupted to say the entire dispute was just the doctrinal paradox wearing a projector costume, and both of them told him to drop it.
