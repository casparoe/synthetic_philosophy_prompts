I have followed the argument about coherence arguments for the better part of two decades — sequence posts, paper drafts, forum threads, the usual cycle — and in all that time I do not believe either side has ever produced a text the other would sign. I would like you to draft that text, as the deliverable of a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

**Party A, the strong reading.** The family of results running from the Dutch book theorem and its converse, through Savage's derivation of subjective probability and utility from preferences over acts, through the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation theorem over lotteries, to the money-pump argument for transitivity, jointly establish something substantive about what agency is: a system whose behaviour is consistent enough to be worth predicting at all will be representable as maximizing expected utility relative to some probability function and some utility function over outcomes, and departures from that form are not aesthetic failings but dispositions to be relieved of resources by anyone positioned to offer the right trades. On this reading coherence is a real constraint on what a successful agent — natural or artificial — can look like, and the fact that laboratory humans display framing effects, preference reversals and intransitivity is a story about the costs and compensations of boundedness, not a counterexample to the theorems.

**Party B, the deflationary reading.** Every one of those results is conditional, and the conditions do the work: completeness and transitivity over a fixed space of outcomes, continuity, independence, a counterparty willing to accept or offer bets at the agent's own rates, costless and indefinitely divisible trading, and an environment in which the relevant sequence of choices is actually available. Strip any of these away and the conclusion loses its bite. Moreover, virtually any pattern of behaviour can be redescribed as the maximization of some utility function over some suitably gerrymandered description of the outcomes, which means the theorems cannot support the inference from coherent-looking behaviour to having goals; and whether incoherence costs anything at all is an empirical question about environments, to which the usual answer is that the pumps are not on offer.

Neither party is a strawman, both know the mathematics cold, and each has already conceded — publicly, at various points — things that a summary of the debate would leave out.

Before drafting, run the collaboration through four cases, and make sure the document handles each explicitly:

1. An agent with pairwise preferences that cycle, A over B over C over A, but which sees entire menus rather than single choices and will refuse any sequence of trades that returns it to its starting bundle with less money. Is it pumpable? Is it coherent? Does its sophistication restore coherence at the level of sequences while leaving intransitivity at the level of single choices?
2. An agent whose choices are fully determined by a rule that looks nothing like maximization — say, a fixed tie-break on a superficial property of how options are labelled. Party B wants to write down the utility function it maximizes and declare the representation empty; Party A owes a reply in terms of what the theorems actually fix in place.
3. An agent that violates continuity — no probability-weighted bundle compensates it for a particular loss, at any odds. Is this a refutation of the axioms as normative requirements, or a redescription of a bounded agent in unbounded vocabulary?
4. An agent that is thoroughly coherent and thoroughly risk averse. I expect both parties sign here, which is exactly why it belongs in the document rather than in the argument.

The deliverable should have four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign — at least eight, and none of them so weak that someone who had never read the literature would be inclined to deny it; I want the substantive common ground, including about what each theorem does and does not say and about the direction of each biconditional. Second, a disagreements table with four columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; the wording Party A would accept as a statement of its own position; the wording Party B would accept as a statement of its own position; and what would have to be true for each of those wordings to be correct. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. I suspect completeness and continuity are one crux and whether exploitability is the right cash-value of incoherence is another, but if you think a crux I have in mind is not a crux at all, say so and replace it with a better one. For each crux, state the case, argument or study both parties would agree bears on it, record each party's prediction in advance, and record what each party would count as its prediction having failed. Fourth, a short section identifying which stretches of the dispute are verbal: name the terms — I would guess coherence, exploitable, and agent are all candidates — and say what each party means by them and how the argument would look once the vocabulary is fixed.

Two constraints. No citations, no bibliography, no named papers or authors: I want the reasoning, not a reference list I would have to check. And no invented statistics — where a crux turns on a quantity, name the quantity and the direction in which it would have to fall to favour one party, and leave the number blank. Finally, the test of the draft is symmetry: if I handed it to Party A and Party B separately, each should find its own position stated at least as well as it usually states it, and neither should be able to point to a sentence that quietly assumes the other side is wrong.
