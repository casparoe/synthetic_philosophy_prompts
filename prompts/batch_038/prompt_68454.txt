Third year in political philosophy, working on the inefficacy objection — the argument that your individual contribution to a collective harm (a flight, a burger, a vote) makes no difference, so it isn't wrong, or at least you have no reason not to. I want to run a real case through it instead of another imaginary supermarket, and I've landed on the Montgomery bus boycott, 1955–56.

Facts I'll take as given so you don't have to hedge them: Parks arrested Dec 1, 1955; the Women's Political Council mimeographs flyers overnight calling a one-day boycott for Dec 5; it turns into a 381-day boycott run by the Montgomery Improvement Association under King, with a church-based carpool of volunteer drivers and dispatch stations; Black residents are around three quarters of the system's riders on the usual figures; the city indicts King and dozens of others under an old state anti-boycott statute and then goes after the carpool; it ends only when the Supreme Court affirms Browder v. Gayle and bus segregation is struck down. I know it's disputed how much the economic squeeze mattered relative to the courts — tell me how much of your analysis turns on that, because if the answer is "a lot" I need to know before I build on it.

Here's the apparatus, it's my own and im not attached to it, so push back on the joints if they're bad. Distinguish: (i) marginal nullity — the act makes literally no difference to the outcome; (ii) probabilistic dilution — the act shifts the probability of the outcome by something tiny but nonzero; (iii) attribution failure — the act contributed, but no counterfactual test isolates it, because the outcome is overdetermined by thousands of similar acts; (iv) uptake dependence — the act's causal value isn't a property of the act but of the act inside an equilibrium, so it swings with what others do and with what others believe others will do; (v) framing effects — the act changes what other agents see as available or normal, running through belief rather than through the outcome itself.

Three things I want. First, map the boycott onto those five, twice: for the person who walks to work instead of riding, and separately for the person who defects and rides anyway, which is the direction the objection almost never gets run in. Second, say what the objection predicts about this boycott and whether the prediction holds — if individual participation really was causally null, the thing should have dissolved in a week, so either the objection is wrong here or it owes a story about why the collapse didn't happen, and "people are irrational" isn't yet a story. Third, turn it around and say what the case does to the theory. My worry, which I'd rather you attack than flatter, is that the objection only ever gets deployed on consumption cases with smooth causal structure, and that it borrows its plausibility from a class of cases that excludes precisely the threshold-y, uptake-dependent ones — in which case it isn't a general argument about individual agency at all. And if the objection is sound, say what it implies about the beliefs the boycotters had to hold for it to work: does it license something like a noble fiction, and is that a cost to the view or a resource for it.

Bottom line up front, three sentences, then the analysis. Hard cap 900 words, I'll cut anything over.
