I teach a first-year proofs course — 24 students, mostly bound for computer science and engineering — and next week we will have just finished equivalence relations and quotient sets. I want to spend one full 75-minute session on the axiom of choice, but not by lecturing about it: I want the class to run into the thing the axiom is actually about before anyone says its name. My sketch is this. Six teams of four receive, round by round, an envelope containing a family of nonempty sets, with the single instruction: write down a rule that picks exactly one element from each member of the family, where a rule counts only if a rival team, handed your rule and one member of the family they have not seen, can execute it and land on a unique answer with no further judgment calls of their own. Round one should be families where the rule is trivial and every team succeeds; round two, families where a rule exists but has to be hunted for and at least one team's candidate turns out to smuggle in a judgment call; round three, a family of the Vitali type — one representative from each equivalence class of reals under "differs by a rational" — where I expect nobody to produce anything that survives challenge. Then the twist I actually care about: once they have failed, teams stake points (no money) on whether such a rule exists at all, and I want to show them that their failure is not evidence either way, that the question cannot be settled from what they already accept, and that it gets settled only by adopting an assumption.

Please turn this into something I can actually run: the precise contents of the cards or envelopes for each round, written in notation first-year students can parse, with three or four families per round rather than one; the adversarial challenge protocol, including the legitimate ways a team can defeat a rival's rule and the illegitimate ones I should overrule from the chair; a scoring scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline; and the debrief questions in the order I should ask them. The debrief has to do two things in sequence. First, connect what they just felt to the familiar illustration usually credited to Russell about infinitely many pairs of shoes versus infinitely many pairs of socks, and then complicate it: say what that story gets right, and in what sense the operative distinction is not that shoes come in distinguishable left-and-right varieties while socks do not, but something about whether a uniform selection rule is available at all — I want the students to see why a family of left-and-right shoes and a family of socks might both be perfectly manageable, or both hopeless, depending on how the family is presented. Second, sort out for me which of the standard pieces of the topic genuinely survive the translation into a card game — countable choice versus full choice, the equivalence with the well-ordering principle, independence from the rest of the usual axioms, non-measurable sets — and which of them I will be faking, with the honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by exposition. I suspect our collective inability to write a rule in seventy-five minutes is an epistemic fact about twenty-four undergraduates, not a theorem about definability, and I want to know how much weight that gap can bear before the whole exercise teaches the wrong lesson.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle it live. I can already foresee three moves and want each one addressed explicitly rather than left to my improvisation. Some team will propose taking the least element of each class, which fails because those classes have no least element in the usual ordering of the reals, and then, when pressed, will retreat to well-ordering the reals and taking the least element in that ordering — which imports precisely what is at issue, and I need a clean way to say so without seeming to move the goalposts mid-game. Another team will propose restricting attention to the representatives lying between zero and one, which narrows each class to a countable infinity of candidates and still selects nothing; I want to use that failure productively rather than dismiss it. And after the whole session, a vocal minority will conclude that because nobody could write the rule down, no such function exists and the axiom of choice is therefore false — or worse, meaningless — and I do not want them walking out with that, since it inverts the point. If you think the round-three family is too hard to make the intended point land and that some other family would induce the same failure more reliably, say so and give me the substitute.
