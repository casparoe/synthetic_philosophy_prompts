ok, so at the end of next month I'm helping run a two-day workshop that's half philosophy, half AI-governance people, and the last session is on compromise between value systems — the whole gains-from-trade line of thought, where two agents who disagree about the good can each do better by trading bits of what they want than by insisting on their own view all the way down, plus the worry that a person who'll trade on their values maybe never had values, just preferences. My co-organizer wants to close the session with something read aloud and after three failed attempts (mine went to a jingle by stanza two) I've decided the form should carry the argument rather than the imagery. So: a pantoum, the Malay form Baudelaire borrowed, where lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the final stanza folds in the first stanza's lines 1 and 3 to shut the circle. Five quatrains, abab if you can get the rhymes without sounding like a greeting card, loose-ish beat, short enough lines to survive a room with terrible acoustics.

The reason I want that form specifically: two unlabelled voices alternating, odd lines one speaker, even lines the other. Because of how the repetition works, from stanza two onward each of them is saying the other's line back, and by the end not one line belongs to whoever started it — I want that to be the content and not a party trick. Voice A only ever talks in totals, per-dollar numbers, expected value, the aggregate; voice B only ever talks in names, promises, and the one thing that isn't up for sale. Neither gets to be a strawman: give A a stretch where refusing to trade is plainly the worse outcome, give B a stretch where the trade is plainly repugnant. Keep it anchored in a single concrete negotiation so it isn't abstract — say a fixed pot of money with two claims on it, one program that buys the most healthy life-years overall and one that keeps a twenty-year-old promise to a particular neighbourhood, plus the shared knowledge that if they cant agree the pot goes somewhere neither of them wants. That last part matters most, because its where the interesting bite is: the split of the gains depends on the deadlock, so whoever can credibly threaten the uglier walkaway takes the bigger share of the middle, and intransigence gets paid. Do that with one recurring line, the sort of phrase people actually say at these tables, that starts out as reassurance and curdles into a threat purely by coming back — don't explain it, let the recurrence do the work.

For the ending, the last stanza should leave the reader genuinely unable to tell whether the two voices have converted each other or corrupted each other: the words have completely changed hands, each is now holding the other's sentence, and the poem refuses to say whether that's a deal worth making. No both-sides-were-right shrug, no closing moral, no image of a handshake or a bridge, and please don't use the words synergy, common ground, or tapestry. If you can also make the two voices grammatically identical — same sentence shapes, same tenses, so you can only tell them apart by what they're willing to count — that'd be a bonus, since the whole session is about how much of the disagreement survives once you strip the rhetoric away.
