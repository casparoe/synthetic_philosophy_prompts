You are to act as a professional philosopher of science who has also sat on engineering failure-inquiry panels. Think carefully and plan before writing; I am not asking for an exposition of the literature, and I do not want references, citations, footnotes, or a reading list of any kind. Work entirely from what you already know. The deliverable is a working tool.

**The material to be operationalized.** Daniel Dennett's account of explanation distinguishes three strategies for predicting and explaining a system's behaviour: the physical stance (reasoning from constitution and law), the design stance (reasoning from what the thing is for and how it is supposed to work, assuming no malfunction), and the intentional stance (treating the thing as a rational agent with beliefs and goals). Alongside this sits his criterion of real patterns, on which the rough test for whether something exists is whether positing it buys a genuine compression of the data — a way of predicting and explaining that no feasible alternative account delivers as well. If no stance gets you traction on it, and its positing compresses nothing, it is a manner of speaking rather than a thing.

**My situation.** I am the methodology lead at a national accident investigation board covering energy and heavy industry. We open roughly twenty-five serious investigations a year. Each produces a public report whose Findings section names causal and contributory factors and whose Recommendations section must attach to those findings — a recommendation with no named finding behind it gets struck out at review. Reports are used in civil litigation and occasionally in criminal proceedings, so every named entity becomes a target for discovery and for blame. Our analysts are engineers and human-factors specialists with no philosophical training; they have six to eight weeks per case; the final report is signed off by a five-member board, two of them political appointees.

**The dispute I need settled by procedure rather than by seniority.** Our findings range across all three stances. At the physical level: fatigue cracking in a weld, a corrosion rate, a relief valve's set pressure. At the design level: a maintenance interval, a redundancy architecture, a procedure that requires only one signature. At the intentional level: what the night shift believed about a recurring alarm; what site management wanted; and group-level posits about organizations — the site's "safety culture," "production pressure from the parent company's quarterly targets," "the contractor's incentives." Senior investigators insist the last group is journalism masquerading as causation and can never yield an actionable recommendation. Others insist that reports stopping at the physical level systematically exonerate the organization that built the system. Both camps appeal to what is "really there." I need a procedure that replaces that argument with checks.

**One complication you must confront rather than paper over.** The real-patterns criterion is framed for compression across many cases; an investigation explains a single event, retrospectively, and much of the physical evidence is often destroyed or unrecoverable. So the procedure cannot simply ask whether a posit predicts well. Tell me what you substitute — counterfactual dependence, transportability to other sites in the industry, something else — and defend the substitution as the best available reading of the criterion rather than as a replacement of it.

**What I want, in this order:**

1. A short preliminary analysis, before any procedure. Identify which features of the three stances and of the real-patterns criterion do genuine work in this setting, which have to be dropped or bent out of shape, and what "existence" can amount to for a report — a finding that licenses a recommendation, not a metaphysical verdict. Flag every point where the theory underdetermines the tool and you had to choose.

2. The procedure itself: an ordered sequence of tests an analyst can run on a candidate finding. For each step, state the question, what counts as a sufficient answer, the permitted outputs, and the conditions under which the procedure terminates with "do not name this as a finding; it is shorthand." Include explicitly: a rule for determining which stance a candidate finding is being offered in, and why that is a property of the claim rather than of the analyst's preference; a rule for the case in which two stances both license the same entity but imply incompatible recommendations; a rule for cases where the physical evidence is gone and only testimony and documents remain; and a rule for an entity that passes the reality test but whose naming will predictably be read by the public and by litigants as an assignment of blame to a person or firm.

3. Say whether the procedure can yield different answers for the same entity depending on the downstream use — technical remediation versus allocation of responsibility — and whether that divergence is a defect to be engineered out or the correct result.

4. Mark, at each step, where the procedure hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values or responsibility rather than fact, and which steps are judgment calls dressed in procedural language. Do not hide these; the board needs to know where the tool runs out.

5. Worked applications to three candidate findings from one investigation of a pipeline rupture: (a) hydrogen-assisted cracking in a girth weld, with the failed section partially recovered; (b) the operating site's "safety culture," which our board chair uses in almost every report; (c) "production pressure from the parent company's quarterly throughput targets," a group-level intentional posit about an entity that is not itself a party to the investigation. Run each through the steps and show the outputs. At least one of the three must come out in a way I will not like; say plainly which one and why.

6. Close with failure modes: two or three ways this procedure will misfire in real investigations, one class of case it cannot handle at all, and what a critic who thinks the whole exercise rests on a category mistake — that existence has nothing to do with predictive or explanatory payoff — would say about the tool, together with your assessment of how much that objection should worry me.

Write in prose with numbered steps and clear headings, not as an undifferentiated bullet list. Keep individual steps short enough to be used at a desk under time pressure, and finish with a one-page summary card that an analyst could keep beside them during a review meeting.
