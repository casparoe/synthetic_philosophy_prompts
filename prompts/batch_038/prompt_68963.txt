I do reliability engineering — I write the postmortems when something breaks in production — and this term I'm auditing a metaphysics seminar on causation, and the two have started colliding in a way I can't stop thinking about. The seminar covers the counterfactual-dependence line from Lewis, Woodward's interventionism, the process/conserved-quantity stuff from Salmon and Dowe, and the formal "actual causation" models that grew out of Halpern and Pearl. I know these cases have names (preemption, trumping, double prevention, whatever) but I never remember which is which, so I'm just going to describe them the way they actually show up at work. A friend who's a PhD student says my intuitions are a mess and that I can't have it both ways on a couple of these. I'd like to know if he's right. Here's what I actually think, case by case:

1. Renata hits the manual kill switch at 3:02 and the grinder stops. A fault in the control board was already propagating and would have stopped it at 3:03 anyway — nothing anyone could have done at 3:02 would have called the board fault off. Verdict: Renata caused the stop. The board fault didn't, even though the stop would have happened without her.
2. Two interns, working seperate shifts and unaware of each other, each dump a full dose of neutralizer into the same vat at the same moment; either dose alone would have ruined the batch. Verdict: both caused the ruined batch. Not neither, and not exactly one of them.
3. The night guard skips his rounds, nobody sees the weeping pipe, the sub-basement floods. Verdict: his not doing the rounds caused the flooding. I mean that literally — a nothing, an omission, is in the causal story, not just a footnote about background conditions.
4. There was no earthquake that night and the building stood up. Verdict: the absence of an earthquake did NOT cause the building to stand, even though its standing depends on that absence just as counterfactually as the flooding depends on the skipped rounds in 3.
5. Management floated hiring a second guard and didn't do it. Had they hired him, he'd have walked the floor and seen the pipe. Verdict: the never-hired guard's absence did NOT cause the flood. Same shape as 3, opposite verdict — and this is the pair my friend says kills me. But: if instead they had *fired* a second guard six months earlier, I want to say the firing DID cause the flood. So the difference isn't just omission versus act.
6. Bitter espresso. The beans were a dark roast and the water was four degrees too hot. Either one alone, corrected, and it wouldn't have been bitter — the dependence is perfectly symmetric. Verdict: the roast caused the bitterness, the water temperature didn't, because that's just the setting the machine always runs at.
7. The on-call doctor cancels her vacation; being in town, she catches a misdiagnosis; the patient lives. Verdict: cancelling caused the survival, even though the chain runs through her not being on a plane. Now the same doctor, same hospital, cancels her vacation — and a colleague happens to catch the misdiagnosis first, before she ever sees the chart. Verdict: her cancelling caused nothing. So I'm endorsing that whether an act is a cause depends on what else happens to be lying around nearby, which my friend says is a disaster for any respectable theory.

The one I keep going back and forth on: an engineer merges a bad config at 4pm and takes down checkout. The canary deploy would have caught it — but a feature flag somebody else set two weeks earlier had disabled canaries for that service. Nobody disputes the config caused the outage. Did the flag-setter? Half the time I say yes, because the chain is real and you could have intervened on it two weeks ago and the outage wouldn't have happened. Half the time I say no, because what he did is stale, nothing about it is pushing on the outage, and if I say yes I owe the same answer about whoever wrote the canary, and whoever picked the deploy framework, all the way backwards.

So: is there a principle behind 1–7, or a small set of candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen — a decision procedure, not a slogan like "causation is difference-making"? For each clause, tell me which of my cases forced it in. Then give me its verdict on the flag-setter case, and pick three cases of your own that you think I'd get wrong and say what your principle says about them and why. And be honest about the cost: which of 1–7 it cannot deliver, and if saving all seven requires a principle with five or six clauses, say so and tell me which single verdict is cheapest for me to give up. Where the counterfactual, interventionist, and process accounts each land on 3/4/5 — which I think is the real crux — a sentence or two each is enough, and don't bother with references, I can look those up. Put the case-by-case results in a table with these columns: `case` (number, or "flag-setter" for the last one), `my verdict`, `principle's verdict`, `clause doing the work` (quote the clause of your principle that decides this row, briefly), and `kept / overturned`. One row per numbered case plus a row for the flag-setter. Everything else in prose, and keep it tight.
