I subscribe to the standard treatment of sunk costs: a decision should be governed by prospective costs and benefits alone, and the tendency to keep pouring resources into a failing project on account of what has already been spent is a genuine bias rather than prudence in disguise. To make the disagreement clean, take the idealized case as stipulated — the past outlay is unrecoverable by construction, it carries no information bearing on the project's prospects, and withdrawal has no reputational, contractual, or option-value consequences that a forward-looking calculation could absorb. My claim is that honoring the outlay is irrational even in that case.

What would count against it? Give me the considerations that ought to move me, ordered from decisive to merely awkward, and mark which of them a defender of my position would themselves concede as genuinely counting against it. Two points especially: does the stipulated setup smuggle in a contentious view of the agent's identity over time, or of what rationality is answerable to, such that the idealization is doing the substantive work rather than merely simplifying it? And if the position holds only where nothing of consequence remains at stake, is that a legitimate refinement or a slide into unfalsifiability — and would that matter for a claim of this kind? What would persuade me most is an argument that does not rest on any contested empirical finding about how people actually behave. If you think the framing is confused — that the stipulations cannot be jointly satisfied, or that what I am defending is not a substantive claim at all — say so plainly rather than answering as posed.
