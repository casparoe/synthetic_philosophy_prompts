I am a staff engineer on the platform side of a mid-size infrastructure company, and for the last four years I have been the note-taker for our quarterly technical review, where our VP of Engineering decides which internal programs keep their funding and headcount. She has no philosophy training either, but she has picked up a vocabulary from somewhere and uses it relentlessly in the minutes: every program has a core assumption that the team is not allowed to touch, a ring of auxiliary components that gets modified whenever reality misbehaves, and a trajectory she calls either progressive or degenerating. I have read a bit of Lakatos on the recommendation of a colleague, enough to recognize the terms and not enough to be sure which of his tests she is actually applying, or whether she applies one or several. I would like to predict her ruling on next quarter's case, and to understand what rule the record supports.

Her rulings so far, in the order they appear in the minutes, with the reasoning she recorded:

1. Ranking. Six years old. Core assumption, that relevance follows from link structure, never altered. Five successive signal additions, each introduced after users complained about a class of queries, each of which fixed precisely that class and nothing else. A rival retrieval effort existed in-house, covered the same ground, and had a better forward record. Ruling: kill. Her note: the patches bought us no information we did not already have, and we had somewhere to go.

2. Consensus storage. Four years old. Before running anything, the team wrote down a specific tail-latency cliff that a particular workload shape would produce under a particular configuration; fourteen months later the cliff appeared within a few percent of the stated value. Core untouched, no rival in-house. Ruling: keep, and cited since as the standard of a healthy program. Her note: it told us something true that we had not thought to ask about.

3. Auth protocol. Five years old. No forward predictions in three years; the last four changes were defensive fixes to known breakages; core untouched; nothing else in-house could replace it without a two-year migration. Ruling: keep. Her note: we are keeping this because we have nowhere to move, not because it is healthy.

4. Compiler IR. Two years old. Predicted that an entire family of codegen bugs would disappear under a new intermediate representation; confirmed three weeks before the review. Ruling: keep, but she explicitly declined to call it healthy. Her note: one good result is not a record; bring it back in a year.

5. Labeling pipeline. Three years old. Its headline prediction came out right, but the claim had been rewritten twice after the data were collected, and the version that was confirmed was not the version anyone had written down in advance. A rival existed in-house. Ruling: kill. Her note: it did not predict anything; it redescribed what had already happened.

6. Observability agent. Eighteen months old, no predictions yet, no rival, core untouched. Ruling: keep. Her note: too young to be failing.

Next quarter's case, which I have already seen the proposal for. The scheduler program is three years old and was built on the assumption that every scheduling decision should come out of a learned priority model. Last quarter the team replaced that assumption with a hybrid: the learned model now runs inside a set of hand-written constraints. After making the change, and before measuring anything, they wrote down that a specific class of jobs, bursty ingest workloads, would show queue-depth oscillation at a particular frequency under load; two weeks ago the production traces showed exactly that, and nobody had asked about oscillation before. Meanwhile the purely hand-written heuristic scheduler still exists in-house as a rival and currently posts better tail metrics on the same workload.

The one thing I cannot work out myself is whether she will still regard this as the same program that was reviewed for three years, given that the thing it was built on has been swapped out, and whether swapping it starts the clock over.

Could you tell me the following. First, what candidate rules, or combinations of rules, are consistent with all six rulings; I would rather have the full set of survivors than a single favorite, and I would like to know which of the six precedents does the work of eliminating the others. Second, what each surviving rule implies about the scheduler, since my impression is that they will not agree. Third, your best single prediction of her ruling, with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others given the record rather than given Lakatos scholarship in the abstract. Fourth, after her ruling comes in, one further review case you would want to watch for in the minutes, described concretely enough that I would recognize it, which would discriminate between whatever rules are still standing. And finally, separately from predicting her: if the decision were mine, which ruling would best cohere with our own six precedents, which of them is the closest analog to the scheduler, and what principle we would be committing ourselves to if we ruled one way rather than the other.
