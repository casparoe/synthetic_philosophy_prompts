You're a philosopher of criminal law who has actually thought hard about legal moralism — write as that person, not as a textbook summariser. Think carefully. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm out loud for a bit: list the candidate theories that could sit underneath the pattern I'm about to describe, then start crossing out the ones that don't fit.

The pattern is England and Wales, roughly the last sixty-odd years, and it has four pieces:

1. Private consensual homosexual acts between adults got decriminalised on the explicit ground that what adults do in private isn't the criminal law's business even when most people think it's immoral. That's the move the whole Hart/Devlin argument started from.
2. But private consensual sadomasochistic activity that causes more than trivial injury is still criminal, and consent simply isn't available as a defence — even where nobody complained, nobody needed medical treatment, and it all happened between adults in a private home. The exceptions the courts do allow look like an arbitary grab-bag: surgery, ritual circumcision, tattooing, ear-piercing, boxing. And a man who branded his own initials onto his wife's buttocks with a hot knife, at her request, got off on the basis that what he'd done was really more like tattooing than like an assault.
3. Cutting the other way: ritual circumcision of infant boys is lawful, while female genital cutting is criminal whatever anyone says they consent to, and can be prosecuted here even when it was carried out abroad.
4. And since 2008 it's a crime merely to *possess* an image which was produced mainly for sexual arousal, which is grossly offensive/disgusting/obscene in character, and which portrays in an explicit and realistic way an act threatening someone's life, or an act likely to cause serious injury to someone's anus, breasts or genitals — provided a reasonable viewer would think the person depicted was real. So a staged image, where nothing happened and nobody was harmed, is enough. No victim, no act, no publication. Possession alone.

Three things I want from you, in this order.

(a) Reconstruct the implicit theory of the criminal law's proper business that makes all four hold at once. Not a theory that explains one of them — I want the single set of commitments, if there is one, that licenses the combination. In particular: what has to be true about consent for it to run out at "more than trivial injury" yet still cover tattooing, boxing and a hot knife on a consenting spouse's backside? What has to be true about bodies for (2) and (3) to come out the way they do? And what has to be true about images for possession-without-an-act to be the right target?

(b) Separate the layers. Which parts of the practice are genuinely *presupposed* — the doctrine couldn't be what it is without them — versus merely fit comfortably with them, versus places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories license exactly the same rules. My suspicion is that (4) is the underdetermined one, and that the candidates are: a Devlin-style view that a society may use law to protect the shared morality whose erosion would come apart at the seams; a speculative claim about effects on viewers and hence on third parties; a pure expressivist thing about which representations the state will tolerate circulating; or no philosophy at all, just the fact that consensual acts in private are nearly unprosecutable for want of complainants and witnesses, so you criminalise what you can actually find on somebody's phone. Test that suspicion rather than accepting it, and tell me which one is doing the real work — including whether the "administrative convenience" reading, if right, undermines the standard way the Hart/Devlin debate is framed.

(c) Now make it counterfactual. What would have to change, concretely, in each of the four pieces under: (i) a strict Millian harm principle; (ii) Devlin's enforcement-of-positive-morality view; (iii) a Feinberg-style offence principle, i.e. the law may prevent serious offence to unwilling observers but not mere immorality. Note that (iii) is public-facing and (4) is a private-possession offence, so I want to see you handle that mismatch rather than paper over it. And finally: is there any single view under which the current combination comes out as actually *right*, on grounds nobody in the case law articulates — or is it just historically layered inconsistency that's been rationalised after the fact?
