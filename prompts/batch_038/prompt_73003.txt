I cover machine intelligence and the philosophy of science for a general-interest science monthly, and this year my editor has asked me to move the column off the announcement cycle and onto arguments that are actually being had inside the field rather than around it. My guest for next month's recorded conversation runs a well-regarded interpretability group; I would rather not name her in this note, and I would ask you to keep her unnamed in the draft as well. The position she has been building for several years is one most of her colleagues hold loosely and she holds tightly: when an interpretability paper reports that a network represents a city, a syntactic category, a direction of comparison, or a policy for continuing a pattern, that is literally true of the system, not a convenient manner of speaking, and the field ought to treat its representational vocabulary the way physics treats electrons. She is articulate, genuinely well read in philosophy of mind and philosophy of science, and her characteristic move under pressure is to agree with a softened restatement of the objection and then reassert the strong claim she started with, so the questions have to be built to survive that move.

Please draft ten to twelve interview questions for roughly an hour of tape, each immediately followed by the follow-up I should ask given the answer she is most likely to give; I want the follow-ups keyed to her probable reply rather than generic probes, and I want at least one question on each of the following. First, superposition and polysemanticity: what it costs a realist to insist that features are real when the inventory of features recovered depends on the dictionary-learning method used to recover them, with the follow-up aimed at her expected answer that held-out validation metrics settle which latents are genuine, and the rejoinder that validation measures prediction rather than existence. Second, the instrumentalist analogy with Dennett's intentional stance and with folk-psychological talk of belief and desire: what principle marks the difference between saying a network represents Paris and saying a thermostat represents temperature, and if she answers that the difference is causal rather than behavioral, press her on whether a causal-interventional criterion is something an instrumentalist can also accept. Third, what fixes content at all, given that one trained function can be realized by many different internal geometries and one geometry can turn up in models trained on quite different data, so that a causal-historical account grounded in training data and a functional-role account grounded in inference-time use come apart; the follow-up should force her to choose and then test the choice against a model whose training corpus has been paraphrased, translated, or synthetically rewritten. Fourth, the disjunction problem: what makes an internal direction a representation of a thing rather than of the whole family of things it is merely correlated with, including the case of entities that do not exist, such as the fictional creatures a model handles fluently. Fifth, the source of representational normativity, given that next-token prediction rewards being predictively useful rather than being true, so that a system can be systematically mistaken about the world while doing precisely what its objective demands; if she answers that error is defined by failure on the training signal, ask her whether that leaves her representations capable of being false about anything at all. Sixth, and the one I most want to land, the intervention problem: her standard evidence for realism is that we can locate a representation, edit or steer it, and watch behavior change accordingly, and I want a question that requires her either to concede or to refuse that this is exactly the evidence an instrumentalist predicts, since instrumentalism does not deny that representational talk is a reliable handle on the system, only that it is true. If she maintains that the two positions diverge empirically, the follow-up should ask her to name the specific observation that would favor realism over instrumentalism; if she concedes that they do not diverge, the follow-up should ask her what the disagreement is then about and why it matters.

Two further threads if you can fold them in without crowding the list. One is her most reliable resource, complete access to the substrate: she will say that unlike the neuroscientist she can read every weight and every activation, and I want a question that grants the point and then argues it does not help, because exhaustive access to an implementation does not decide which among many equally accurate descriptions of it is the representational one. The other is track record: the field has a history of confident claims about single-unit selectivity, from the grandmother-cell debates of the last century through recordings in human epilepsy patients of neurons that fired selectively to one particular celebrity's face, and then more recently through circuit-level findings that later proved method-dependent; ask her whether representation realism is exposed to a pessimistic meta-induction of the usual sort, and whether the completeness of her access is a real disanalogy or a change of subject. If you have room, close with a question about downstream stakes, namely whether any of this changes how we should weigh claims that models have world models and can therefore plan or deceive, or whether the debate is verbal; the follow-up there should ask what she would advise a safety team to do differently on the realist reading than on the instrumentalist one.

Please send it to me as continuous prose in the manner of an email: number the questions within the flow of the text, but use no headings, no bold or italic emphasis, no bullet characters, no tables, and no nested indentation; each numbered item should be a single short paragraph containing the question in the words I would actually say on tape, with its follow-up in the same paragraph. Keep the whole thing under about fifteen hundred words, and do not include a preamble about how you approached the assignment.
