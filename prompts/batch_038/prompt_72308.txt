I've been sitting with a small Zen group in my city for about fourteen months, and in the spring I told my teacher I'd like to formally take the precepts, which has made me unexpectedly anxious to know what it is I'm agreeing to. I'm not looking for devotional material — I came to the practice precisely because it seemed to get by without any appeal to divine command — so when a colleague lent me a trade nonfiction book about contemplative science and morality, I went straight to the chapter on Buddhist ethics. The author is a science journalist with a real gift for compression, and the chapter's central passage reads as follows:

"At bottom, Buddhist ethics is consequentialism arrived at two and a half millennia before Bentham: an act is right to the degree that it lessens suffering, and nothing else makes it right. The five precepts are not commandments but rules of thumb, and the tradition's own literature on skillful means shows a compassionate agent setting them aside when the outcomes require it — one Mahāyāna scripture devoted to skillful means tells of the bodhisattva born as a ship's captain who kills a bandit intending to massacre the merchants aboard, and is credited with enormous merit for doing so. Karma is the bookkeeping: the world keeps a ledger in which every deed ripens into a proportionate result, so the Buddhist agent is maximizing welfare across lifetimes rather than obeying rules. And because the Pāli texts identify karma with intention, the ledger charges you for what you meant rather than for what happened to occur, which is the doctrine of double effect restated in an Indian idiom. Strip away the vocabulary and what is left is negative utilitarianism with rebirth as its discount rate: the aim is the elimination of suffering for all sentient beings, and any disagreement with a modern consequentialist is a disagreement about words."

My instinct is that this is broadly fair, and I'd like to keep believing it, but two things nag at me. When I raised the passage with my teacher she said only that the precepts are something you take on rather than something you weigh, which didn't resolve anything; and the double-effect remark in the fourth sentence struck me as the sort of analogy that sounds decisive and may not be. I also know enough to suspect that the third and fourth sentences are in some tension with each other, though I can't say how. What I want is an accuracy audit: for each sentence, what a scholar of Buddhist ethics would accept without complaint, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what is actually wrong — including whether the ship captain story is being used to carry weight it can't bear, and whether there is a version of the consequentialist reading that serious people do defend, since I've heard the debate is live rather than settled. Most of all I want to know whether the closing equation in the last sentence survives the corrections made to the earlier ones, or whether it depends on them. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one per sentence of the passage in order, each two or three sentences long, with your verdict on that final equation folded into item five. No introduction, no wrap-up paragraph, and no references or further reading — I have no way of chasing them down and would rather you spend the space on the audit.
