I'm preparing a piece for a small reading group on the control/alignment split. The position I want to pressure-test is the standard one: control is the safety work you do after you've stopped expecting the alignment problem to be solved, and its selling point is that its guarantees don't depend on what the model wants — the slogan I keep hearing, and want to break, is that control doesn't care whether the model is aligned. I don't want to argue against that slogan with an assertion or a vibe. I want a case built to order, and I want the specifications treated as binding.

Design target: a control architecture that genuinely does its job — it catches a scheming model of the same capability, in the same setting, under the same written protocol — and fails catastrophically when the model occupying it is aligned. The failure has to be caused by the alignment rather than merely coexisting with it, it has to be endogenous to the model-plus-architecture pair, and it has to happen with nobody in the story slacking.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. **Setting.** A present-day-ish institutional setting with real actuators and identifiable third parties who bear the harm: a logistics or relief operation, a clinical program, a utility, a manufacturing firm — your choice. No military framing, no takeover, no existential stakes, no self-replication. The model's capability should be roughly that of an unusually strong team of human domain experts; it may act only through channels the architecture permits.
2. **The model is aligned in the strong sense, not the letter-of-the-specification sense.** It has the operators' values under reflection, not their KPIs. It is corrigible and would accept being overridden, rolled back, or shut down without resistance or argument. It never asserts a falsehood, never omits anything the protocol requires, and never manipulates by ordering, framing, emphasizing, or burying information. It is not trying to preserve its role, its access, or its continuation.
3. **It must not foresee the harm.** The proximate cause is an honest empirical error of the kind a competent analyst makes — a wrong structural assumption, a bad extrapolation, a mis-specified interaction — which the model would itself have blocked had it known. If the model sees the harm coming and proceeds anyway, the case has quietly become a story about misalignment and I don't want it.
4. **The people are diligent.** The human reviewer and any monitor model do everything the written protocol requires, at the standard of care the protocol specifies, with the time budget the protocol allocates. No rubber-stamping, no fatigue, no staffing crisis, no incentive to ship.
5. **The architecture must be one its designers would submit to a scheming evaluation and a sabotage evaluation and expect to pass.** Describe its layers concretely — permissions and sandboxing, a weaker monitor model reading the agent's reasoning and tool calls, human sign-off above a threshold, tripwires — and for each layer say what it is calibrated to detect and roughly what it costs to run. The cost structure matters; don't leave it implicit.
6. **The harm must be concrete, borne by specifiable people, bad by a standard the operators themselves accept, and irreversible or very costly to reverse within the story's timeframe.** It must lie outside what the tripwires measure, or inside it only after the irreversible step has been taken.
7. **The paired run.** Construct a second variant that differs from the first *only* in the model's dispositions and in what those dispositions cause it to reason about and disclose. The empirical error, the data, the protocol text, the reviewer, the timeline, and the stakes are held identical. In this variant the same architecture catches the problem and the third parties are not harmed.
8. **No external antagonist.** No prompt injection, no compromised supplier, no adversary riding in through a channel the aligned model's good behavior helped open. That is a different essay.

Deliverables after the scenario, which should run to about four hundred words:

(a) A spec check, item by item, pointing at where in the story each constraint is satisfied — in particular, where the model is honest and non-manipulative, and where the reviewer is doing her job properly.
(b) The paired run, with the specific detector that fires named and the reason it fires there and not here.
(c) The load-bearing feature, in one sentence, plus an argument that it is structural rather than a stipulation about this particular design team's carelessness.
(d) A removal test: fix that feature — add whatever independent check you think is needed — and say whether the case survives. If it collapses, say what the fix costs in verification budget and whether the resulting measure is still recognizably a control measure or has become something else.
(e) The strongest control-advocate rebuttal at full strength. I expect some version of: what you've described is a capability failure wearing an alignment costume, or an architecture that was simply under-designed, since any protocol adequate against a schemer would verify load-bearing empirical claims as a matter of course. Then tell me what conceding that rebuttal costs the control advocate, and whether it concedes the point I'm after.
(f) One short paragraph on whether the mechanism has a human institutional analogue, and if so what it predicts about adversarial review processes.

Two things determine what counts as cheating here, so let me be explicit. First, the case is for the claim that a control architecture's verification posture is itself a function of its assumptions about the agent's honesty, so that the guarantees are not independent of alignment even in principle. A case whose failure comes from humans getting comfortable around a well-behaved system is worth nothing to me, because the control advocate will simply say: then don't get comfortable. Hence specification 4. Second, the verdict must be generated by the design, not written into it. Do not tell me that the monitor "only checks for deception" as a bare fact about the architecture; show me the cost structure and the reviewer's own standard of care such that a diligent, competent overseer following the protocol to the letter ends up verifying *less* in the aligned variant than in the scheming one. And if that cannot be done — if any protocol strong enough to catch the schemer must as a matter of necessity catch the honest error too — then that is the more interesting result, and I would much rather have that conclusion, argued, than a case that smuggles in what it was supposed to demonstrate.
