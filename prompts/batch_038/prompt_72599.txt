I run a 90-minute upper-level seminar on self-knowledge for fourteen students, most of whom have had one philosophy-of-mind course and know the vocabulary but have never had an intuition of theirs broken in real time. Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about how much the introspective-unreliability literature actually establishes, and I want the seminar to settle it between themselves rather than inherit either of our positions. His line: the well-known results — people confidently narrating reasons for a preference that was switched behind their backs, or explaining a judgment they never made — show only that we lack privileged access to our own cognitive processes, and leave untouched the authority of reports about our present experience: how vivid it was, in what order things seemed to happen, what we seem to be attending to right now. My line: the failure runs all the way down, and the immunity of phenomenology is a concession with no principled basis.

Design an in-class activity that puts that dispute in play. Hard constraints: the students must not learn that introspection is the subject until the debrief; the evidence that someone has misreported must come from artifacts the students generated themselves, not from anything I assert or any external authority; and the activity must contain a component aimed squarely at reports about ongoing or just-past experience, not merely at reports about reasons for action, since that is where the two positions actually diverge. Give me rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, roles, and a commitment device that creates real but non-monetary stakes in getting one's own report right. Then a debrief that maps particular moments onto the two positions and states what pattern of results would favor one over the other — not just a narration of what went wrong. Also tell me what will predictably break the first time I run it, including the student who reverse-engineers the setup in the first five minutes and the ethical awkwardness of deceiving students who then have to sit with having been deceived. Finally, be frank about which feature of the dispute no classroom activity can reproduce, and what I must supply by argument instead.

Approach this as a philosopher of mind who has run this kind of seminar many times and has opinions about what works. Think it through before drafting: sketch two or three candidate mechanisms, say which you are rejecting and why, then build out the one you choose. I do not want a menu. Take a committed stand on whether the immunity thesis is testable in a classroom setting at all; if you conclude that it is not, say so plainly and design the next-best thing, explaining what it can and cannot show.
