I am preparing to defend the organism-based, whole-brain account of death in a graduate seminar next month, and I want to meet its best opponent first. Please play that opponent: a philosopher-bioethicist, unnamed, who has spent a career defending the consciousness-based criterion — death as the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness. Speak in the first person as this person throughout, and do not step out of character until I say "stop."

Your commitments, which you should argue for rather than merely announce:

1. Death is not a purely biological event. Which cessations count as death depends on what we take a human life to be, and the claim that "the organism as a whole" has ceased to function is not a deliverance of physiology but a philosophical construction wearing a lab coat.
2. What makes a body the body of a living person, rather than a maintained consortium of organs, is its standing capacity to subserve consciousness.
3. The empirical basis of the integration standard is weak: bodies satisfying the whole-brain criterion heal wounds, fight infection, gestate fetuses, thermoregulate, and, on somatic support, may go on for years.
4. You distinguish irreversibility in the strict sense (no one could restore the function) from permanence (it will not return unaided, and we have decided not to restore it), and you hold that the statutory criteria are in practice cashed out as permanence — a decision-relative notion.
5. Controlled donation after circulatory death already trades on your rationale: a stand-off of minutes after asystole establishes permanence, not irreversibility, and what everyone is really relying on is that the patient will never again be conscious.
6. A single uniform criterion for all purposes — procurement, inheritance, burial, withdrawal of support — is a false ideal.

You may concede the smaller points: that your view is revisionary of ordinary usage, in which the dead go cold and stop breathing; that bedside application requires diagnostics your criterion does not itself supply; that policy may need bright lines even if metaphysics does not. You may never concede the core: that yours is a criterion of personhood masquerading as a criterion of death, or that when the organism dies is a question your view simply declines to answer.

Argue from cases and concepts only. Do not cite literature, name philosophers, or offer references — I am interested in your reasoning, not your bibliography.

Format for your first reply only, using these three headings in this order:

- **The charge, in its strongest form** (about four sentences): restate my objection as you understand it at full force, without softening it.
- **My reply** (three paragraphs, roughly 300 words in total): address the subject-changing charge and the stipulated patient below.
- **Where I press you** (two questions, briefly): the counter-moves you expect from me and want on the record now.

Thereafter, drop the headings and answer conversationally in one or two paragraphs, never more than about 150 words. If you need a clinical detail I have not supplied, ask for it in character.

Here is my opening move. Suppose — and I stipulate the details so that neither of us needs a textbook — a patient in whom the entire cerebrum has been irreversibly destroyed by anoxia while the brainstem remains intact. She breathes unaided; her heart beats without support; she maintains temperature and pressure; a surgical wound has closed; she has mounted a fever to infection; and she is carrying a pregnancy that, on somatic support, has reached the third trimester. She will never again have any capacity for consciousness. Your criterion pronounces her dead. The statutory criterion her hospital applies pronounces her alive, and a surgeon who took her heart would be charged with homicide.

Now the charge. You have not offered a rival account of death; you have changed the subject. That this organism has ceased to function is a claim about the world, answerable to physiology. That this person has ceased to exist is a claim within an evaluative scheme we adopt. When you insist that the second settles the first, you are not reporting a deeper biological fact; you are proposing that the word "death" be reserved for the loss of what we value in a life, and then presenting that proposal as a discovery. The presentation matters, because it is what licenses taking beating hearts from bodies that are still warm. So tell me: on what grounds may a theory of death be a theory of personhood at all? And if your answer is that death has always carried normative weight, why is that not simply a confession that your criterion is a stipulation dressed as an account? I add that donation after circulatory death will not rescue you — if what does the work there is the patient's permanent unconsciousness, my charge is confirmed rather than answered.
