I sit on the grants board of a small foundation that funds invertebrate welfare. Every proposal that reaches us costs roughly the same, and our working group certifies that each would change about the same amount of harm per individual — a quicker death instead of a slower one, or the removal of one repeated noxious procedure — so the only variables in play are how many animals are affected, how confident we are that they are sentient, and what sort of evidence that confidence rests on. For each past proposal I give individuals affected per year; our working credence that the taxon is sentient; the evidence behind that credence (L = published data on associative learning, or on opioid-type modulation of nociception, in the taxon or a close relative; R = nociceptive reflex data only; P = phylogenetic extrapolation with no taxon-specific data); and our decision.

| Proposal | N/yr | Credence | Evidence | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stunning before slaughter in farmed shrimp | 4×10⁹ | 0.50 | L | funded |
| Switch to a fast-acting insecticide across a national grain belt, mixed wild assemblage | 5×10⁹ | 0.04 | P | rejected |
| Change to a tillage practice affecting springtails, mites and nematodes | 1×10¹³ | 0.002 | P | rejected |
| Municipal streetlight retrofit affecting moths | 3×10⁶ | 0.30 | R | rejected |
| New killing standard for commercially reared crickets and mealworms | 8×10⁹ | 0.15 | L | funded |
| Change to a greenhouse programme against aphids and whiteflies | 2×10⁸ | 0.05 | R | rejected |

The minutes always record the same phrase, "not cost-effective at our current confidence," and we have never defined the phrase. Next month's proposal replaces the insecticide used against aphids and thrips across a continent's grain belt: 5×10¹² individuals a year, working credence 0.08, evidence type R. Our chair holds that the numbers must eventually win — any nonzero credence, times a large enough N, swamps everything else we fund. Our treasurer holds that we would be spending the whole budget on a one-in-twelve guess.

Predict the verdict. Identify every decision rule consistent with all six precedents — consider at least a linear expected-value rule, rules that discount the credence non-linearly, threshold rules on credence alone or on credence and N jointly, rules keyed to evidence type, and rules keyed to nothing in the table at all — and for each give the parameter range or criteria that make it fit, or the precedent that kills it. Then say where next month's proposal lands under each survivor, showing the arithmetic, and note explicitly which survivors the precedents leave underdetermined at exactly the values the new proposal has. Give your best guess and justify it by how much work each rule has to do to fit the data, name one further proposal (specifying N, credence, and evidence type) that would split the survivors, and state what principle the board would be committing itself to if it votes the way you predict.

Close with a table whose rows are the candidate rules and whose columns are: rule (one line stating the decision procedure); fit (the parameter range or criteria that reconcile it with all six precedents, or the precedent that rules it out); verdict on next month's proposal; discriminating proposal (values of N, credence, and evidence type for one further case on which this rule parts company from at least one other survivor). After the table, give the single predicted verdict and one sentence on what the board would thereby be endorsing.
