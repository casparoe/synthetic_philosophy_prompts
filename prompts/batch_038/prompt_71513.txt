Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about the silences in the archive. He holds that a silence is essentially a gap: with enough patience a researcher can reconstruct an unrecorded life from the margins of what survives — a signature, a baptismal entry, a line in a wage book. I argued that the harder problem is not the missing records but the extant ones, that a person who was written about survives only inside the categories of whoever did the writing, and that the descriptive apparatus around her (creator, custodial history, scope and content, access restrictions) is composed from the position of the institution holding the paper. He thought this was defeatism dressed as rigor.

I would like a poem that makes my case without arguing it. Please write a sestina in the voice of a woman who exists only as an entry in some institution's records. The six end-words should be words the archive supplies for her — the sort of terms a ledger, a case file, or a schedule of deportees would offer — and the form should carry the point that no seventh end-word ever arrives: the rotation can only reorder the vocabulary she was given, which is precisely why my friend's patience, however admirable, cannot get her out. The envoi, taking all six in three lines, should be where it becomes plain what the permutation cannot reach, and it should read as her sentence rather than the institution's without letting her use a word the six do not contain.

Someone who has handled finding aids should feel the difference between a gap and a category; someone who has not should still find it a good poem. Keep the diction plain and administrative — no gothic ruin, no parchment-and-dust mood, no apostrophe to Silence.
