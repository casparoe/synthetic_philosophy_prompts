I am in the final year of a political theory degree, and my seminar this term is on the democratic boundary problem: the question of who is entitled to constitute the demos, and the worry that the boundaries of a voting body cannot themselves be fixed democratically without either an infinite regress or a first decision made by someone with no standing to make it. Alongside the seminar I sit on the steering committee for my city's participatory budgeting cycle, in which residents propose projects and then vote on how to allocate a fixed capital sum from the municipal budget. The council has pledged to fund the winning projects, so the vote is formally advisory but practically binding. The committee has to publish a standing eligibility rulebook this term, because at present we relitigate the same disputes from scratch each cycle, in public, under deadline.

Our current rule is one sentence long — any city resident aged sixteen or over may vote — and it is doing almost no normative work. Each cycle we are petitioned by some combination of the following: commuters who work full time here and use our infrastructure but sleep in a suburb; undocumented residents of ten or more years' standing who are unwilling to register anything with a city office; fifteen-year-olds enrolled in our schools; second-home owners and university students present nine months a year; people sleeping rough with no address that can be verified; a shop owner whose livelihood depends entirely on a street up for repaving; and residents of an unincorporated pocket the city expects to annex within three years but which currently pays no city tax.

Please write the eligibility procedure the committee could actually adopt. It should have two tiers: a committee-level procedure for deciding, once per cycle, whether a petitioned category is admitted, admitted in a restricted role (proposing but not voting, voting only on projects that affect it, casting an advisory ballot), or refused; and a table-level flowchart derived from whatever rules the committee settles, runnable by a volunteer with no theoretical training in under three minutes per person. For each step, state what it is operationalizing from the theory (all-affected interests, all-subjected-to-the-rules, coercion, membership, contribution, capacity, and so forth), what counts as a sufficient answer, whether the answer is a matter of fact or of value, and where the step hands the decision back to the committee rather than resolving it. Include an explicit stopping rule, a tie-breaker for cases where two principles point in opposite directions, and the documentation requirement — noting that we have no budget for verification, no database access, and a city attorney who must confirm we are not creating a parallel electorate for real municipal elections.

Then run the procedure on three petitions: the commuters, the undocumented residents with no verifiable address, and the fifteen-year-olds. I expect it to admit the fifteen-year-olds and refuse the commuters; if that is right, tell me which of my own commitments that outcome contradicts, and what would have to be changed in the rulebook to reverse it honestly rather than by patching the case in afterward.

Finally, flag the steps where the procedure is really laundering a value judgment as a technical criterion, say plainly which of these disputes the boundary problem leaves undecidable such that the committee is simply choosing, and identify the affected parties the procedure structurally cannot reach — future residents, nonhuman interests, taxpayers in other jurisdictions. I would also like a short paragraph of published justification, in plain language, that a person turned away at the table could be given and understand.
