So I've got about twelve minutes at the end of next month in front of our town's park and tree committee. I'm not a scientist — I fix bikes full time — but I've been reading about insect sentience for two years now and I can't get it out of my head. The actual proposal: we stop the routine blanket treatment of the street ashes for emerald ash borer, the every-couple-years all-of-them thing, and instead only treat the handful of specimen trees people actually give a damn about, the ones by the school and the senior center.

My argument isn't "insects feel pain and here's the proof." It's a numbers argument with a twist I came up with myself, so please tell me if it's garbage. When one side of a decision involves a few hundred trees and the other side involves something like a hundred million individual insects in a single season, the usual rule — weigh the evidence, go with the balance — shouldn't apply unchanged. The bigger the gap in how many individuals sit on each side, the less confident you should need to be that the huge side matters morally at all. So a genuinely tiny chance that insects are the kind of thing that can have a bad life ought to beat near-certainty about the trees. I've been calling it the asymmetry rule in my head.

The room is four people, none of them philosophers: the city arborist, twenty years in, watched the ash die off block by block and cares about canopy cover above everything; a county extension entomologist who advises them, a pure populations-and-biodiversity person who will hate the word "individuals"; a beekeeper who also sits on the conservation commission and is suspicious of anything that sounds like letting trees die; and a council liaison whose whole job is budget and angry residents.

Give me a prep sheet. The objections I'll actually get from these four, in the order they'd do me the most damage, each phrased the way that person would say it out loud rather than in paper language, with a reply I can deliver in under a minute and an honest note on where each reply is soft. Tell me what I should just concede in my first two minutes to buy goodwill, what I absolutely cannot give up, and where the asymmetry rule is weakest — I'd rather hear it from you than from the entomologist. And finish with the one question I'm praying nobody asks, plus what I say if they ask it anyway. No reading list, I can't cite anything and wouldn't have time.
