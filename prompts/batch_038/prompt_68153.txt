I work as a software engineer, mostly on capacity forecasting: my team builds models that predict how much load our systems will carry three, five and seven years out, and we are wrong in ways that only become visible years later, which makes the post-mortems awkward. That work has left me in an unresolved argument with a colleague, and it is the reason I am writing to you. I help run a small evening reading group, a dozen or so people who meet in the back room of a pub near the office, mostly engineers plus two schoolteachers, and nobody in the room has any formal training in philosophy, myself included. Next month's session is on predictions that have not come true, and I have been asked to open the evening by reading something aloud, five minutes at most. I would like that to be a poem about Thomas Malthus, whose Essay on the Principle of Population, first published anonymously in 1798, strikes me as the cleanest case in existence of a model that may well have been right and was almost certainly early. I would like the poem to be a pantoum of six quatrains, twenty-four lines.

In case it matters, the rule I have in mind is the standard one: the second and fourth lines of each stanza recur, word for word, as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza completes the circle by bringing back the poem's third line in second position and its first line as the very last line. I would like loose unrhymed pentameter; rhyme is not required, and I would much rather you did not force it, since the repetitions need to be audible on their own. The repeated lines should be identical in wording, with punctuation the only thing allowed to change, and I would like at least one recurrence to turn on precisely that shift. I have chosen this form because it seems to me to be the shape of the argument rather than a decoration for it: the same sentences come back generation after generation in a changed context and slowly come to mean something else, which is what happened to Malthus's claim, and the poem ends where it began, which is either the trap closing or a tautology. Please do not tell the listener which of those it is.

For content, here is what I would like worked in, and do correct me where I have got it wrong, since I know the essay only from extracts and a few secondary summaries: the two ratios, a population that doubles against a food supply that merely adds, expressed as images rather than as arithmetic; the checks, both the violent ones of famine, epidemic and war and the prudential one of marriage postponed and children not had; the fact that the mechanism seems to have held for something like ten thousand years and stopped holding a century or two after the book appeared, once yields, fertiliser and then falling birth rates broke it; the inversion associated with the Danish economist Ester Boserup, on which population pressure is not the brake but the spur to invention; and the wager Julian Simon settled with Paul Ehrlich in 1990 over the price of five metals, which Simon won, though the same wager struck over a different decade would very likely have gone the other way, which is exactly the trouble with long-run predictions. Underneath all of that I want the philosophical question, but earned rather than stated: was the theory refuted, or was it rescued each time by another patch, a longer horizon or a fresh auxiliary assumption about technology, or was it right and merely early? Please name no philosopher in the poem and avoid vocabulary such as falsifiable, paradigm or research programme; render the point in concrete things, a ledger, a harvest, a field left fallow, a cheque in the post. After the poem, add a note of about 150 words in plain English for an audience with no philosophy, explaining how the repetition rule carries the argument and saying which of the three verdicts the poem in fact leans towards, if any. Please write both the poem and the note in British English, and do not include a bibliography, a reading list or any citations.
