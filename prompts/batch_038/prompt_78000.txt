Last night a friend and I spent close to three hours on Chase and Sanborn's 1989 paper on functionalism and qualia — the two-author piece that he keeps referring to by the phrase "dope on a rope," which I take to be from its title, though neither of us had the text in front of us. We ended the evening unable to agree on the most elementary question about it, namely whose side the paper is on, and I would rather have a careful adjudication than a confident reconstruction I have no way to check.

Here is the summary he gave me, twice, with increasing conviction, and which he offered as settling the matter:

> "Chase and Sanborn are running a reductio against functionalism. They grant the functionalist the usual cases for the sake of argument — inverted spectra, gradual replacement of the machinery — and then press the consequence. If two systems can differ in what it is like to be them while agreeing on every input, every output, and every internal causal role, then whatever differs makes no causal difference. Their coffee-taster cases are meant to bring that out: one lifelong drinker's experience of the morning cup changes over the years while everything he says and does stays exactly the same, and in the mirrored case his reactions change while the experience stays put. The functionalist cannot tell those two stories apart, because they are functionally identical descriptions, so the distinction between the experience and the functional role cannot be doing any work. Hence qualia, on the traditional conception, are epiphenomenal — inert passengers, dope dangling on the end of a rope whose other end is attached to the real machinery. And since nobody should be willing to say that his own experience is a decoration bolted onto the causal order, they argue we should drop the notion of qualia altogether. That is all quining qualia amounts to: once the functional facts are fixed there is nothing left over that could be the feel of the coffee."

My own going-in position was that functionalism cannot capture what it is like to taste coffee, and that any argument implying otherwise has changed the subject; his was that the paper dismantles precisely that intuition, and that in stating my objection I was already relying on a notion of qualia the paper shows to be unusable. I suspect now that each of us was reading his own view into it, but I could not say where.

Please work through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on this debate would accept, what she would regard as a fair simplification, and what she would reject outright. Specifically:

1. Which way does the argument run? The two readings we defended were (i) that the paper uses the threat of epiphenomenalism to refute functionalism, and (ii) that it uses that threat to argue that anyone who is a functionalist about belief and desire is committed to being a functionalist about qualia, so that the epiphenomenalism charge is aimed at the traditional conception of qualia rather than at functionalism. I suspect I have stated at least one of these badly. Say what role the epiphenomenalism actually plays in the dialectic — premise, conclusion, or reductio of the opponent's conception — and whether the paper is best classified as anti-functionalist at all.

2. Does his description of the coffee-taster cases get their direction right, and is "the functionalist cannot tell the two stories apart" what the authors claim? Or is their point rather that an alteration in what it is like which left every discriminatory, memorial, and reporting capacity untouched would not be an alteration at all? Those two claims sound nearly identical to me, and I want to know whether the difference is load-bearing.

3. What exactly is inert on the "dope on a rope" image — qualia as such, or qualia as the traditional conception characterizes them, i.e. intrinsic, ineffable, and incorrigibly given to their subjects? Does his summary slide from the second to the first, and if so, does that slide do any work in his conclusion?

4. Does the final claim — drop the notion entirely, nothing left over once the functional facts are fixed — follow from the causal-relevance argument, or does it need an extra premise about what it takes for a property to be real? Is the upshot eliminativism about experience or revisionism about a concept? And is "quining qualia" even their phrase, or has he borrowed it from elsewhere in the literature and assumed it was theirs?

5. He credits them with granting inverted spectra and gradual replacement of the machinery for the sake of argument. Are those the cases the paper actually works with, or has he imported stock examples — possibly from work written later — and backdated them?

6. Setting exegesis aside: is the inference from functionalism about the attitudes to functionalism about qualia sound? Two objections came up and I could not choose between them. The first is bullet-biting — if an epiphenomenalist about qualia is perfectly willing to accept that experience makes no causal difference, does the argument simply lose its purchase on him, or is there something independently wrong with that position? The second is that causal relevance does not obviously require functional characterizability: a property might be efficacious in virtue of what it is rather than in virtue of the role it plays, which would make the step from "not functionally characterized" to "makes no causal difference" a non sequitur. Which is the more serious threat, and is there a third objection I should have pressed?

7. Finally, tell me which single sentence in his summary, once corrected, would have ended the argument fastest, and which of us — the person who thought functionalism cannot capture what it is like, or the person who thought the paper dismantles that intuition — was closer to what the paper actually does.

I am not after a bibliography or a list of further reading, and I would rather you flag any point where your recollection of the paper is shaky than fill the gap with plausible-sounding detail: where you are reconstructing rather than recalling, say so explicitly and mark how much of your answer depends on that reconstruction.
