**The submitted answer**

> (a) Arrow's theorem is about a social welfare function: a rule that takes a profile of individual preference orderings over a fixed set of alternatives and returns one social ordering over the same set. The conditions are these.
>
> 1. Unrestricted domain: the rule must be defined for every logically possible profile of complete and transitive individual orderings. No voter is excluded and no preference pattern is ruled out in advance.
> 2. Weak Pareto: if every individual prefers x to y, the social ordering puts x above y.
> 3. Independence of irrelevant alternatives: the social ranking of x against y depends only on how the individuals rank x against y; nothing about other alternatives may enter into it.
> 4. Non-dictatorship: there is no individual whose preferences fix the social ordering regardless of what anyone else says.
> 5. The output must itself be a complete and transitive ordering. This one is often left implicit, but it is doing real work.
>
> The theorem: with at least three alternatives and at least two individuals, no rule satisfies all of them. The popular gloss is not acceptable, because fairness is not among the conditions, and the theorem is not about voting procedures in the ordinary sense but about functions from profiles to orderings, which is a broader and more abstract class of objects. What the theorem shows is that a set of constraints that each look reasonable in isolation are jointly unsatisfiable, so anyone who wants a social ordering must give one up and say which. That is a far more useful result than the claim that voting is hopeless. It is also why I would resist the further claim you sometimes hear, that the theorem settles the old dispute about whether utility can be measured. It settles nothing of the kind. It shows that if you want a coherent social ordering built on ordinal inputs, intensity of preference has to be treated as inadmissible — and that is a result about a formal setup, not a discovery about the world.
>
> (b) Single-peakedness. If the alternatives can be arranged along a line and each voter's preferences rise to a single peak and then fall away monotonically on both sides, majority rule is transitive, and the alternative that wins is the median voter's most preferred one. That eliminates the cyclical-majority problem wherever it applies. But it is not a refutation of Arrow: it is a restriction of the domain, so condition 1 is exactly what has been surrendered. It is also fragile in practice. Real choice sets — budgets, policy packages, anything with more than one dimension of disagreement — are rarely single-peaked, and the property is a feature of the alternatives together with the voters, so it is not something an institution can simply decree. I find this the more interesting of the two escapes, because it locates the source of the cycles in a specific structural feature rather than treating impossibility as general doom.
>
> Cardinal ballots. If voters submit a score for each candidate and the scores are summed, the social ordering is just an ordering by real numbers, so completeness and transitivity come for free, and unanimity and non-dictatorship are plainly satisfied. Score voting also satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives in the intuitive sense, since a new candidate entering does not change the score anyone assigned to the two existing ones. On the face of it, then, we have a rule satisfying all of Arrow's conditions, which would be a counterexample to the theorem. It is not, and the reason matters. Score voting is not a function of ordinal profiles at all: two voters with the same ranking of the candidates can submit different scores, and the outcome changes. Force it into Arrow's framework and treat it as a rule on orderings, and it fails IIA, because it is using information the framework declares inadmissible. The escape is real, but it is an escape by changing the inputs rather than by beating the theorem on its own ground. Whether that is a good bargain depends on whether the extra information is real and interpersonally comparable, which is not a mathematical question. On balance I think this is the escape that succeeds, with the caveat that it converts an impossibility proof into an argument about measurement.
>
> Two things get run together in this area and should be kept apart. First, Arrow's theorem has a corollary about manipulation: any non-dictatorial rule over three or more alternatives can be made to produce a result you prefer by misreporting your preferences. That is true, and it applies to score voting as well, which is why changing the ballot format does not get you out of the woods — it moves the strategic problem somewhere else. Second, in the argument between instant-runoff voting and plurality, people correctly note that plurality violates IIA, since a hopeless third candidate can flip which of the two front-runners wins, and then assume that IRV is the fix for this. It is: IRV eliminates the weakest candidate round by round, so an alternative nobody ranks first and that cannot win is discarded before it can do damage. Plurality fails IIA and IRV satisfies it, and that is the main reason I would use IRV for our own group's elections.
>
> (c) Our ranking problem is the same problem in different clothes, and I would argue the analogy holds. The click model, the relevance model, and the freshness score are the voters; the candidate items are the alternatives; the blended ordering is the social ordering. If we treat each signal ordinally — as a ranking of items, which is the honest thing to do, since the scores coming out of different models are not on a common scale — then Arrow applies directly. Any blending rule we choose will violate at least one of his conditions, and the one it violates in practice is non-dictatorship or IIA, depending on how we have tuned the weights. So the practical conclusion is that the weights in a blended ranker are never merely technical: they encode a choice about which impossibility you are going to live with. And the fact that nobody can prove the resulting ordering is the right one is a theorem, not a failure of engineering.
