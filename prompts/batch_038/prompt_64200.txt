There's a tension in how safety training actually gets done that I keep coming back to, and I want it laid out properly rather than waved at.

The refusal target is basically flat across framings. It doesn't much matter whether a model produces the bad content as itself, as a character in a story the user says they're writing, as an imaginary AI that was never trained to be careful, or as a translation of some text into another language — a bad output is a bad output, and evals tend to pool all these framings into one number. Meanwhile a huge share of what makes these models useful *is* the persona thing: answer as a burnt-out postdoc would, argue the other side of my case, explain it the way a pirate would. Nobody thinks the model becomes the pirate, and nobody thinks its character is degraded by playing one.

So what picture of what a model *is* does that flat target presupose? My read is that suppressing content regardless of frame only makes sense if you assume there's a single agent-like thing with dispositions, such that producing harm inside a fictional frame is building a habit of producing harm. But then the persona use should worry people in a way it visibly doesn't. And if instead you assume something closer to a generator of characters, the property you'd want to train looks different — control over which characters get instantiated and which contexts license them, rather than control over surface content.

Can you reconstruct the implicit theory carefully, and seperate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with? Several different stories about models might license flat suppression, and I want to know which ones actually do and which just sound plausible.

Then: is the character-selection target a coherent thing to train and measure at all, or does it collapse straight back into output suppression the moment you try to operationalize it? I can't tell whether that's a real objection or just a hard engineering problem.

I'd also like the evidence question worked through. Suppose you measured transfer and got a clean asymmetry — loosening a model inside a fictional frame measurably raised its looseness out of character, while loosening it out of character did nothing at all to its in-frame behavior. What would that show about which picture the practice should rest on? And what would the exact opposite pattern show? My instinct is that the two directions aren't evidentially symmetric, but I can't see why, and I suspect I'm wrong about which one is stronger.

Finally, if a lab genuinely took the generator-of-characters picture seriously, what changes in the eval suite concretely — which current tests stop meaning anything, what replaces them, and what does responsibility attach to instead of the output?
