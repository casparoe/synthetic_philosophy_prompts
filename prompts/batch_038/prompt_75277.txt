I'm six months into a novel and there's one scene I keep fiddling with. My narrator's sister — call her Ines — runs the values side of things at a lab somewhere off the A14, and she gets interviewed for a weekend magazine long-read. I wrote the journalist's summary of what the lab is up to, working from what I could dig out of a couple of popular books, and now I cant tell whether it's the sort of thing a real researcher would nod along to or wince at. Here it is, as it appears in the piece she's reading:

"The idea, broadly, is that the people building these systems have accepted they can't agree about what's right, so rather than teach the machine a moral theory they teach it a procedure for thinking. You load it with a description of idealised deliberation — a version of us that knows more, thinks faster and has had the emotions and tribal loyalties stripped out — and you tell it to do whatever that version of humanity would sign off on once it's finished. In the best-known version, called coherent extrapolated volition, the machine works out what we'd want after a thousand years of reflection and then does that; some people picture it as running a simulated conference of a million improved humans and taking whatever they settle on. The appeal is obvious: nobody has to win an ethical argument first, because the machine wins them all for us, and if our better selves turn out to be utilitarians, so be it. Which is also the fatal flaw. Nothing guarantees that the idealised version of us will agree with the actual version — it may decide we're wrong about almost everything, in which case we've built something answerable to nobody — and a thousand years of reflection may never produce a single verdict at all. So indirect normativity doesn't solve the problem of what to value. It postpones it until it's too late to do anything about it."

So: go through that more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd call a fair simplification, and what's just wrong. In particular I want to know whether "nobody has to win an ethical argument first" and "the machine wins them all for us" are things a defender of the view would happily say, or whether they're close to the opposite of the point — and whether the stripping-out-the-emotions picture and the thousand-years picture are metaphors I can keep or whether they're doing real damage. Then: does his ending follow from the bits he got right? He treats non-convergence and the gap between idealised-us and actual-us as knockdown objections, and I don't know if proponents have an answer to those or whether they just eat them.

Second thing, which is what the scene actually turns on. Ines's design has a clause I made up: the system may only act on the verdict of the deliberation if that verdict is one present-day humans wouldn't find monstrous, and if it is, it goes back to thinking while doing roughly what everyone currently agrees is fine. My instinct is that this is definately a sensible safety valve, but a friend who reads a lot in this area said it "collapses the whole thing back into direct specification", which didn't make sense to me at the time. Is he right? Does a veto like that turn indirect normativity into something else, or is it the sort of thing a careful proponent would already want?

Third, and shorter: if Ines has to believe one of the errors in that summary — has to be wrong in a way that costs her something in the book — which one is most interesting to be wrong about, and which one would an expert reader of the novel be most likely to forgive? I'd rather the mistake be a genuine fork in the ideas than a strawman I've invented.

Bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't repeat my question back at me.
