I co-facilitate a monthly philosophy-of-psychiatry reading group whose membership is split roughly evenly between philosophy graduate students and practicing clinicians. For three years the same argument has recurred whenever Szasz's case against mental illness comes up, and it has never once moved anyone. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself — not a survey of the positions, and not an adjudication of who is right.

Here is how the exchange reliably goes. The clinicians say that Szasz's criterion — that disease requires a deviation from the structural or functional norm of bodily organs, ideally a demonstrable lesion — is a stipulation smuggled in as though it were a discovery; that plenty of accepted medical diagnoses (migraine, irritable bowel syndrome, essential hypertension, and much of what internal medicine manages syndromally) were held confidently without identified pathology; that his argument therefore proves far too much; and that it erases the difference between no lesion has been found and no lesion is possible. The philosophers answer that categories ratified by committee around clusters of distressing or socially disruptive behavior are value-laden all the way down, so that calling them diseases moves a moral and legal question — what a person is answerable for, and when the state may intervene — into a technical register that conceals it. Then, near the end of every session, someone observes that Szasz argues at one point the strong thesis that there is no such thing as mental illness and at another the weak thesis that the phrase is merely a metaphor for problems of conduct and living, and the two camps spend the remaining hour attacking and defending different versions of the same slogan.

My working hypothesis is that this is largely a verbal dispute over the word illness, with a substantive normative dispute about coercion running underneath it that neither side is prepared to name as the real issue. Treat that as a hypothesis to be tested, not as a premise.

Specifically, I would like you to: separate the claims actually bundled into the thesis and say how many distinct theses there are; then, for each, identify what kind of disagreement the group is having — about the meaning of a term, about which criteria a concept ought to be revised to meet, about a matter of fact, or about what counts as a reason for intervention — and whether the two camps are addressing the same one. Say which strand is load-bearing, meaning which one, if conceded, would take the rest down with it. Then assess whether anything empirical could break the deadlock: what a biomarker finding would actually establish against the conceptual strand, and what it would leave untouched. Finally, state what would count as progress here — a distinction, a stipulation made explicit, a reframed question, evidence — or argue that none should be expected and explain why. Please do not recommend readings, and do not attempt to settle whether mental illness is real.
