I run the personalization review group at a mid-size consumer software company: eight people, two of whom are engineers, and one engineer who can actually read a training loop rather than a model card. Every feature that claims to adapt itself to an individual user comes to us before launch — feeds, adaptive notification scheduling, wellness nudges, the routines in our smart-home app — and we handle roughly fourteen submissions a year, with a two-week window per review. The claim in almost every deck is the same sentence in different clothes: the system learns what each user wants from how that user actually behaves, so what it serves reflects user preference rather than our product team's guesses. I have taken to writing that down as a single fixed target and calling it the preferential-fidelity claim, and my problem is that our group has no disciplined way to decide when behavioral data licenses it and when it does not. We argue from intuition for three hours, the most confident voice wins, and the verdict I sign goes to a vice president who wants a two-line rationale she can repeat to legal.

What I want is a procedure my team can run in order, not an essay about the literature: the questions to ask, sequenced, with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each given the evidence we actually have (interaction logs, an onboarding questionnaire, occasional A/B tests, a model card, and one engineer who can be borrowed for a day). It should say where the procedure terminates in a clear, where in a hard fail, where in "insufficient evidence, and here is the cheapest experiment that would settle it," and where it stops being a factual question and hands the decision up to a person because it concerns what the company owes users. I want it grounded in whatever is actually established about inferring a reward from behavior, translated for a team that has not read any of it: that a reward recovered from behavior is only pinned down up to transformations that leave the behavior unchanged, so the object the model contains is not automatically the object the deck talks about; that an inferred reward describes whatever policy generated the data, and nobody's endorsement of it follows from that; the well-known systematic divergences between what people do and what they would avow — immediacy and effort costs, defaults, friction, option sets the interface itself fixes, incentives attached to the act of logging; and the loop that worries me most, that our system shapes the behavior it later infers from, so part of the training data is manufactured by the artifact under audit. Please do not let "just ask the user" function as an escape hatch: the procedure must say what to do when enacted and stated preference conflict, and why stating a preference is not the same as having ground truth. It must also say what to do at every step where no number and no counterfactual is available. I do not want a reading list, a bibliography, or named citations — I want the working procedure and the reasoning it depends on, in terms my analysts can use without further reading.

Then run it on three submissions. First: a short-video feed whose reward model is trained on completion and immediate replay, where watch time is up eleven percent, a quarterly survey shows a plurality of users saying they would like to watch less than they do, and the only in-product counterweight is an occasional "take a break" interstitial with a dismiss button. Second: a diabetes-management app that infers meal preferences from what users log and pre-orders groceries accordingly, where log completion is very high but logging is reinforced by a streak counter and tied to an insurer rebate. Third: a workplace focus tool configured once at onboarding, in which the user picks hours during which distracting sites are blocked, with an override that is deliberately slow and makes the user retype a sentence about why they want out. Be prepared for the procedure to embarrass me: I have blocked the first feature twice and I personally lobbied for the third, so if the procedure honestly clears the feed or fails the self-binding tool, say so plainly and show which step produced that result, rather than softening the verdict or burying it in caveats. Finally, give me an honest audit of your own audit — which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, the modes in which the procedure will issue a confident false clear, the questions it can never settle from behavioral data no matter how much we collect, and what the whole exercise presupposes about preferences being the kind of thing a model could recover at all, which a skeptic would deny.
