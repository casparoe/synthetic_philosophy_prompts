I am running a 110-minute session for eighteen students in a final-year undergraduate seminar on historical explanation. They have had no prior coursework in probability or in judgment and decision-making, and they will have read nothing for this class. I want a self-contained simulation that makes them feel, rather than be told, why the outside view is so hard to adopt when one is holding a rich and vivid particular case in one's hands, and why it is nonetheless what ought to govern the number one writes down. Please design the activity in full, to the specification below.

The setting should be invented, so that no student can substitute remembered facts: a fictional basin containing some thirty polities over roughly four centuries, each with a recorded lifespan, plus dossiers on a handful of them. You should generate the materials yourself rather than sketch them in outline. I want the following built into the data.

1. Real but weak signal. The dossiers should contain features that genuinely correlate with durability in the table of lifespans you supply, but modestly: a forecaster who reads a dossier well should beat the bare base rate by a few years, against a spread of recorded lifespans running from a decade or two to well over a century. Students must be able to discover in the debrief that the case detail felt enormously informative and was only mildly so.
2. Reference-class ambiguity. At least two defensible reference classes for the target polity — say, all polities of comparable territorial scale, versus all polities sharing its constitutional arrangement — whose base rates differ by decades. Choosing the class must itself be a judgment the teams make under time pressure.
3. One exceptionalism hook. At least one dossier should be so compelling — a reforming ruler, a settled succession, an army that has never been beaten — that a reasonable reader is tempted to remove the polity from every class at all.
4. Right-censoring. A few polities should still be standing when the record closes, so that a naive average of recorded lifespans is systematically biased, and a team that averages the numbers on the sheet without thinking inherits that bias. Do not flag this anywhere in the student-facing materials; I want it to emerge, if it emerges, from the sharpest team or from the debrief.

Mechanics: teams of three. In round one each team forecasts the remaining lifespan of a single polity from its dossier alone, recording a point estimate plus a 50 percent and a 90 percent interval. Between rounds teams may spend a limited allotment of archive tokens to purchase the recorded lifespans of comparable cases, choosing for themselves which comparisons to buy. In round two they revise. The token scarcity is the point: the outside view should cost something, and teams should have to commit, before they know how much they need it, to how much they will pay. Give me a scoring scheme that runs on paper points, penalizes both error and intervals that are too narrow, and is simple enough that I can compute it in front of the class in five minutes. Include the exact handout text for each dossier (compressed to a page is fine), the token prices, a minute-by-minute timeline, and what I say — and what I pointedly refrain from saying — while it runs. The vocabulary of the domain should not be spoken aloud until after round two.

Then the debrief. It should map specific moments in the play onto the ideas at work: the drift between the round one and round two numbers as a measure of how far the inside view actually moved them; the interval scores as a measure of how confident they were entitled to be; the token purchases as reference-class selection; the exceptionalism hook and who fell for it; and the censoring problem, which I want explained properly, including what those teams should have done with the unfinished records. The names may be attached only here — the planning fallacy, reference class forecasting, regression toward the mean.

Finally, two frank notes. First: what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run — teams finishing at wildly different speeds, one team spotting the censoring issue and broadcasting it, students gaming the intervals, the hook dossier provoking an argument that eats the clock — and what I should do about each. Second: which feature of reasoning from base rates this simulation cannot reproduce, and which I will therefore have to supply by argument afterwards. I suspect it has to do with the fact that my table of lifespans is settled and uncontested, whereas in real forecasting the reference class is itself a thing people fight over; tell me whether that is the largest gap or whether there is a worse one I have not thought of.
