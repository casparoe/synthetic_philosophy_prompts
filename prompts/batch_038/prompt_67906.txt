Consider the following stylized version of the scale argument for taking insect suffering seriously, in which the quantities are treated as independent parameters:

- Let N be the number of relevant animals alive at a given time. For the sake of the schema, suppose N is at least a billion times the number of human beings.
- Let p be the probability that any given such animal is sentient.
- Let w be the moral weight of that animal's suffering, conditional on its being sentient.

The expected moral significance of insect suffering is then the product N × p × w, and the argument's force comes from the observation that because N is so large, the product remains decisively large even when p is very small — so that near-agnosticism about insect sentience should not license near-indifference to it.

I would like you to explain a conceptual difficulty with this schema, one that concerns the presumed independence of its three factors rather than the accuracy of any of their values.

The difficulty, stated roughly, is this. Neither N nor p nor w is an intrinsic property of the world; each is a function of a prior choice about how to carve the relevant population and about what counts as evidence. Widen the carving — include every small arthropod with a nervous system of any kind — and N grows enormously while p and w shrink. Narrow the carving to animals possessing whatever integrative, learning, or nociceptive-modulatory machinery one takes to be the basis of affective experience, and p and w rise while N collapses. The schema presents itself as neutral between these carvings, on the grounds that a sufficiently large N compensates for a sufficiently small p. But it is not obvious that compensation holds, because the very features that make p small in the wide carving are the features that make w small in it as well: if phenomenology is graded rather than threshold-like, and if its grade tracks the same neurobiological properties that ground our estimate of p, then the low-probability region of our uncertainty is not a region of high-but-unlikely intensity. In that case the "tiny p, gigantic N" move trades on a picture in which probability of sentience and intensity of sentience can come apart freely, and that picture may be exactly what the evidence does not support.

Please explain this difficulty properly. In particular, I would like you to address:

1. Why, precisely, the three factors fail to be independent, and what the dependence is a dependence on. Distinguish the case where p is small because the machinery is rudimentary from the case where p is small because we simply do not know, and explain whether the argument is entitled to both at once.
2. Whether the correlation between p and w strengthens or weakens the conclusion, and why the answer is not settled in advance.
3. How this difficulty differs from the banal observation that our estimates have error bars, and from the generic reference-class problem in statistical inference. I want the version of the worry that is specific to expected-value arguments over vast populations of possibly-sentient beings, not a version that would apply to any uncertain quantity.
4. What the argument looks like once reformulated — presumably as an integral over a joint distribution of phenomenal intensity and moral weight, with N itself a function of the carving — and whether the reformulated version still delivers the practical upshot the original was meant to deliver.
5. Whether an analogous structural worry applies to expected-value arguments generally, where a minute probability is multiplied by an enormous stake.

Please work through at least one concrete pair of contrasting carvings of the population, with rough illustrative magnitudes, rather than staying at the level of abstraction. You need not cite any literature or supply references; what I want is the conceptual structure spelled out and defended.
