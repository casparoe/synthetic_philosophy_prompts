I'm preparing the final for an upper-level seminar I'm teaching this term, cross-listed between our philosophy department and the history of science program, on Victorian attempts to put the mind's unobservable contents on an evidential footing — the students have read James's *Principles* selections, some of Myers, Janet on automatism, and a good deal of Sidgwick, including the closing chapters of *The Methods of Ethics*. It's a three-hour sit-down with two questions, and I want you to draft one of them: an essay question worth 25 marks, designed to be written in roughly 75 minutes, on Sidgwick's involvement with psychical research. I should say up front that last year I set a version of this question badly — I asked students to discuss Sidgwick's agnosticism, and eleven of the fourteen produced a competent précis of a man who doubted revealed religion while remaining an earnest investigator of table-rapping and apparitions, and I could not in conscience separate most of them by more than a few marks. The question you write must not be answerable by narrating that tension, nor by listing the Society for Psychical Research's founding, its committees, and its famous members.

The crux I want the question to force is the gap between two things Sidgwick is often said to have wanted from psychical research and conflated by students who have only read secondary summaries. On the one hand there is the methodological problem the Society actually addressed in the 1894 *Report on the Census of Hallucinations*, of which Sidgwick was the lead author: a nationwide canvass in which some 410 interviewers put the same question to roughly 17,000 people, found that about one respondent in ten could recall at least one vivid hallucinatory experience, isolated 350 first-hand cases in which an apparition of a recognisable person coincided with that person's death within twelve hours, and concluded that this rate stood roughly 440 times above what chance would predict, entitling the committee to assert that "between deaths and apparitions of the dying person a connexion exists which is not due to chance alone." On the other hand there is the ethical problem that report can do nothing for: the dualism of practical reason, which Sidgwick thought could be closed only by postulating a moral government of the universe that guarantees the ultimate coincidence of duty and happiness. A first-class answer has to see that the Census conclusion is at most a claim about a *connexion* — compatible with telepathy from a dying but still living person, and with a host of non-survival explanations — that even a conclusive demonstration of post-mortem survival would establish persistence and not cosmic justice, and that a statistical excess over a chance baseline is a very different kind of warrant from the one Sidgwick's ethical system required. Please build the question so that it hands the students the figures they need in a short stem rather than testing recall of them, and so that it invites a normative assessment as well as a historical one — something along the lines of whether Sidgwick's evidential standards were too permissive, too demanding, or simply aimed at the wrong target.

What I need back from you, as a single self-contained package: the question as it would appear on the paper, with its stem; a model answer of about 700 words written at the level of a strong undergraduate who has done the reading but has nothing to consult in the exam room; and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves the answer must make and says what each is worth, so that I can defend a borderline grade to a moderator. I'd like the scheme to state explicitly which moves are necessary but not sufficient for a first, and to list four or five characteristic errors that should cost marks even in an otherwise fluent essay — for instance, treating the dualism as a simple stand-off between egoism and utilitarianism, reading the Census as evidence for survival rather than for a connexion, or assuming that a 440-fold excess over chance settles the metaphysics without asking what the baseline assumed about the independence of deaths and reports, or about how canvassed respondents were selected. If you think the question would be sharpened by attaching a short invented document — a paragraph of fabricated minutes from a hypothetical SPR subcommittee weighing a borderline coincidence case — please write that too, and make sure the marking scheme rewards using it. Do not include a bibliography or expect the students to cite anything; the question must be answerable from the material in the stem plus ordinary seminar knowledge.
