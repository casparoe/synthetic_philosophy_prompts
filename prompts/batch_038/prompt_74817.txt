I'm putting together a problem sheet for my third-year ethics seminar on the moral dilemmas debate — the one about whether genuine dilemmas are even possible, or whether every apparent case can be defused by denying that both requirements are really obligations. Every example the students have met so far (Sartre's student, the borrowed weapon in the Republic, Agamemnon, the usual hospital ones) gets dissolved in about five minutes by one of three stock moves, and I want a case that can't be. So I need you to build one to spec and then show me it meets the spec.

Four conditions, all of them. These aren't flavour, they're load-bearing — each one is there to block an escape route:

1. Asymmetric. No Sophie's-Choice structure, no two identical claims pulling in opposite directions. Make the requirements different in kind — something like a role or promise obligation on one side, preventing serious harm on the other — and let's stipulate that the agent judges, correctly, that one is weightier than the other. The interesting bit is that the dilemma has to survive that judgement: the lesser obligation mustn't just evaporate once you've ranked them.

2. Not self-inflicted. Nothing the agent did earlier put her in this position. No negligence, no rash undertaking, no failure to foresee what any reasonable person would have foreseen. I want to cut off the move where someone says the real obligation was not to be here in the first place.

3. No third way out, and no override. She's been diligant, she's looked for alternatives, and there's no available act that discharges both. Seperately, the principle that ought implies can has to hold for each obligation on its own: each act is one she can perform, its doing both that's impossible.

4. Mundane, contemporary, and one paragraph long. A small firm, a hospice, a housing office, a family — your pick. No trolleys, no lifeboats, no pandemic allocation committees, nothing science-fictional. Stakes a recognisable person could actually face, and the setting specific enough that the students can't wave it away as underspecified.

Then the working. Set out what someone who thinks genuine dilemmas are impossible does with your case — which of the four conditions they attack, and what they're left saying about the moral residue afterwards, the apology or reparation or agent-regret that seems to survive the choice. Then set out the reply from the other side. And tell me which of my four constraints is doing most of the work in actually generating the dilemma rather than just describing a hard choice, and walk through what happens to the case if I delete each of the other three in turn: does it collapse, and collapse into what?

Two more things, and this is the part I care about most. Be honest about whether you've succeeded. If your case smuggles something in — an agent who is quietly at fault after all, a requirement that stops looking like a genuine obligation once you spell out the circumstances, a third option hiding in the background detail — flag it yourself before my seminar does. And tell me what would change your mind about whether a case of this shape can be built at all: which argument or consideration would get you to concede the spec is unsatisfiable rather than merely hard, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail. If you think condition 2 can't be met without quietly giving up condition 1, say so, and say what the closest achievable case looks like instead.

British English throughout, please. Assume the students have done a term of normative theory but haven't touched the dilemmas literature, so gloss any technical term on first use and don't lean on any particular philosopher's vocabulary.
