The topic is coherence arguments for expected utility maximization: Dutch books, money pumps, the various representation theorems, the whole "if your credences and preferences aren't coherent you're a pumpable idiot" genre. Plus the standard reply from our side of the internet, which is roughly that those theorems are vacuous, because literally any behavior whatsoever can be rationalized by some probability/utility pair if you're willing to make the utility function baroque enough.

Here's the draft. Sections 1 and 2 are mostly written, 3 and 4 are notes, 5 is a list of things I'm worried about.

---

**Coherence theorems are about compression, not about rationality**

**1.** The genre. Take an agent, observe its choices over gambles/acts. The theorems say: if the choices satisfy axioms A1...An (completeness, transitivity, some independence/sure-thing condition, continuity-ish stuff), then there's a probability function and a utility function such that the agent is choosing as if maximizing expected utility. Flip it around and you get the polemic version: violate the axioms and you're money-pumpable or Dutch-bookable, so incoherence costs you money, so incoherence is irrational.

The well-known deflation. The theorems are existence claims about an interpretation, and the interpretation is unconstrained. You can always rationalize a choice function by defining utility directly over the things chosen. So the theorems never rule any behavior out, and the normative punch ("you'd lose money!") is doing work the mathematics isn't. People usually conclude the whole genre is decorative and move on.

**2.** My move: the deflation assumes interpretation is free, and it isn't. Any behavior is rationalizable by *some* pair, but the pairs differ wildly in how long they take to write down, and the vacuity objection silently lets you spend unlimited description length. So define, for a given pattern of behavior B, the interpretive cost: roughly, the size of the shortest description of a credence/utility pair that rationalizes B, plus the cost of searching for it. Now the coherence theorems become a different claim — not "incoherent agents get exploited" but "axiom violations buy you descriptive slack that you have to repay in a more baroque interpretation."

Examples of the repayment:
- An agent whose preferences flip with framing needs a utility function with a frame argument, or one indexed to the presentation of the problem rather than its content.
- An agent that's ambiguity-averse can't be captured by a single probability plus utility over outcomes, since that's exactly what the independence/sure-thing condition forbids. The interpreter has to reach for something heavier: utility whose arguments include acts rather than just outcomes, a set of priors, second-order probabilities.
- An agent with cyclic preferences needs either a utility function over sequences, or the claim that it's really several agents taking turns.

On this reading "coherent" stops meaning "rational" and starts meaning "admits a short p,u interpretation." That's a property of the modeler's situation, not of the agent's reasons. Which suggests both sides of the old fight have been talking past each other: the deflationist is right that nothing is ruled out, and the decision theorist is right that the axioms buy you something — the something is compressibility, which is an epistemic/predictive virtue, not a normative one.

**3.** (Notes.) This leaves a gap: why should an agent care about being compressible? Need a bridge from "your behavior has a short interpretation" to any reason to act. Candidate bridges: (a) self-knowledge — a short interpretation is one you can use on yourself, i.e. to notice you're doing something; (b) coordination — other agents can only cooperate with you if they can model you; (c) adversarial — see 4. Bridge (a) feels weakest and I might cut it.

**4.** (Notes, and this is the part I think might be the actual point.) Adversarial framing. Incoherence is only exploitable if the exploiter can *find* the cycle, and finding it costs description length too. So incoherence is partly obscurity, and obscurity has defensive value against other bounded agents who model you in order to trade with you or pump you. If that's right, the conclusion is embarrassing for the rationalist moral: a bounded agent surrounded by bounded adversaries may have instrumental reasons to be less cleanly modelable — to look ambiguous, to keep preferences slightly intransitive in domains where the cycles are hard to locate, to be unpredictable in exactly the way coherence theorems call irrational. Coherence becomes something you want to be seen as having rather than something you want to have, which is a commitment/threat story rather than an accuracy story.

**5.** Worries, in rough order of severity:
(i) Is interpretive cost just the vacuity objection with new paint? Does section 2 predict anything, or could it be made to fit any behavior after the fact — in which case I've committed the sin I'm accusing the deflationists of.
(ii) Section 2 smells strongly of the intentional stance: interpret the system as rational because that's the shortest predictive strategy, and treat departures as noise. If that's what it is, I've reinvented something from the 70s/80s and dressed it up.
(iii) Does section 4 produce a norm or only contingent instrumental reasons? If the only reasons to be coherent come from there being exploiters around, then coherence drops out entirely for an isolated agent, and I'm not sure I want to accept that.
(iv) I'm hand-waving about which theorem I'm leaning on. Savage-flavored setups and vNM-flavored setups don't fail in the same places, and "the interpreter reaches for a heavier representation" means different things in each. I don't want to write section 3 until I know whether the examples in section 2 survive that.
(v) Is "compression" the right word, or does it invite people to think I mean Shannon coding of choice data, which I don't?
