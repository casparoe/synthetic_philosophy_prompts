**Borrowed Eyes: why a 4.2 star rating isn't really testimony**

*1.* The old puzzle goes like this. If a friend tells me the last train leaves at six, I can come to know it on her word and repeat it later without any weirdness. But if she tells me the Matisse in the next gallery is beautiful, and I've never laid eyes on it, then me walking around saying the Matisse is beautiful feels like a small fraud. Kant set this up in the third Critique: a hundred voices praising a building won't force your inner agreement, and the fact that something pleased other people can't serve as the ground of an aesthetic judgement. Hume pulled the other way in his 1757 essay on the standard of taste — there are true judges, people with delicacy, practice, comparison, good sense and freedom from prejudice, and their joint verdict is the standard. Current philosophy inherits that split as pessimists versus optimists about aesthetic testimony. Hopkins mapped the terrain and then argued you can be a pessimist without being a sceptic about the evidence: the trouble isn't that aesthetic testimony is unreliable, it's something about how the belief is based and what you can do with it once you have it. Meskin and Robson push back from the optimist side. A third move says what you lose by deferring isn't knowledge but understanding. Underneath all of it sits what's usually called the acquaintance principle — traced to Wollheim, and behind him to Kant — saying judgements of aesthetic value have to rest on first-hand experience of the thing.

*2.* My problem with the whole framing is that it assumes a shape: one person, one work, one assertion, and you deciding whether to assent to it. That's the shape of a friend at a party or a named critic in a magazine. It is not the shape of how most verdicts about art now reach most people.

*3.* What reaches them instead is aggregate: star averages, percentage scores, ranked feeds, "because you watched this, here's that". Call it statistical testimony, or better, taste infrastructure. Two things distinguish it from the party case. First, it doesn't invite belief. A 4.2 isn't asking me to hold that the film is beautiful; it's determining whether I ever encounter the film at all. It operates on attention, not assent. Second, there's no testifier to evaluate. Nobody's track record to weigh, no delicacy to assess, no sincerity to suspect — just a distribution over anonymous raters, plus an engineer's ranking function. So the Humean true-judge model can't get a grip even in principle: there's no critic there, only a crowd whose composition I can't inspect.

*4.* Which suggests both sides of the debate are arguing over a channel that carries a shrinking share of the traffic. The acquaintance principle tells me I can't form the judgement second-hand. The infrastructure never asks me to form a judgement — it decides, beforehand and seperately, what will be available for me to judge first-hand. That looks like a bigger deal for aesthetic life than the deference question, because it doesn't corrupt my verdicts, it curates the pool of things I'll ever be in a position to have verdicts about. And perversely it might make me an optimist: if aggregate signals only steer exposure, deferring to them costs nothing the pessimist should care about. I still meet the work with my own eyes. I just didn't choose where to point them.

*5.* Objection I can already hear: the leak is total. I watch the beloved film knowing it's beloved, and that primes me, so the belief channel was never bypassed, it was just anonymised — and then my distinction collapses back into the old one and this essay is relabelling. Related: is there really no testifier in a recommender, given the raters, the labels, the engineers? Maybe the honest description is testimony from an enormous and badly behaved committee.

*6.* Where I want to land is a question rather than a thesis: whether first-hand experience is still the right ideal when the first-hand experience was arranged in advance by the aggregate.
