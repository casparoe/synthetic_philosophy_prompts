Our Thursday philosophy society meets in the back room of a pub, and last week someone brought up the old dust-specks thought experiment again — one person tortured for fifty years versus some astronomically large number of people who each get a single speck of dust in the eye for a few seconds. We'd all seen the setup before so I won't belabor it, but two of the regulars, Nadia and Theo, went at it for the better part of an hour and the rest of us mostly just ate crisps and watched. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Nadia (1): the torture is worse and no number of specks ever catches up, because a speck isn't a harm at all — it sits below whatever threshold makes something start counting as bad. Theo (2): that's just the conclusion restated in different words; if a speck is bad by even a tiny amount then arithmetic takes over and enough of them beat anything, and anyway her view can't explain why she's perfectly happy to trade a mild headache off against a bad one without invoking any threshold. Nadia (3): fine, there's a cutoff and she can't say exactly where it falls, but that's no worse than not being able to say exactly how many hairs makes someone bald — the view is imprecise, not arbitrary. Then she offered what she took to be a real criterion: what makes a harm count is whether it's the kind of thing that can shape a life, something you can resent, remember, build around, lose sleep over, and a speck can't do any of that. Theo (4): two problems. Vagueness is tolerable when nothing rides on where the boundary sits, but here crossing it means a googolplex of misery stops mattering completely, and a predicate can't be that load-bearing and that fuzzy at the same time. And run her shape-a-life test on an immortal who gets one speck a second forever: by her lights that life isn't made any worse, which is obviously false, since a life of unbroken low-grade irritation is worse than a life containing a handful of genuine joys. Nadia (5): she took the hit on the immortal, but said it supports her rather than refuting her — specks do pile up inside a single life, what they don't do is pool across lives; you can't gather a googolplex people's specks into one lump of badness and weigh it against one person's torture, and that's the same mistake the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism is about. Theo (6): said she'd changed the subject, because the original question wasn't about distributing anything, it was about which of two states of the world is worse, and even a non-consequentialist still needs to know whether a speck is a harm at all; and her within/across split commits her to saying that a googol of people each tortured for a day is no worse than one person tortured for a googolplex of days, which she won't accept either. At that point someone ordered another round and the thing dissolved.

I've been mulling it over for a week and I honestly can't tell who was ahead at any given point — I think I changed my mind about every third move while reading it back. Could you go through the six numbered moves and give a verdict on each one, on a scale from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per move rather than a general impression? Then tell me which specific points Nadia actually concedes or loses, which of Theo's attacks fail to land and why, and — this is the bit I care about most — what the two of them are jointly assuming that makes the whole argument feel unresolvable from inside their framing. If there's a third position that would have broken the stalemate, sketch it. Don't bother with references or a bibliography, this is just for my own notes, not something I'm submitting anywhere.
