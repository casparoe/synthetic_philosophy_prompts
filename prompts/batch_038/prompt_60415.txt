I am a novelist, and in six weeks I give a twenty-five-minute paper with twenty minutes of questions at a two-day interdisciplinary conference run by a university philosophy department on the theme of fiction and the future. I have never presented to philosophers. I have spent four months reading on future contingents, which means I have the vocabulary but not the reflexes: I can hold a line I have prepared, but I cannot invent a distinction at a podium, and I will not recognize a technical term being used as a weapon until it has landed.

The paper is about my new novel, and it defends four claims.

1. The book is not one determinate story plus a reader's ignorance of it. What is unsettled is which events the fiction makes true; the gaps are in the fictional world, not in the reader's head.
2. The commitment is carried by form, not by content. No sentence in the book asserts indeterminacy, any more than a sonnet's turn has to announce itself. The duplication does the work.
3. Two features are load-bearing: there is no instruction about reading order, and there is no coda reconciling the versions. Either one would install a privileged branch and turn the book into a story about a future that is merely unknown.
4. Reading it under those conditions gives the reader something an argument cannot: anticipatory states directed at nothing determinate. I want to call that weak evidence for openness — the felt version of the puzzle Aristotle poses with the sea battle in De Interpretatione 9, which I intend to name in my second minute precisely so that nobody thinks my book is an illustration of it.

The facts about the book that matter. It is set in an archipelago during an unnamed war. At the end of Part Two an admiral must decide whether to send his squadron out to engage at dawn. The volume then contains two sections, both headed Part Three, set in different types and bound one after the other with no foreword, no note, and no third section: in one the squadron sails and loses a ship; in the other it stays in harbour and the town is taken. The last paragraph of both versions is word for word identical — ordinary domestic description, the same sentence in the same place on each page. I know what is wrong with that, and I know three other things about myself that are worse, so please use them rather than spare me: both Part Threes are physically printed, so every sentence already exists; I drafted the harbour version first, months before the other; and in a magazine interview last year, talking about the sequel I have contracted to write, I referred to the fate of one of the two squadrons as what happened to my characters.

The room will be about fifty people. The two designated respondents are, first, a metaphysician who works on future contingents and holds that there is a single determinate future, and who is separately interested in what deliberation presupposes; and second, a narratologist who believes form cannot carry metaphysical commitments and who knows the entire history of branching and game-like fiction, Borges onward, better than I do. In the audience: a supervaluationist, a philosopher of language who works on fictional truth and pretense, several metaphysics graduate students, a few novelists, and a general public who came for the author.

What I want from you is a question-period preparation sheet and nothing else. No reading list, no citations, no titles of works I ought to have read — by then I will have no time for any of it. I want sentences I can say out loud. Give me the eight to ten objections this particular room will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, with likelihood marked separately; for each, phrase it first-person in that person's own idiom, including the politeness with the knife in it, and give me the glosses I need for any term they will assume I know. Where an objector's own position does not actually license the objection they are making, say so plainly — that is my best opening and I will not spot it live.

Return a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "the_one_thing_that_must_land": string, two or three sentences, the single reply without which the paper fails.
- "audience_map": array of objects with "seat" (respondent one, respondent two, supervaluationist, philosopher of language, graduate students, novelists, general public), "what_they_come_for", and "how_they_open" (the first clause of the question they will actually ask).
- "objections": array, most damaging first, each an object with "rank" (integer); "targets" (which of my four claims, or "the book's facts"); "raised_by" (seat); "speaking_from" (the position they argue from, in a few words); "likelihood" ("near-certain" | "likely" | "possible"); "damage" ("fatal-if-fumbled" | "serious" | "manageable" | "irritant"); "as_they_would_ask_it" (first person, their idiom, about sixty words); "terms_i_need" (array of objects with "term" and "gloss", one line each, saying what it means and what they will expect me to know about it); "reply" (about one hundred and twenty words, speakable aloud); "concede" (what I give up at once); "hold" (the one thing I must not surrender); "soft_spot" (honestly, where my reply is weakest); "if_they_push" (the second exchange, two or three sentences); "their_view_licenses_it" (boolean plus one clause of explanation); "invite_it_early" (boolean, whether I should raise it myself in the talk and turn it).
- "the_question_i_hope_nobody_asks": object with "how_it_would_come", "why_it_could_end_the_paper", "the_honest_answer", "what_i_say_instead".
- "my_sketch_is_wrong_here": array of objects with "claim", "problem", "repair" — the places where my four claims are inconsistent with one another or with the facts I have told you about the book.
- "do_not_say": array of objects with "phrase" and "why" — philosophical words I should keep out of my mouth because I will be asked to cash them out and cannot.
- "sequencing": array of three to five strings, the order in which to take questions if several arrive at once and how to close the twenty minutes.
