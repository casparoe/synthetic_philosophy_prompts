For four years I have chaired what our faculty senate calls the Committee on Collegial Grievances, and I am writing to ask for help turning a piece of moral philosophy I teach into an instrument my committee can actually use.

The committee hears between ten and fourteen cases a year. It comprises two faculty drawn from outside the complainant's department, one doctoral student, one member of the professional staff, and myself. No one but I has read Smith, and I do not propose to assign him; whatever I give them must be workable from a two-page sheet. We have ninety minutes per case, take no testimony under oath, and possess no fact-finding powers. We issue a written finding — a recommendation, not a verdict — which goes to the dean, who may accept, modify, or ignore it. There is no appeal, and the recommendation's principal effect is reputational, which is why the senate reviews our findings for consistency each spring.

The cases are of a kind: a doctoral candidate who says her supervisor stopped reading her work; two co-authors who cannot agree who did what; a lecturer who believes a colleague has quietly undermined her teaching assignments; a staff member who says a professor's manner has made the office untenable for her. In every one, the question the committee must answer is not, as the parties suppose, what happened, but whether the sentiments reported are such as a spectator appropriately informed and appropriately situated could enter into, and to what degree — precisely the question Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator is built to answer. What I lack is the translation.

Please write that procedure. I have four requirements, and I would rather see them met than see the whole thing elegant.

First, give the steps in the order the committee should take them, with the question to be asked at each, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what terminates the inquiry there rather than carrying it forward. Ninety minutes is the binding constraint; a procedure needing three sittings is not a procedure I can use.

Second, tell me which of Smith's distinctions must survive the simplification and which may be dropped without loss for a lay audience. My own view, which I want tested rather than confirmed: the separation of the propriety of a sentiment from the merit or demerit of the conduct that provokes it is indispensable and must appear explicitly; the claim that concord is reached from two directions at once — the spectator raising his emotion toward the sufferer's pitch, the sufferer lowering his toward the spectator's — is the most useful thing Smith gives a committee, because it explains why we hear both parties before forming any view; the treatment of custom and fashion can be reduced to a cautionary step; and self-command must not be named to the committee at all, only built into how we ask people to state a complaint. Where am I wrong, and what am I about to lose by these economies?

Third, mark every point at which the procedure runs out and hands the matter to judgment, and do not let the phrase impartial spectator absorb the handoff. In particular, resolve three difficulties I have met and not solved. (a) The members do not converge: one enters readily into a doctoral student's humiliation, another cannot enter into it at all, and since Smith's spectator is an idealization the procedure cannot simply average the room or put to a vote who felt what. (b) The recurrent case in which resentment is exactly proportionate to the provocation, yet the manner of its expression is vengeful and the remedy demanded is one no spectator could endorse; our findings here have been inconsistent, some of us treating manner as evidence against substance. (c) Custom: the respondent says his conduct was ordinary in the field when he trained and remains ordinary at the institutions where he now presents, while the complainant says it is not ordinary here and now. I want a step that neither capitulates to the way things are done nor treats our local practice as though it were the moral law.

Fourth, examine a tentative rule of mine that I suspect is a foreign import wearing Smith's coat. I have been telling members that we may rely only on a description of the situation that both parties would recognize as accurate, even while each rejects the evaluation the other builds on it. Is that his? Can it be operationalized when one party refuses every description on offer? State it in the form the procedure needs, or strike it.

Then run the procedure end to end through two cases, showing the answers the steps elicit rather than a summary conclusion. The first: five months of unanswered chapter drafts; a supervisor who says he read everything and withheld comment because he judged the work not ready and thought saying so would devastate the student; a complainant who reports abandonment and humiliation and asks for a second reader and a formal reprimand. The second: a junior colleague who alleges that a senior professor presented his analysis in a grant application as the senior's own, whose evidence is good, whose manner is vindictive, who circulated the allegation through the department before bringing it to us, and who asks that the senior's name be struck from the application. I expect one of these to end in a finding I shall not like — either that a complainant I am predisposed to believe has no ground for the pitch of resentment she reports, or that a remedy everyone on the committee considers mild is one no spectator could enter into. Give me that finding, and say how the written rationale should be worded so that we can defend it to the senate.

Finally, state the procedure's limits: the classes of case a Smithian committee handles worse than one working from rights or from compliance with written policy; the point at which a disputed fact we have no means of settling should send a case elsewhere; and what I should decline jurisdiction over altogether. Gloss every Smithian term parenthetically at first use, since the sheet will be read by people who have never encountered it and I shall not read the treatise aloud to them.
