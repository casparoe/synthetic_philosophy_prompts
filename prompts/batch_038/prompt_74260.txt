I am drafting a short account of when an inherited practice may be abolished, and before I commit it to paper I want it tested against the verdicts I actually hold, which I suspect are not fully consistent with one another. The cases below are a mixture of things I have encountered and stipulated variants. The stipulations matter: please take them as given rather than reaching for a closer real-world analogue, and please do not offer me references or a reading list — I want the argument worked through the cases themselves.

1. A lane with a gate across it. No living resident can say why the gate is there and the parish records are silent. Verdict: nobody may remove it until somebody can say what it was for, even though the gate costs the village a detour every day and even though the reason, once found, may prove trivial and long lapsed.

2. A firm requires two signatures on purchase orders below a trivial threshold. The rule dates from a currency-control regime that made such orders a reporting event; that regime was repealed decades ago, in public, on the record. Verdict: the rule may go at once, with no further inquiry, because the repeal is documented and because reinstating a signature requirement would cost almost nothing.

3. A licensing restriction on a trade. A diligent reformer does the archival work and discovers precisely what the restriction was for: it was written to keep that trade out of the hands of a particular group of immigrants, and it has gone on doing so ever since. Verdict: removal is not merely permitted but required, and the reformer's success in stating the function is what licenses it.

Case 3 is the one that most embarrasses my account of case 1. In the first, ignorance blocks removal; in the second, knowledge enables removal, even though the fence is still doing exactly the job it was built to do. I want to know whether one principle can govern both, or whether I am trading on two different ideas — something about the reformer's ignorance and something about the practice's worth — that merely happen to point the same way in ordinary cases.

4. Two customs, identical in age and equally indefensible on their stated rationales. The first survives in a small association whose members could leave at any time and do not. The second survives in a large organization because a form nobody reads contains a checkbox nobody questions. Verdict: keep the first, drop the second. Now complicate the first: its members cannot realistically leave, because leaving would cost them their families and their livelihoods. I still want to say keep it, and I cannot say why, since the feature I appealed to a moment ago — that its persistence reflects choice — has been removed by stipulation.

5. A three-hundred-year-old practice of rotation and seed selection in a region where one bad season meant starvation within the year. And a three-hundred-year-old formality governing inheritance whose consequences, if it has any, fall on people who never learn they were affected and arrive two or three generations on. Verdict: the antiquity of the first is strong evidence in its favour; the antiquity of the second is very nearly no evidence at all. I want to know whether my principle is therefore secretly a claim about selection and feedback rather than about deference to the past — and if so, whether that means I am not a conservative in Burke's sense at all, but an evidentialist who happens to think that most old things have been unusually well tested.

6. Abolishing a practice, and declining ever to introduce one, where by stipulation the costs and benefits are exact mirror images. Verdict: the first is blameworthy and the second is not. I suspect loss aversion. But there may be something real in the asymmetry: a standing practice can be observed, interrogated and found out, whereas an absent one leaves nothing to examine. If that is the reason, my principle needs a clause about the direction in which knowledge accumulates, not merely about stakes.

7. A ritualized step in a laboratory protocol that everyone performs and nobody can explain. Establishing whether it does anything would take years of controlled work; the step costs a few minutes a week. Verdict: drop it. Now the same unexplained step at the same cost of inquiry, in the shutdown procedure of a plant whose characteristic failure is catastrophic and occurs about once a century. Verdict: keep it indefinitely, without ever knowing why. So stakes must enter somewhere, but I cannot tell whether they enter as a threshold on how much searching is owed or as a discount on the practice's cost.

8. A regulation copied wholesale from another jurisdiction by a legislature that had no local reason for adopting it and said so at the time. Verdict: this practice does not enjoy the protection I gave the gate in case 1, because there is no local history that could have selected for it. But I worry that this makes the principle a claim about origins rather than effects, and that a transplanted practice which has now run well for fifty years ought to acquire the standing of a home-grown one. Does the principle track the builder's reasons, the practice's present effects, or the population that has lived under it? The three come apart and I keep switching between them.

9. This is the case I keep going back and forth on. A safety rule adopted after a disaster, on the basis of a causal diagnosis that has since been shown to be wrong. The rule nonetheless coincides with a marked fall in accidents, for reasons nobody has pinned down; alternative explanations are plausible but none has been established. Some weeks it feels to me like case 2 with the repeal unfound — a fence whose stated purpose is a myth. Other weeks it feels like the strongest possible case for retention: a fence that is load-bearing for reasons its builders never understood. Tell me which it is, or tell me that the case is underdescribed and name the further fact that would settle it.

Here is my own tentative principle, which I expect will not survive contact with 4, 7 and 9. Removal is permissible only where either (a) the practice's function can be stated and, once stated, is judged not worth its cost, or (b) a proportionate search for functions has been conducted and has found none — where the search owed is proportionate to the harm the practice does and to the difficulty of putting the practice back.

What I would like from you:

- A principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely and clause by clause, with the case above that motivates each clause and its verdicts on cases 1 through 9.
- Which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Where a tension is real, I would much rather be told that the simpler principle overturns one of my verdicts than be handed an epicycle for each case; so say plainly which verdict you think is simply wrong, as opposed to merely unexplained.
- Whether clause (a) is doing any work distinct from clause (b), given case 3 — and, more generally, whether what I have is one principle or two, an epistemic one about the reformer's ignorance and an evaluative one about the worth of the old. If they are two, give me a case in which they conflict.
- Whether the principle needs to fix what counts as a function: the builder's intention, the practice's present effects, or the account its adherents give of it. Cases 8 and 9 seem to turn on this and I have not chosen.
- Predictions on three cases I have not listed: an opaque module in a legacy system that no living engineer can explain and whose deletion would be trivially reversible; a constitutional provision whose original rationale is a matter of endless scholarly dispute; and a word in a liturgy that nobody now understands and that the congregation has been mispronouncing for a century.

Finally, please be explicit about your confidence. For each of your main claims, say how sure you are of it, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, together with what would change your mind about it.
