I'm writing the final for a senior seminar in philosophy of history. Two-hour written exam, students pick one of three essay questions, and I need you to draft one of the three for me — on Whig history — plus a model answer and a marking scheme.

The angle I want it to turn on: basically every gloss the students have met makes the Whig fallacy out to be an *evaluative* mistake, cheerleading the past as a march toward the glorious present. But the complaint I actually want tested is structural — it's about selection, about building the narrative out of whatever happens to have led to us so that the dead ends and the losers get abridged away. Those two things can come apart, and I want the question sitting right in the gap. So build it around a concrete case, and invent the case rather than reaching for a real book: a history that is anti-progress in its verdicts but Whiggish in it's architecture. Say a study of the Enlightenment argued throughout as a catastrophe whose costs we're still paying, but which only ever follows the causal lineages that terminate in present-day problems and treats everything that died out as beside the point. Or a labour history told exclusively through the ancestors of institutions that survived. Students should have to decide whether that counts as Whig history at all, and whether the label is earning its keep beyond "teleological" and "presentist," which they've been throwing around all term.

Constraints: closed book, roughly 50 minutes for this one question, so nothing can hinge on knowing some seperate historiographical debate we didn't cover — everything they need has to be in the stem itself. State an expected word count in the stem too. No bibliography or reading list from you, I'll point them at the readings myself.

Then give me a model answer of around 700 words that actually commits to a position instead of dissolving into "it depends what you mean by Whig." And a marking scheme out of 25, broken into the moves an answer has to make, where the A/B line is pinned to specific moves and not to "more nuance" or "better prose" — I want to be able to justify a 17 versus a 21 to a student who pushes back on it. Finally, list the three or four answers I'll see most often and where each one tops out on the scale. I'm predicting a chunk of them will just say it can't be Whig because it's pessimistic, and another chunk will say everything is Whiggish so the term is useless — but tell me if you think the common failures are something else.
