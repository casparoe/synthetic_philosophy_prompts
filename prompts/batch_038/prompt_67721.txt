I spent twenty-nine years running a mid-sized logistics company, and for the last six I have sat on the board of a private foundation in our state. Twice a year we allocate roughly four million dollars across science and public-interest projects, and this spring one proposal has split us badly. I am the only member of the board without a degree past high school and I have no training in game theory or decision theory, but I have been reading on my own for a few years now, and I would rather bring my colleagues a memo they can vote on than another round of impressions. Please draft that memo in my name.

**The case.** A research group is asking for 2.6 million dollars over three years to build a working prototype of an autonomous system for detecting and suppressing faults in the regional power grid. Their application is unusually candid about why they want the money *now*. Two well-funded corporate laboratories are pursuing much the same design. The group argues that whichever team reaches a demonstrable prototype first will end up writing the interoperability standard the rest of the industry then adopts, that the standard-setter captures the field for a decade, and that if our foundation waits even one grant cycle the opportunity is gone. Two of my colleagues find this compelling; one says the foundation exists precisely to back work worth doing fast. Three think it is a race dressed up as science and that we would be subsidizing panic.

**Why I am asking instead of writing it.** Some months ago I came across an argument about Hex, the game played on a board of hexagonal cells in which each player tries to connect their two opposite sides. The claim is that the first player can always win, and the proof never constructs a winning strategy. It says: suppose the second player had one. Then the first player can place one arbitrary stone, and thereafter follow the second player's strategy, treating that extra stone as a free gift; if the strategy ever calls for the cell already occupied, the first player plays somewhere else instead, because in a connecting game an extra stone on the board can never be a disadvantage. So the second player cannot have had a winning strategy after all, and since the game cannot end in a draw, the first player must have one. I have not been able to stop thinking about this, because it seems to say something general: an advantage consisting purely in moving first — in a game where extra moves never hurt and where both sides play by the same rules with the same information — is no advantage at all, since the other side can simply steal it.

Out of that I have built myself a rough test, and I want the memo to apply it to the proposal explicitly and to state it in a form my colleagues can argue with in writing. I call these the four theft questions:

1. **Reproduction.** If a rival team arrived two years behind but could see everything the leader had done, could it reproduce the benefit? If it could, the benefit comes from being good rather than from being first.
2. **Visibility.** Is the advantage genuinely observable and copyable, or does it depend on secrecy and tacit know-how that a follower cannot lift?
3. **Symmetry.** Are the leader and the follower playing the same game — same resources, same rules, same objective — or are they effectively different players in different games?
4. **Cost of moving.** Does going first consume something the follower gets to keep: safety margin, goodwill with regulators, or the freedom to design a second system only after watching the first one fail?

The memo should take the grid proposal through each of the four and say what each answer does to the case for funding.

**Where I expect my borrowing breaks down.** I would rather the memo name these than have a colleague discover them at the meeting. First, the Hex argument is existential and not constructive: it proves a winning strategy exists without ever exhibiting one, and a board that must decide what to do is poorly served by a proof that something can be done. Second, the argument belongs to a finite, two-player, perfect-information, zero-sum board game with no draws, whereas our decision involves many players, hidden information, payoffs that are not zero-sum, and no defined end of play. Third — and this is the one I am least sure about — the strongest thing the applicants say may simply be true: whoever writes the standard does something a later arrival genuinely cannot undo, because every follower must thereafter interoperate with an architecture it did not choose, and at that point an extra move *can* hurt, since committing early forecloses options the follower keeps. That looks to me like a real failure of the symmetry question rather than a rhetorical flourish. Please give the applicants the strongest version of this objection and engage it at length; do not straw-man it, and if you conclude it defeats my framework, say so plainly in the memo rather than around it.

**Then land somewhere.** Recommend one of three dispositions: fund the full 2.6 million; fund a reduced or staged amount under conditions we could realistically enforce; or decline. If you recommend conditions, make them concrete and time-bound — the size and duration of any first tranche, what the applicant must demonstrate or disclose to unlock the rest, and what the board should do at the re-review gate. In particular, address whether conditions forcing early public disclosure of designs and interfaces would help. My instinct is that making the work copyable is a way of testing whether the claimed advantage survives contact with the very theft I have described; the obvious counter is that it would hand the two corporate laboratories precisely what they are trying to buy. Tell me which of those you think is right and why.

About a page and a half to two pages, plain and formal enough to circulate to seven people who disagree, no jargon beyond what I have used here, and no footnotes, bibliography, or reading list — the argument has to stand on its own.

Close with one line in exactly this form, so I can log the outcome in our records:

Verdict: [fund in full | fund with conditions | decline] — [amount and tranche structure, or "not applicable"]
