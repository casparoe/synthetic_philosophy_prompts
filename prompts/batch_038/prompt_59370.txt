I am a fourth-year doctoral student in political theory, and in the spring I was appointed, somewhat reluctantly, to my university's governance review panel. Last month the panel circulated a draft recommendation that has left me with an argument I cannot quite state. Under the proposal, votes in university-wide budget referenda would be weighted by length of service and by documented familiarity with the relevant portfolio; postdoctoral researchers and hourly staff, who mostly satisfy neither condition, would cast ballots in a separate, purely advisory tally. When I said in the meeting that this struck me as indefensible, a colleague asked me the obvious follow-up question: was I objecting to the fact that a group of people would be shut out of the decision, or to the fact that among those allowed in, some would count for more than others? I discovered that I did not know, and that every answer I tried seemed to commit me to something I did not want to accept. If the objection is to exclusion, then a scheme in which everyone votes but with unequal weight should be unobjectionable, which seems wrong. If the objection is to unequal weight, then it is hard to see why the exclusion cases in the literature generate so much more moral alarm than the weighting cases, which are usually discussed as though they were a mere technical variant of the same thing.

I want to turn this confusion into a paper of about five thousand words for a graduate conference four months from now, and I would like you to write the proposal for it. The question as I currently formulate it is this: does the democratic objection to epistocracy track exclusion from the franchise, or inequality in the weight of votes within an otherwise universal franchise, and do the two features carry independent normative force? The proposal should state that question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer rather than dissolving into the general debate about whether knowledge should be a precondition of political power. It should explain why the standard positions leave the question open: I have in mind that contemporary defences of restricted suffrage and of plural voting tend to be argued for as a single package, while the principal objections — that citizenship is a matter of equal standing, that excluded groups lose the means of protecting their interests, that reasonable disagreement about the good rules out handing authority to the holders of one contested conception of competence — appear to be aimed at one or the other of those features without anyone saying which. Please include a scheme for sorting the arguments by which feature they actually engage, a plan for testing the distinction against concrete institutional designs rather than against abstractions (electorates selected by lot, graduated vote weights, expert bodies with agenda-setting but no veto power, deliberative assemblies whose recommendations bind no one), and a defence of why that method is preferable to the two obvious alternatives, namely going straight to the empirical question of whether better-informed electorates decide better, or reconstructing the historical pedigree of weighted voting. Set out what the possible conclusions are, what considerations would decide among them, and what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the distinction turns out to be one without a difference, in which case I want the proposal to say how I would demonstrate that rather than merely concede it. Give an honest assessment of what makes this too large for four months and what I should cut. Do not supply a bibliography or any citations; I will add those myself, and I would rather you name the positions and arguments you are drawing on in prose so that I can go and find the texts.

Two further requests, and the second matters more than the first. Be decisive inside the proposal: an application reader should come away knowing what claim the paper will defend, not merely what terrain it will survey. Then, stepping outside the voice of the proposal, tell me what would change your mind. Which verdict do you think most likely to survive sustained argument, what specific objection or consideration would most plausibly overturn it, and where do you think my framing is weakest — for instance, whether exclusion and weight can really be separated once one notices that weighted voting is exclusion by degree, or whether the whole question depends on a notion of equal political standing that I have not earned the right to assume. Please mark clearly which parts of your assessment you hold loosely and which you would defend against a sceptical reader.
