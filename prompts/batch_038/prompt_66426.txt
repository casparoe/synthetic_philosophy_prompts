I sit on an advisory panel at a national statistics office that must decide two things: whether to adopt a headline wellbeing indicator alongside our economic accounts, and whether our government should treat its position in the international life-evaluation rankings as something to be actively improved. In the consultation document circulated to us, an outside adviser defends both moves with a single image. The ladder item at the centre of the main international ranking — the question asking people to place their current life somewhere between the worst and the best life they can imagine — is, he writes, a thermometer for happiness. You put the instrument to the population, take the reading, and compare it with readings taken elsewhere in exactly the way you compare temperatures in two cities; and if the reading is low, the rational response is to warm the place up. He treats methodological objection as pedantry: on this picture, doubting that mean life evaluations are comparable across countries is like doubting that a thermometer in Reykjavík reads lower than one in Cairo.

A colleague on the panel has proposed a rival image that I find more promising. The ladder reading, she suggests, is like a patient's self-reported pain score during a ward round. It is the patient's own report; it is authoritative for some purposes and close to useless for others; clinicians do not assume that raw scores are comparable between patients, or even within the same patient over time; and no one supposes that the aim of treatment is to reduce the number on the scale.

Please audit both analogies. In particular:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly for each — what plays the role of the instrument, the quantity it is supposed to track, the scale and its units, the calibration procedure, the object whose state is being read, and the intervention the reading is meant to guide. A table is fine.

2. Identify where each mapping fails, and separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the adviser's conclusions from the ones that are merely decorative. I would like the following pressures applied specifically: that the ladder's endpoints are defined relative to each respondent's own best and worst imaginable life, so it is not obvious that different respondents are using a common interval scale in the way that different thermometers use a common one; that asking the question appears to change what is reported, in a way that reading a thermometer does not change the temperature; that the national figure is an average of something defined only at the level of persons, with weighting and composition choices built in; that the word the public hears is happiness while what is collected is an evaluation of one's life as a whole; and that the ranking report itself explains cross-national gaps by reference to income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity and corruption, which raises the question of what independent work the reading is doing over and above those variables.

3. Say what each image quietly imports into the target that the target lacks — and, in the other direction, what real feature of the target each image hides from view.

4. State, for each analogy separately, whether the two conclusions survive: that cross-national comparison of mean scores is legitimate, and that a government should try to raise its ranking. Where an analogy fails, say whether it fails against both conclusions or only one of them, and whether the pain-score image supports them better, worse, or merely differently.

5. If both images are inadequate, propose a better one, and be explicit about how much weight the replacement can bear. In particular, say whether any analogy can carry the further claim that there is a single determinate quantity being measured at all, or whether that claim has to be argued for independently of the comparison.

I am not asking for a literature review and I would rather you did not supply references; I will add whatever sourcing the final report needs. What I need is the reasoning: which features of the source domain are doing the argumentative work, and what is left of the adviser's policy recommendations once they are removed.
