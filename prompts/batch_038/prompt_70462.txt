Four years ago I retired from a chair in applied mechanics, where the greater part of my working life was spent on the large-deformation behaviour of materials: rubber, biological tissue, and the slow flow of ice and of the mantle beneath it. Since then I have taken up philosophy of physics with the seriousness of a former student rather than that of a dilettante, and I have become preoccupied by the hole argument and, more particularly, by the family of responses it has attracted.

The argument itself I take to be clear enough. A relativistic spacetime theory posits a manifold of events and distributes fields — metric and matter — over it. Where the field equations are generally covariant, any solution yields a further solution by dragging those fields across the manifold with a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside some bounded region. If manifold points are individuals whose identity is independent of the fields they happen to carry, the two solutions assign different field values to one and the same point within that region while agreeing everywhere outside it; and since the region may be taken to lie to the future of an initial surface, the laws appear unable to fix the future from a complete specification of the present.

I should like you to rank the standard responses. I set them out here in my own words, so that we are agreed about what is being ranked:

(A) Accept the conclusion. Manifold points are individuals independently of the fields, the theory really is indeterministic in the region, and it is better to say so plainly than to purchase determinism with metaphysics.

(B) Deny that the dragged solution represents a distinct possibility at all. The diffeomorphism is a gauge transformation; the two mathematical models describe one and the same physical situation; nothing has been left undetermined because there was never a second future to determine.

(C) Retain manifold points as genuine individuals, but deny that their identity across models is independent of the fields: a point is the same individual in two models only if it carries the same metric and matter in both. The dragged model therefore does not reassign the fields to the same individuals in a rival way.

(D) Structuralism. There are no individuals prior to the structure they instantiate; a manifold point has no identity beyond what the relations among fields confer, and the question which point is which was never a question the theory undertook to answer.

(E) The argument rests on a conflation. An active diffeomorphism is not a symmetry of anything: dragging the fields alters the physical situation described unless one has already resolved that it does not, and the step from general covariance — a claim about the form of equations — to the required symmetry among solutions is simply invalid.

(F) Determinism has been formulated at the wrong level. The theory is deterministic once determinism is defined over equivalence classes of models, or in terms of diffeomorphism-invariant quantities; the hole argument then shows only that a point-by-point formulation, which no gauge theory was ever entitled to satisfy, fails.

Please score each of the six on two axes, each running from 0 to 5. The first is purchase: the extent to which the response removes the difficulty rather than redescribing it, where 5 means the worry cannot be reconstructed at all and 0 means the argument survives intact with its conclusion merely renamed. The second is portability: how well the response would survive the loss of the smooth manifold, that is, in a fundamental theory with a discrete or algebraic substrate from which manifold structure emerges only approximately. A response that keeps its content in that setting scores highly; one that exists chiefly to police the point-set of the manifold scores low. Then order the six from best to worst and group them into tiers, explaining in two or three sentences per item why it received the scores it did, and telling me how you combined the two axes to reach your ordering.

I should like one further test applied throughout, because I believe it does real work. In the mechanics of deformable bodies one has a reference configuration of material points, a deformation map, and a choice between Lagrangian and Eulerian description; but there the material points are identifiable independently of the deformation field — one may follow a grain, mark it with dye, or cut it out. My suspicion is that several of the responses above acquire their appeal only because the analogy with a continuum invites one to treat manifold points as though they were material points, and as though their independence of the fields were an empirical matter rather than a stipulation. Where you think the analogy genuinely supports a response, say so; where you think it misleads, say that, and let it be reflected in the scores.

Do not give me a survey, and do not supply references or a reading list: I have what I need on that score, and I would rather have your judgement than a bibliography I cannot check. I should prefer a committed ordering to a caveat that the matter is underdetermined. If you consider any of my six formulations to be itself confused, say so, and rank the confusion along with the rest.
