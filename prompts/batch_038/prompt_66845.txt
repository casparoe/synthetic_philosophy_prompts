I work on evals at a smallish AI lab and I'm stuck on a phrase one of our senior researchers keeps using. The project: we're trying to tell whether a model has knowledge it isn't showing in its normal outputs. Concretely we take factual documents, redact one key span, and then ask the model a bunch of indirect questions about the document (who would have written this, what plausibly happens next, which of these two continuations is consistent with the rest). On the indirect questions it lands on the right redacted fact way above chance, but when we just ask "what was redacted?" it says it can't tell. So there's clearly something in there, and now we're arguing about how to get it out.

His answer to two seperate proposals has been the same: "everything is elicitation." First, when I suggested we train a probe on the model's internal states to read the fact out directly, he said don't bother, you can always get it out with the right question, training a reporter just reshuffles what the model would have said anyway. Second, when a teammate worried that our indirect questions might be leading -- that the model could be picking up on what we seem to expect and handing that back to us rather than reporting anything it actually represents -- he waved it off with the same phrase, on the grounds that if a probe scores well the model must have known it all along.

I keep feeling like those aren't the same claim and that at least one of them is being made to carry weight it can't hold, but I can't articulate it. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding in "everything is elicitation"? I specifically want the version that's about training dynamics (post-training only selects or reweights stuff already sitting in the base model) kept apart from the version that's about the gap between what a model internally represents and what it can say when asked, and from any version that's really a claim about whether a good probe score is evidence of knowledge. For each, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive empirical claim that could come out false, or just wrong. Format: exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion -- items 1 through 4 are the candidate readings with a verdict on each, and item 5 says which reading, if any, actually supports the two moves he made, including whether the leading-question worry survives every one of them.
