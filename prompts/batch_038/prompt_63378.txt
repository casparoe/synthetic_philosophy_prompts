I sit on the ceremonies committee of a mid-sized European university that is deciding what to do with its charter-day procession: a copy of the founding charter is carried from the old hall to the newer one by the senior professor and two junior fellows, and the assembly repeats a formula of words after the presiding officer. The university dates from the fifteenth century; the procession in its present form appears in the records somewhere in the 1870s, and the formula of words is in no earlier statute.

At our last two meetings the phrase "all traditions are invented traditions" has been deployed three times, each time to do a different piece of work, and each time it has ended the discussion rather than advanced it.

1. A colleague arguing for abolishing the procession said that because the ritual is an invented tradition, its claim to embody the university's continuous life is false, and a claim known to be false cannot ground an obligation to preserve it.
2. A colleague arguing for composing an entirely new ceremony said the same thing and drew the opposite conclusion: if everything of this kind was made up by somebody at some point, then making up a better one now is not a loss of authenticity but the same activity continuing.
3. A third colleague, invoking Anderson rather than Hobsbawm, said the authenticity question is confused from the start, because a community of this size is an imagined community anyway, and imagining is what such communities do rather than a defect in them.

My working hypothesis is that at most one of these is a claim about the world, that the others are either trivial or non-sequiturs, and that the phrase is being treated as a single premise when it is really several different premises wearing the same clothes. I would like that hypothesis tested rather than confirmed.

What I want is a disambiguation, laid out as a menu, that I can bring to the next meeting.

- Enumerate the distinct precise theses the slogan can express, ordered from what no one could deny to what would be a substantial empirical claim about the historical record. For each, state it in one or two sentences precise enough to be true or false; say what would have to hold for it to be true and what kind of evidence would settle it; and give a verdict — platitude, substantive but defensible, contingent on which cases are examined, or false.

- Address at least the following joints. Whether "invented" requires identifiable agents acting deliberately at a datable moment, or whether it also covers practices that accrete, drift, and are only retrospectively formalized and backdated. Whether the slogan asserts that the tradition's own account of its antiquity is false — a claim about beliefs people hold — or that this account is irrelevant to its authority, which is a claim about normative force, since these two come apart and only the second does any work in an argument about obligation. Whether "invented" presupposes a contrast class of non-invented tradition, and if that class turns out to be empty, what work the word is left doing. Whether the slogan licenses the levelling inference in (2) — that if all traditions are invented, none can be more or less authentic than another — or whether that inference requires an additional premise about authenticity that the slogan does not supply. And whether "imagined" in the Andersonian sense invoked in (3) is even the same operator as "invented," given that a community's being imagined does not thereby make it fictitious, fabricated, or optional.

- Then, for each of the three uses above, tell me which specific reading from your menu it would need in order to succeed, whether that reading is one its user is entitled to on the evidence we actually have, and what is left of the argument once the slogan is removed: which premises would still be doing the work, and on which of them the committee would genuinely have to argue rather than gesture.

Please write it as a numbered list of readings followed by a short section on the three uses — roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words. No bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the taxonomy, not the sources. And keep any intellectual history to what is needed to fix the term: the interest here is in the logic of the slogan, not in the career of the people who coined it.
