I write a science-and-ideas column and I've got about three weeks to file a feature on simultaneous discovery — the whole "two labs, same result, same week" phenomenon. My anchor case is the November 1974 mess where Ting's team, running experiments on Brookhaven's synchrotron, called the new particle J while Richter's group at SLAC called the identical thing psi, and they ended up splitting the physics Nobel two years later. On the modern side I'm using CRISPR: the Doudna–Charpentier paper in mid-2012, Feng Zhang's group getting it working in human cells a handful of months after that, and the patent war that followed, which the US patent office eventually decided in the Broad's favor while the chemistry Nobel went to Doudna and Charpentier. Both cases make the same question unavoidable — was the result sitting there waiting, or did particular people actually do something? — but what's driving me nuts is the language people use to answer it.

Every third person I interview says some version of "it was in the air," and I'm now pretty sure they don't mean the same thing by it. The Broad-adjacent folks say it to shrug off the priority fight, roughly *anyone with the right plasmids and a decent cell line would have had it inside a year*. A historian I talked to said it about Darwin and Wallace, to explain why the joint reading at the Linnean Society in 1858 wasn't the scandal it looks like from here. And my own editor said it to me, impatiently, when I pitched making the piece about credit: *if it was in the air nobody did anything special, so why does your story need a hero at all?* That's three different jobs for one six-word sentence and I don't think they're compatible — I'm fairly confident the second and third uses would be undermined by the kind of facts that make the first one sound plausible.

So can you take it apart for me? I want a menu of the distinct precise claims somebody could be making with "it was in the air," running from whatever's basically a platitude up through the strong, testable ones that are probably false. For each reading, say what it's actually a claim *about* — the availability of tools and techniques, the state of the background ideas, how many people were in fact working on it, a probability conditional on the field being where it was, a counterfactual about a specific group not existing, the amount of skill versus luck-of-position the discoverer contributed, an estimate of how much the world would have differed — and then give it a verdict: trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, plus how you'd check it or argue against it without just asserting it. Most important for me, say which of my three interviewees each reading would actually serve, and where the slogan slides from "this was likely to happen anyway" to "therefore no credit is owed," since I suspect thats exactly where my editor is loosing the thread. Two things I'm especially fuzzy on: whether the counterfactual readings change status depending on the time window ("within eighteen months" versus "sooner or later somebody"), and whether "it would have happened anyway" is even a claim about the same result or only about a result that plays the same role in the textbooks, arrived at by a different route, framed differently, a couple of years off. Lay the readings out as a numbered list, each with a one-sentence plain-English translation I could actually print, and no references or reading lists — I'm working offline on a train and I just need the disambiguation clean enough that a fact-checker could point at a sentence in my draft and tell me which version of the slogan I've committed to.
