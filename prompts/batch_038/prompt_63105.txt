I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Lewis on convention, and the argument has hardened into a workshop paper I present in three weeks. My thesis is that Lewis runs together two questions that must be separated: what makes a regularity a convention, and what keeps it in place. His analysis makes each agent's reason for conforming run through beliefs about the others' likely conformity; but there is a wide family of stable, arbitrary, coordination-solving regularities — queueing, forms of address, citation practice, tipping — in which the operative reason is moral or evaluative rather than predictive. People conform because not conforming would be rude, or because that is simply how the thing is done, and they would go on conforming on quite impoverished beliefs about what others actually do. My friend, who works on the psychology of norms, says this is no threat at all: the expectation-tracking does the real work, the moralized reasons are rationalization, and any regularity that survives a shock to people's expectations will have proved expectation-driven all along.

The room will be mostly social ontologists plus several evolutionary game theorists who model norm emergence and care about dynamics rather than analysis; there are no Lewis specialists present.
