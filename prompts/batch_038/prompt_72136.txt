I'm drafting an internal training memo for a small fact-checking unit, and I keep running into a phrase that does a great deal of unexamined work in our arguments: "cui bono? — ask who benefits." This week a senior colleague used it to close a discussion. The case, stripped of identifying detail: a municipal records system suffered an unexplained corruption event eleven days before the deadline for filing objections to a zoning decision; the corruption prevented publication of a set of objections that had already been lodged; by the time the system was restored, the developer whose project those objections targeted had submitted a revised application that complied with a plan amendment adopted during the blackout. My colleague's argument was that the developer was the obvious beneficiary of the malfunction and that the malfunction therefore was not accidental. When I objected that plenty of accidents have beneficiaries, he said I was missing the point of the maxim, and added that in our line of work you follow the benefit.

I want the memo to get this right, so I'd like a careful unpacking of what "ask who benefits" can actually be taken to claim. Please lay the readings out as a numbered menu rather than settling on a single favoured interpretation. For each reading, give: (a) a one- or two-sentence statement of the precise claim, in whatever formal or semi-formal idiom suits it; (b) what it would take for that claim to be true; (c) a verdict tag — platitude (true but doing no work), substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or irredeemably vague; and (d) a note on whether the reading supplies a heuristic for deciding where to look or a warrant for believing a conclusion.

Along the way the menu should cover at least the distinctions I suspect are being run together here: between benefit that was foreseeable in advance and benefit that only became visible afterwards; between benefit as a sign of motive, of means, and of nothing whatever; between "some identifiable party gained" and "this particular party gained in this particular improbable way"; and between the maxim as a rule of investigation — cheap, non-committal, perhaps harmless — and as a rule of inference, which as I understand it has to be assessed against how often accidents turn out to have had beneficiaries. Please also say whether "ask who benefits" and "follow the money" are one slogan in two costumes or two slogans with different logical force.

Here is the datum I want the memo to confront, because it is why I'm not satisfied with a shrug. Of the 41 corruption or outage events in our region's public systems over the past decade that we could document, 34 had at least one identifiable party with pending business who stood to gain from the delay; and in the four cases later confirmed to involve deliberate interference, the beneficiary was no harder to identify than in the accidental ones. Treat these figures as given — they are illustrative of our own case files, not anything you would find published, and I am not asking you to check them. Tell me what they do to the evidential force of "the developer benefited," and what further fact about the benefit would be needed before it could discriminate between accident and interference at all.

Finally, address two uses that point in opposite directions while surviving on the same words: my colleague's (the beneficiary of an anomaly is probably its author) and the one I hear constantly from the people we fact-check (the party challenging the official account stands to gain from casting doubt on it, so the challenge is manufactured rather than sincere). Say whether these are two readings of one slogan or a single reading applied twice, and whether the maxim can coherently be used both ways by opposing sides in the same dispute.

Please write it as prose and short lists that I can lift directly into the memo, with no references, no reading list, and no citations — I want your reconstruction, not a bibliography. End with one final line in exactly this form, giving the label of the reading (if any) that would actually support my colleague's conclusion from the premises he offered, or the word none if no reading does:

Verdict: [label or none]
