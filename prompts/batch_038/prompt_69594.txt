I'm writing a seminar paper on the sorites for a graduate philosophy-of-language course, and I've come away persuaded by epistemicism — largely because it appears to be the only theory on the market that lets us keep bivalence and classical logic without qualification. What I want the paper to do, though, is something more constructive than rehearsing the familiar dialectic between epistemicism, supervaluationism, and degree-theoretic accounts. My idea is to ask what it would actually take to locate one of the sharp boundaries the view is committed to. Take a predicate like "poor" applied to annual household income rounded to the dollar, or "bald" applied to an exact hair count. If epistemicism is right, there is a particular dollar figure at which a household ceases to be poor, and a particular hair count at which a man ceases to be bald. And since the view holds that these boundaries are fixed by a community's actual patterns of application rather than stipulated or legislated, they look to me like empirical facts about that community — facts that are, at least in principle, the sort of thing an investigation could track down.

So my question is about the protocol. Would the right method be something like the forced-choice procedures borrowed from psychophysics: presenting a long series of cases straddling the suspected transition, collecting binary verdicts from many competent speakers, and running an adaptive staircase that homes in on the cutoff the way one homes in on an auditory threshold? Where verdicts near the boundary are erratic, I'd have thought the noise averages out with enough trials and enough respondents, so that one could attach a confidence interval to the true boundary and watch it shrink as the sample grows. My supervisor pushed back by invoking the margin-for-error principle, which I understand as the claim that knowing a vague predicate applies to some case requires that it also apply to nearby cases. But I take that to be a remark about the limits of our discriminatory powers, structurally similar to the impossibility of measuring a length to infinite precision — and limits of that kind are exactly what better instruments, finer stimulus spacing, and larger samples push back. I can't see why the semantic case should be any different in kind.

The reason I want to nail this down is that my supervisor's objection struck me as proving far too much. If no procedure, however refined, could reveal where the boundary lies, then epistemicism and supervaluationism would seem to make identical predictions about every investigation we could carry out, differing only in that epistemicism posits an undetectable fact behind them. And supervaluationism looks like the more economical package in any case: it accounts for our inability to locate a cutoff by denying that borderline claims are determinately true or false, and it is prepared to give up excluded middle for precisely those claims rather than pay for a boundary no one could ever reach. So I would like help developing the methodology into something defensible, and, more urgently, help formulating a reply to the margin-for-error objection that doesn't concede the collapse. My instinct is that the objection trades on treating a semantic claim as though it were a claim about perceptual resolution, and that once the two are kept apart the epistemicist has nothing to worry about — but I'd like the argument spelled out in enough detail to survive scrutiny in the paper, including whatever auxiliary assumptions about the relationship between use and meaning the reply has to lean on.
