I'm teaching an upper-level philosophy of time seminar — 16 students, one 100-minute session, week four. They've had a week of special relativity: they can do time dilation and length contraction and they'll recite that c is invariant, but relativity of simultaneity is still just words to them. I want to spend the whole session making the Rietdijk-Putnam argument land on them before they ever hear it named, using the Andromeda version, and I want them doing arithmetic and making commitments rather than listening to me talk.

Here's roughly the shape I have in mind. Improve it, push back on parts of it, or tell me it won't run.

- Phase A: role cards. Each student is a "synchrony clerk" assigned a state of motion relative to the room — parked car, pedestrian walking toward Andromeda, pedestrian walking away, cyclist, city bus, etc. Each gets the Andromedan council's schedule in local days and the anchor (in the Earth frame, right now the council is partway through its debate). One multiplication on the worksheet, phone calculators allowed. They write their answer on an index card — which day of the council's timeline is simultaneous with the moment they pass the front of the room — and post it on the board before seeing anyone else's.
- Phase B: the board fills in and the spread becomes visible. Then a walk-off: pairs go out into the corridor, walk in opposite directions, come back, and each has to state out loud whether the vote has happened yet. No appealing to "my frame is the right one."
- Phase C: symmetry. Each clerk computes the other's offset from inside their own frame and finds that both of them can claim the other's "now" is the shifted one.
- Phase D: scale. How fast would you have to be moving for a cafe 100 m down the street to shift by a full second? Have them express the answer as a multiple of c, then sit with it: the whole effect only exists at ridiculous distances. Does that count against the argument or not?
- Phase E: small stakes. Everyone antes a chocolate coin into a pot at the start. At the end, the pot goes to anyone who can state a claim about the invasion that every one of the clerks' frames agrees on. (I think nobody earns it, or somebody earns it with something trivial about light signals, which is its own lesson — tell me what you expect here.)
- Phase F: only now do they get the name and the one-paragraph statement of the argument. Then objection cards, written for students, in groups of three or four: the one about stitching local simultaneity into a global present and whether that relation is even transitive across frames; the presentist reply that nothing obliges inertial frames to be metaphysically privileged; the line that openness in the sense that matters for deliberation is local and epistemic and untouched by any of this; and the distance-dependence point they just found for themselves in Phase D. Each group has to say what the eternalist is forced to concede if the objection is granted.

Things I really need you to nail:

The actual numbers. Give me the conversion factor for the worksheet — days of offset per m/s of relative speed, one-way, and separately the total swing when someone turns around and walks back — and derive it on the page rather than just asserting it. Check it, because someone in my department insists the effect washes out at walking speeds since gamma is essentially 1, and I want to be able to put the arithmetic in front of them instead of just telling them they're wrong.

Pick the speeds on the role cards and design the council's timeline so the spread is maximally annoying: I want at least one clerk for whom the council hasn't convened yet, one in the middle of the debate, one who lands within a day or two of the vote, and one for whom the fleet left weeks ago. Keep every speed at or under about 30 m/s so a single timeline covers the whole spread. If that forces the council's schedule to be implausibly long, say so and fix it some other way.

Also: board layout, a minute-by-minute run sheet for the 100 minutes, and a fallback version that works with 8 people if half the class doesn't show up.

The debrief mapping. For each phase, what they physically did → which premise or move in the argument it corresponds to, including which phase is supposed to produce the "oh no" and which is supposed to arm them against it.

Failure modes on a first run, and what to say when they happen. I already expect sign confusion, and I expect at least three students to add the 2.5 million year light travel time to the simultaneity offset and get nonsense — build the worksheet with seperate columns for the two so that's structurally hard to do. Also the physics major who derails things by invoking curved spacetime, the walk-off collapsing into giggling, and the deflation move ("nothing travels faster than light, so who cares").

Finally, be straight with me about what this activity can't deliver. My worry is that everything they compute is third-person and about a place 2.5 million light years away, so they might leave knowing a fact about coordinate systems without their own sense of an open future being touched at all. Tell me whether that worry is right, whether anything in the design mitigates it, and which piece I'm going to have to supply by hand no matter how good the game is.

Treat Andromeda's distance as roughly 2.5 million light years — I dont care about astronomical precision here. No references or reading list, I just need materials I can print.
