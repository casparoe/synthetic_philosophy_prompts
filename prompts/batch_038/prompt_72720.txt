So I've talked myself into a pretty strong view about why pain is bad, and I'd like you to try and break it rather than summarize the literature for me. The view, which I'll call the affective bottleneck thesis (my coinage, not anyone else's), has two halves. First: nothing about a pain makes it bad for the subject except its unpleasantness — the affective-motivational component. The sensory-discriminative stuff (where it is, how intense, whether it's sharp or burning or throbbing) is morally inert on its own, an information-carrying event no more harmful than a bit of proprioception. Second, and this is the part I actually lean on at work: because unpleasantness is separable, a subject can be in pain and not be harmed or suffering by it, and the existence of pain asymbolia is the proof of concept — people with insular or limbic damage, and to a lesser extent people on certain analgesics, who report the sensation but say they're not bothered by it, who don't nurse the limb, don't wince, don't ask for relief. I've been reading Grahek's Feeling Pain and Being in Pain, which is roughly where I got the second half, and my friend who's an anesthesiologist thinks I'm overreading a handful of case reports. I sit on a palliative care ethics consult service, and the practical payoff I'm claiming is that reported pain scores in patients who show no distress whatsoever should be discounted when we're weighing things like palliative sedation — which people find pretty distasteful, so I need the view to be better than merely consistent.

Tell me what would count against it. I want the defeaters ranked from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to be explicit about which ones a committed holder of the thesis would themselves concede as counting against them versus which ones they'd wave off, and why the difference isn't just special pleading. Specifically: does the asymbolia data actually support the dissociation, or is there a reading on which it's neutral or even hostile — for instance if the reports turn out to be a language or belief problem rather than an experience problem, if the patients are generally affectively flattened so nothing bothers them, if the withdrawal and guarding reflexes show the aversive machinery is intact and just disconnected from report, or if they simply fail to believe their body is endangered, which is the evaluativist line. Which empirical findings would you want to see in the next decade — insular stimulation studies, opioid and cingulotomy data, congenital insensitivity cases, comparative work on animals, whatever you think is load-bearing — and which of those, if they came out a particular way, would kill the thesis rather than embarrass it? And what does the thesis predict that its rivals don't, where the rivals being roughly Bain-style evaluativism, Martínez's imperative account, Klein's objections to both, and the simpler view that suffering just consists in the subject's negative attitude toward the sensation?

Also please give me an honest answer on the insulation worry, because I suspect it's the real problem. If unpleasantness is whatever makes pain bad, then the first half of my thesis looks close to a tautology, and the whole thing can survive anything by redrawing the line between the affective component and everything else after the fact. Say plainly whether that's what's happening, whether it's fatal or just typical of a claim in this area, and what I'd have to give up or operationalize to make it risk something.
