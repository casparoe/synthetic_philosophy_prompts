OK so I've just come off a three-hour argument with a mate who's doing a PhD in consciousness science and I genuinely can't tell which of us is talking nonsense, so I want an answer that actually picks a side rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose.

The thing we were arguing about is the adversarial collaborations — the charity-funded projects (COGITATE and the ones that followed, I think) where the global workspace people and the integrated information people sit down with a bunch of neutral labs, jointly preregister their divergent predictions with explicit pass/fail criteria, and then run the same couple of hundred people through the same visual experiments under fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG. In these experiments people are shown pictures, sometimes briefly or washed out, sometimes plainly visible for varying lengths of time, and they press a button to say whether they saw something and what it was, and sometimes rate how clearly they saw it. Then everyone looks at where and when information about the stimulus is decodable, whether there's a sudden late amplification of activity out in frontal cortex (what the workspace people call ignition), whether posterior areas carry the content for longer, and both camps come away saying the results partly support and partly embarass their own theory, and the field declares the whole exercise a triumph and a model for how to do this properly.

My friend says that's just science working as it should — two theories, shared predictions, decisive data. I have this nagging feeling the setup has already settled something important before the first subject went in the scanner. Every measurement in there is about whether information is available for a button press, a verbal report and the control of behaviour. But I keep hearing about a distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness — information being available to the rest of the system versus there being anything it's like — and about the whole no-report movement, where people deliberately stop asking subjects anything at all precisely because they think reporting drags in attention and decision-making and contaminates the measurement. So the same field treats report as a confound to be engineered out, and then uses report-based tasks to arbitrate between two theories of experience.

So: what does this practice actually presuppose? Does it require that access and phenomenal consciousness never come apart, or only the weaker claim that the second can't be measured except through the first — and which of those is doing the work? What views does it quietly rule out, e.g. a view on which experience is intrinsic and not a matter of information being available for use by anything, or higher-order views where what matters is a thought about the state rather than the state's availability? Relatedly, the write-ups concede they're testing the theories' biological implementations rather than their formal cores, so a theory can lose the experiment and survive by relocating itself elsewhere in the brain — is that a small-print caveat, or does it mean the pass/fail criteria could never have been decisive, and if so what sort of thing is being treated as a testable theory of consciousness here?

Please be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. If several mutually incompatible theories would license exactly these experiments, then my worry is toothless and I want to be told so. And say whether the practice is internally consistent given the no-report stuff, or whether those are two different projects that just happen to share a department.

Then, if you think the worry has teeth, redo the thing concretely: what would the collaboration have to change — the tasks, the measures, the predictions, the pass/fail criteria — if we took seriously the possibility that phenomenal consciousness outruns what's reportable and usable? If your answer is that no experiment could do that, say so plainly and tell me what the exercise is actually for instead.

Don't give me the balanced menu version. Commit: tell me whether my friend is right that this is just good science or whether the practice is quietly question-begging, name the one presupposition you think is load-bearing, and give me your reasons.
