For about fifteen years I've been the person in every criminal-justice thread who says the same thing, and I'd like an honest assessment of whether I should stop saying it.

My view, roughly: the Scandinavian correctional model — normality maintained behind the wall, short sentences, officers doing welfare and training work rather than guard work, Halden as the usual poster child — isn't the soft option its critics say it is, it's the option that actually works. And American mass incarceration is self-defeating on its own stated terms: not merely unjust, or racially skewed, or ruinously expensive, but bad at the one job it claims to be doing, which is making people less likely to offend again. The standard move is to put two recidivism numbers side by side and declare the argument settled. I've made that move a lot and it always gets the same 300 comments back.

Play it straight and tell me what would count against it. I want the exposure map, not a debate-club steelman.

Specifically:

1. How much of the recidivism comparison survives contact with measurement? Two countries with no shared counterfactual, wildly different offence mixes and sentence lengths, and — I suspect — different definitions of the event being counted (rearrest vs reconviction vs back inside) and different follow-up windows. Walk me through what a careful person can and can't conclude from a comparison like that, and whether a gap of the size usually quoted is large enough to survive all of it.

2. Is the active ingredient the prison or the society people get released into? If the causal work is being done by housing, benefits, a functioning labour market and social services that pick up where the sentence ends, then the policy conclusion I keep drawing — build better prisons — is wrong and the real conclusion is something much harder. What evidence could in principle seperate those two, and does any of it exist?

3. There's a Norwegian economics paper I half-remember, something involving random assignment of cases to judges so that judge leniency works as an instrument, and I think the headline finding was that imprisonment *lowered* recidivism and improved employment outcomes, mostly among people who weren't in work beforehand. If I'm remembering it roughly right — correct me if I'm mangling it — does that help me or hurt me? Because I've simultaneously been running the prison-as-criminal-university line, that harsh incarceration is itself criminogenic. Can both of those be true at once, or have I just been collecting whichever claim suits the thread?

4. Incapacitation. I basically ignore it, which is probably a mistake. Its value surely isn't linear in how many people you lock up: the marginal prisoner at 3,000 is not the marginal prisoner at 1.9 million. If that's right, how much of the American crime decline could be real incapacitation and still leave my position intact, and at what point does that stop working?

5. The defeaters that bite even if every number I have is correct. The less-eligibility worry, the standing of victims, the fact that a punitive system can be simultaneously bad at preventing crime and politically durable for reasons that have nothing to do with efficacy. Also: Norway runs a preventive-detention back door with indefinite extensions for the worst cases, which suggests the humane model may be doing less work than it looks, and it never had to handle an atrocity on the scale the American system handles routinely. Does the Norwegian model actually cope with atrocity, or does it cope with a small system full of short-stay prisoners?

6. Scale. If a large US state ran Halden-style units for 100,000 people, on fifteen-year sentences, with the American offence mix and the American staff turnover, which parts of the model survive and which are load-bearing on the system being tiny and homogeneous? Point me at anything close to a real attempt at adopting it at scale and what happened.

7. The unfalsifiability check, which is the one I actually care about. Have I hedged this into safety? Pre-register it for me: which observations would make me abandon the view, which would make me revise the mechanism but keep the conclusion, and which would only earn a caveat. And tell me what my position predicts that a retributivist or a deterrence-first person doesn't predict — anything genuinely checkable.

For each item, say whether a serious defender of the rehabilitative model would concede it as counting against them or wave it off, and then rank the whole lot from fatal to merely awkward. And where I've got something wrong above — a mechanism, a Norwegian institution, a finding — say so bluntly rather than working around it.
