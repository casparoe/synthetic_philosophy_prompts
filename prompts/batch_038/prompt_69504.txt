I work as a policy adviser in a mid-sized voluntary-voting democracy where national turnout has slid into the low sixties over the past two decades, and I have been asked to help draft a submission to an electoral reform inquiry examining whether we should adopt the Australian model. The features of that model that I keep coming back to are structural rather than statistical. Federal compulsory enrolment and compulsory attendance at the polls have been in place since 1924; turnout moved from a voluntary-era range of roughly half to three-quarters of the eligible population into the low-to-mid nineties, and it has stayed there ever since. The sanction is a small administrative fine, cheap enough that almost nobody would call it a serious cost, and it can be waived on the basis of an acceptable excuse such as illness, absence from the country, or objection of conscience. Crucially, because the ballot is secret, the authorities can confirm that you attended a polling place and collected a ballot paper but can never confirm what you did with it; submitting a blank or deliberately spoilt paper is not an offence and is not detectable, and a substantial minority of ballots cast under compulsion are informal in exactly this way. So the state compels bodily presence, verifies presence only, and is constitutionally indifferent to the content of what is deposited in the box.

What I want is a reconstruction of the theory this practice is only intelligible under, not a survey of arguments for and against compulsion. Start from the apparent incoherence that puzzles me: the act being mandated has, for any individual in an electorate of tens of millions, an essentially zero probability of affecting the outcome, so no straightforwardly causal expected-utility calculation can make it worth doing, let alone worth being fined for not doing; and yet the same legal system compels jury service, where a single juror's absence can actually abort a trial and where the juror is paid for the inconvenience, while the voter is neither causally indispensable nor compensated. What conception of political obligation, and of what makes an electoral result legitimate, licenses compelling the causally impotent act and not merely the causally load-bearing one? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what merely fits it comfortably. I have in mind at least four candidate grounds that seem to license an identical institution: a fair-play or reciprocity account on which beneficiaries of a cooperative scheme owe a contribution; a Kantian universalizing account on which the individual's reason to vote derives from what would follow if everyone reasoned as she does; the argument sometimes called evidential cooperation in large worlds, on which an agent facing a decision problem she knows is replicated across many structurally similar agents should treat her own choice as evidence about theirs and cooperate accordingly even absent any causal link; and a purely pragmatic, non-moral engineering rationale on which compulsion is valued because voluntary electorates over-represent the old, the affluent, and the polarized, so dragging everyone to the polls forces parties to compete for the median. Which of these does the Australian arrangement actually depend on, and which could be swapped in without changing a single rule?

Two further questions I would like treated explicitly. First, is the arrangement internally consistent, given the parts I have described? It prosecutes non-attendance but not donkey voting or blank voting; it justifies itself by reference to the mandate an elected government receives and to the deliberative quality of a fully participating electorate, yet its enforcement machinery is blind to whether any preference was expressed at all. Does that combination presuppose that the value of voting is aggregative and symbolic rather than expressive or epistemic, and if so, what becomes of the standard defences? I would also like you to tell me which rival views the practice quietly forecloses — a consent-based account of political obligation, for instance, or a competence-restricted view on which the uninformed should stay home, or any view on which the worth of a political act depends on its being freely undertaken rather than extracted. Second, consider a variant my committee has been asked to cost: keep the obligation and the excuse provisions but replace the universal small fine with a large fine imposed on a randomly selected five per cent of detected non-voters, holding expected cost constant. What does the choice between these two enforcement structures presuppose about whether the law is doing deterrent work, norm-signalling work, or merely bookkeeping work? Finally, tell me what would have to change institutionally if we replaced the presupposed view with a strictly causal-instrumental one — what would take the place of compulsion, whether a lottery that pays one voter enough to offset the decisiveness deficit, sortition, or something else — and what would have to change if we instead adopted the evidential-cooperation reading, where the state's problem is not motivation but the salience of the fact that millions of similar agents are making the same choice at once.
