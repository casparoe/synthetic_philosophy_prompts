I am working on diachronic coherence for my dissertation in formal epistemology, and I want a carefully engineered counterexample to a claim I keep encountering in seminar: that van Fraassen's Reflection principle does the same normative work as a requirement to conditionalize on one's total evidence. Take Reflection in its bare conditional form — your present credence in a proposition, given that your future credence in it will be r, should itself be r — and set aside any proviso about the future self's rationality until the final part of your answer.

Construct a single case in which an agent satisfies Reflection without exception and yet violates conditionalization, and in which that violation can be exploited by a diachronic Dutch book built entirely out of bets the agent willingly accepts at the times in question. Constraints: one agent, two times, one ordinary empirical proposition; the evidence the agent receives at the later time must be veridical and genuinely probative, so that this is not an anticipated-misleading-evidence case — the failure has to lie in how the agent responds to the evidence, not in what the evidence says; no drugs, coercion, memory loss, shifted preferences, or self-locating content; the agent must be exactly right about what their own future credences will be; the scenario proper should be one realistic paragraph set in something like a research or workplace context rather than a casino; and all probabilities should be simple enough to check by hand.

Then show that the design works. State the agent's credence function at the first time, including the conditional on the anticipated evidence, and their credence function at the second time, and verify that Reflection holds for every proposition — explaining why certainty about one's future function forces the match. Exhibit the bets with explicit prices and a payoff table, flagging any branch in which the agent does not come out behind. Say precisely which conditionalization requirement fails and at which time. Identify the single structural feature that lets the two principles come apart here, and say what happens to the case if you remove it — for instance, if the agent is only moderately confident about their future credences rather than certain. Finally, give the strongest reply available to a defender of Reflection: which proviso, read broadly rather than narrowly, would let them disown this agent, what the case costs them if they read it narrowly, and whether you think the result shows Reflection is too weak a constraint or that the Dutch-book strategy is the wrong way to diagnose diachronic irrationality.
