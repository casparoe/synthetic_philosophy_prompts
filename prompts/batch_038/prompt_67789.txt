Draft:

Start from something that looks like a curiosity and turns out to be load-bearing. Statutes sometimes contain a clause telling the court how to read them: a rules-of-construction section saying the Act is to be construed liberally so as to advance the purposes laid out up front. The UK Human Rights Act goes further, requiring that legislation "so far as it is possible to do so" be read compatibly with Convention rights — which courts have taken as licence to depart from what the words would naturally bear. So the legislature sometimes writes an interpretive method into the law itself.

Textualism handles this easily, maybe too easily. The clause is text; its public meaning at enactment is what it is; so the textualist obeys it. Textualism isn't refuted by a statute that orders it to be purposivist — it just reports that the law enacted here happens to include an instruction to go consult purposes. Nothing gets conceded. The method is still fixed by meaning rather than by purpose; the purpose only enters because the meaning said to let it in.

Purposivism handles the mirror case much worse. Suppose Congress writes the opposite clause instead: this Act shall be construed according to the ordinary meaning of its terms, and no purpose, policy or legislative history shall be considered. Now the purposivist is told by the law to stop being a purposivist. She can't just comply, because complying is precisely what defeats her method's rationale — if the ordinary meaning frustrates what the statute was for across a range of cases, a method that treats purpose as authoritative can't treat that clause as conclusive. So she has to say the clause is a rule adopted for a reason, and rules adopted for a reason get construed so they don't defeat it. But that is her method declining to follow a directive that her own method counts as law.

Hence the asymmetry. Textualism can absorb an instruction to be purposivist with no internal strain; purposivism can't absorb an instruction to be textualist without either betraying its own criterion or explaining why the clause doesn't bind. And it isn't just a cute result. It suggests textualism is methodologically self-sufficient in a way purposivism can't be: textualism's authority is the enacted words, so the words can settle how they're to be read, including by delegating; purposivism's authority is always answerable to something outside the words, so no statute can fully settle how it gets read. The legislature can outsource its interpretive instructions but it can't tie its own method down.

Three things:

1. Does the asymmetry survive the obvious replies? The one I keep running into is that a textualist facing a "construe liberally to advance the purposes" clause still has to figure out how much purposivism the clause actually delegates, which looks like exactly the legislative-intent guessing textualism is supposed to refuse. And symmetrically, that the purposivist's handling of the anti-purposive clause is no more unstable than any purposivist handling of any rule — no vehicles in the park, ambulance included.
2. Is there a regress here that flattens the whole thing? Reading the interpretive clause itself needs a method the clause can't supply, so both positions get stuck at the meta-level in the same way, and my asymmetry evaporates. Tell me if that's right or if there's a principled stopping point.
3. Bluntly: have I built a straw textualist or a straw purposivist? My characterisations are second-hand. And is the conclusion mildly interesting, or has everybody already said it?
