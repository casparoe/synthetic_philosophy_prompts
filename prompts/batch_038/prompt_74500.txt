I am drafting the methodological section of a monograph on the coordination of rational credence with objective chance, and before committing to any particular bridge principle I want the specification fixed: what a principle of this kind has to deliver in order to be adopted at all. The purpose is stipulated as follows. The principle is to serve as a normative constraint on the priors of an ideally rational agent reasoning about genuinely stochastic physical theories — decay processes, statistical mechanics, drift in population genetics — and it must be usable by an agent who does not already know which chance theory is true.

Take the familiar schema as the starting point: for a chance function ch indexed to a time, a rational credence function Cr, a proposition A, real x, and admissible evidence E,

  Cr(A | E ∧ ch(A) = x) = x.

Everything below concerns what must be true of a candidate principle of this shape, rather than which candidate to pick.

Write the desiderata list. For each requirement give: (i) the requirement, stated specifically enough that one could check a formulation against it; (ii) the case or consideration that motivates it — ideally a concrete situation in which a principle ignoring the requirement yields a clearly wrong or clearly undefined answer; and (iii) your verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, with a sentence of argument for the verdict.

The list must cover at least the following territory, and you should add what I have omitted.

1. The four situations below. For each, say what an adequate principle is obliged to say, and treat that obligation as a constraint on the list.
 (a) Self-undermining chance. A stochastic theory T assigns chance 0.5 to a certain coin landing heads at t, and also assigns chance 0.2 to the proposition that T itself states the chances at t correctly; so T is, by its own lights, probably but not certainly true. The agent's total evidence includes T. What does the schema deliver here, and what must a principle do about the fact that a chance hypothesis is not certain of its own correctness?
 (b) Frequency clash. The agent is certain that ch(A) = 0.5 for repeated trials of one setup, and then learns that in a hundred such trials, 82 were A — an outcome of vanishingly small probability on that chance hypothesis. On one metaphysics of chance the credence in the next trial stays at 0.5; on another it must move. Does this show that the bridge principle cannot be metaphysically neutral, and must a principle declare which side it takes?
 (c) Chance zero. A continuously distributed quantity takes a value that had chance zero, and the agent observes that it has. The conditional in the schema is then either undefined or zero-over-zero.
 (d) The blameless ignoramus. An agent with no access to chance information holds credences that track observed long-run frequencies closely but violate the schema. Is she irrational? Must the principle bind her; and if not, at what level — priors, ur-priors, or every credence function at every time — should the principle be stated?

2. Status. Is the principle a priori or a substantive empirical claim? Can it be both non-trivial, in the sense that chance is not simply defined as whatever quantity credence ought to defer to, and knowable independently of experience? If these cannot be had together, say which to give up and what is lost by giving it up.

3. The restriction on evidence. Admissibility, or whatever replaces it, has to be characterized. What counts as an acceptable characterization, and what would make a characterization a mere device for saving the schema from counterexample?

4. Existence, consistency, and stability. There must be credence functions satisfying the principle, and satisfying it jointly with the probability axioms and with conditionalization; and compliance must survive the accumulation of evidence, or the loss of compliance must be explicable.

5. Distinctions that must not be collapsed: chance at a time versus chance relative to a history; the chance function versus the agent's evidence about the chance function; a principle's being false versus its being inapplicable; objective chance versus propensity versus limiting frequency versus epistemic probability.

6. Accessibility. A principle applicable only by an agent who already knows the chances does no work; a principle on which known chance overrides all further evidence makes ordinary statistical learning inexplicable. Address both.

Then answer these directly. Which single requirement does the most exclusionary work — which one rules out the largest number of otherwise attractive formulations, and why? Is any subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable? If so, exhibit the subset, give the argument for the incompatibility, and say which member should be dropped or weakened; if the list itself amounts to a no-go result, that is precisely what I want to see.

Finally, in a few sentences per candidate and no more than a page in total, score the main contenders against the list: the original schema with an admissibility restriction; the reformulation that treats the principle as a constraint on ur-priors, or on the credence function's response to a whole chance hypothesis, rather than on arbitrary conditional credences; a derivation of the principle from accuracy-dominance considerations; and the view that chance just is whatever quantity occupies the deference role.

Do not cite literature or supply a reading list; where a technical notion is needed, state it in the text.
