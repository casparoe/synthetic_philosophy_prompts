A colleague and I are drafting teaching material on self-referential entrenchment, and I would like your credence — not a survey of positions, but a number with its derivation exposed.

Consider the following charter. It is hypothetical; please treat it purely as a structure and do not try to match it to any real constitution.

- Article 1 vests the Assembly with the power to amend any provision of the charter by a two-thirds vote.
- Article 7 provides that Articles 1, 7, and 9 are unamendable, and that no amendment may alter, suspend, or transfer the powers those three articles confer.
- Article 9 vests a Constitutional Court with authority to certify an amendment before it enters into force, and provides that no amendment takes effect without such certification.
- Article 12 provides that, where the charter admits of two readings, the Court shall adopt the reading that gives effect to the greater number of its provisions.

The Assembly now passes Amendment 47-B, which reads in its entirety: Article 7 is repealed; the certification requirement in Article 9 is abolished; and the Assembly's amending power under Article 1 becomes unlimited. If the Court certifies 47-B, it has used Article 9 to abolish Article 9, and has certified an amendment whose express content violates Article 7, which Article 12 tells it to give effect to. If it refuses certification, Article 7 has operated to repeal itself without any act of amendment, and the Assembly's Article 1 power has been nullified by the Court in a way no provision authorizes.

Call this the deadlock thesis: the Court faces a genuine dilemma, in the sense that no certification decision it can make is both procedurally authorized by the charter and consistent with the charter's substantive entrenchment provisions. My colleague puts her credence in the deadlock thesis at about 0.8. Mine is nearer 0.25, because I think a court can locate authority for either horn in some other provision, and that a genuine dilemma requires the absence of any authorized route out.

Give me your own number, and then decompose it. I would like at least four sub-questions that you take to be load-bearing — for instance, whether Article 12's "greater number of provisions" criterion has a determinate answer here at all, whether Article 7's unamendability should be read as a limit on content or as a limit on the Assembly's competence, whether a court's power to refuse certification is itself an exercise of the power being abolished, and whether "genuine dilemma" should be indexed to the text or to the best reconstruction of the drafters' scheme. Assign each a rough probability, give one or two sentences of justification, and then show how you combine them into the final figure. Weighting them equally will almost certainly be wrong; say what you used instead.

Then tell me which single sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what kind of consideration — a textual point, a point about the logic of self-reference, a point about what institutional actors actually do under such charters — would move you furthest. Also say where you think my colleague's 0.8 and my 0.25 most likely diverge: which term in the decomposition we are scoring differently, as opposed to merely phrasing differently.

Two constraints on the response. First, do not supply citations, bibliographies, or named authorities; I am interested in the arguments, and I would rather you reason from the structure than reconstruct a literature. If you invoke a distinction or a line of argument that has a recognized name, describe it instead of citing it. Second, and importantly, keep the settled separate from the contested. Where practitioners of constitutional theory and formal work on self-amending rules genuinely agree, say so plainly. Where the field is split, or where you are adopting one side of a live disagreement — about whether entrenchment can be absolute, about whether hierarchies of norms dissolve or merely relocate such paradoxes — identify it as contested and say which side you are taking and why. Do not present a contested reading of Article 7 or of the deadlock thesis itself as though it were the default position.
