I teach a second-year module, "Probability and Its Interpretations," to eighteen philosophy undergraduates. We meet once for seventy-five minutes in a seminar room with movable desks. The students have had one term of formal logic and an introduction to the axioms of probability; none has taken measure theory, and none may be assumed to know what a change of variables does to a density. No computers or phones may be used during the session, and the materials budget is limited to paper, card stock, pins, string, drinking straws, a ruler, a protractor, dice, coins, and a photocopier.

Design an in-class activity for that session built around the following puzzle: an equilateral triangle is inscribed in a circle; a chord of the circle is chosen at random; what is the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle?

The activity must satisfy the following requirements.

1. No framing lecture. The students must encounter the conflict before they are told it exists. They are not to hear the puzzle's name, or the phrase "principle of indifference," until a point in the timeline that you specify and justify.

2. Four generating devices. Give construction instructions, from the materials list above, for four low-tech devices, each of which a student can operate in seconds and each of which realizes a different way of choosing a chord "at random": two endpoints taken on the circumference; a direction together with a point along a radius, the chord being drawn through that point perpendicular to the radius; a point taken anywhere inside the disk and used as the chord's midpoint; and a tossing device in which straws are scattered onto the sheet and the resulting lines are extended or truncated to chords. For each device, state the exact procedure the student follows, the value the device should converge to, and a derivation of that value in two or three lines these students can follow. Also specify a measurement-free criterion the students can apply in seconds to decide whether a given generated chord is longer than a side of the triangle, and say when the reason that criterion works is to be revealed.

3. The nested-circle audit. Students are to be given a cut-out card ring whose radius is roughly fifty-five percent of the drawn circle's, together with a sixty-percent reduction of the whole worksheet on the photocopier. For each device they generate a batch of chords on the large circle, then place the ring inside it — once concentric, once clearly off-centre — and record whether the pattern of chords falling inside the ring is distinguishable from the pattern on the whole circle, and whether it changes when the ring is moved. They must also compare the large sheet with its reduction. Before running any of this, each group writes down a prediction about which devices will pass both tests. Tell me which devices in fact pass and which fail, explain in language available to these students what the two tests have to do with choosing among the four values, identify the assumption the tests themselves depend on, and say what a group that reports the opposite of the correct prediction should be shown.

4. Posted credences and scoring. Design a token-based scheme in which every student posts a numerical credence in the target proposition before each device is run, and is scored on the outcome. The scoring rule must be computable by hand on paper, with no more arithmetic than these students will do willingly. Explain why the rule you chose rewards honest posting, and say plainly where that explanation fails, given that the students cannot compute expected scores for arbitrary rules.

5. A cross-group market. Each group is assigned exactly one device and instructed to post a buying and selling price for the sentence written on the board — the same sentence for all four groups, phrased in terms of a chord chosen at random. Trading between groups then opens for a fixed period. The outcome you must engineer is that no arbitrage or sure loss arises even though the posted prices differ substantially, because the shared sentence expresses a different proposition in each group's hands. Supply the facilitator's script for the five minutes in which this is made explicit, and state the underlying point precisely enough to answer the student who objects that all four groups said the same words.

6. Sample sizes and statistical power. Say how many trials per device fit the timeline, and show by a rough calculation whether that number of trials lets a class of eighteen distinguish the four values from one another on a blackboard. If it does not, say what you substitute — pooled class totals, a pre-computed table revealed at a specified moment, a device with less variance, something else — and defend the substitution against the charge that it hands the students the answer.

7. A minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes, including setup that must happen before the students arrive.

8. The debrief. Map particular moments of the activity onto the conceptual content: which quantity each device treats as uniform; why applying indifference to arc length, to distance along a radius, and to area are three different acts rather than one act badly performed; the fact that the four devices are not exhaustive and that further answers can be generated indefinitely; what the scale and translation tests do and do not settle about which device, if any, answers the question as posed; and what is left standing at the end. State what a student should be able to say on the way out that they could not have said on the way in.

9. Predictable failures. List at least six, each with a remedy the facilitator can execute in the room. These must include: hand-generated "randomness" that is biased in a way the students will not notice in their own data; clustering in the device that places a point inside the disk; straws landing outside the circle, nearly tangent to it, or bunched by the throw; a group reverse-engineering the instructor's intended lesson and reporting accordingly; the market phase collapsing into a single confident student setting all the prices; and empirical frequencies too noisy to separate values that are close together.

10. The honest limitation. Name the feature of the puzzle that this activity cannot reproduce: the original question specifies no generating mechanism whatsoever, while every device here supplies one, so the exercise quietly adopts the resolution it is meant to dramatize. Tell me exactly what to say or stage in order to recover the step that abstracts away from the devices back to the bare question, and state candidly which part of the puzzle's force is lost no matter how I run the session.

Write the deliverable under headings corresponding to these ten points, in imperative instructions I can follow, with a separate materials list and a description of the tally sheets. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any quotation from a primary text; where you need to refer to a known argument or a named position, describe it rather than cite or quote it. Keep the facilitator's spoken script to the minimum the moments you identify require.
