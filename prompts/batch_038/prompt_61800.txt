I am in my final year of secondary school and I run our school's philosophy society. Once a fortnight I set the members a practice question of roughly examination length, they submit answers, and I mark them before we discuss the topic together. I would like your help marking one of these submissions. I have deliberately not said anything about how I read the answer myself, because I want the marking to be independent of mine.

Here is the question as I set it.

> **Practice question 4.** The label "inference to the best explanation" was introduced by Harman in 1965 for the move from the fact that a hypothesis would explain the evidence to the conclusion that the hypothesis is true. One well-known objection, pressed by van Fraassen, is that such a move can at best deliver the best of a bad lot, since nothing guarantees that the true hypothesis is among the candidates anyone thought to propose.
>
> Now consider the following arrangement, which is a thought experiment rather than a description of any existing system. A research team connects a hypothesis-generating program to a body of observational data. The program works inside a single fixed formal language and, by assumption, succeeds in listing every hypothesis expressible in that language which is consistent with the data. It then ranks the listed hypotheses by explanatory merit — scope, simplicity, unification with other accepted theories — and reports the top-ranked hypothesis as its result.
>
> (a) Does the completeness of the listing answer van Fraassen's objection in this case? Say what it answers and what, if anything, it leaves untouched. [5 marks]
>
> (b) Is the program's report still an ampliative inference, or has exhaustive comparison changed its logical character? Give your reasons. [4 marks]
>
> (c) Lipton distinguishes the loveliest explanation from the likeliest. Explain the distinction and say what bearing it has on your answers to (a) and (b). [3 marks]

And here is the rubric I mark against, in case it helps you allocate the marks:

- (a): 2 for stating the objection accurately; 2 for a defensible verdict on whether the arrangement answers it; 1 for correctly identifying what the arrangement leaves in place.
- (b): 2 for the verdict on whether the inference remains ampliative; 2 for the reasoning that supports the verdict.
- (c): 1 for the distinction itself; 2 for its application to the earlier parts.

Below is the submitted answer, reproduced as written.

> (a) Van Fraassen's point is that a rule telling us to believe the best explanation we have come up with cannot reliably reach the truth, because the true explanation may never have occurred to anyone, so we end up choosing the best of a bad lot. In the arrangement above the lot is not bad in that respect. The program lists every hypothesis in its language that fits the data, so the winner did not win by default against an absent rival: if the truth is expressible in the language at all, it is on the list and has been compared with everything else that fits. Whether the language was a sensible one to choose is a question for the engineers who built the program, not a question about the logic of the inference, so it does not revive the objection. What is left over is the ranking rule. The program orders by explanatory merit, and nothing said so far explains why being the loveliest hypothesis should make a hypothesis likelier to be true. But that is a separate difficulty, and not one van Fraassen's objection raises: it is really Hume's problem, and it applies to any ampliative rule whatever, including plain enumerative induction, so it is not a reason to single out inference to the best explanation.
>
> (b) Because the comparison is complete, the report is no longer an ampliative leap of the familiar sort. The program has discarded every hypothesis the data exclude and then ordered the survivors by a criterion fixed in advance; given the data and the criterion, the identity of the top-ranked hypothesis follows, so the report stands to its premises more as a deductive consequence stands to its premises than as a generalisation stands to its instances. It is ampliative only in the trivial sense that the conclusion says more than the raw data say, but adding a criterion to the data always does that, and it would be strange to call every such inference an inductive one.
>
> (c) Lipton distinguishes the loveliest explanation, meaning the one that would provide the most understanding, from the likeliest, meaning the one best supported by the evidence, and he insists the two come apart conceptually. The program ranks by loveliness, since scope, simplicity and unification are what make an explanation lovely, and then reports the top entry. On Lipton's view that step is in order, because he holds that our judgements of loveliness are how we arrive at our judgements of likeliness, so a ranking by loveliness is in effect a ranking by likeliness, and parts (a) and (b) stand as written. A program built the other way round, ordering hypotheses by how well supported they are without asking how much understanding they yield, would be doing conditionalisation rather than inference to the best explanation, and that is the response people give when they say a Bayesian needs only degrees of belief and never needs the extra step.
