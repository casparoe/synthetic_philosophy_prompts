So — second-year metaphysics, 22 students, and next Tuesday I've got 75 minutes on causation by omission: whether an absence can be a cause. They've already had counterfactuals and they've had the regularity/INUS material, and every one of them can recite "but for the gardener's failure to water, the plant would have lived." That's precisely the trouble. They think they already know this topic and they don't, and I need a plan for running the session, not a transcript of a lecture I could give in my sleep.

The order first. What goes on the board before what, with a line of justification for each piece about why it can't be moved earlier. My instinct is to open on a case where everyone, me included, is happy to say the omission caused the harm, and only later reveal that the very same machinery licenses verdicts we can't live with. But tell me if that's backwards — specifically whether they need to hit the profligacy problem *before* they'll give a damn about the difference between an omission and a mere absence, because I genuinely can't tell whether that distinction lands for them as a solution or as a dodge.

Then the carrying case. I'm banning the plant, the hiker and the poisoned canteen, and anything with Suzy and Billy in it, since half the room met those in intro and will coast on recognition. Two candidates of my own, pick one or throw them both out and build me something better:

- Case A: the lifeguard on duty leaves the platform for the bathroom; a weak swimmer goes under at the deep end and drowns. The relief guard is in the locker room. Seperately, a retired guard three towns over is also not at that pool.
- Case B: a maintenance tech signs off a scaffold inspection; one clip that's supposed to be on the frame was never fitted, and a painter falls. Here the absence is an object rather than an action, and there are a hundred identical scaffolds in the yard where the clip is present.

Whichever case you land on, it has to do four things: exactly one agent whose omission is a plausible cause and at least one whose isn't, so the class feels the line instead of being told about it; a baseline that isn't smuggled in by the way I've rigged the setup; a harm the students already think somebody is to blame for, so nobody can slip out through "well, nobody's responsible anyway"; and it has to survive being re-described positively — he was in the bathroom, he was doing paperwork — without the class concluding that the wording is doing all the work. Tell me which of those four the case buys you cheap and which one it strains, because I'd rather know before Tuesday.

Misconception map, with timings. The ones I'm braced for: "the counterfactual account already settles this, obviously"; "so everyone who didn't water your plants caused them to die" — they'll say that as a joke and mean it as a refutation; "an omission is just an event under a negative description, so it's all phrasing"; the opposite overcorrection, "then nothing is ever caused by an omission, it's just talk"; and the flat conflation of failing to prevent with causing. There's also one kid who's read a paper arguing omissions are never causes at all and wants to make that the entire class. Tell me where in the sequence I can park him without either swatting him down or letting him win the room by minute twelve. For each misconception: the minute it gets headed off, and whether the fix is a distinction, a case, or an honest admission that the question is open.

One exit question, answerable in a paragraph, that would tell me they got the problem rather than the vocabulary — plus what a bad-but-passing answer looks like, since I'm going to read twenty-two of them.

And what I should refuse to cover at this level, and announce that I'm refusing: contrastive accounts, the metaphysics of absences as entities, thier status in criminal law, whatever else you'd cut. If something on that pile actually has to come in for the session to hang together, say which and defend it. One whiteboard, no slides, and I'd rather spend the last ten minutes being straight about where the literature is stuck than pretend we resolved it.
