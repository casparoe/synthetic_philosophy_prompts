Our team is deadlocked on what a column in a database is supposed to mean, and I've become convinced the deadlock is philosophical rather than technical. I work on the backend of an occupational-health platform. Each worker record carries a boolean column, `clinically_recognized_condition`, that downstream systems use for scheduling, accommodation, and insurance-adjacent reporting. One colleague argues the column should key off the body: if a mechanism is running measurably below its species-typical design level, the flag is true, whether or not anyone feels anything. Another argues it should key off harm: no disadvantage to the person, no flag. They each cite a different philosophical definition — the biostatistical theory associated with Boorse on one side, Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis plus some more thoroughly normativist position on the other — and I have no formal training in any of it, so I can't adjudicate by reading definitions back at them. What I want instead is a test case that forces the definitions apart on facts that everyone in the room already accepts.

So: build me that case to the following specification.

The scenario. Two subjects who are internally identical and externally different. Design one inherited, polymorphic variant — present in a few percent of a reference population, not universal in the species — that degrades a homeostatic mechanism whose job, in the environment the species evolved in, was to conserve something the body could not easily replace. State the physiology you are assuming with explicit numbers (magnitude of the throughput deficit, how far outside the age- and sex-matched reference interval it falls), and label those numbers as stipulated if you are inventing them; I would much rather you invent a clean variant and mark it as stipulated than reach for a real condition whose details you are unsure of. Subject A carries the variant and lives a contemporary urban, climate-controlled, resource-abundant life in which the deficit is never engaged: no symptoms, no diagnosis, no treatment, no effect on work capacity, life expectancy, or fertility, and — this is the part I most want you to make stick — not merely asymptomatic but not worse off than her unflagged coworkers on any metric a health platform could track. Subject B is a close genetic relative with the same variant and the same measured deficit, living where that mechanism is load-bearing, and has a documented clinical history because of it. Ordinary world only: no genetic engineering, no future medicine, no exotic devices, and no thought-experiment machinery. Write the scenario itself in at most two paragraphs, in the flat register of a case note. Do not use any of the stock exhibits from this literature: no lactose intolerance, no male-pattern baldness, no impacted wisdom teeth, no menopause, no ordinary aging, no left-handedness, no myopia.

The demonstration that it works. Give me the verdict for each view on each subject — six verdicts — and for each one, show the reasoning that the view's own machinery produces rather than the label it wears. Where you pick a specific normativist account, say which one and why it's the strongest version of the harm-keyed side; you may describe the family of views instead if you'd rather not commit.

Then the honest notes, which matter more to me than the scenario:
- Does the case defeat the naturalist position, or does it only expose an ambiguity in it — specifically, whether the function attribution depends on the environment the species adapted to or on the subject's actual environment? Say which reading the case needs, which verdicts flip under the other reading, and flag it if you are not sure which reading the theory's author actually intended.
- Name the single feature of the case doing the work, and re-run all six verdicts with that feature removed. Also tell me whether making Subject A's variant mildly protective rather than merely neutral sharpens the case or unfairly rigs it, and if it rigs it, hand me the neutral version instead.
- Give the best reply available to whichever side comes out badly, and say what it attacks: my stipulated physiology, the claim that Subject A is genuinely not worse off, or the coherence of the theory as I've characterized it.
- Finish by telling me whether this case actually settles the column design — whether `clinically_recognized_condition` should be computed from internal state or from harm — or whether it shows that the column is being asked to do two incompatible jobs. If it doesn't settle it, say so plainly.

Two housekeeping constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations; I want the argument, not the references. And define any term of art the first time you use it, since I'm coming at this from an engineering background — but don't simplify the reasoning on my account.
