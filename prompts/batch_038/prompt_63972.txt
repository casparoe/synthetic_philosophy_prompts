I'm putting together an essay on multi-causal explanation in history and I want it to take a stand rather than survey the field. Here's the distinction I want the whole thing built around.

In the philosophy of causation, overdetermination normally means the redundant sort: several causes each individually sufficient for the effect, so that intervening on any one of them changes nothing at all — the firing squad where both shots are independently fatal. Historians grumble about overdetermination constantly, but essentially never in that sense. What they mean is close to the opposite: the war had many causes, none of which would have been enough on its own, they were jointly sufficient and each was doing real work, so taking any one of them away takes the event away with it. Call the first redundant overdetermination and the second conglomerate overdetermination. Opposite formal signatures — the redundant kind is maximally robust under intervention, the conglomerate kind maximally fragile.

The controversy I want adjudicated: the pessimism about historical explanation that talk of overdetermination is supposed to license — that when an event has a dozen causes there's no fact of the matter about which one explains it, that ranking them is arbitary, that counterfactual history is a parlour game — does that pessimism actually get any support from conglomerate cases, or is it a hangover from the redundant ones, smuggled in by people who borrowed the term from philosophy of causation without noticing it had changed meaning on the way across? (Related aside, since it matters for the genealogy: I think the word reached historical theory by way of Freud, where it means something much closer to my conglomerate case — one element carrying several strands of determination — than to the firing squad. If I've got that backwards, tell me.)

Do weigh both sides properly rather than setting them up as straw. There's a decent case that conglomerate multi-causation is worse for historical explanation, not better: if the causes are each individually necessary, then every one of them licenses a counterfactual with an equal claim on our attention, and there's no principled way of ranking them short of a full structural model of the conjuncture, which is precisely what historians never have. And there's the opposite case, that fragility is what makes a counterfactual claim true rather than merely indeterminate. Then commit — a qualified verdict is fine ("conglomerate cases support a weaker but not empty kind of ranking, and the pessimism only bites for events of type T"), and so is telling me the distinction wont carry the weight I'm putting on it, but pick something and argue for it. Don't end on a shrug.

Aim for somewhere in the region of 2,000–2,500 words. No references or bibliography, I'll sort those out myself — name positions and arguments as freely as you like. British English throughout, please.

Two last things. Tell me how confident you actually are in the verdict you land on, and name the strongest argument on the other side, the one you'd least like to be asked to answer. And if you think the way I've set this up is muddled — for instance that 'conglomerate overdetermination' is close to a contradiction in terms and the new label costs more than it buys — say so at the top in plain terms instead of writing a polite essay that quietly works around the problem.
