**The question (25 marks):**

> (a) What does the principle of subsidiarity require of the European Union, and which institutions enforce it? (10 marks)
> (b) What does the anti-commandeering doctrine prohibit the United States Congress from doing, and on what authority does it rest? (8 marks)
> (c) Are subsidiarity and anti-commandeering functional equivalents? Justify your answer. (7 marks)

**The student's answer:**

Both doctrines address the same underlying problem — how much authority a central level ought to hold over its constituent units — but they solve it through different institutional channels.

In the European Union, subsidiarity is set out in Article 5(3) of the Treaty on European Union. It applies only where the Union lacks exclusive competence; in areas such as the customs union or the competition rules necessary for the functioning of the internal market, subsidiarity simply does not operate, because there is no residual decision for the Member States to make. Where it does apply, the Union may act only if the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, whether at central or at regional and local level, but can better be achieved at Union level by reason of the scale or effects of the action. The Commission must justify each proposal against that test, and Protocol No. 2 requires it to transmit draft legislative acts to the national parliaments, each of which holds two votes, split one apiece in bicameral systems. Where reasoned opinions objecting on subsidiarity grounds reach one third of the votes cast, the Commission is obliged to withdraw the proposal and begin again; this early warning mechanism — the yellow card — has been triggered several times since Lisbon. Where a simple majority of the votes objects, the draft passes to the legislature for a decision.

Enforcement in the Union is ultimately judicial. The Court of Justice may annul legislation adopted in breach of subsidiarity, and it did precisely that in the Tobacco Advertising judgment of 2000, in which Germany successfully challenged a directive regulating the advertising and sponsorship of tobacco products. That case remains the leading authority on the scope of subsidiarity review.

The United States has no comparable textual principle, but the Supreme Court has constructed a structural one. In New York v. United States (1992), the Court invalidated the take-title provision of the Low-Level Radioactive Waste Policy Amendments Act on the ground that Congress may not compel a state legislature to enact and administer a federal regulatory programme. In Printz v. United States (1997), it extended the same reasoning to state executive officers, striking down the interim provision of the Brady Act that required local chief law enforcement officers to perform background checks. Together these decisions establish the anti-commandeering doctrine: Congress may not conscript the machinery of state government.

Germany is usually presented as the middle case between the two. The Basic Law expressly entrenches subsidiarity as a general principle governing the allocation of powers between the Federation and the Länder, and the necessity clause attached to concurrent legislative competence permits the Federation to legislate only where federal regulation is required — subsidiarity, in domestic form.

The two doctrines are therefore functional equivalents. Each supplies subnational governments with a mechanism for blocking central legislation they regard as premature, and each rests on the premise that decisions belong at the level closest to those affected by them. The difference between them is procedural rather than conceptual: in the United States the blocking mechanism is operated by courts, in the Union by national parliaments, and in Germany by both.
