I build and calibrate agent-based models of norm dynamics: populations of boundedly rational agents embedded in a network, each carrying a strategy, updating by imitation or best response, with payoff functions that include reputation, conformity, and coordination terms. I am fully fluent in that vocabulary — strategies and payoff functions, update and imitation rules, network topology and clustering, absorbing states and basins of attraction, critical mass, tipping thresholds, replicator-style dynamics, stochastic drift, exogenous payoff shocks, private versus common knowledge. I have no philosophical training beyond summaries, and I would rather not be given any.

The position I want restated is a two-part claim about how deeply entrenched practices actually come to an end. The first part is Bernard Williams's point about the moral argument from inconsistency: telling a society that some practice it tolerates contradicts principles that society already accepts is, historically, a very weak explanation of the practice's abolition, since the arguments against slavery were available for centuries and by themselves moved nothing; what changed was something more like a shift in sensibility, and it appears to have tracked social and economic change rather than the working-through of an inference. The second part is Kwame Anthony Appiah's account of moral revolutions, on which practices such as dueling among gentlemen, footbinding, and the Atlantic slave trade collapsed when the honor code governing them was rewritten — when continuing the practice stopped conferring standing and started costing it — together with his insistence that honor is a motive of a distinct kind, irreducibly social, because it consists in being publicly recognized as entitled to respect.

Restate both parts, claim by claim, in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. For each claim, say whether it maps cleanly onto something I already have — equilibrium selection, coordination failure, a payoff shock, a change in network structure, a shift in the update rule — or whether it only translates with distortion, and if so, name the distortion precisely. Above all, identify what has no expression in my framework at all.

Two things I want you to be scrupulous about. First, do not smuggle: it would be easy to define honor as a reputation term in the utility function, define the rewriting of an honor code as an exogenous change to that term, and then announce a successful translation. If that is in fact what the translation requires, say so explicitly and mark it as an interpretive imposition rather than a mapping. Second, tell me whether the residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophers are saying less than it appears. Specifically: is there a genuine difference, expressible or inexpressible in my terms, between an agent's believing a practice permissible, finding it honorable, and finding it intelligible as something one might do at all — or is that tripartite distinction rhetorical padding over a single preference parameter? And if honor just is a reputation payoff, does the Williams–Appiah thesis reduce to the empty claim that preferences changed for reasons outside the model, or does it retain structural content about how the change had to be organized — public rather than private, discontinuous, requiring the standing of the relevant population to be redefined?

Finally, if any part of the account does survive translation as a substantive structural claim, state what it would take to represent it in a model of the kind I build, and whether it yields a prediction that distinguishes an honor-driven transition from an ordinary tipping phenomenon driven by updated beliefs about payoffs.
