Jury duty in ten days — assault case that comes down to one eyewitness ID — and to keep myself honest I've written out what I actually think knowledge is. Tell me whether this hangs together.

1. Knowing p requires that if p were false I wouldn't believe p. That's the Nozick thing I like, and it's why I distrust the ID: if it hadn't been the defendant, that witness would still have pointed at whoever in the lineup matched the description she gave.

2. When you run that test you hold the actual belief-forming process fixed and you individuate it finely — "seeing that face in that lighting," not "vision." Otherwise my grandmother wouldnt have known her grandson was alive when she saw him in her doorway, since in the worlds where he'd died she'd have been spared the news and told he was fine.

3. Safety matters too: knowledge shouldn't be belief that could easily have been false. I hold that on top of (1), as a second necessary condition.

4. Closure under known entailment fails. I know I have hands, I don't know that I'm not a handless brain in a vat. I'm fine living with that pairing.

5. Knowledge is the bar for asserting something flat-out and for relying on it in a decision — which is roughly what I plan to tell the other jurors.

6. My own rule, call it the same-work rule: whatever does the work in the exotic cases has to do the same work in the boring ones, and vice versa. No special pleading.

Don't take my word for where the problems are; I'm deliberately not telling you which of these I'd sacrifice. Rank the tensions by severity, say which ones are only apparent and why they dissolve, and tell me what the cheapest fix to the worst one actually costs.
