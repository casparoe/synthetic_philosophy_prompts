I am in the fourth week of a seminar on Buddhist moral philosophy, and our current unit is the dispute over whether Buddhist ethics is a form of consequentialism. Our two assigned readings take opposite sides, and after class two of my classmates, Noor and Ellis, argued about it for the better part of an hour. I came away unable to say which of them had the better of it, and I would like a ruling on each step rather than a survey of the literature.

Here is their exchange, as closely as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Noor began from the claim that the tradition's own criterion is outcome-shaped: an act is skillful insofar as it ripens well, and the precepts are training rules whose entire point is the reduction of suffering. Her exhibit was the Mahāyāna story of the bodhisattva as a ship's captain who kills a passenger planning to massacre the merchants aboard, reasoning that the killing saves the merchants and also spares the would-be killer the far worse karmic fruit of his intended deed — and who is said to gain merit rather than demerit for it. For her, that shows the prohibitions are defeasible in precisely the way a consequentialist would predict.

(2) Ellis replied that the same tradition treats intention as the karmically decisive element, and that the monastic code makes this vivid: the rule entailing expulsion for destroying life covers not only killing, but hiring a killer, urging a person to die, and even describing the advantages of death to someone who wants to die. In that last case, everything a consequentialist could want is present — the person consents, and on the story's own terms goes to a better rebirth — and the act is still a defeat. So the tradition recognizes agent-relative constraints that no balance of consequences overrides, which is exactly what consequentialism denies.

(3) Noor said the constraint reading is optional. The prohibition on praising death is explicable from its consequences: a settled practice of helping people die destroys the trust a community depends on and becomes a cover for killing the vulnerable. And the permission to eat meat that one has not seen, heard, or suspected to have been killed for oneself is a rule about one's own causal contribution to harm — consequentialist structure again. Then she pressed what she took to be decisive: Śāntideva's argument, in the meditation chapter of his guide to the bodhisattva path, that because there is no owner of suffering, suffering as such is to be warded off, and that the distinction between my pain and yours is a convention with no normative weight. That, she said, is impartial maximization, and it explicitly refuses the agent-relative shelter Ellis wants.

(4) Ellis answered that this argument is an exercise in transforming a mind rather than a decision procedure — it sits inside a practice of exchanging self and other — and that the self-sacrifice stories do not maximize anything either. In the hare's case the recipient turns out to be a deity in disguise, so no welfare is actually produced; what the story displays is the perfection of giving. More broadly, he argued, the tradition's highest good is cessation, which is not a pleasant state of experience, so a theory whose utility is the extinction of the experiencer cannot be consequentialism in any recognizable sense: there is no one for whom the outcome is good.

(5) Noor said that objection proves too much. Consequentialism is neutral about what the good is, and agent-based versions locate it in states of character rather than in episodes of pleasure. On that reading the hare story and the bodhisattva's vows are exactly what an agent-based consequentialist would expect, since non-attachment is the reliable means to reducing suffering everywhere.

(6) Ellis said that if any value theory qualifies, the contrast with virtue ethics collapses, and the live question — whether Buddhist ethics admits constraints that outcomes cannot override — is left where it began. Noor said the label still earns its keep, because the question is whether the rightness of an act is fixed by the value of the resulting state of affairs, and that question has a determinate answer.

They then turned to the case our instructor circulated, and split on it. A nurse in an epidemic hospital knows there is a concealed reserve of treatment reserved for the patients likeliest to die without it. Families are pressing at the doors; a surge the previous week killed two people. She can either (a) tell the families that the ward is at capacity and the supply exhausted — false, but it disperses them — or (b) leave her post, in which case a colleague, entirely willing, will say exactly the same thing to exactly the same effect. Stipulations: the number of patients saved, the families' distress, the hospital's functioning, and the subsequent mental trajectory of every third party are identical under (a) and (b). The only difference is who speaks the falsehood, and hence who bears the breach of the precept and the small hardening of heart she knows will follow. She would lie from compassion; so would the colleague. Our instructor also noted that the case is built so the captain analogy does not carry over, since here the better outcome is secured either way and absorbing the karmic cost is gratuitous rather than necessary.

What I would like from you:

1. A ruling on each of moves (1) through (6), individually: who has the better of it and why. Where a move merely restates a datum the other side can absorb equally well, say so plainly — I suspect several of them do that, and I want to know which.
2. Flag any step where you think one of them has mischaracterized the doctrine or the case, as opposed to simply drawing a different inference from it.
3. Which single item in the whole exchange does the most discriminating work — the one hardest for the opposing side to reinterpret — and why that one rather than another.
4. A verdict on the instructor's case: required, permitted, or forbidden, under each of the two readings, with the reasoning. If the two readings do not in fact diverge on it, say so and describe what alteration of the case would force them apart. Also say whether you agree that the captain analogy fails here.
5. Whether the disagreement between them is substantive or partly terminological. If it is partly terminological, isolate the substantive residue and give me your own view on that residue.

I am not looking for references, a bibliography, or a list of further readings — just the reasoning, including where you think it is weakest.
