I have been trying to establish what I actually believe about why it is worth seeking out art that makes one miserable, and I have gone about it case by case rather than by theory. Below are twelve verdicts I consider settled, one pair I cannot reconcile, and then what I would like in return.

The verdicts.

(1) There is a film I know will devastate me — an animal dies near the end, and I have seen it often enough to recite the scene. On an ordinary day I choose it deliberately, and afterwards I say the two hours were worth it, the desolation included.

(2) The same film, in a week when I am already low: I avoid it, and if it comes on anyway what I feel is not enrichment but something adjacent to resentment. The film has not changed.

(3) A sad song is among the few things I will play thirty times in a week. The ache appears to be the point rather than the price.

(4) A sad song was played once at the funeral of someone I loved. It spoiled the funeral, and I have not been able to listen to it since. Something valuable seems to have been destroyed, and to have taken the song with it.

(5) Of two works that devastate me equally, I rank the one whose suffering turns out to be pointless above the one whose suffering is redeemed, meaningful, or instrumental to a lesson. Consolation cheapens it.

(6) But pointlessness works on me only if I am already attached to the sufferers. Where the characters are merely unpleasant, an unredeemed catastrophe leaves me cold and I abandon the book.

(7) Afterwards I describe the devastation to friends with something like pride. I seem to enjoy having been wrecked, and to want it known.

(8) When a work visibly strains to make me weep — swelling strings, a dying child in slow motion — I feel handled, and nothing of value occurs. Yet the film in (1) is engineered to devastate me as well, and I know it is.

(9) A documentary about real, documented suffering: worth watching, and I am glad I did. A letter from an actual person describing the same events to me directly: I find that I want it to stop, and I am faintly contemptuous of myself for wanting that.

(10) The grief I feel for a fictional character is not something I am pretending to feel. Nevertheless I would refuse an offer to alter the story so that they survive, and I would not intervene if I could. For a real death I would accept any such offer at once.

(11) A sad ending in a game that my own decisions produced is harder to bear than the identical ending merely witnessed — and I judge it the better experience precisely for being harder to bear.

(12) I sometimes weep at works I think are bad, and I do not retract the judgment. That it is terrible but that it got me is something I can say with no sense of contradiction.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (8) against (1) and (11). If engineered sadness is what I resent, why is the devastator I choose on purpose welcome, and why does having more authorship over my own pain make for a better experience rather than a worse one? A close second is (5) against (6): pointlessness is what I prize, but only under a condition — prior attachment — that looks suspiciously like the beginning of consolation.

What I want back:

- A principle, or a small set of candidate principles, precise enough that I could apply it to a case I have never encountered. It should state which features of the situation matter and how they combine into a verdict of worthwhile, not worthwhile, or actively spoiled. If two or more factors interact, name each and specify what happens when they conflict.
- For every clause, the verdict above that forces it. I want to see which of my cases is doing the work of which part of the principle, so that I can tell whether a clause is load-bearing or decorative.
- Its verdicts on three situations I have left unadjudicated: someone who seeks out grief-inducing art about a loss they have not suffered, in order to rehearse it; a work that devastates me and of which I am ashamed to have been devastated; and a case in which the sadness arrives exactly as designed, but I notice the design at the very moment of the tears. If the principle is silent on any of these, say that it is silent rather than improvising.
- A candid statement of which of the twelve verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If there are two competing versions — one that saves all twelve at the cost of complexity, one that is simpler and overturns one or two — give me both, and say which you would adopt and why.
- An explicit ruling on whether (10) is consistent with the principle at all, or whether it compels me to surrender one of its two halves: that the grief is genuine, or that I would refuse to change the story.

I am not asking for the literature on this question, nor for the names of positions or a list of what to read; I would rather have the principle than the taxonomy it belongs to.
