I co-teach a one-off joint session for third-years — about 20 of them, half physics, half philosophy of science — 80 minutes, a room with movable chairs, two whiteboards, index cards, and a jar of pennies. No lab, no laptops. Topic is Maxwell's demon, and I want them to leave having actually run into the problem instead of hearing me summarize it. Last year I lectured the Bennett line (measurement can in principle be done reversibly, so the bill comes due later, when the demon's notebook fills up and it has to wipe a slot to keep going) and it went straight over their heads. They nodded politely and then in the take-home essays wrote things like 'the demon spends energy when it looks at the molecule,' which is exactly the wrong place to put the cost.

So: design me a game that puts them in the demon's shoes. The single thing I most want them to feel is the asymmetry between acquiring information and getting rid of it — that learning seems free, that hoarding what you've learned also seems free, and that the wall you eventually hit is the finite notebook. Give the demons a genuinely small memory, a handful of slots, and make the rounds long enough that they run out and are forced to clear one under pressure. I'd like the "gas" to be actual students or coins on a desk rather than a simulation, since the physicality is part of the point, and I want a token economy for the scoring so nobody needs real money, with something playing the role of the second-law side that collects whenever a demon pays. One variant I'd like built in as a role, if you can make it work: a demon with an unlimited scroll who writes everything down and never erases anything, who appears to be winning, and whom the rest of the class has to rule on — legal win or cheat? Give me roles, exact rules, what's literally written on the cards, how many minutes each phase gets, and a room arrangement where all 20 people are doing something rather than 4 playing and 16 watching. And they shouldn't hear the words Landauer or irreversibility until after they've hit the wall.

Two things I want flagged explicitly. First the debrief: map the moments they'll actually remember — the notebook filling, the argument over whether wiping counts as a move that ends your turn, the demon who insists on memorizing everything in their own head rather than writing it down — onto the real physics, and tell me how far the card-game analogy survives before it breaks, because 'erasing a card' is not paying kT ln 2 and I don't want them leaving with a fuzzy bookkeeping version of the principle. Be blunt about which feature of the argument the game cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply verbally. Second, there's always at least one student who says the whole thing is rigged — that the erasure cost is just a rule the facilitator invented so the second law would win. Give me a real answer for that person that doesn't require equipment, and then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: where the rules will turn out ambiguous, where the timing will blow up, and what I should cut if we're ten minutes behind at the halfway mark.
