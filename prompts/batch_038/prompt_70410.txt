Forty-one years of solid-state physics and now I sit around reading philosophy, which is a bit like being a grad student again except nobody grades you. Lately I've been chewing on Scheffler's *Death and the Afterlife* material — the setup where you learn the Earth will be destroyed by an asteroid shortly after you die, versus a world where you just die normally yourself. His point, roughly, is that most of us would find the first world much worse, and from that he draws the general claim that we're more invested in humanity carrying on after our deaths than we are in our own survival. Fine by me. But I keep feeling like the discussion jumps from "this intuition feels strong" straight to "here's the reason why" without much time spent on which *mechanism* is actually doing the work.

So I made my own list of candidate mechanisms — the things that might make my projects depend on there being people around later:

1. Audience: papers, gardens, luthier work, buildings are partly made *for* someone to take up.
2. Contribution: an activity is valuable partly as a move in a collective project that outlives it, basic research being the cleanest case.
3. Legacy/testimony: wanting to have been the person who did the thing, and to be known as such.
4. Completion: projects have internal arcs, and part of the value is the arc finishing, whether or not anyone marks it.
5. Curiosity: I simply want to know how it turns out, and a world with no "how it turns out" frustrates that appetite.
6. Derivative/insurance: much of the apparent depth is instrumental — archives get maintained, pensions get paid, journals keep existing — and it only looks like deep value because it's tangled up with things I do care about directly.

Here's my twist, and I want you to take it seriously rather than politely. Rank these six into tiers by what I'll call robustness-under-blindess: how much of the mechanism's explanatory force survives if you stipulate that the future people exist and flourish but are permanently and completely unaware of us — no records, no ruins, no traceable traditions, they never learn our names or that we were ever there. My suspicion is that the standard discussion quietly assumes an audience, and that when you switch it off the list collapses to two items.

Also give each one a number, 0 to 1, for behavioural distinctness: could you in principle tell this mechanism apart from the others by going and looking at what people actually do — surveys, lab studies, the way physicists behave about uncredited priority, whatever. 0 means it predicts nothing the others don't already predict, 1 means it's cleanly seperable.

Two more things. Tell me where you think my list is wrong — is one of the six bogus, are two of them the same thing wearing different hats, is there an obvious seventh I've missed because I'm not a philosopher. And then the part I actually care about: what would change your mind? Which argument or piece of evidence would move a mechanism from your top tier to your bottom tier, and where in the ranking is your reasoning most likely to break down? In particular, if you think the blindness stipulation is incoherent or just unfair — that you can't really conceive of a future with no causal or informational connection to the past — say so plainly and tell me what to swap in instead.
