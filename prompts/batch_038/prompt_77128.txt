The question, as it appeared on the exam:

Consider the following two-player normal-form game. Player 1 chooses a row, Player 2 chooses a column; Player 1's payoff is listed first.

```
            L          M          R
     A   (0, 2)     (3, 1)     (1, 0)
     B   (3, 1)     (0, 2)     (1, 0)
     C   (1, 0)     (1, 0)     (0, 3)
```

(a) [9 points] Find the set of strategies that survives iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies, where a mixed strategy is permitted to act as the dominating strategy. At each stage, exhibit the dominator and verify the required inequalities.

(b) [6 points] Your deletion in (a) proceeded in a particular order. Explain why the final set does not depend on that order. Would the same explanation establish that iterated deletion of *weakly* dominated strategies is order-independent? Explain, and if your answer is no, say concretely what can go wrong.

(c) [4 points] Is every strategy that survives part (a) rationalizable — that is, a best response to some belief about the other player's play? Justify your answer.

(d) [6 points] Find all Nash equilibria of the game. Explain the relationship between your procedure in (a) and the equilibrium set.

The student's answer, transcribed in full:

(a) Look first for pure dominators. Neither A nor B dominates the other: A = (0, 3, 1) and B = (3, 0, 1) each win in one column and lose in another, and they tie at R. C = (1, 1, 0) is not beaten by A alone (A is worse at L) or by B alone (B is worse at M). So nothing pure strictly dominates C.

Now take the mixed strategy σ = ½A + ½B. Against P2's mixture ½L + ½M, σ earns ½(1.5) + ½(1.5) = 1.5, while C earns ½(1) + ½(1) = 1.0 against the same mixture. So a rational Player 1 would never choose C: it does strictly worse in expectation. C is strictly dominated and can be deleted.

With C gone, Player 2's payoffs are L = (2, 1), M = (1, 2), R = (0, 0) across the surviving rows A and B. R is strictly dominated by L (and also by M), so delete R.

In the remaining 2×2, A = (0, 3) and B = (3, 1) are incomparable for Player 1, and L = (2, 1) and M = (1, 2) are incomparable for Player 2, and no mixture of two strategies can strictly dominate either member of the pair, since a mixture's payoff always lies between the two payoffs it mixes. The surviving set is therefore {A, B} × {L, M}.

(b) Deleting a strictly dominated strategy cannot change which of the remaining strategies are strictly dominated, because strict dominance requires a strict inequality against every strategy the opponent might play, and an inequality that holds against all of them holds against any subset of them. Hence any order of deletion yields the same final set. The same reasoning shows that iterated deletion of weakly dominated strategies is order-independent as well: a weak inequality that holds against every strategy of the opponent also holds against every strategy in any subset. Weak dominance simply licenses more deletions, so the surviving set may be smaller — but it is not order-dependent.

(c) Yes. In a two-player game, a strategy survives iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies if and only if it is a best response to some belief about the other player's play, so the survivors are exactly the rationalizable strategies. Concretely: A is a best response to M and B is a best response to L for Player 1; L is a best response to A and M is a best response to B for Player 2. Each survivor is a best response even to a pure strategy, so all four are rationalizable.

(d) Deleting a strictly dominated strategy never deletes a Nash equilibrium, because in any equilibrium each player's strategy is a best response to the others' and a strictly dominated strategy is never a best response to anything. Since (a) deleted only strictly dominated strategies, every equilibrium of the original game survives into the reduced 2×2, and no equilibrium of the reduced game is lost.

The reduced 2×2 has no pure equilibrium: against L, Player 1 prefers B; against M, Player 1 prefers A; against A, Player 2 prefers L; against B, Player 2 prefers M. So the equilibrium is mixed. Let Player 1 play A with probability a; Player 2 is indifferent when 2a + (1 − a) = a + 2(1 − a), giving a = ½. Let Player 2 play L with probability l; Player 1 is indifferent when 3(1 − l) = 3l + (1 − l), giving l = 2/5. The unique Nash equilibrium is therefore Player 1 playing (½, ½, 0) over (A, B, C) and Player 2 playing (2/5, 3/5, 0) over (L, M, R), with expected payoffs 9/5 and 3/2.
