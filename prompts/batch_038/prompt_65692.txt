drafting the methods chapter of my thesis on counterfactual history and my supervisor told me to write down what I actually believe first, then we'd go looking for cracks. so here's the list, no hedging:

1. I evaluate counterfactuals roughly the Lewis way — closest worlds where the antecedent is true, laws held fixed, only small localised departures from actuality, and no rewriting of general background conditions just to get the verdict I want.

2. I think that if the motorcade in Sarajevo hadn't taken that wrong turn and stalled with Princip standing on the corner, there is no war in the summer of 1914. Europe muddles through another July Crisis the way it muddled through the previous ones.

3. I also think the war was coming within about a decade anyway, given the alliance system, the mobilisation timetables that ran faster then diplomacy could, and the belief among the German leadership that their window of opportunity against Russia was closing.

4. I don't think 2 and 3 conflict, because a counterfactual is about what happens next, not about the long run. 2 is a short-window claim, 3 is a long-window one, different questions.

5. I'm sceptical of great-man explanation. At real decision points the individual agents are close to interchangeable — the structural conditions do the work and whoever is standing where they stood gets pushed roughly the same way.

6. I think people who insist the Industrial Revolution was a British fluke are wrong: something like it was going to happen somewhere in western Europe within a couple of decades regardless of which country got there first.

7. The methodological rule I want to be held to: whatever class of background facts I hold fixed when I assess one counterfactual, I hold fixed for the comparable ones too. I don't get to run a tight, structure-preserving edit on a case where I like the result and a loose one on a case where I don't.

8. And I think the credibility of a counterfactual falls off with how much background you have to rewrite — a cheap one needing a single localised change beats one needing a cascade.

so: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I want them ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad, and for each real one I want what has to go and what giving it up costs me — including whether the war case and the Industrial Revolution case are being handled by the same machinery or by two different ones that I've been pretending are the same. also tell me whether 7 and 8 end up doing the same work or fighting each other, and what the single cheapest revision is that cleans up the most. don't give me the general state of play on counterfactuals in historiography, I've read that, I want the audit of this specific package.
