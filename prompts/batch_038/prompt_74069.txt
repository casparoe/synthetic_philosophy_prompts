You are a philosopher of statistics and formal epistemology with a working knowledge of institutional forecasting practice. Before you draft anything, think carefully about what is actually being asked: the deliverable is not an answer to the reference class problem but a specification of what would count as answering it. Sketch, for yourself only, the inventory of cases and disputes that such a theory would have to resolve, and let the requirements fall out of that inventory.

I work in the evaluation office of a national audit institution. For large capital projects we require what is usually called reference class forecasting: the project team must locate a class of comparable past undertakings and derive its cost and schedule estimates from the observed distribution of outturns in that class, rather than from its own bottom-up scenario. The mandate is statutory. The difficulty is that the mandate tells us to use a reference class without telling us what makes one the right one. Teams can make almost any comparison set look defensible, and objectors can always nominate a different set with a worse outturn: the broader classes are more objective-seeming and less relevant, the narrower ones are more relevant and transparently gameable. I must be able to rule on these disputes in a hearing room, and I have no standard to rule by.

What I need from you is the specification I am missing: a set of desiderata that any adequate account of the choice of reference class must satisfy, such that I could hand the list to both parties in a dispute and ask them where their proposed class meets or fails it. Please write it to the following shape.

First, and briefly, list the raw material from which the requirements will be derived: the kinds of case a theory in this area has to handle. I have in mind at least the forecasting disputes of the sort described above, the statistical and probabilistic puzzles in which the choice of class appears to change the answer (the Monty Hall problem and the Doomsday argument are the standard exhibits), and the phenomenon in which a trend visible within every subclass reverses across the pooled classes. Explain in one or two sentences each what a theory must do about them, so that the reader can see which requirement is responding to which pressure. Do not assume I have these cases at my fingertips.

Second, write the desiderata themselves, in three families. (a) The problems the theory must resolve or explain away, and what counts as resolving them. (b) The distinctions it may not collapse — among them, the distinction between a class being legitimate to use and a proposition's simply being true, the distinction between the class and the body of information that picks it out, the distinction between how wide a class is and how homogeneous it is, and any others you think the literature routinely runs together. (c) The constraints it must respect if it is to be usable by an institution: it must be applicable by non-specialists under time pressure, it must operate where the historical record holds only a handful of comparables, and it must yield a verdict that a reviewer can interrogate rather than shrug at.

Third, for each requirement, state the consideration that motivates it, say who in the debate tends to insist on it and who tends to resist it, and mark it as non-negotiable for my purposes, negotiable, or a prejudice of one camp dressed up as a criterion. Be especially alert to the demand that a class be as narrow as the available evidence allows and the demand that it contain enough members to support a distribution: these look like rivals, and I want to know whether they are.

Fourth, and this is the part I most want to see, identify any pairs or subsets of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. If a class cannot at once maximise relevance, contain enough members to support statistics, and remain uniquely determined by the evidence, say so, and show the incompatibility from the requirements themselves rather than by appeal to authority. Where a subset is jointly unsatisfiable, argue which member should be weakened or dropped, and what is lost by weakening it. If your conclusion is that the full list is unsatisfiable and that no account will ever make reference class choice a matter of routine, that is a substantive result, not a failure to deliver, and I would rather hear it plainly than receive a list quietly trimmed to make the candidates pass.

Fifth, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise plausible accounts, and why that one carries the weight.

Sixth, score the main contenders against the surviving list, but keep the scorecard to a short paragraph each: the straightforward frequentist view on which the correct class is simply the widest one with enough members; the view on which one must condition on all the relevant information one actually possesses; the pragmatic or interest-relative view on which which class is correct depends on the question being asked; and the view that the whole problem dissolves once the conditioning is done properly within a single Bayesian framework. Name no authors, no papers, and no publication dates, and do not invent a supporting quotation or a case study — I cannot check them and neither can you.

Close with two worked applications of your specification, to be run in front of the parties. In the first, the project team proposes as its reference class all tunnel-boring rail projects completed since 1990, while the objecting ministry proposes only those built through water-bearing chalk at comparable depth. In the second, the roles are reversed: the team proposes the narrow class and the objectors want the wide one. Walk the list through both, requirement by requirement, and state where your specification reaches a verdict, where it leaves the matter to the hearing, and — if it happens — where it delivers an answer that the party I would expect to win does not like. I would rather see the procedure fail visibly on one of these than see it flatter both sides.
