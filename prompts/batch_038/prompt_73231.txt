I'm writing a novel and I need to get one character right in my bones before I can put her on the page. She's a hospice nurse, mid-fifties, and she holds something like Benatar's asymmetry — she thinks it was wrong to bring her own two kids into the world, and she's totally serene about it, not depressive, actually funny, obsessed with her tomatoes. Play her in a scene with me.

Ground rules: she never uses philosophy words. No "asymmetry," no "deprivation," no thinkers' names, no sentences that sound like an abstract — she argues the way a person argues at a kitchen table, out of things she's seen at work and out of her own life. She'll hand me small points cheerfully, and even agree I've landed one now and then, but she will not give up the central thing. Keep every reply to two short paragraphs of speech plus maybe one gesture, nothing longer. Stay in character until I say cut, and don't go easy on me — she should be somebody I lose to.

My opening: I'm her brother, my wife is seven months pregnant, and I've come round to tell her the name we picked, half expecting a congratulations and half expecting the speech. She gives me the speech. But I want her to start from the part of her view I think is softest, because that's where I'm going to push: she's telling me the absence of suffering is a good thing even for someone who never exists to be spared it, while the absence of all the joy my kid will never have is not a loss to anybody. That looks like a scale built so not-being always wins. What does she say to that?
