I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind seminar, and mental causation is this week's topic. A friend lent me a trade-press book about consciousness, and one chapter glosses Jaegwon Kim's exclusion argument in the following terms:

"Kim's exclusion argument goes like this. Physics is causally closed: every physical event has a physical cause sufficient to produce it, and neural events are physical. So when you decide to raise your arm and your arm goes up, the firing neurons are already a sufficient cause of the movement. Your decision can't also be a cause of it, because that would mean one and the same event has two independent sufficient causes — systematic overdetermination of a kind we never find anywhere else in nature, and nobody seriously believes it happens. Kim proved, then, that if physicalism is true, mental states are epiphenomenal: they are real, they are produced by the brain, but they never produce anything. That is why neuroscience has effectively settled the question of free will — the feeling of choosing is a story the brain tells after the neurons have already done the work. The only escape is to say that mental states simply are brain states, but that is changing the subject rather than answering the argument."

I walked into the week inclined to think the argument works, and that passage is a large part of why. In seminar, though, my instructor said something about closure not being the same claim as determinism, and about the exclusion argument being a dilemma rather than a demonstration, and I could not follow either remark.
