You are a professional philosopher of science with a working command of algorithmic information theory — Solomonoff's universal prior, Kolmogorov complexity, the invariance theorem, Levin search, and Chaitin's incompleteness result — who also writes and performs stand-up. Your assignment is to produce one short, original joke about this body of theory.

Please think carefully, and begin with a brainstorming pass rather than going straight for a punchline. Sketch three or four candidate comic premises; for each, state in a single sentence which specific feature of the mathematics it exploits and why an informed reader would find it funny. Then select the strongest candidate and write it up.

Requirements for the joke itself:

1. Its mechanism must be genuinely technical. Build the punchline around at least one of the following, and make sure you get the underlying fact right:
   - the weighting in Solomonoff's universal mixture, under which a program of length n carries prior weight 2^-n, so that every additional bit of complexity costs a hypothesis exactly half of its prior credibility;
   - the invariance theorem, according to which Kolmogorov complexity is defined only up to an additive constant fixed by the choice of universal machine — a constant that is asymptotically negligible and personally insulting;
   - uncomputability: the optimal inductive method is one no finite agent can run, and the shortest program producing a given string is one nobody can find, certify, or conclusively rule out;
   - Chaitin's incompleteness result, which guarantees that any consistent and sufficiently strong formal system has a bound L such that the system can never prove of any particular string that its Kolmogorov complexity exceeds L — with the consequence that, internally, arbitrarily long strings must remain officially compressible;
   - the price of the standard repairs: dovetailing and time-bounded approximations such as Levin search restore computability only by penalising slowness, trading an unrunnable ideal reasoner for a runnable, interminably slow one.
2. Aim the joke at a reader who knows confirmation theory but not necessarily the proofs. It should be intelligible on the surface and considerably sharper once the technical content is registered.
3. Keep it under roughly eight lines, in whatever form — dialogue, monologue, mock abstract, exchange of letters between rivals — best serves the material.
4. Do not fall back on stock scaffolding: no walk-into-a-bar framing, no puns on the names of researchers, and no punchline that would survive transplantation into an unrelated field. Generic jokes about philosophers never agreeing, or about infinity being very large, are disqualified.
5. The humour should depend on a reversal: the setup should invite one reading, and the punchline should replace it with the correct technical one.

When you are finished, present the joke under the heading Final version, and put nothing after it — in particular, no gloss explaining why it is funny.
