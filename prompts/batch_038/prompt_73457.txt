Long story short: I'm a postdoc in history and philosophy of science and our physics department's required "physics in context" module has a hole in it this term, so I've been volunteered to give one 75-minute session to about twenty-five third-years on how science changes its mind. Friday, 4pm, whiteboard only, the projector's been broken since October, and nobody in the room will have read a word of Kuhn — a couple will have picked up paradigm shift from a podcast or from thier school teachers using it to mean any big change at all. I've read Structure twice, once over Christmas, and I thought I understood it. Then I spent four hours on Sunday arguing about it with a friend of mine who's three years into a philosophy PhD, and I came away much less sure I understand the thing I'm supposed to teach, which is not a great position to be in eleven days from now. So: don't explain incommensurability to me, I've more or less got the explanation. Plan the session. What has to be in place before what, and why the order matters, given that half of them will be on their phones if the first ten minutes go badly.

The argument that wrecked me is also I think the useful bit. Her line was that the famous version — different paradigms see different worlds, the gestalt switch, the whole duck-then-rabbit business — is early, crude Kuhn, and that the late version is a much smaller and much more annoying-to-state claim about vocabulary: a handful of terms get re-sorted into a new taxonomy, so sentences that used to be true in the old arrangement can't even be written down in the new one, and the failure is local and demonstrable by actually attempting the translation rather than by imagining everybody got a mind transplant. She also said the well-known kill shot — that if two schemes really were untranslatable you couldn't recognise the second one as a scheme at all, so the whole idea collapses — misses the late version completely, because it never claimed total untranslatability, only that a residue of terms has no clean one-to-one mapping. And then she said the thing that's actually stuck with me for teaching purposes: the two failure modes here are exact opposites. Give them the early version and it comes out as magic, worlds multiplying, mysticism with a lab coat. Give them the late version badly and they do what she called the jargon shrug — oh, it just means they used different words, that's trivial, why are we here. I need the plan to thread between those and I can't see how, partly because I can feel myself sliding into the shrug already.

So build me the sequence. I want to use the atomism dispute as the carrying example rather than phlogiston or Copernicus, which is what every version of this lecture I've sat through uses and which they'll have half-heard anyway — Boltzmann against Ostwald and Mach, then Perrin's Brownian motion work at the end of it, with Ostwald apparently conceding and Mach never once doing so. But tell me honestly whether that case can bear the weight, because there's an obvious problem: Perrin looks exactly like the data settling the argument, which is the one thing my students already believe about science and which is arguably true in this instance. If it can't carry it, tell me what to use instead, and either way tell me what the example has to have and what it has to not have. Then the misconception map. The one I'm sure of is experiment decides eventually so this is all just delay; but there's also a contingent in the class who will love Kuhn for the wrong reasons and want to walk out saying science is just politics with better PR, and I don't want to hand them that. So: which misconceptions they arrive with, which ones the session itself will create if I sequence it wrong, and the exact point in the 75 minutes to head off each one. Plus a single question I can put to them in the last five minutes that would tell me whether the idea landed or whether they just learned to say the word — and tell me what the confident wrong answer to it sounds like, because that's what I'll actually be listening for. And say what I should leave out (I'm assuming the realism debate, whatever the philosophy department's schemes objection is called, and probably any normative claim about normal science being a good thing) and give me a line to say out loud about leaving it out, since I'd rather be upfront than get ambushed at minute seventy. No reading list and no suggestions about handing them a chapter — nothing gets read.
