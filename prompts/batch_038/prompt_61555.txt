I'm a postdoc finishing the systematic section of a monograph on a genus of small freshwater snails confined to the spring systems of a single arid basin, and my co-author — the senior author, a curator who has worked on the group for thirty years — makes every taxonomic call while I write the prose. Our meeting is next week, and I would rather anticipate her verdicts than be ambushed by them, so I want to reconstruct the rule she has actually been applying. Her track record across the populations we have already handled is as follows. Two populations in adjacent drainages with no contact, each diagnosable by shell characters and each monophyletic in mitochondrial data: split, two species. Two populations coexisting in the same spring complex, breeding in different seasons and on different substrates, nearly identical in shell but differing fixedly at two nuclear markers, with a few percent of introgression at other loci: split, two species. A ring of populations around the basin's dry rim, each link exchanging migrants with its neighbors, the two terminal links markedly different in shell frequency and ecology and not interbreeding where the chain closes, but with no shell character fixed in one terminal population and absent from every other population around the ring: lumped, one species. An obligately parthenogenetic lineage in the saline reaches, deeply divergent mitochondrially and morphologically distinct: called a species, but with a footnote conceding that the term was doing bookkeeping work there and nothing more. Two allopatric populations with no genetic data and a single shell character that varies within one of them: withheld, declared unsplittable on that evidence. Subfossil shells from a sediment core unlike anything now living in the basin: named as a species, flagged as a convenience, with an explicit refusal to say anything about their reproductive isolation. Two populations meeting at a narrow hybrid zone with strong assortative mating on both sides, stable diagnostic differences, different prey, and low but nonzero gene flow across the zone: split, two species. In an introduction she wrote years ago she remarked that a species name is a claim about a lineage's history rather than a measurement of its differences, which I have never known how to cash out.

The case I have to write up now is a spring on a mesa top above the basin floor. It was cut off from every other population during a documented dry interval roughly four thousand years ago; it has had no surface connection to anything since; its snails are diagnosable by two shell characters that occur in no other population in the genus in fixed form; and they occupy a thermal and chemical regime unlike any of the lowland springs. Mitochondrially, however, they sit nested inside the variation of one lowland population — call it the North Fork population — rather than forming a sister group to it, and the divergence times are shallow. Then, thirty years ago, a road cut reconnected the mesa outflow to the North Fork stream, and a colleague has just recovered first-generation hybrids in the contact reach below the cut. The hybrids appear fertile; no backcrosses have been found yet, and the mesa population is still diagnosable in the spring itself. Separately, the mesa spring is scheduled to be tapped for a municipal water supply, so the status we assign it is not idle, and the wording I draft has to survive review by a committee that reads taxonomic decisions as conservation decisions.

What I want is a prediction, argued rather than guessed at. First: what will she call the mesa population — a species, part of the North Fork population, or another withheld judgment — and does her answer differ depending on whether she reasons about it as it stood during four thousand years of isolation or as it stands now that the road cut exists? Second: lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with all seven of her past verdicts. I suspect at least a biological-species reading with an ecological fallback, a diagnosability-based phylogenetic reading, a cohesion-style reading keyed to exchangeability and common fate, and a lineage-as-individual reading are all still in the running, and that the ring case plus the parthenogenetic case may be what rules out the crudest versions; check that suspicion, discard or amend any rule the seven verdicts actually exclude, and say for each surviving rule what it predicts here. Third: give me your best single guess about her verdict and explain why you prefer that rule over the others given the pattern of her calls, including why she bothered with the footnotes she bothered with. Fourth: name the one further case or the one further observation — whether in this complex or a hypothetical one you construct, such as a population that is monophyletic and deeply divergent but freely interbreeding and undiagnosable, or the eventual fate of the mesa hybrids once backcrosses appear — that would most sharply separate the two strongest candidates. Finally, tell me what I should write in the draft so that the section coheres with her precedents even if she rules against my expectation, and state plainly what commitment that wording adopts either way.
