ok so our allotment committee had its AGM on tuesday and it went sideways over a flea beetle outbreak on the brassicas. Two of the members, Rob and Nadia, had a real arguement and I was supposedly taking minutes, so I wrote down roughly what each of them actually said. Neither of them is stupid, which is the problem — I couldn't tell you who I agreed with at any point in it. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each one, and then say what the two of them are jointly missing? Answer in British English please.

1. Rob: we should spray. The alternative to spraying isn't "no insects die", it's that our crop fails and we buy the same vegetables from the normal supply chain, which kills vastly more insects per kilo than one targeted spray on one plot. So spraying is the smaller harm here, and possibly the insect-saving option.

2. Nadia: you have no idea whether that's true. Nobody has counted the insects per tonne of either system, so "vastly more" is doing all the work and it's made up. And even if the numbers were there, you're aggregating across creatures that don't persist — an insect population isn't a person, so fewer deaths isn't automatically the right scorecard. Most of those eggs would never have made it to adulthood anyway.

3. Rob: fine, throw the numbers out. But then you have to tell me what's actually wrong with spraying. If you don't think fewer deaths is better, and you don't think there's anyone for whom dying is a misfortune, then your objection is about our own sensibilities rather than about the beetles. That's aesthetics wearing an ethics costume.

4. Nadia: no, my objection is about the manner of dying. A pyrethroid doesn't drop them dead — it knocks them down, some recover, the rest go slowly and paralysed, and plenty get picked off by birds while they can't move. If insects have anything like nociception at all, which is contested but not settled in your favour either, then how they die matters more than how many do. I'd sooner have a method that reliably kills fast even if it kills more than one that kills fewer but kills them badly. And separately, the spray hits everything that isn't the target: the pollinators, the predatory beetles, the whole non-target community. That's a different kind of harm from the flea beetles dying, and it doesn't show up in your tally at all.

5. Rob: that's your best point and it backfires. Your fast-kill options are the broad-spectrum ones. The tools that leave non-targets alone are the narrow ones — Bt, growth regulators, pheromone confusion, releasing parasitoids. A growth regulator stops the larvae moulting, so yes it's slow, but those larvae never become adults laying hundreds of eggs each, which makes it the lowest total-death option available. You're optimising for the phenomenology of one insect's last hour and ignoring the arithmetic that decides how many insects exist next year.

6. Nadia: and you're treating lowest-total-death as if it were self-evidently good. Preventing larvae from ever existing doesn't benefit anybody — there's no subject who is better off for not having hatched. So your humane-on-paper option is only better on a metric that doesn't track any individual's welfare, and the one thing we can both agree is bad, an animal going through a bad death, is exactly the thing your metric can't see.

After that someone from the far end of the hut said that if insect numbers are really falling as fast as people claim then the obvious answer is to do nothing at all, and both of them said no, for completely different reasons, and we ran out of time.

Please rate each of the six moves somewhere on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating in a sentence or two, then give a verdict on who's more right overall and what the pair of them are both ignoring. I don't want a reading list or any citations — just the adjudication.
