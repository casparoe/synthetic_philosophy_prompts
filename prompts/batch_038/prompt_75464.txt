I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind course and I need one essay question for the final — 90 minutes, worth 25 marks — on whether the brain is literally a computer. I want it built around a case I've been turning over in my head, so that nobody can get through it by reciting Putnam and Searle on triviality or Block on Blockhead. The case, roughly: a neuroscientist convinces you that some creature's neural dynamics admit two computational descriptions that fit the actual causal structure equally well, including every counterfactual dependency you'd want to check, but the two disagree about content. Under one, the system carries representations with determinate propositional contents and does belief-desire style inference; under the other it's a hierarchical prediction machine whose states are just error-signalling parameters and nothing has content in that first sense at all. There's no outside interpreter to appeal to in either case, and no third description that both are approximating.

The question should make students say what would have to be true for this to be a genuine problem for computationalism rather than a merely verbal redescription, and what could possibly settle which description is correct — or whether "correct" is a confused thing to be asking for here. A top answer has to engage with whether causal and counterfactual constraints on implementation can do the tie-breaking work, and it has to say something about wether fixing content is even part of the job description of an implementation relation. Please answer as a philosopher of mind who has set exams like this many times, and think carefully before committing to anything: start by brainstorming out loud about what the question is really testing and what the two or three most tempting bad answers are, then give me the question exactly as I'd print it on the paper, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme saying which specific moves earn marks, which common errors cost them, and how an A differs from a solid B.

Write the whole thing in American English and keep it in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. The marking scheme should read like paragraphs in an email, with the mark allocations stated in the sentences themselves.
