Three weeks from now I am presenting a dissertation chapter at a joint session of our history of economic thought workshop and the economics department's monetary seminar. The room will run to about fifteen people: monetary economists, one finance person who works on payment systems and central bank digital currency, two or three historians of philosophy who work on eighteenth-century British thought and know Berkeley properly, and nobody who works on idealism or on the philosophy of language. The economists have encountered at most a paragraph of Berkeley and are expecting a pamphleteer; the Berkeley people are allergic to anachronism and will be listening for it.

The chapter argues that Berkeley's monetary writings in The Querist are not amateur quantity theory dressed in patriotic rhetoric, but a deliberate application of the theory of signs he develops in Alciphron VII. On my reading, money for Berkeley is a sign that stands for no idea — he asks whether the true idea of money "be not altogether that of a ticket or counter" — and its whole content is practical, consisting in its capacity to act on the will, which is why he asks whether money "be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry." Value is then not a relation between money and commodities but a relation between a token and the appetites it moves. That is supposed to explain three otherwise awkward features of the text: his indifference to giving any account of the price level; his willingness to contemplate a currency in which the denominations survive the disappearance of the bullion entirely; and the queries on his plan for a national bank — the very ones he later struck from the combined edition — whose point is that a purely conventional paper sign can do everything metal does. I want to claim the Alciphron parallel is load-bearing rather than decorative, and that it is what makes Berkeley a serious precursor of the view that money is a social technology for motivating labor rather than a commodity with a price.

I get twenty minutes and then questions, and I expect the questions to be brutal in two distinct registers.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: roughly the eight objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the chapter if I fumbled it. For every one, phrase it the way the person asking would phrase it — I want the economist's idiom and the Berkeley scholar's idiom kept sharply apart, since they will object to different features for different reasons — and then give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes while standing up, plus a note on precisely where that reply is soft. I know they will reach for the query about less money circulating swiftly being equivalent to more money circulating slowly, which reads like a velocity proposition before its time, so please deal with that one honestly rather than letting me off.

Alongside the list, tell me which of these I should concede in my opening remarks before anybody has to ask; which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive the session; and the question I am hoping nobody asks. I have a suspicion about what that last one is — something about what would falsify my reading, or about whether the semiotic parallel does any work that a plain reading of the text does not, or perhaps about whether anything in this follows from Berkeley's immaterialism at all — but I would rather you named it than have me guess.

Please be candid about your confidence: mark which objections and replies you think are well grounded in what Berkeley actually says and which you are extrapolating from how audiences of these types generally behave, and at the end single out the one point in the whole sheet you are least sure of and say why.
