I'm a third-year comp neuro PhD student and I've been roped into running one 75-minute session in our philosophy of mind grad seminar — the week is on the unity of consciousness and they're using split-brain cases as the centerpiece. I know the disconnection literature okay, but I've never taught philosophers and I'm honestly worried about teaching the lesson they'll arrive wanting, which is that the callosotomy experiments show there are two of them in there, full stop.

What I actually want them to be able to do on the walk out is see that as an inference the data doesn't obviously license. My framing, which you're welcome to attack: the lateralized-presentation experiments measure what each hemisphere can do when you force information down a single channel and force the response out through a single effector — they don't measure how many subjects there are. Moving from "the information couldn't get across in this apparatus" to "there are two streams of consciousness" smuggles in a claim about what a stream is supposed to be, and I want that smuggling to be the thing they catch.

So plan the session for me rather than explaining split brains at me. I don't need the content, I need the sequence and the load-bearing choices: what has to be in place before the counting question is even intelligible to them (I suspect something about what the tachistoscopic setup does and doesn't control, and something about what "one stream" is supposed to mean, but you tell me which comes first and why); which single case should carry the whole thing and what it needs to have and not have — my instinct is that the left-hemisphere interpreter paradigm, the chicken-claw / snow-scene one where the patient confabulates a tidy reason for what his left hand just did, is a trap, because its the most memorable thing in the literature and it'll hijack 75 minutes into a confabulation-and-free-will debate, but maybe I'm wrong and it's the only paradigm concrete enough to do the work; the misconceptions they'll bring or generate, in the order they'll surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off — I'm expecting "two hemispheres therefore two people," "the interpreter shows all consciousness is post-hoc story," and "so normal brains are just mildly split-brained," plus whatever else you think philosophers specifically cook up; one question I can drop in the last ten minutes that would distinguish the underdetermination point landing from them just concluding the science is fuzzy; and what I should deliberately leave out and announce that I'm leaving out. I've got a whiteboard, one slide of the split-field display, and at least one hard-nosed physicalist who will want to say two subjects and go to lunch.

Don't give me a menu of viable orderings or a both-sides treatment of the example choice. Pick one, commit, and give me your reasons, including wherever you disagree with my hunch above. If you think the framing I've stated is itself a bad way to teach this, say so plainly and tell me what to swap in instead.
