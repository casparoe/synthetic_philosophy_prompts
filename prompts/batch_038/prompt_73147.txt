ok so I work on a cross-country survey consortium — we field the same questionnaire in something like 20 languages and then compare scores across countries, which means the translation step is basically the whole ballgame. Our protocol is really rigid and I've started to find it weird. Here's how it goes:

1. two independent translators render each item from the source language into the target language
2. a third person reconciles those two into a single version
3. a fourth translator, who has never seen the original, back-translates the reconciled version into the source language
4. a panel reads the original item and the back-translation side by side and rules on whether they are substantively equivalent
5. if the panel says no, the item goes back for another round — and after three failed rounds the item is dropped from every language version, including the original. So one untranslatable item about, say, whether you feel respected in your community kills the item for everybody.

the part that bugs me is step 4, which is doing an enormous amount of work and nobody on the panel can tell me what property they're actually adjudicating. they'll argu for twenty minutes about whether the back-translation "captures the same thing," and the evidence they accept is the translators' say-so, plus how they imagine respondants would answer, plus whether the two words feel swap-in-able in made-up sentences. and the verdicts arent consistent about what they're tracking: sometimes an item passes because people in both countries would answer it the same way, and sometimes an item fails because the target word also covers cases the original doesn't, even though the panel agrees people would still answer it identically. those two judgements cant both be about the same feature of the item, can they?

My advisor says this is just Quine's gavagai problem in a lab coat, and I think he's overselling it — our translators arent meeting a previously uncontacted people, they know the culture, and crucially they can just ask speakers what they meant, which is supposed to be the thing the radical translator can't do.

So what I want: what theory of meaning does this protocol presuppose, concretely? Spell out what step 4 has to assume for its verdicts to be the sort of thing that can be correct or incorrect at all, and where in the procedure that assumption is actually being relied on rather than just being vaguely in the air. Then — does the drop-from-all-languages rule in step 5 follow from that theory, or is it sitting there unsupported? if the alleged problem is that the item means different things in different languages, why would that license any claim about whether it means something determinate in the source language? And whether my advisor is right that the ability to ask speakers what they meant actually dissolves the gavagai worry, or whether asking speakers just reproduces the problem one level up.

there's also a proposal on the table to replace step 5: keep the item in every version, but flag it as non-comparable across countries while still using it for within-country analysis and for tracking change over time inside one country. Is that coherent, or does it just relocate the problem? and finish by telling me which rival views of meaning would license a totally different protocol, and what that protocol would actually look like — I want to be able to say to the committee "if you believe this instead, you'd have to change steps such-and-such," not just "philosophers disagree."
