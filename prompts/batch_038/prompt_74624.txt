I'm teaching an upper-level philosophy of history seminar this spring — 16 students, mostly philosophy majors with a history course or two under their belts. We meet once a week for 100 minutes, and next week we're on Braudel: the three time scales, the idea that geography and climate move so slowly they function less as background than as the actual subject matter, and the deflation of the event — the whole business about foam on the surface of a slow tide. I could lecture on that for twenty minutes and they'd nod and retain nothing, so I want to run something instead.

Design a game they play in class that makes them *feel* the asymmetry between the longue durée and event-history rather than being told about it. Two things I want them to run into on their own:

1. Event-level explanation is cheap and instantly available — you can always tell a story about the minister's mistress, the bad harvest that October, the misread dispatch — and that very availability is what makes it feel satisfying and conclusive.
2. Structural explanation is slow and expensive to build, and if you let people stake structural claims without saying what those claims *rule out*, the longue durée starts to explain everything and therefore nothing.

I want that second failure to happen to them, in the game, before I say a single word about falsifiability or underdetermination.

Constraints and required pieces:

- Rules, materials, and a scoring scheme that needs no real money — points, tokens, dried beans, whatever.
- Card decks or information packets at three scales, with an asymmetric cost/timing structure built in: the event-layer material should be cheap, plentiful, and decay in value as the game goes on; the structural-layer material should be expensive, arrive late, and keep paying across rounds. Don't give me a plain resource-allocation game — the decay and the lag have to live in actual mechanics so they notice it without anyone narrating it.
- A staking rule that stops structural claims from being a free win: to collect, a player commits in writing to some specific outcome their claim rules out, and gets penalized if the revealed history turns out compatible with their claim either way.
- The middle scale — conjunctures, cycles, price series, demography — is the one that always gets collapsed into one of the other two. Give it a genuinely distinct role so it isn't just a slightly pricier event card.
- A twist somewhere around the midpoint that makes at least one player discover that their confidently-held event explanation would have come out the same had the event gone the other way.
- Players should recieve packets at the door, and no player-facing material may use the phrase longue durée until the debrief.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes, including what the debrief actually gets.
- Concrete content. Real episode or invented, I don't care, but the cards have to name particular things — a soil type, a mountain pass, a failed monsoon, a tax farm, a dynastic marriage — not abstractions like economic conditions. If you go real, pick something you're sure about; I don't want to fact-check you.
- The debrief: map each mechanic onto the piece of Braudel it stands in for, and be explicit that he isn't saying events are unreal, just thin.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and the fix. I already expect at least two: someone games the staking rule with a trivially safe commitment, and the event-layer players quit once they watch their cards rot. Tell me the others.
- Close with an honest paragraph on what the activity structurally cannot reproduce. My guess is the archive — the years of parish registers and grain prices that make a structural claim feel earned rather than asserted — but say so if you disagree, and tell me what I'd have to say out loud to paper over the gap.

No fishbowl, no jigsaw reading, no think-pair-share dressed up as a game.
