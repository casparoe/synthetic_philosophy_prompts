I run welfare-assessment schemes for farmed animals. My working vocabulary is entirely empirical and aggregative: valenced affective states, the reliability of welfare indicators, functioning-based versus feeling-based accounts of welfare, lifetime welfare and thresholds for whether a life is worth living, the provision of positive experiences, and trade-offs across a herd or flock. I have no real philosophy training.

Restate as faithfully as you can the position on animals Robert Nozick sketches in Anarchy, State, and Utopia — that animals count for something and deserve weight in our deliberations but not the weight of moral side constraints; that rights-hood is reserved for beings possessing capacities such as rationality, self-consciousness, free will, moral agency, and the ability to shape a life in accordance with some overall plan of their own; that a maximizing principle for animals combined with a constraint-based principle for people is a slogan he floats without fully endorsing; and that eating meat turns on whether the marginal gustatory gain outweighs the moral cost. Translate this claim by claim into my vocabulary, and do not smuggle in the commitments of welfare science and present them as translation.

Then give me the residue report: which parts land cleanly on indicators, affective states, and lifetime welfare; which you had to bend to fit; and which have no expression at all in a framework built out of nothing but states of individuals and their aggregate balance. Finally, tell me whether the residue indicts my framework — that it has no way to represent an entitlement as distinct from a welfare outcome — or shows that Nozick's dividing line was doing less work than it appeared to. Use his own comparison case as the test: swinging a bat for the extra pleasure it gives, with the destruction of a cow's skull as an unintended side effect. Does the translated version still yield the verdict he wants?
