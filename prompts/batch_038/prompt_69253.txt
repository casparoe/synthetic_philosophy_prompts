> **Case.** In 2018 the transit authority of a mid-sized city replaced its conventional low-floor bus fleet with an automated guideway shuttle system. Three features have attracted comment. (i) The shuttles run on a fixed elevated guideway and cannot be boarded from street level; every station is entered through a fare gate and reached by lift, and the lifts are serviced by a private contractor under a separate agreement. (ii) The drive and control systems are leased rather than owned, and the manufacturer's contract provides that only its certified technicians may work on them; the authority's own mechanics were retrained or let go. (iii) The guideway's alignment was fixed by the authority's engineers on grounds of gradient and land cost, and the line now runs through the two neighbourhoods with the lowest car ownership in the city.
>
> **(a)** Explain the two ways in which, on Winner's account, artifacts can have politics. Assign each of the three features above to one of these ways, to both, or to neither, and justify your assignments. In your view, where does the politics sit in this case — in the artifact, in the decisions surrounding it, or in its consequences? **[6 marks: 2 for stating the two senses correctly, 2 for the assignments and their justification, 2 for the concluding claim about where politics sits]**
> **(b)** Does Winner's account commit him to technological determinism? > **(c)** Identify one feature of the case whose political stakes depend on a choice that could plausibly have been made differently, and say who would have to be involved in remaking it. **[3 marks]**

Here is the script, reproduced as submitted, including its slips:

> (a) Winner says artifacts can have politics in two ways. The first is when the design of something settles an issue in a community, and the second is when a technology is inherently political, meaning it is complicated and dangerous enough that government has to regulate it and hold public consultations about it. Feature (i) is the first way, and it is the clearest example in the case: the fare gates and the lifts decide who can physically get onto the system, exactly like the parkway bridges that were built too low for buses, so the politics is built into the design and was put there deliberately. They are also political because they are unfair to people who cannot pay electronically. Feature (ii) is the second way, since a leased control system on an automated line is risky and the council has to audit it. Feature (iii) is neither, because the engineers were only following gradient and land cost; nobody had a political motive, so there is no politics in the alignment, only efficiency, and Winner would agree that where no political motive exists the artifact is neutral. So the politics sits in the artifact when someone designs it that way on purpose, and otherwise it sits in the consequences, which are not politics at all but just facts about the technology, like a load limit.
> (b) Winner is a determinist, though a moderate one, and not a hard one. He is not a hard determinist because he thinks human beings choose designs, and for him technology is never neutral since every engineer has political opinions whether they admit it or not. But he is a determinist in the sense that matters, because once the guideway is poured and the fleet is leased the consequences are locked in for thirty years. Citizens can vote for a different mayor, but they cannot vote away the guideway, and that is what determinism means: the technology has already decided the shape of social life and the only politics left is administration.
> (c) The certified-technician clause in feature (ii) is the contingent one. Nothing about an automated shuttle requires that only the manufacturer's people may open it, since plenty of transit agencies maintain proprietary stock in-house; it is a contractual term, so it could have been written differently and could still be renegotiated at the next lease review. Remaking it would take the authority's procurement board, the mechanics' union, the city council's transport committee, and probably disability advocates, since the same clause governs the lifts.
