Ok so I've been going back and forth on the Cruzan case — the Missouri one, 1983 car crash, Nancy in a persistent vegetative state, her parents Joe and Bernice fighting to get the feeding tube out, Supreme Court in 1990 says a competent person has a constitutionally protected liberty interest in refusing treatment but Missouri can demand clear and convincing evidence of what an incompetent person wanted, tube comes out that December and she dies. Everybody analyzes it as an autonomy case, rights versus state interest. I want to try care ethics on it instead and see if it does any work.

Walk me through the actors in Tronto's phases — which phase each of them is actually occupying and where the loop fails to close. My hunch, and this is the part I want you to push on hardest because I'm not sure it survives contact: the state was doing care-giving, competently, through the hospital, with no possible care-receiving, since Nancy could never register anything at all. If responsiveness is what completes the circuit, then Missouri wasn't caring for her, it was maintaining her. Is that a real distinction or am I just dressing up a quality-of-life judgment in care-ethics vocabulary? Say which.

Then the harder problem. Noddings makes reciprocity pretty central — the cared-for has to receive and respond for a caring relation to be complete. Read literally that seems to imply the Cruzans, who did everything for seven years, were never in a caring relation with their own daugther. That has to be the wrong result, so tell me what care ethics has to give up or revise to avoid it, and whether the revision costs it anything it wanted to keep.

And the evidentiary standard: Missouri said the family's informal reports of what Nancy had once said to friends about not wanting to live that way weren't clear and convincing. From a care ethics standpoint isn't that backwards on its face? The people with the most situated, particular knowledge of her are the ones whose testimony counts for least, and the state substitutes a stranger's procedural threshold for the knowledge generated inside the relation. Does care ethics have the resources to make that an *epistemic* complaint about who gets to be a knower here, or only a sentimental one about families being special?

Last bit, keep it short: what changes when the one-caring is itself contested, like in the Schiavo mess a decade later, where husband and parents each claimed to be the one caring for her? Does care ethics have a tiebreaker, or does it just describe the standoff more warmly?

Bottom line up front — one sentence on whether care ethics actually illuminates this case or just relitigates the autonomy fight in different words. Then the analysis. Hard cap of 600 words for the whole thing, and don't bother citing anything, I know the literature, I just want the reasoning.
