I've been reading Dennett on real patterns and the three stances, and I'm trying to work out what my own verdicts actually commit me to. Here's the list. A chess engine, when I say it believes my queen is attacked: yes, real belief, and I'd argue with anyone who calls that a metaphor. My robot vaccum: I say "it thinks the couch is a wall," and I mean it, sort of — the prediction works, and it keeps working if I move the dock or swap the battery. A thermostat: no, useful fiction, everyone agrees. A tic tac toe program that's just a giant lookup table of every legal position: no — even though it plays perfectly and I can predict it with 100% accuracy. That one really bothers me, because if what matters is that the ascription is cheaper than the bit map, the table should come out *better* than the chess engine, not worse. Slime mold taking the short route through a maze: yes, it prefers the short route. A feedforward image classifier at 99%: no. A corporation — "the board believes rates will fall": yes, though I'm probably inconsistent, since I refuse to ascribe unified beliefs to a split-brain patient. My dog while she's asleep: yes, she still believes the treats are in the top drawer. Someone under general anesthesia: no. An LLM that passes a false-belief test but gives a different answer when I reorder the sentences in its prompt: I keep flipping on this one.

So — is there a single principle about when a pattern is real enough to license intentional ascription that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it in clauses, not vibes, and for each clause tell me which verdict above forced it in. Then run it on cases I didn't list: a self-driving car, a market, a person mid-confabulation, and the same chess engine rebuilt on completely different hardware. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it has to overturn — I'd rather lose the corporation one than the vacuum one, but tell me if it's the other way around.
