I chair a six-person integrity review panel at a company that's basically two companies stapled together — a Charlotte-headquartered specialty gases business that bought a family-owned plant outside Stuttgart about four years ago. Three of us are American, three are German, and we meet Thursday at 8 their time, which is 2 a.m. for me, so I want to circulate a written opinion tonight rather than have to explain anything live.

The case: on a night shift in March, a process engineer on the German side saw a reading drift out of spec on a line that fills calibration gas mixtures. Our documented procedure for that situation is what we call hold-and-disposition — log the deviation in the quality system, quarantine what's already been filled, and wait for the quality manager to make the call the next business day, because she's the one who's actually accountable to the regulator. He didn't wait. He stopped the line himself, dumped the batch, called a maintenance contractor directly without going thru procurement approval, and then, instead of telling his plant manager, emailed the American VP of Operations, because he'd concluded over the previous few months that the plant manager was leaning on supervisors to keep lines moving to hit a delivery commitment. Afterwards it turned out the deviation was real, the valve was genuinely bad, nothing out-of-spec reached a customer, and the line was down eleven hours.

The panel is split and not along the lines you'd expect. The VP wants to commend the guy, or at worst coach him informally. Two of the German members, including HR, want a formal written warning, for two reasons they insist are seperate: bypassing a procedure that exists precisely so the deviation gets judged by the function that has the competence and the regulatory responsibility, and going over his plant manager's head, which they say corrodes the authority structure that lets 900 people predict what each other will do. They also note that two years ago we warned someone for skipping a step in a much smaller incident, and the works council has asked about consistency. Here's what I've been chewing on and what I want the memo built around: I don't think the two sides disagree about values — everyone in that room agrees safety beats schedule and that honesty about deviations is non-negotiable. I think they disagree about where the burden of justification sits by default. On the reading the German members are working from, a written rule is a stored judgment of the organization: it was made by people who had more information than the guy on the night shift and who carry responsibility he doesn't, so deviating is something the deviator owes a justification for, and if he can't produce one the organization can actually check, the default verdict is "violation" — a good outcome doesn't retroactively supply the justification. On the American reading, rules are heuristics that a person standing right there has to be free to override when he can see the rule failing, and the burden sits on whoever wants to punish the override; the default verdict is "praiseworthy" unless his judgment was reckless. Same facts, opposite defaults. My hunch is that the panel is stuck because it keeps trying to answer "was he right or wrong?" when the decision actually in front of us is which default we want installed in the head of the next engineer standing there at 3 a.m.

So: draft the memo I'll sign, maybe 900 to 1200 words. It needs an actionable bottom line — warning, no action plus recognition, or some third option you think is better than either — and then the reasoning in a form both the VP and the plant manager could answer without feeling strawmanned. Take the German position seriously as a moral claim about competence, reliability, and the division of epistemic labor, not as rigidity or a cultural quirk; and don't let the American position off as just "initiative good" — I want the worry addressed that follow-your-conscience quietly transfers risk onto people who can't see it coming, meaning the customers and the colleagues on the next shift who were counting on the procedure being followed. Say something about the consistency problem with the earlier warning, and about whether hold-and-disposition itself should be amended (my instinct is an explicit emergency override with after-the-fact written justification, but tell me if that's a cop-out that'll just get invoked every Friday afternoon). And I want one honest paragraph saying which default this panel should adopt and why — including whether the answer would change if the plant were in Ohio instead of outside Stuttgart, or whether I'm flattering myself that the setting matters at all. No citations, no bibliography, and don't do the employment-law analysis, our Frankfurt counsel handles that and I'll add the references to our own code myself. Commit as far as the reasoning actually supports — if you land somewhere, land there, don't soften it into "it depends on the organizational culture." But end with a short note to me, marked not-for-circulation, saying where the memo is on weakest ground: which of the two readings it's quietly favoring, and what the smartest person on the losing side will say back Thursday morning. And if you think I've framed this badly — like if "German rule-morality versus American conscience-morality" is just a national stereotype with nothing in it and I'm asking you to dress up a fairly ordinary disciplinary case in folk anthropology — say that plainly instead of playing along, and tell me what you think the real question in front of the panel is.
