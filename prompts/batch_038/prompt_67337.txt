I teach a graduate seminar on what a theory of distributive justice is supposed to equalise, and this year I intend to fix the success conditions before anyone reads a candidate theory. The seminar reliably degenerates into a contest in which each camp quietly supplies its own criteria: the welfarist is faulted for not tracking functionings, the capability theorist is faulted for not tracking preferences, and neither criticism bites, because nothing has been agreed in advance about what would count as getting the matter right. What I want from you, therefore, is the standard itself — the specification a currency of justice must satisfy to be adequate — with the reasoning behind each requirement.

The specification must serve two purposes at once, and you should say so plainly if they pull against one another. First, it must be usable as the target variable for a public agency of ordinary administrative capacity, distributing a fixed sum across a heterogeneous population within a budget cycle, without intrusive enquiry into citizens' inner states. Second, it must be something one could read aloud, without embarrassment, to the people it ranks as worse off; a metric whose routine output is a published statement of deficiency has to answer for that. If the two cannot both be honoured, tell me which takes priority and on what grounds.

I have drafted seven candidate requirements. Treat them as raw material rather than as given: assess each, restate it at whatever level of generality you judge correct, and say whether it is a genuine desideratum or a partisan import dressed in neutral clothing.

(i) The clerk condition: a competent official must be able to assign the currency to a person from records, without that person's testimony about their own states being either necessary or advantageous to offer.
(ii) The endorsement condition: a person counts as better off on the currency only where they could recognise the relevant good as theirs; a metric satisfiable behind someone's back is measuring something other than their standing.
(iii) The authorship condition: the metric must separate what a person has done from what has been done to them, without either rewarding or penalising them for the shape of their own earlier self.
(iv) The standing condition: the metric must not, in the ordinary course of its outputs, describe an identifiable group as constitutionally deficient in a way that licenses others to intervene in their make-up.
(v) The trajectory condition: the metric must be able to rank lives across time and not merely moments, since two people identical today may face very different declines.
(vi) The sphere condition: the metric must say which goods may not be alienated for money — votes, organs, school places, development rights — and explain why the objection is not mere squeamishness.
(vii) The testimony condition: the metric must distinguish adaptation that has corroded a person's sense of what is available to them from a cultivated economy of desire that is genuinely a good way to live.

Three cases I keep returning to, so that you can see the sort of thing the list has to adjudicate. Callista and Tobias have identical incomes and identical native endowments. Callista spent a decade cultivating a practice — historical keyboard performance — whose continuation now requires expenditure she cannot meet; Tobias needs a costly supplement he detests and would rather receive as cash. A metric that calls the two equally well off is blind to something, yet a metric that tops Callista up rewards her own prior self-formation, and Tobias, who endorses nothing about his bundle, is precisely the one who will insist on the money. In a certain valley, a signing community has flourished for four generations: on any metric keyed to capacities it scores badly along one dimension, on any metric keyed to its own endorsed life it is doing very well indeed, and the intervention the first ranking recommends is the one its members experience as an attempt to unmake them. And in a district whose factory closed thirty years ago, the older residents want little and report themselves content while the younger ones want a great deal and report themselves deprived. A distribution that takes the first report at face value looks like an injustice to the second group; but a metric that discounts all adaptation as distortion misdescribes the older residents, some of whom have found in a modest practice a satisfaction their better-off neighbours lack.

Deliver, then:

- The requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it and a marking of whether it is non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp. Revise my seven as you see fit, and add up to three you think I have omitted.
- Any subset of the requirements you believe to be jointly unsatisfiable. If the list itself contains an impossibility, that is the most valuable part of the exercise: state the subset, give the argument in a few sentences, and name the requirement you would surrender to escape it.
- Which single requirement does most of the work in excluding candidate theories, and which does least.
- Which of my seven drafts is the weakest, and why.
- Finally, briefly — a few sentences each and no more — how welfarism, resourcism, the capabilities approach, and a relational view that denies the whole project of specifying a currency fare against your list. Keep this short; the scoring is not the point, and I shall do it in class.

I am not asking for references, a reading list, or attributions of any of this to particular authors. I want the specification argued for, not footnoted. A numbered list or table is welcome wherever it helps.
