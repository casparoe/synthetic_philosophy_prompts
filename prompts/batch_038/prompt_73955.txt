For four years I have covered moral psychology and the history of reform for a magazine's ideas desk, and I am now drafting a long piece about the expanding circle — the claim that the shape of moral history is the widening of the set of beings whose interests count. My editor wants the piece to have a spine, and the spine would have to be a criterion: when does a change count as moral progress, as opposed to mere drift, or to a lucky accident that will reverse itself the moment conditions shift? I have discovered that I cannot state such a criterion, although I turn out to have firm verdicts, case by case. I would like help extracting a principle from those verdicts, and being told which of them I should give up.

The cases, roughly in the order I encountered them.

1. Judicial torture disappearing from European legal practice across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: progress, and the cleanest case I own.
2. The franchise extended to women: progress.
3. Executions moved out of the public square and behind prison walls — not fewer of them at first, sometimes more, but no longer a spectacle: not progress, and arguably a deterioration. A cruelty nobody witnesses is a cruelty nobody is moved to stop.
4. Slaughter moved out of the town square and into windowless sheds, and then, a century on, infiltrated by investigators whose footage produced enforceable confinement standards: progress. I hold this even though the concealment is identical in kind to case 3, and even though the reform came from reputational cost rather than from anyone's change of heart. Cases 3 and 4 are the pair I cannot reconcile; they differ in almost nothing that my own language picks out, and I judge them in opposite directions.
5. Debtors' prison abolished and replaced by a penal apparatus that incarcerates far more people for far longer: I want to call the first progress and the second neither progress nor regress but a separate ledger, though I cannot explain how two ledgers can be kept by the same institutions on the same ground.
6. A society that widened its circle toward a persecuted minority and then contracted violently within a single generation: I say the widening was real progress while it lasted, and the contraction a genuine loss rather than the revelation that there had never been anything to lose. I notice this commits me against any durability requirement, which I nonetheless keep reaching for.
7. A nation that now tends its own wounded and veterans with a thoroughness unimaginable a century ago, while describing the enemy it is fighting in terms that would have been considered inflammatory in that same century: I refuse to call this progress, and I equally refuse to call it a wash. The two changes do not seem to me commensurable. Yet in case 4 I weighed suffering against suffering without hesitation.
8. Norms of speech inside institutions changed largely by the public shaming of named individuals: the outcome is progress; the mechanism makes me queasy. I do not know whether that queasiness has any theoretical standing or is only temperament dressed up as judgment.
9. Giving redirected toward strangers on other continents: progress. The same impulse redirected toward people who will not be born for ten thousand years, in a way that reduces what is spent on the living poor: not progress. I cannot state what separates the two beyond my own impatience with the second.

Underneath several of these sits a distinction I have half-invented and would like pressure-tested: expansion by conviction, in which a society comes to see a group as counting, and expansion by price, in which a group's interests begin to be weighed only because harming it has become expensive — legally, reputationally, commercially. My instinct says only the first is moral progress and the second is a coincidence that reverses when the price changes. Case 4 looks like pure expansion by price, and I still want to call it progress.

The tentative principle I have been carrying around, which I expect you to break: a change counts as moral progress only if it (a) gives weight to interests that were previously given none, (b) survives at least one serious stress test rather than holding only in comfortable conditions, and (c) is produced by a mechanism that the newly included would themselves endorse if they understood it.

What I want back is not a survey of the field and not a reading list; I can assemble either myself. I want one principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never seen. For each candidate: which of my nine verdicts it delivers and which it contradicts; which clause of it is motivated by which case; what it says about three further cases of your own choosing; and where it is weakest. Then tell me plainly which verdicts I must abandon if I want the simplest principle that is not obviously false, and which I may keep if I am willing to pay for them in complexity. Give cases 3 and 4 particular attention: I want to know whether any principled line separates them, or whether one of those two verdicts is a prejudice I caught from covering the wrong beats.
