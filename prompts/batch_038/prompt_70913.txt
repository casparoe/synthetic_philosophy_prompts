Georg Simmel died in 1918 and never saw a database, a census form processed by machine, or a blacklist that follows a person across national borders. I would like you to reconstruct the position he would take on a question that only became intelligible after his death: how much of a citizen's life ought to be hidden from the state that governs it?

Treat this as a reasoned reconstruction rather than a pastiche, and build it out of concepts he actually worked with — social forms as against their contents, reciprocal interaction, reserve, tact, the secret as a positive form of socialization rather than a mere shield, the trust that consists in believing what one cannot verify, the stranger, the widening gap between objective and subjective culture, and the claim in his philosophy of money that the money economy translates personal qualities into quantities and makes possible a relationships-by-records that would otherwise be impossible.

To keep the exercise honest, here are three posthumous regimes you must have him judge.

First, Germany. In 1983, confronted with a planned national census, the Federal Constitutional Court derived a constitutional right to informational self-determination from the general personality right together with human dignity, holding that a person must in principle be able to decide when and within what limits facts about their private life are disclosed, and that limits on this are permissible only against an overriding public interest. A load-bearing part of the court's reasoning was psychological rather than proprietary: someone who cannot survey what is known about them in their social surroundings, and cannot estimate what their possible interlocutors know, will begin to restrain themselves, and a polity of such self-restrainers cannot be a free one. Note that this coexists with private credit bureaus whose scores decide who gets a loan, a flat, or a phone contract.

Second, the United States. There is no general constitutional right of this shape; protection is sectoral, and the tradition prizes the exposure of officials over the concealment of citizens — sunshine statutes, presumptively public dockets, published property and voter rolls, donation records. Crucially, under the third-party doctrine first articulated in a 1979 pen-register decision, information a person voluntarily conveys to a bank or a telephone company is not protected against government acquisition at all, on the theory that one who has handed it over for economic purposes cannot reasonably expect it to remain private; a 2018 decision narrowed but did not abolish this for historical cell-site location data.

Third, China. Set aside the popular image of a single national citizen score. The social credit system as actually built is a credit-rating, blacklist and redlist governance strategy, run jointly by planning authorities, the central bank and the courts, whose characteristic sanction is the joint punishment of judgment defaulters — exclusion from flights, high-speed rail, certain contracts and professions — layered over local pilots of widely varying ambition, and aimed as much at firms and at government agencies as at individuals.

Your reconstruction must do the following. Explain why Simmel would refuse to frame the issue as a right to be left alone, and what he would put in its place. Deliver a ranking of the three regimes by which is most corrosive of social life, and defend a ranking that is not the obvious geopolitical one — I want to see the argument, not the reflex. Offer at least one genuinely unwelcome observation about the German remedy: that turning the secret into an administrable entitlement, discharged through notices, consents and purpose limitations, may be an instance of the very objectification he diagnosed. Answer the nothing-to-hide objection in his terms, by distinguishing the secret from its contents. And mark the strain points: name at least two places where the reconstruction has to stretch his concepts past their 1918 breaking point, and say what he would have to abandon or revise.

Keep him from using posthumous vocabulary as though it were native to him; where he must take account of a modern fact, let him react to it as an alien social form. Around 1,100 words, in headed sections. No reading lists, references, or citations.
