Okay so I've been sitting on this for a while and I want someone to genuinely try to break it rather than nod at it.

The position: I think the physical Church-Turing thesis is safe. Not safe in the sense of proven -- I don't think it was ever a theorem and I don't think the original one is the kind of thing that could be -- but safe in the sense that every hypercomputation proposal I've actualy read dies the same death. As far as I can tell they all fail in one of exactly three ways: (1) they burn an infinite resource -- unbounded acceleration, infinitely many steps packed into a finite interval, infinite proper time along one worldline that a finite observer somehow gets the output of; (2) they hide the noncomputability in a continuous parameter that has to be physically real and infinitely precise, and any noise floor or finite-energy constraint collapses it back down to something a Turing machine can do, just slowly; or (3) they assume a background solution to GR that nobody can get to from realistic initial conditions -- the Malament-Hogarth stuff, the Kerr-Newman proposals, where the objection isn't that the metric is inconsistent but that you can't build or fall into it without the Cauchy horizon blowing up in your face. Plus one extra condition I add: it only counts as computing anything if a finite observer with finite resources can extract the answer, otherwise you can claim a waterfall is computing chess and the whole debate is empty.

So: what would count against this? I want real defeaters, not the usual hand-wavy stuff about incompleteness.

Things I'd like covered:

- Is my three-way taxonomy actually exhaustive, or is it a post-hoc rationalization of my priors? If there's a live proposal that doesn't fail in any of the three ways, name it and tell me what it would take to make it work. If the taxonomy is loaded, show me where the loading is.
- Is the extractability condition doing legitimate work, or am I just defining hypercomputation out of existence? There's a version of this worry where my position turns out to be equivalent to the trivially-true claim that we can't do it, and I'd rather know.
- Undecidability results that live close to physics -- the spectral gap undecidability result, MIP*=RE, the busy beaver stuff. Do these bear on my position at all? My current move is that they show the halting problem is embedded in the idealized theory, which is not the same as nature computing a noncomputable function. Tell me whether that move is legitimate or whether it's exactly the insulation I should be worried about.
- Whether the position risks anything. Any finite experimental record is computable, so no experiment could ever show me a violation. Is that fatal, or is it just what a thesis about the limits of a method looks like? And what would a rival predict that mine doesn't -- the Gödelian anti-AI line at least seems to stick its neck out, does mine?
- Where the defenders of my own view would concede the ground is soft. My guesses: the "finite region, finite energy, finite bits" argument quietly assumes quantum gravity behaves; and if you're a many-worlds realist who thinks the amplitudes really are exact real numbers, objection (2) gets uncomfortable in a way I haven't faced.
- Rank all of it: fatal / serious / awkward / merely annoying. Then tell me straight whether the position is basically immune, and whether immunity is a bug or a feature for a claim that might be about the concept of a mechanical procedure rather than about the world.

For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a concrete system, in a theory we already take seriously, where the finite-precision approximations don't converge to anything -- i.e. where the strip-out-the-idealization move provably fails instead of just plausibly failing. If that's out there, I want to hear about it first.
