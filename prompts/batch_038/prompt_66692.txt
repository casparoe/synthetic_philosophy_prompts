I help write scenarios for a crisis-management exercise at a staff college — the annual one where two teams play out a conventional conflict that keeps drifting toward the nuclear threshold. Part of my job is maintaining a tagging scheme in the umpire's handbook: every scripted move gets marked E (escalatory) or N (not), and the tag decides how much risk budget the move costs the team that plays it. We've built up about a decade of these tags and theyre mostly consistent, but a new submission has split the panel and I want to know what our own record actually commits us to.

The tags so far, with the one-line reason the panel gave at the time:

- Dispersing strategic bombers to civilian airstrips, announced at a press briefing: E ("hard to walk back once the other side has re-tasked its air defences").
- The same dispersal, done quietly with no announcement, discovered later by the other side's intelligence: still E, though a weaker one ("same physical posture, less theatre").
- Declaring a public red line about a specific geographic trigger, well before anything happens: N ("clarifies, so makes miscalculation less likely").
- Jamming the adversary's early-warning downlink for six hours and then stopping: E, even though nothing was launched and the jamming was self-terminating ("you took away their ability to see").
- One conventional strike on a radar site deep inside the adversary's homeland, telegraphed to a neutral party 40 minutes ahead: E.
- Moving warheads out of storage and mating them to delivery vehicles, then reversing the whole thing twelve hours later: E ("what you did in between counts").
- Restoring the hotline after a crisis and adding a second, redundant channel: N.
- A deliberate leak revealing that the head of government has been briefed on nuclear options: E ("it wasn't an accident that they found out").
- Shifting to a posture where launch decisions have to be made inside minutes: E, and the panel called this the worst entry in the file.

The new submission, and this is the one we're arguing about: Blue has lost contact with two of its ballistic-missile submarines for 36 hours. Blue announces publicly that it has temporarily pre-delegated release authority to the commanders of the remaining boats, to take effect only if national command authority cannot be reached, and says the delegation lapses the moment contact is restored. Half the panel says N — nothing physically moves, no weapon is mated, the arrangement cancels itself, and it's arguably reassuring, since it tells the other side there's no payoff in a decapitating strike. The other half says E — it's the same family as the minutes-long launch window, except now the window belongs to a twenty-eight-year-old lieutenent in a steel tube who has never met the head of government.

So: which tag does our own record push us toward, and which of the nine is the closest analogue for it? I'd like the candidate rules laid out properly. I can think of at least four that seem to fit everything above — does the move raise the objective probability of a nuclear detonation; does it shorten a decision interval or delete a human buffer; is it hard to undo once the adversary has reacted to it; was it done in order to make the other side feel risk rather than to reduce risk — and for each one I want to know what it says about the submarine case. If they all land on the same tag, fine, but I doubt they do, and if they don't I want to be told which verdict in the nine each rule is quietly buying at the cost of the others.

Then: what single extra move could we write into next year's file that would break the tie between the rules? Something where two of them point in opposite directions, so the panel can't fudge it. Because whatever we tag this one, we're adopting a principle whether we intend to or not, and I'd rather pick it on purpose.

One more thing — is any entry in the nine actually out of line with the rest, in the sense that a rule which handles eight of them has to be contorted to cover the ninth? I have a private suspicion about the public-red-line one and I can't articulate why. Maybe it's the leak, since a leak and a red line are both just talk.

British English in the answer, please.
