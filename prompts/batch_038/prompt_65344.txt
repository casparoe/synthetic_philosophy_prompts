Six weeks ago I left a position in structured credit to work at a small animal-welfare organisation, and the change has turned out to be philosophically more disquieting than I expected. When I explained my reasoning to an old friend who did a master's in philosophy before going into journalism, she did not dispute a single premise. Instead she said that my conversion was exactly what one would predict of someone in my position, that the moral urgency I now experience was assembled by forces that cared nothing for its truth, and that the very fact I find the case compelling is therefore no evidence in its favour. I have since been reading about evolutionary debunking arguments, and I find the familiar replies — the third-factor strategy, the claim that our moral faculties track mind-independent truths, the idea that reflective equilibrium can filter out the distortions — strangely unsatisfying in this context, because each of them seems to address the *content* of a moral belief, whereas her challenge was really about its *grip*: the felt authority, the motivational weight, the fact that it does not come loose when arguments pull on it. I would like to see the strongest version of that dispute worked through in dramatic form.

Please write a dialogue, of roughly 1,100 to 1,500 words, between two philosophers who are stranded together in an airport bar after a cancelled evening flight following a workshop at which they had just disagreed publicly. One of them, whom I will call the tenacity debunker, holds that the real damage from our evolutionary and cultural history is not that it supplied us with false moral contents but that it calibrated how *tenacious* those contents are: selection favoured beliefs that resist revision and come equipped with motivational force, because stickiness secures cooperation and deters defection whether or not it is true. On this view the standard realist replies all miss the target, since a belief can survive reflective scrutiny precisely because the scrutiny is being conducted by a mind whose sense of what counts as obvious was itself tuned for fitness rather than truth — the audit inherits the bias of the auditor. The other, whom I will call the revocability realist, concedes both content-shaping and stickiness, but argues that what matters epistemically is whether the believer *would have given the belief up* had she detected a mismatch between it and the moral facts; and that the costly, status-destroying capacity people demonstrably have to abandon deeply held moral commitments — apostates, converts, moral revolutionaries who act against every incentive — shows that this capacity cannot be wholly fitness-serving, and so must be at least partly responsive to how things are. The realist should also press the question of whether the debunker's own epistemic norms are any less sticky, and what the debunker is entitled to if her argument generalises.

To keep them from trading abstractions, have them argue over a specific case, which they can introduce themselves: a woman raised in a herding community whose norms hold that a household failing to host the winter feast for three successive seasons forfeits its grazing rights, who moves to a city at thirty-two and discards almost every norm she was raised with — the feast obligations, the blood-price, the sharp in-group loyalty — yet finds that one conviction will not budge: that a promise extracted from her under duress still binds her, even in a situation where breaking it would plainly benefit everyone concerned, the original promisee included. The two characters should disagree about what her case shows, and in particular about whether the fact that everything else was revised makes the survivor more credible, as the one residue that passed the audit, or less credible, as exactly the kind of deposit the audit was too weak to dislodge. Both speakers must be fully competent advocates of their own view: no strawmen, no unforced errors, no character who exists only to be convinced. They should respond to what the other actually said rather than to a convenient reconstruction, and at least once each should restate the opponent's argument in terms the opponent explicitly accepts as accurate; at least once each should make a real concession that costs them something. Please keep the exchange self-contained — argue from the case and from first principles rather than by name-dropping philosophers or pointing at the literature, and if a position needs a label, give it a descriptive one. The dialogue should not resolve and neither speaker should get the last word as a kind of victory; instead, have them end by drafting, together and aloud, a single sentence that states precisely what remains in dispute between them.
