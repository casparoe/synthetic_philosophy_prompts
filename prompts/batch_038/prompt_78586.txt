I'm putting together something for a philosophy-of-physics reading group newsletter and I want the centerpiece to be a piece of satire, so this is a bit of a strange request.

The conceit: a consumer-magazine lab test — think Which? — run by an invented testing body, reviewing competing "probability kits" for people who've swallowed the many-worlds interpretation and now need to work out what on earth to believe about their own future. So Everett is just true in-universe, and the products on test are the various fixes for the Born-rule problem, each sold under a made-up brand name. No real physicists named and no paper titles, but a specialist should recognise every product immediately from what it claims to do and what's in the small print.

Write it as a proper buyers' guide, roughly 800–1000 words, in deadpan consumer-testing prose: a short intro about how the testing was done, then a rated entry per product (star score out of five, a "what we liked / what we didn't" pair of lines, and a few sentences of write-up), and then a Best Buy, a Don't Buy, and a one-line editor's verdict.

Cover at least these, with your own invented brands:

- a branch-counting kit — cheap, cheerfully intuitive, and the write-up has to make clear exactly why it collapses, i.e. that branching isn't a discrete countable thing and the tally gives the wrong numbers anyway;
- a decision-theory model where the axioms are sold separately, and the manual keeps quietly assuming you already care about your future successors in proportion to… something it never says;
- an envariance self-assembly unit that claims to get the weights out of symmetry alone, and a tester who complains the derivation only runs once you've assumed what you wanted;
- a self-location / uncertainty device whose instructions are unclear about which copy is reading them;
- and, if it fits, a no-frills house-brand budget option that ships with no probabilities at all and a card in the box saying the problem has been dissolved.

The humour has to come from getting the philosophy right, not from quips about parallel universes or doppelgängers. Two gags I definitely want:

1. A methodology sidebar on the nightmare of lab-testing a probability rule in a world where every outcome happens. You can't fail a product on frequency grounds, because some branch has exactly the failure rate the manual promised and some branch has a million heads in a row — so the lab reports results weighted across branches, and then has to say where the weights came from, which was the whole problem in the first place.

2. At least one entry that turns on the difference between "why should I feel uncertain at all, when everything happens?" and "fine, but why these particular squared-amplitude numbers?", and a customer who conflates the two gets their refund denied under the terms.

Please keep the irony legible without ever winking — no narrator aside admitting that it's all circular. If the reviewer's own conclusion turns out to be circular, that's something the reader should notice on their own, not something the piece tells them. And it should still be funny to someone who's never heard of Everett: the consumer-review genre ought to be doing a lot of the work (the star ratings, the "we wouldn't recommend", the complaint about the size of the packaging).

No references, reading list or citations needed — it should all be self-contained. British English throughout, please, in both spelling and idiom.
