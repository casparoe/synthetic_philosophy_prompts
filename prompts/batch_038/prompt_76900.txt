I run a small MA seminar on medieval philosophy of mind and the self, and every year the same thing happens: students read the flying man and immediately announce that Avicenna just beat Descartes to it by six centuries. He didn't, or not exactly, and I want a worksheet that makes the difference visible. Please act as an expert on Avicenna's psychology and on contemporary debates about bodily self-awareness, and think hard about the logical space before you write anything down.

What I want is a decision tree that starts a student from "I accept the setup: a person created all at once, floating in void, eyes covered, limbs splayed so he isn't touching himself, and then nothing else" and ends them at a named position about what the case actually shows. Somewhere between nine and thirteen branch nodes, each phrased as a question a student could answer honestly without already knowing which leaf it sorts them toward.

A few things I need in there:

- The tree should branch first on what survives the setup at all. Does the flying man have any self-awareness, or is the case empty? If it does, is that awareness of a persisting self or just an occurrent thisness with no claim about substance?
- Then on which kind of argument Avicenna is running: an elimination argument (the body and its parts are absent from what he's aware of, therefore the self isn't the body) versus something more like a self-luminosity argument (awareness of self is present no matter what gets stripped away, so it can't depend on the stripped-away stuff). Students conflate these constantly.
- Which faculty does the work for you: intellect, imagination, estimation, or plain bodily sensation? And are the inner senses genuinely screened off in the case, or is that a stipulation doing all the heavy lifting?
- What the conclusion licenses: substance dualism, or only that self-awareness is individuated differently from body-awareness, or only that the soul is known first-hand rather than inferred.
- A branch that takes the empirical objections seriously. Phantom limbs, deafferentation, hemineglect and anosognosia, split-brain, rubber-hand and full-body illusions, depersonalization, human infants, nonhuman animals. Each objection should attach at the specific node it actually threatens, not get dumped in one pile at the end.

Every leaf gets the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody really holds it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then mark any combination of answers the tree permits that has no occupants, and say why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If there are two leaves your questions can't really tell apart, say so and lump them instead of pretending.

Format: lead with the bottom line, meaning the tree itself, nodes labeled with their questions and leaves labeled with their positions. Then the notes on unoccupied corners and inseparable leaves. No preamble, no restating what I asked for, no bibliography.
