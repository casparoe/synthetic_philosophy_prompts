The idea. Everyone frames interpersonal utility comparison as a question about information: what's contained in the utility representations, whether you can compare levels or only units or neither, uniqueness up to positive affine transformation, Harsanyi needing a shared zero and a shared unit before aggregation gets off the ground, Sen's partial comparability as a way of getting something without conceding everything. My worry is that this framing makes the whole thing look like a metaphysics-of-other-minds problem, when the live difficulty is actualy a normalization problem — and normalization is something economists have thought about very carefully for a century under the name of index numbers, without philosophers picking any of it up.

Concretely: the unit of well-being can be pinned down by risk attitudes, or by willingness to pay, or by self-report anchors, or by just-noticeable differences. The zero can sit at death, or at a life-not-worth-living, or at subsistence. Same data, different anchor, opposite distributive verdicts. And here's the bit I think is genuinely interesting: you can't pick an anchor on grounds that are themselves anchor-neutral, because any criterion for the choice has to be stated in units that the choice is supposed to fix. So there's a circle sitting in the middle of the debate, and I suspect a lot of what passes for disagreement between the aggregation people and their critics is disagreement about where to put the zero, dressed up as disagreement about what's measurable.

Second half, and I think this is the wedge that keeps it from being just skepticism: exactly the same problem shows up for one person across time. Was the operation worth it, was the decade in grad school a net gain — we make those cross-time comparisons constantly and nobody thinks there's a deep problem about them. So treat the cross-person case as the same species of problem with the index relabeled, and ask what's doing the work there that we're refusing ourselves in the interpersonal case.
