I have served for six years on the five-member grants committee of a private foundation with an endowment of roughly $40 million, a mission stated in terms of reducing mortality we can measure, and a bylaw requiring that each grant rest on the committee's own cost-effectiveness assessment. Our next quarterly meeting faces an offer that has split us three-to-two, and I have undertaken to circulate a written opinion in advance. Please draft that opinion, in a form the other four members could argue with line by line.

The offer. A donor unconnected to our founding family proposes to give $2.4 million to the program we currently rank first and cannot otherwise afford — our staff model credits it with roughly one averted infant death per $4,000 — on the express condition that we simultaneously commit $600,000 of our own endowment to a residential deterrence program for adolescents adjudicated for minor offenses. Our staff assessment of that program is negative: the published evaluations of interventions of this type point consistently toward increased subsequent offending rather than less, and we have been unable to construct a defensible estimate of the harm, which is itself part of the difficulty. The donor is frank that the money is not the whole of what he wants. His letter states that a grant from a foundation known for cost-effectiveness analysis would be worth more to the program than twice the sum arriving from any other source, and he asks that the two grants be announced together as a deliberate package, in our name.

The majority position is that declining is squeamishness at other people's expense: the arithmetic is the arithmetic, we would not be the ones operating the program, and a refusal to trade toward the outcomes we favor is a luxury belief purchased with infant deaths. The minority position is that what is being sold is our credibility, which is not ours to sell; that publishing our endorsement of an intervention we believe injures its participants is a harm of a different kind from a bad grant made in ignorance; and that accepting one conditional gift of this shape invites every future donor to price our name.

The opinion should reach a recommendation — accept, decline, or counter-offer — tied to the date the donor has asked us to answer by, and should be explicit about whether this decision sits with the committee or must go to the full board. I would especially like it to work through the following, in whatever order makes the argument flow:

- Whether this is genuinely a case of gains from trade, or whether that description conceals the fact that we are simply being paid to do something we judge wrong. What turns on which description is right?
- Whether a counter-offer is available that satisfies the donor's values as he states them — a different program, or a modified version of this one funded at the same level — and what his response would tell us about what he is actually buying. If a substitute would satisfy him, does that dissolve the complicity worry or sharpen it?
- Whether our own uncertainty about the negative assessment (staff puts it at something like 80/20) changes the calculation, and how the committee should reason when the two sides of a proposed trade cannot be expressed in a common unit.
- Whether the good the $2.4 million does and the harm the $600,000 does belong in one ledger or two, and what the committee's duty to the foundation's stated purpose contributes over and above the aggregate arithmetic.
- What the precedent is worth, and whether the majority is right that refusing to trade is itself a species of self-regard.

Two constraints. Argue from the case: no literature review, no citations, no philosopher name-dropping — I want reasoning the other four members could answer rather than a bibliography. And keep the register appropriate to a document that will be entered into the minutes; roughly a thousand words. I have read the arguments about moral offsetting, and about whether one ought to shut up and multiply, for the better part of two decades, and I find both reflexes available on demand. The opinion should notice which one it is relying on at each step, and say why that is the right one there.
