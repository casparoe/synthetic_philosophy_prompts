Consider an invented naming case. In 1908 a photographic plate survey entered a faint moving point of light in its catalogue as "Obj-9." In 2019 a team, unaware that the old entry existed, named the same body "Vantor" after a decade of tracking it. Both terms are used as ordinary names, so both rigidly designate the body; and Vantor is Obj-9. From this I have built an argument that appears to abolish the necessary a posteriori, and I cannot locate the slip.

1. Any competent user of the name can know a priori that Vantor is Vantor.
2. Since everything is necessarily self-identical, and identicals are substitutable salva veritate even in modal contexts when the terms are rigid, it is necessary that Vantor is Obj-9.
3. The content of "Vantor is Vantor" and the content of "Vantor is Obj-9" are, on the direct-reference view, the same singular proposition p — a single object, standing in identity to itself; there is nothing else in either content to differentiate them.
4. Epistemic status attaches to what is believed rather than to the words used to believe it: if p can be known without experience at all, then p can be known without experience, and every sentence expressing p inherits that status.
5. Therefore "Vantor is Obj-9" is knowable a priori — and, by (2), knowable a priori to be necessary.
6. Nothing in (1)–(5) depended on these names, this object, or this science. So every true identity between rigid designators is knowable a priori.
7. The standard exemplars of the necessary a posteriori are all identities of exactly this form. So the category is empty: such truths are necessary and a priori, and a century of astrometry was not needed to know the one above.

I am unwilling to accept (7), and equally unwilling to surrender the necessity of identity or the rigidity of both names, since those carry the rest of the picture I want to keep. So: which step fails? Identify it, explain what made it look unobjectionable, and then price the alternatives — for each way out you would seriously consider, say what it costs elsewhere in the theory of reference and modality, and whether the necessary a posteriori survives it in recognizable form or only as a re-labelled distinction.
