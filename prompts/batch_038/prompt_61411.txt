I am working through a chapter in a recently published edited collection on the metaphysics of science. Its section on objective chance offers the following gloss of Lewis's Principal Principle:

"Lewis's Principal Principle says that chance is nothing over and above a constraint on rational belief: to say that the chance of A at a time is x is to say that a rational agent who learns this must believe A to degree x, whatever else she may know. This is what makes his paper a subjectivist's guide to objective chance — the objectivity in question belongs to the norms governing our credences rather than to the world. Nor is the Principle an extra assumption bolted onto some independent account of chance; it is the entire content of the notion, since whatever plays this belief-guiding role deserves the name. The proviso is easy to state. Certain evidence is inadmissible, namely evidence about how the event in question actually turns out, for such evidence would show the chance to be one or zero and thereby destroy the chance hypothesis it accompanies. With the proviso in place the Principle is safe from the well-known worry that Humean chance hypotheses are self-undermining, and the so-called New Principle of Hall and Thau is nothing but the old Principle with the proviso folded into the chance operator; Lewis's account therefore required no revision. Two consequences follow. No evidence, of any kind, can ever count against a chance hypothesis. And a rational agent's credence in a proposition can never come apart from that proposition's chance."

The chapter is by a professional philosopher, so I assume I am missing something, but I came to it inclined to think that objective chance is supposed to be a genuine feature of the world rather than a shadow cast by norms on belief, and the first two sentences strike me as hostile to that thought. Before I rely on the passage in my own work, I would like an accuracy audit of it.

Could you take the gloss sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a specialist on chance and credence would accept without qualification, which are simplifications that would survive charitable expansion, and which are misrepresentations? More specifically:

- Does the gloss state the Principle's logical form correctly, and what turns on its being a constraint on conditional credences rather than a requirement about what one must believe once one learns a chance? Is anything lost by rendering it as a claim about what an agent who learns the chance must believe, whatever else she knows?
- Is its reading of the phrase subjectivist's guide to objective chance one Lewis himself could accept, given the role he assigns to the Principle in fixing the concept of chance and his separate views about what makes some function the chance function for a world? Where exactly does the gloss overreach?
- Is its account of the admissibility proviso right — both as to what the proviso excludes and as to why it is needed? Does the diagnosis that outcome information would show the chance to be one or zero capture Lewis's actual reason for restricting the Principle?
- Does the proviso in fact dispose of the self-undermining worry, or is the difference between the original Principle and its successor more substantive than the gloss suggests? Is there a live dispute about whether the newer principle can be recovered from the older one, and if so, which side does the gloss's confidence presuppose?
- Do the two closing consequences follow from anything true in the passage, or do they collapse once the earlier claims are corrected? If they collapse, say what the defensible nearby claims are that a careful reader might substitute for them.

Where the gloss is defensible, please say so explicitly rather than treating every sentence as suspect; I want to know which parts I can cite.
