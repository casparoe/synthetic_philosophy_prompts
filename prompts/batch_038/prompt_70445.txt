Ok, slightly unusual request. I'm giving a paper at a workshop in the spring on the socialist calculation debate and I have a draft that I think is either my best idea in years or a pile of clever nonsense, and I genuinely can't tell which. Better to find out now. Below is a condensed version (the real thing is longer and more careful, promise). I want comments, not encouragement — and you don't need to look anything up, I want your judgment rather than your references.

Working title: "Who Gets to Be Wrong: Residual Risk and the Calculation Debate"

§1. Setup, as usually told. Two moves, two replies. Mises: without genuine market prices for capital goods there's no common denominator for comparing heterogeneous inputs against outputs, so rational economic calculation is impossible in a complex economy. Lange: fine, then have a Central Planning Board do what Walras's auctioneer does — announce parametric prices, instruct managers to minimize cost at those prices and to expand output until marginal cost equals price, observe the resulting surpluses and shortages, revise, iterate. Hayek: that reply concedes everything important. The knowledge that matters isn't the kind that arrives in statistical aggregates; it is dispersed, local, often inarticulate, and the board's iteration would be hopelessly slower than the discovery process it pretends to imitate. Then everyone argues about who won.

§2. My wedge. Both the impossibility claim and the reply, I think, are almost always read in what I'll call a *computational* register: is the allocation problem solvable, by whom, with what data, how fast? In that register Lange is at worst an optimist about compute, and the last fifteen years have been kind to him — RL agents learning to allocate inside simulated economies, gradient methods converging on fixed points nobody writes down, logistics and ad-allocation systems doing continuous re-pricing that looks an awful lot like the tâtonnement Lange described. If the debate is only about computation, I'm prepared to say Lange's side is no longer obviously losing.

But I want to argue that the deep function of prices isn't informational at all. It's editorial. Prices determine which mistakes get made cheaply and which get made expensively, by determining who absorbs the loss when an estimate turns out wrong. Call this the *residual* reading: the price system is a device for distributing the burden of error across many small, mutually insulated bearers, so that being wrong is survivable and therefore frequent — and its frequency is precisely what generates the local knowledge Hayek says the board can't have.

§3. Reframing Hayek. On this reading the tacit-knowledge argument is really about the *locality of error*, not the locality of knowledge. The man on the spot matters not primarily because he knows things the board cannot be told, but because he is the person who discovers fastest that he was wrong and who pays for it most directly. Knowledge and liability are co-located. Pry them apart — let the person holding the knowledge not bear the loss — and you don't merely slow the flow of information, you degrade the quality of the knowledge being generated, because it is no longer being edited by consequences.

§4. What follows. (a) Lange's board shouldn't be criticized as a slow computer; it should be criticized as a device that concentrates loss. Its pathology is that error becomes correlated and systemic rather than dispersed and idiosyncratic. (b) That's why the "but a modern multinational is internally a planned economy" retort cuts both ways: firms can plan internally exactly because they can push residual risk out onto a market that prices it, and because internal failure has a bounded blast radius. (c) It also gives a prediction about AI-centralized allocation: it should work where losses are cheap to observe and quickly attributable (routing, inventory, ad auctions) and fail where they're slow, diffuse, or politically absorbable (health capacity, schooling, infrastructure siting).

§5. The part I'm least sure about. Whether "distributing the burden of error" is doing real work or is just Knightian risk-bearing plus property rights wearing a new hat. Also whether I'm smuggling in the assumption that error is attributable at all under centralization — maybe the best socialist reply is that the board can *simulate* liability, e.g. by making managers' careers and reputations the residual claim. I suspect that reply fails, but I don't have a clean argument for why, and I don't want to hand-wave it.

So, what I'd like from you:

1. Tell me whether the computational/residual distinction in §2 is load-bearing or just relabeling. Bluntness appreciated.
2. Is §3 a fair reading of Hayek, or have I turned him into something he'd disown? And flag anything in §1 you think I've garbled historically — from memory is fine, no citations, just say what looks off to you.
3. Give me the single strongest objection to §4(c) that I haven't already anticipated in §5, and say which tradition it comes from.
4. One concrete case, real or constructed, that would badly embarrass the locality-of-error thesis.
5. Overall: does any of this strike you as genuinely interesting, or is it the sort of thing that sounds deep in a seminar and dissolves under questioning? Point at the specific parts that survive and the ones that don't. I'd rather hear it from you than from the commentator, who is a Marxist economist and will not be gentle.
