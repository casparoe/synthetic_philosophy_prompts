I dispatch ambulances for a night shift outfit and for about a year now I've been slowly working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments on my phone between calls. No philosophy training, never went to uni, I just got curious after a coworker mentioned the impartial spectator thing and it turned out to be about my job basicly. I've been keeping a list in my notes app of the verdicts Smith hands down, in my own words, and I want to use them to work out what he'd say about some cases off my shift.

His verdicts so far, as I've got them:

1. Sympathy isn't a direct read on what someone else feels, it's us imagining ourselves into their spot. So approval tracks how well their feelings line up with what we'd feel in their shoes.
2. Feelings can be too low as well as too high. Overshooting grief and anger he calls weakness and fury; undershooting he calls stupidity, insensibility, want of spirit, and he says we can't enter into either, we're just baffled by them.
3. But the person who feels the whole weight of their own disaster and still holds themselves to a pitch others can actually accompany, he's the one Smith says gets the real respect and admiration, not merely the sympathy.
4. The China one. A humane European hears an earthquake swallowed the whole empire with millions dead: he's sad, makes melancholy reflections, maybe can't sleep, but he'll sleep soundly enough tonight. Tell him he's losing his little finger tomorrow and he won't sleep at all. Smith doesn't shrug at this, he brings in conscience and the man within the breast to correct it.
5. The actual crowd's applause doesn't settle your self-judgement: a man praised for something he knows he didn't do gets no comfort from it. So the imagined spectator is a check on the real ones, not a summary of them.
6. Justice can be forced out of a person, gratitude and beneficence can't. Not helping is blameworthy in sentiment but not punishable; injuring is punishable.
7. The poor man's son who chases wealth his whole life and never gets the peace he pictured. Smith says the illusion is worth having because it keeps industry moving. He seems able to approve a system whose fuel is a deception.
8. Fortune gets a vote in our actual moral feelings whether we like it or not. The careless driver who happens to kill someone gets condemned far harder than the equally careless one who kills nobody, and Smith names this as an irregularity in us and then defends it as useful.
9. Fitness and utility count for something in approval, we like a thing for being well suited to its purpose, but he doesn't think that's the main engine.

Now the cases I want run through him.

A: two crews at the same bad crash. One paramedic has been doing it 22 years, walks up, does everything right, voice flat, and afterwards told me she doesn't feel much anymore, it just doesn't reach her. The other is six weeks in, white faced, hands shaking, visibly fighting through it, and also did everything right.

B: a woman who's given ten percent of her take home to an overseas charity for years, purely because she read the numbers and worked out it did the most good per pound, isn't especially moved by it, and has never told anyone, including her husband. Beside her, a guy who jumped in a canal last month to pull a stranger out, was terrified the whole time, saved the man, and now tells the story weekly.

C: two callers I've actually had. One reported his wife's death completely calmly, no distress, gave me the address perfectly, and I remember thinking something was off about him. The other was sobbing so hard she couldn't give me the address for two minutes and I remember thinking she was being hysterical.

So: what's his verdict on each of A, B and C, and I want the reasoning rather than a label. Then the bit I actually care about. It seems to me like more than one rule fits all of 1 through 9: approval means your feeling sits at the pitch a spectator could go along with; or admiration scales with what self-command cost you; or motive is what counts and outcome doesn't, except Smith keeps letting outcome back in; or the fitness/utility one is doing more work than he admits. Tell me which rule generates which prediction on A, B and C, be explicit about where they split, and say which one you think he's really running. Then give me one extra case, one not five, that would separate the top two candidates better than anything I listed. And if I've mangled him somewhere in the paraphrases, or if one of these is just outside what the framework can handle, I suspect B is because of number 4 and because that woman's spectator is a spreadsheet no one can imagine themselves into, say that plainly instead of papering over it. Don't send me a bibliography or tell me which edition to get, I just want the reasoning.
