I am in the second year of a comparative history program, and this term I am in a seminar on philosophies of history, which has meant a lot of Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, and Toynbee, plus a token week on a popular generational scheme that sorts the past into four turnings of about twenty years each. The reading has been fine; what has been nagging at me is a single recurring feature that none of my instructors has ever named, and I have come to suspect that it has a name and that I simply do not know it. In every one of these theories, the length of the cycle is not fixed before the evidence is examined. It is chosen from the material afterward, and once chosen, the material obligingly confirms it. Ibn Khaldun gives a dynasty roughly three or four generations, on the order of a hundred and twenty years; when it is objected that the Ottoman house endured about six centuries, the answer is not that the theory has been refuted but that the dynasty had in substance already died and merely persisted as a shell, or that the longer span is composed of shorter ones nested inside it. Spengler allots each culture a life span on the order of a millennium and divides it into seasons, but the boundaries between seasons are located by matching styles — the mathematics, the music, the architecture — rather than by dates, so a culture that lingers is said to have a long winter, and the cultures in the catalogue are the ones that happened to fit. When a polity declines to cycle at all, Polybius can call it a mixed constitution and exempt it, at least for a while. When the announced crisis of the generational scheme fails to arrive on schedule, the reply is that turnings are elastic and stretch under strain. In each case something substantive seems to be claimed, and historians argue productively about it, yet I cannot identify any way the claim could have come out otherwise.

I have worked through the obvious labels and none of them fits exactly, which is why I am asking rather than settling it myself. It is not the familiar complaint that a theory survives by patching itself after a failed prediction, because here there is never a failed prediction to patch: the parameter is set, silently, in advance of anyone being in a position to be wrong. It is not the habit of drawing the target around the bullet holes, because the theorist is not cherry-picking a few striking hits out of many misses — the whole sequence, rises and falls alike, is being read as one periodic pattern. And "unfalsifiable" seems too blunt an instrument, since it does not capture what strikes me as the specific defect: the period is a free dial that can be turned until the data fit, and turning it is not registered by anyone, including the theorist, as a concession. Underneath this is a second worry I cannot shake. History is a single series that happens once, and I suspect that any sufficiently long sequence of rises and falls could be described as cycling at some scale or other by a patient enough author. Economists, I gather, have catalogued cycles of several different lengths — ones measured in half centuries, ones measured in a decade or so, several in between — and I cannot see why the coexistence of all of those would not cover whatever the record turns out to show. Whether that is the same problem as the one in my seminar or a cousin of it is part of what I would like you to tell me.

So: does this configuration have an established name, in the philosophy of history or in the methodology of the sciences more generally — a theory whose characteristic period is fixed retrospectively and can be re-chosen without the re-choosing counting against it? If it does, I want the term, a precise statement of what the term picks out, and an explicit comparison with the near neighbors I listed, saying for each how the thing I have described departs from it and which of them fits best. If it does not, please coin one, and define it sharply enough that I could apply it as a test: what would I have to find in a theory to know that it has this property? I care more about that test than about the label, so please mark the boundaries — in particular, what a cyclical theory of history would have to do to lack the property, whether any real theory manages to lack it, and whether a theory that announced its period in advance and stuck to it would still be in trouble for the separate reason that one series is not enough to detect a cycle. Since much of this is a matter of judgment about how the literature carves things up rather than settled fact, please also state how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the one point you are least sure of; I would rather have your uncertainty marked than a smooth answer.
