I'm in year 3 of a philosophy-of-history PhD and the chapter I have to present at a workshop in about a month is on whether contrastive why-questions in macro-history are even well posed. My working idea: when someone asks why P rather than Q, a huge chunk of the explanatory work is being done by how Q is carved, and I suspect a lot of the standard answers to the Needham-type question are only answers to the coarsest possible carving — "Britain" versus "China," two thousand years and a dozen regions bundled into each side.

So here's the test I've half invented. Take the question of why the industrial revolution happened in Britain and not China, and re-specify the contrast in several ways at once: swap China for the Yangzi delta or Jiangnan; swap Britain for the whole North Sea region including the Low Countries; slide the window from 1800 to 1850, or back to why it didn't happen in Song China; and, the nastiest one, switch back to what Needham actually asked, which was about the emergence of the modern scientific worldview rather then about industrial output at all.

Rank these five candidate answers by how much of the contrast they still explain once you run them through all of that, where 1 means the explanation does basically the same work under every re-carving and 0 means it only exists because of the coarse one:

- coal and the New World (Pomeranz: lucky geology plus imported ghost acres)
- high wages and cheap energy (Allen: British input prices made labour-saving machinery worth inventing, Chinese ones didn't)
- political fragmentation and the competitive market for ideas (Mokyr's version, and the older Landes version behind it)
- Elvin's high-level equilibrium trap
- the "useful knowledge" culture — treat this as the version that emphasizes printed how-to literature, instrument-makers, and the circulation of technique, not competing patrons

Give me exactly five numbered items, most stable first, and nothing else: no intro, no closing summary, no throat-clearing about how contested the literature is (I know). Each item should name the answer, give the score out of 1, and then one or two sentences — one identifying the single re-carving that hurts it most, one saying why. If you think one of them can't really be scored on this dimension, still slot it in, score it, and say so in the same item. And if two of them are close enough that ordering them against each other is arbitrary, note that inside the item rather than as a separate remark.
