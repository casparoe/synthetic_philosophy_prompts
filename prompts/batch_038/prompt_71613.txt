Can you write me a short story, maybe 2,000 words, more if it needs it, that dramatizes the future-contingents problem? I dont want the usual thing where a guy in a toga muses about tomorrow's naval engagement — I want the philosophy to come out of institutions and paperwork instead of out of people talking about it. So here's a setup I made up; rename the pieces however you like.

A maritime republic runs its whole legal system off a register called the Ledger of Pending Things. Every official statement gets a stamp from the office of the Futurary, and there are three stamps. Fixed: true and not able to be otherwise — the past, the present, and whatever follows from them. Pending: neither true nor false yet, because it concerns something that may or may not come about. Void: nonsense, self-contradiction, talk about nobody. And the crucial rule: only a Fixed entry can found a claim in court. A Pending entry is legally weightless. This is why the city's entire marine-insurance trade rests on a standing fiction that everyone agrees not to examine too closely.

There's also a much-fought-over rule of construction, three clauses that are supposed to fit together:
1. whatever is Fixed cannot be otherwise;
2. nothing impossible follows from anything possible;
3. therefore nothing is possible that neither is nor will be — so the only real possibilities are the ones that actually come off.

One school in the city swears by all three. Another says clause 3 is absurd, so one of the first two has to break, and they attack clause 1 hardest, on the grounds that some entries were stamped Fixed yesterday and are Fixed today only in virtue of how tomorrow turns out — a past fact that leans on the future. Nobody's ever won that argument and both sides know it.

Now the story. There's supposed to be a battle tomorrow off a shoal outside the harbor. The Futurary — make her a career clerk, not a philosopher, someone who cares about the forms being correct — has been asked by the underwriters to stamp the battle's entry Fixed a day early. And there's a genuine reason to want it: the grain convoy's insurance is unenforceable while the entry sits Pending, the convoy won't sail uninsured, and the city eats badly within a month if it doesn't sail. But the admiral has already told her, in as many words, that if the outcome is Fixed there's no point in his night maneuver, and his captains agree with him — no one needs to do anything about what's already true.

Give me three things. The scene where the request lands on her desk, and what she notices about how it's worded. An argument in a counting house or a tavern where somebody actually runs the three clauses like a proof and somebody else takes a swing at clause 1 with the leaning-past objection. And an ending where she stamps or doesn't, and the cost is concrete — bodies, bushels, a contract, a career — rather than a metaphysical verdict.

A few constraints. Don't name real philosophers or real schools; if a reader can work out which ancient argument you're staging, all the better, but nobody in the story says so out loud. Let the reasoning show through what people do with stamps, wagers, cargo manifests and orders — no speeches about fatalism or free will, and no purple prose. And don't resolve the metaphysics. I want it left genuinely open, right to the last line, whether her stamp was a report or a cause.
