I have just spent three hours arguing with a close friend — call her Nadia — about how much we owe to people who are drowning, literally and otherwise, and I have come away able to report her verdicts on almost anything but unable to state her position. She is a water-systems engineer who spent six years doing field work and now runs a municipal utility. She has extremely strong rescue reflexes and no philosophical training, which may be precisely why she is immune to the usual ways of pinning her down.

We began, as everyone does, with the child in the shallow pond, and I built outward from there. Her record for the evening, in the order I put the cases to her:

1. A small child face-down in knee-deep water twenty metres off; you would ruin your trousers and arrive twenty minutes late to something that matters. Verdict: you go in. She considers the case uninteresting, and said that anyone who has to be argued into it is not the sort of person argument will fix.

2. The same pond, but you are wearing a suit you cannot replace and you would miss a flight you have been saving toward for two years — call it a thousand dollars of damage. Verdict: you go in. Her gloss was that the comparison is not the suit against the child but the suit against the other things you spend a thousand dollars on without noticing.

3. The same pond, but there are nine other able adults on the bank, all watching, none moving. Verdict: you go in, and you start shouting at the nine while you do. Her words: "That they aren't is a reason you must. It isn't a permission."

4. The water is not a pond but the settling lagoon of an electroplating works, and going in will very likely cost you the use of your legs. Verdict: you may go in; you need not. She said only: "There's a point where the demand stops being morality's to make."

5. A letter arrives naming and photographing a six-year-old on another continent. A wire transfer the size of a good dinner will fund her treatment, and the organization will send the file back with the procedure date on it. Verdict: you send it. She added that this case is easier than the pond, since you don't get wet, and that she liked knowing where the money went.

6. You pass a clinic where a nurse says they are sixty dollars short on a procedure that will keep the patient in the chair from going blind; the patient is right there. Verdict: you pay. She said blindness and death differ in degree and not in kind, and that she has no patience for any view that makes the difference turn on which.

7. Would you be obliged to forgo treatment your own daughter needs in order to fund the same treatment for five strangers? Verdict: no. She would not elaborate beyond saying she was not going to be argued out of that one.

8. Two organizations, one of which is, on its own published and independently checked figures, roughly twice as effective per dollar. Verdict: if you are going to give, you must give to the better one. She insisted that being obliged to give and being obliged to give well are two separate demands, and that both are real.

She also disowns several labels. She is not a utilitarian and resents the suggestion. She will not endorse the general proposition that distance is morally irrelevant: "I hold that it didn't matter in the cases you gave me." She will not say whether she has a threshold, and when pressed she said she would rather have unreliable verdicts than a formula.

On Sunday I am putting one more case to her, and I want to know what she will say before she says it. Her uncle dies and leaves her forty-five thousand dollars, unexpected and unencumbered. She has been saving toward a down payment for eight years; the money covers it outright. There is an organization she already trusts — the one from case 5 — which would deploy the forty-five thousand, on figures it publishes and that outsiders audit, to avert something between fifteen and forty deaths among people who will never be named to her and will never learn her name. The windfall is genuinely one-off: her salary, her rent, and her future obligations are all unchanged by it. Is she required to hand it over?

What I want is not advice about the money and not a survey of the debate, but a reconstruction of her decision procedure, argued from the record above:

(a) Your prediction of her verdict — required, permitted but admirable, or something she would refuse to classify — with a rough confidence and a sentence or two of what she will actually say aloud.

(b) The candidate rules that account for all eight verdicts without strain. There should be more than one; give me at least four if four genuinely exist, including rules she would repudiate if you named them to her. For each, state what it delivers on the new case and why.

(c) Which of those the record best supports, and which particular items tip it. Where the record is simply silent, say so plainly instead of filling the silence with plausibility.

(d) How much evidential weight her incidental remarks should carry — above all her comment in case 5 about liking to know where the money went. Is that a clue to the rule, or texture I should discount?

(e) If the rule you favour carries an implication she would find distasteful, which of the eight verdicts is she most likely to abandon rather than accept it?

(f) A further case, of your own design, that would best separate the surviving rules. State each rule's predicted verdict on it and say which pair it separates; if no single case separates all of them, give me the second case as well.

Please argue from the cases themselves. I don't want a bibliography, a reading list, or a roll call of philosophers — the names would only tell me which shelf you took this from, and I have no way of checking any of it. Something I can read in ten minutes will serve me better than something exhaustive.
