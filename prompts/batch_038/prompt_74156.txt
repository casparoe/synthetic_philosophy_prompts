I am finishing a dissertation chapter in comparative historical sociology. It argues that, given fiscal exhaustion and the fragmentation of the elite coalition holding a fiscal-military monarchy together, the collapse of that regime was highly probable by a certain date, and it says so in explicitly probabilistic terms. At the workshop, a commentator dismissed the whole enterprise with the proverb 'for want of a nail the kingdom was lost': since some trivial and unforeseeable event could have deflected everything, he said, any probability attached to an outcome of that scale is false, and the honest description of history is simply that it is contingent. I suspect the proverb is being made to do work it cannot do, but I want the available work laid out before I reply.

Please give me the menu of precise claims that one sentence could be making, distinguishing at a minimum claims about the dynamics of a system from claims about probabilities from claims about how much difference a particular event made, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical thesis that could be true of history, or false, along with what would count as evidence for or against it. Then tell me which of those readings, if any, is genuinely incompatible with attaching a probability to a coarse-grained outcome such as regime collapse; which would actually license my commentator's conclusion rather than merely sounding like it; and which pairs of readings that people run together cannot both be true. Finally, say whether anything in the proverb's own logical form — a chain of successive 'for want of' links, each presented as sufficient for the next — cuts against the use he made of it.
