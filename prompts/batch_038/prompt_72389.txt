I'm a third-year PhD student, nominally in philosophy but I spend most of my time in the logic group, and I'm working on the epistemology of set-theoretic justification — basically trying to push back on the mood in the field where you just keep climbing the large cardinal heirarchy and treat the climb itself as evidence. Last week I threw this argument at our foundations reading group as a piece of provocation and it landed way harder than I intended: one of the math students said it was obviously broken somewhere but couldn't say where, another said he thought it was basically right and that I should publish it, and I've been unable to look at it neutrally since. So: could you act as a proof theorist who has also done serious work on the epistemology of mathematics, think carefully rather than reaching for the standard deflationary reply, and start by brainstorming a list of candidate failure points before you commit to one? I'd like you to be explicit about which reading of each loaded term you're working with.

Here's the argument. (1) For any recursively axiomatized theory T extending a modest amount of arithmetic, if T is consistent then T does not prove Con(T), so any proof that T is consistent has to be carried out using principles not already available inside T. (2) Whenever S proves Con(T), S sits strictly above T in the consistency-strength ordering — that's more or less what the ordering means, so I take (2) to be close to analytic. (3) Moving strictly up that ordering means asserting more: you commit to the existence of bigger infinities, or to induction principles you didn't have before. More assertion, more ways to be wrong. Gentzen is the clean example — to get Con(PA) he had to trade ordinary induction for transfinite induction up to ε₀, and nobody thinks he got that for free. (4) So a consistency proof never increases your total warrant. It just swaps the question "is T consistent?" for the question "is S consistent?", and by (3) the second question is strictly worse than the first. (5) Iterate: every consistency claim about any theory above primitive recursive arithmetic ultimately rests on principles whose consistency can only be secured by going higher still, and there's no top of the hierarchy. (6) Therefore for any such theory we have no non-circular reason to think it's consistent; our confidence in ZFC has no foundation whatever, it's just a habit. (7) And an inconsistent theory proves everything, so if ZFC turned out to be inconsistent, every theorem of ZFC would be false — including the boring stuff about integers and continuous functions that we never worried about. (8) Hence we ought to suspend belief in essentially all of modern mathematics.

I'll tell you where I stand, since you asked me to be honest about it: I'm not giving up (1), and I don't want to be told that consistency strength is a purely technical ordering with no bearing on risk, because the connection between the two is the whole engine of my project. If the thing has to break I'd much rather it break late, at (7) or (8), than early. So tell me which step you reject and why that step looked unimpeachable on the way in — I want the diagnosis, not a lecture on incompleteness, and no reading list, I just want the reasoning. Then price the alternatives: for each of (3), (4), (6), and (7), say what it costs me to drop it and what the cheapest available repair looks like, and rank those repairs. Last thing, and the part I actually can't sleep on: is the argument self-undermining? (6) is supposed to be established by Gödel's theorem and the facts about the strength ordering, which are themselves mathematics of exactly the sort (8) tells me to stop believing. Is that a straight reductio of the whole package, or does it just add to the bill? I suspect the answer depends on a distinction I've been sloppy about somewhere between "consistent", "true", and "justified", but I can't find which one it is.
