So this started as an argument about my friend Dave, who is a genuinely decent guy except that he cheated on his ex, and who last weekend went off on our mutual friend for flirting with somebody's boyfriend at a party. Everyone at the table agreed Dave was being a hypocrite. Then my roommate said something I haven't been able to shake: apparently the Dave problem generalises into a proof that nobody has standing to blame anybody, ever. He sketched it on the back of a menu and I've tidied it up a bit:

(1) Blaming someone for a fault F is at least partly a matter of presenting F as counting against them — as a reason to think worse of them and to hold it against them.
(2) Presenting F as counting against a person requires that the blamer not herself be committed, by her own conduct, to treating F as acceptable; otherwise the presentation contradicts the presenter.
(3) So if B has ever committed F, or something relevantly like it, B's blaming someone for F is self-undermining: either she withdraws the judgment, in which case there's no blame left to have standing for, or she keeps it, in which case her own act condemns her along with the blamed. Hypocrisy horn.
(4) Now the other case: B has never committed F. Then either B can take credit for having avoided it, or she can't.
(5) Whether a person ever ends up where F is even a live temptation — her upbringing, her temperament, who she happens to fall for, whether anyone ever actually offers her the bribe or the second glass — is overwelmingly a matter of luck, and nobody deserves credit or blame for luck.
(6) So B can't take credit for having avoided F.
(7) But blame from someone with no relevant superiority is just smugness: it carries the implicit message that I'm not the sort of person who does that, and if there are no grounds for that claim the blame has nothing behind it.
(8) Either B has committed F or she hasn't; by (3) and (7) she lacks standing either way.
(9) Therefore no one has standing to blame anyone for anything.

He's very pleased with himself and says the whole notion was always a courtroom import doing illegitimate work — federal courts need an injury in fact, a causal trace to the defendant, and a remedy the court can actually give, and moral blame has none of those, so of course the concept falls apart once you haul it out of the courthouse. Part of me agrees with that. Another part thinks (9) is flatly false — my mum blaming me for being nasty to my sister when we were kids was not a null event and she was not being smug — but I can't locate the joint where the thing breaks. It's the second horn that worries me most. The hypocrisy horn at least smells like textbook tu quoque, which I was told in first year is a fallacy, though I now suspect that was too quick, since plenty of serious people think hypocrisy really does do something to blame, and the reactive-attitudes stuff my TA loves seems to put the blamer's own attitudes right at the centre.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (5) if I possibly can — (5) feels like it has to be true of nearly everything — and I don't much care about (7). Walk me through why whatever you pick looked completely unobjectionable when you first read it, and then price the other exits: what has to be true about blame, or about luck, or about credit, for each of them to work. I want an actual diagnosis rather than a shrug, and if your honest answer is that (9) is right and my intuition about my mum is a prejudice I should give up, say that and argue for it rather than hedging around it. Also, tell me where your own diagnosis is shakiest: what argument or consideration would flip you to a different step, or convince you the whole thing is valid after all? I'd rather know the failure modes up front than have to go looking for them myself.
