I've spent the last year reading philosophy of biology on function mostly as a side effect of an argument I keep having in AI-adjacent spaces. People around me — LessWrong and the Alignment Forum crowd, my own Discord included — say things like *the function of the reward model is to encode preferences* or *this module is malfunctioning under distribution shift*, and I've become convinced that almost none of them has a determinate notion in hand. The rationalist reflex is to ask what a claim would have to be true of, and when I try that here, the phrase "the function of X" splits at least three ways: the Cummins-style causal-role reading, on which a function is just the contribution an item makes to some capacity of the containing system that the analyst has picked out as interesting; the ancestral selected-effects reading in the Wright–Millikan lineage, on which a function is the effect that explains why the trait's ancestors were retained by selection; and Godfrey-Smith's modern-history variant of the etiological approach, on which what matters is not the trait's origin but what has lately been maintaining it under selection. I want to write a short piece showing that these come apart, and that "function" and "malfunction" come apart *from each other*, but I don't want to lean on any of the canonical illustrations, because my intended audience has already pattern-matched those to death.

So: build me a thought experiment to specification. I want a single biological case, described in one paragraph, in which all three accounts above give a different answer on each of two questions — what the trait's function is, and whether one particular individual's copy of the trait is malfunctioning. Concretely, the target grid is: the ancestral selected-effects account assigns function F and says the individual *is* malfunctioning despite being in perfect health and leaving normal numbers of offspring; the causal-role account, with its explanatory interest fixed by the case, assigns a different function F* and says the individual is *not* malfunctioning — indeed that it performs at least as well as its conspecifics; and the modern-history account says the trait has *no* function at all right now, because neither F nor F* explains its recent maintenance under selection, so the individual is not malfunctioning either. All three verdicts must be generated by the case, not asserted in it.

The constraints are the point of the exercise, so please treat them as binding:

1. Invent the organism and its ecology. It must be biologically plausible, with a stated mechanism concrete enough that a reader could check it, and with its selection history fully specified in the case so that nobody can retreat to "we'd need to know more about the phylogeny."
2. None of the usual suspects: feathers and flight, the panda's thumb, the appendix or wisdom teeth, cavefish eyes, male nipples, snake pelvic spurs, whale hind limbs, sickle cell, lactase persistence, and nothing involving domestication or artificial selection.
3. The causal-role account's explanatory interest must be fixed inside the case, as a specific capacity a real biologist might want explained — otherwise Cummins's relativism dissolves the disagreement. But the trait must make a genuine, mechanistically real contribution to that capacity, not a token or strawman one.
4. The variant individual must be broken with respect to F and intact-or-better with respect to F*, and the case must say *how* — the mechanism, not just the outcome.
5. The scenario paragraph must be written in neutral biological description: no use of *function*, *purpose*, *malfunction*, *proper*, *adaptation*, and no "in order to" or "so that" constructions. If the verdicts are smuggled into the description, the case fails.

After the scenario, give me the grid explicitly, with the reasoning behind each of the three verdicts and each of the three malfunction verdicts. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to each cell if I remove it — in particular, what happens if the trait's current contribution turns out *not* to be redundant, and what happens if the ancestral selective agent is still around. Then give the strongest attack available to each of the two accounts that come out badly (I expect a causal-role theorist to insist functions attach to the trait-type rather than the token, and a modern-history theorist to argue that a handful of generations still counts as recent), and say whether you think each attack lands.

Two epistemic asks. First, I'd rather be contradicted than accommodated: if you conclude that some combination of these constraints is unsatisfiable, or that one of the three verdicts can only be secured by stipulation rather than derived, say so plainly and tell me which constraint to loosen — please don't quietly bend the case until the grid appears. Second, tell me which of the six cells you're least confident about and roughly why; I'm happy to hold the whole thing loosely, but I'd like to know where the load-bearing part is.
