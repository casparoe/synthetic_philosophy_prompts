I run coach training for an employee-assistance style program — strictly peer coaching, not therapy, 45-minute sessions, about six a week per coach, and the coaches are smart people with zero philosophy training. A lot of our material is borrowed from the modern Stoicism stuff, mostly the dichotomy of control, because it's the one idea clients latch onto instantly. Problem: it gets used at least four different ways in a session and only one of them is actually helping anybody. I need a triage procedure a coach can run in the first five minutes whenever a client says some version of "that's not in my control," and can re-run mid-session when the same line comes back later.

Ground it in the distinction the Stoics were actually drawing — the narrow reading where only assent, desire and aversion are up to us, so that your own body, your property and your reputation aren't strictly up to you at all, versus the popular modern reading that adds a third bucket of things partly up to us and tells you to internalize your goals. I know the popularizers argue about whether that third bucket is a genuine category or just the dichotomy restated, and I don't want the procedure to sneak past that argument. Pick a side, out loud, and tell me what the procedure gives up by picking it. Whatever you choose, it has to be usable by someone who has never read Epictetus and never will, so the procedure has to encode the reading rather than explain it.

Then give me the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, the branches where it comes out as "this is a healthy use, park it and get on with the session," the branches where it comes out as "this is a misfire," and for those, how the coach names it without sounding like they're grading the client, and the branches where it stops dead and hands the decision back to the client because it's a values question and not a facts question. Include a short list of the phrasings that should trip it — the ones that sound correct and aren't.

Run it on these four, anonymized and slightly mashed together from real sessions:

- an engineer facing a reorg who says the decision is above his pay grade, and who has stopped prepping for interviews and stopped speaking up in the skip-levels where he used to argue for keeping two of his teammates;
- a ward nurse who's adopted "I control my effort, not the outcome" and has stopped filing incident reports about a monitor that keeps falsely alarming, on the grounds that the hospital's budget isn't hers;
- a father whose adult son stopped taking his calls two years ago, who now says his son's choices were never his to make and reports sleeping properly for the first time in ages, nothing else about the situation changed;
- a client who says she can't help feeling jealous of her sister's promotion, so the feeling isn't something she's answerable for, and wants the coach to agree with her.

At least one of these should come out as a legitimate use, and I want the procedure to say so plainly even where it's structurally almost identical to one that shouldn't. Mark every step that's really a judgment call wearing a flowchart costume. And where what's in front of the coach isn't a philosophy problem at all and needs handing to someone with different training, I want the procedure to say that in words a tired coach can follow, not leave it to tact.

Start with one sentence giving me the bottom line — which reading you operationalized and the biggest thing the procedure will get wrong because of it — then go straight into the procedure. No preamble and don't restate any of this back to me.
