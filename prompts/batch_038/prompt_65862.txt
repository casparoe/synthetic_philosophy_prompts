I'm assembling a small anthology of short fiction about the philosophy of mind and need one story on pain asymbolia: the condition in which sensory discrimination of pain survives — the person can say where it is, how intense it is, whether it is sharp or burning — while its aversive, bothersome quality is abolished, so that patients report having pain but are not troubled by it. Write a story of 1,200 to 1,600 words under the following constraints.

Setting and premise. Not a brain lesion, and not a lobotomy ward. Make it a reversible pharmaceutical or neuromodulation intervention in a clinical trial, so that the protagonist has had the experience both ways and knows what he is missing. You choose the condition being treated, but it should be one where the ordinary trade-off is genuinely hard: he is neither a torture victim being offered mercy nor a malingerer escaping something, and he should have real stakes in the outcome beyond curiosity.

The distinction to preserve. The intervention must not be anesthesia, sedation, distraction, stoicism, or bravery, and the story has to make that difference visible rather than asserted. At some point he must be fully awake, unmedicated in any other respect, and accurately reporting the location, quality, and intensity of a noxious stimulus being applied right now — while showing no impulse to make it stop. No character may call this high pain tolerance, grit, or being used to it, and the narrator may not settle the matter by telling us he has made peace with it.

Philosophy carried by events. Two positions must each receive concrete dramatic support, in scenes rather than speeches: first, the view that what makes a pain bad is its felt unpleasantness, a property of the experience itself, so that removing it removes the badness and leaves him straightforwardly better off; second, the view that pain's badness is bound up with the subject's wanting it to cease — that the experience issues a demand, however inarticulate — and that the intervention leaves him with an experience that no longer demands anything of him. Then handle a third complication, which is the one I most want: at least one character must raise the possibility that the protagonist's own reports are no longer good evidence about what he is undergoing, because his use of the word pain has quietly shifted, or because he has lost the contrast needed to mean what he says, so that "it hurts but I don't mind" may be a sentence he is merely disposed to utter sincerely. Let the reader weigh this. Do not resolve it with a revelation, a confession, or a relapse.

Evidential discipline. Include at least one scene giving the reader something that could count as evidence about which picture is right — a protective behavior he does or does not perform, an injury he keeps walking on, a choice he makes while the intervention is paused, a measurement he can see but not feel — and keep that item ambiguous enough to be read both ways. No clinician may explain what it shows.

Prohibitions. No character may say qualia, phenomenology, hedonic, or asymbolia in dialogue; no character may state a philosophical thesis as a thesis; no sadistic doctor; no last-minute scream. The ending must leave it genuinely open whether he has been cured or diminished, and must give the reader usable grounds for both verdicts.

After the story, add roughly 300 words under the heading Craft Note. There, say where you took liberties with the clinical phenomenon and where you compressed, simplified, or invented; identify which contested readings your scenes lean on; and separate what is broadly settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely disputed — do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus, and do not imply that your story decides anything it does not. Where a detail in your story is one you are not confident about, flag it as uncertain rather than asserting it. No citations, no reading list, no authors, books, or papers named: the note should read as intellectual honesty, not scholarship.
