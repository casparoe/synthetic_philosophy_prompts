I've spent about two years in a working group drafting a proposal for a lotted second chamber for our region — sort of a standing citizens' assembly with real powers, members drawn by lot from the population, staggered renewal, paid, with research staff of its own. Half the group are sortition true believers and half are skeptical, and the argument between them has gone completely dead in a way I find weirdly fascinating. Nobody is dumb, nobody is arguing in bad faith, we all read the same case studies — Ireland, the various climate assemblies, juries — and I dont think anyone actually disputes the empirical record much. And yet two years in we are not one inch closer.

Roughly the shape of it. The sortitionists say elections systematically select for the ambitious, the well-funded and the good-at-arguing; that they make representatives responsive to donors and to the next election rather than to the merits of a policy; that a body drawn by lot is descriptively representative in a way no elected body has ever been — actually half women, actual working-class people, actual twenty-somethings — and deliberates better because nobody in it is up for reelection or under a whip. They also lean on an equality claim: with a lottery every citizen literally has the same chance of holding office, which elections cannot deliver. The skeptics answer that a lotted body has no accountability, since you can't vote its members out and their terms expire regardless, so the ordinary sanction mechanism isn't weakened but categorically absent; that lots of drawn people decline to serve, so who actually shows up depends on who can afford the months away, quietly reintroducing a class filter; that amateurs without party machinery end up dependent on their staff and on whoever briefs them, so real power moves to unelected aides and lobbyists; and that a chamber nobody chose will struggle to make unpopular decisions stick, whereas an elected government can at least say we were authorized to do this. They add that mini-publics seem to work precisely because they are small, temporary and advisory, and that scaling that up to a legislature changes what the thing is.

My hypothesis — tell me if it's wrong rather than just developing it — is that this isn't a factual dispute at all, and that there are three seperate disagreements stacked on top of each other while everyone argues about the top one. First, the baseline: the sortitionists compare the lottery to the electoral system we actually have, the skeptics compare it to a democracy in which authorization and accountability genuinely work, which doesn't exist anywhere. Second, the word accountability is doing two jobs, sanction-based accountability and having to justify yourself publicly to the people affected, and my side thinks you can have the second without the first while the skeptics think the second is decorative without it. Third, underneath both, a value disagreement about whether legitimacy comes from the fairness of the procedure that produced the decision or from the decision being traceable back to the governed — and I don't think either camp knows they're making that one.

So: is the diagnosis right, partly right, or am I missing something structural — maybe it is empirical after all, e.g. whether lotted bodies would actually produce different policy, or whether compliance and perceived legitimacy would fall, in which case tell me which study design or which piece of evidence could decide it. And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress here: a distinction to be drawn, a fact to go look for, or a design change — veto-only powers, partial renewal by lot each year, a mixed chamber with an elected minority — that would dissolve the argument rather than relocate it? Be honest if the answer is that this one can't be resolved but only settled by choosing a value, and say which choice it is.
