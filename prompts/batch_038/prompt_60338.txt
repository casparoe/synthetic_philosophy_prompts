I read a long essay in a general-interest magazine in which a development economist argues that the quarrel over why some nations are rich was effectively settled by a famous 2001 economics paper, and I want an accuracy audit of the passage before I repeat any of it in a seminar. Here is the core of the argument as I have transcribed it:

'Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson settled the question with a single instrument: the death rate European settlers faced when they first arrived in a colony. Where Europeans survived, they settled in numbers and transplanted the property-protecting institutions of home; where they died in droves of malaria and yellow fever, they built extractive states designed only to pull resources out, and those institutions outlived independence. Settler mortality is a clean instrument, because it can influence a country's income today only through the institutions it brought about, so the very large association they estimate between institutional quality and income per capita is a causal effect rather than a correlation. Their clinching evidence is the reversal of fortune: the most prosperous parts of the world in 1500 are among the poorest today and the poorest then are rich now, which no geographic or cultural account can generate, since the climate has not changed and culture moves far too slowly to reverse a continent's fate within five centuries. And once institutions are held constant, being in Africa or near the equator has no effect on income whatsoever — which is simply what it means to say that geography does not matter. The policy lesson is that development aid to countries with extractive institutions is wasted money: nothing else will work until the institutions change.'
