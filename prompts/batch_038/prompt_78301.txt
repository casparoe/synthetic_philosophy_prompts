I would like a disciplined analysis of the My Lai massacre as a test case for the explanatory dispute between Christopher Browning and Daniel Goldhagen. Please first state that dispute as you understand it, in causal rather than merely moral terms. On one side, Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101: middle-aged reservists from Hamburg, drawn largely from social cohorts that had not been most intensively socialized into Nazism, who became shooters in occupied Poland, and whose commander at Józefów in July 1942 offered his men the chance to step out of the first massacre at no cost to themselves, an offer only a small minority accepted. On the other side, Goldhagen's reply: that the load-bearing cause was not obedience, peer pressure, careerism, gradual brutalization, or the machinery of the state, but the perpetrators' own conviction, shared with a great deal of German society, that Jews ought not to exist, so that the killing was in a relevant sense voluntary and even eager. Both accounts accept that the shooters were, in the end, willing; they disagree about what made them so.

Take the following features of the episode as given. On 16 March 1968, Charlie Company of Task Force Barker entered the hamlets of Sơn Mỹ, in an area its troops had nicknamed Pinkville, expecting to find a Viet Cong battalion; it found no organized enemy force. Over the course of a morning, several hundred unarmed villagers — overwhelmingly women, children, and old men — were killed, some shot in an irrigation ditch. The company had, by that point, suffered roughly twenty-eight casualties to mines and booby traps in three months in country without ever fixing an enemy force. Its commander was acquitted; twenty-six men were charged and one, the platoon leader, was convicted, sentenced to life, and released to house arrest after the president commuted the sentence, a commutation much of the American public greeted with open sympathy. A second company killed additional villagers in an adjacent hamlet the same day. A helicopter crew landed, confronted the shooters, and evacuated civilians. Accounts differ on how many men in the platoon actually fired, and on whether some refused or fired away from the victims; treat that as contested rather than resolved. The institutional setting included body counts as a metric of performance, designated free-fire zones, and among soldiers a shorthand for the expectation that killing Vietnamese civilians would go lightly punished if it was punished at all.

With that on the table, please do the following.

1. Sort the features above into the three candidate explanatory variables — situation, culture, individual character — and say which features are load-bearing for which account. Flag anything that belongs to neither: for instance, whether the quantitative production of incentives through body counts and zone designations is a situational mechanism, a cultural one, or a distinct organizational variable that both Browning and Goldhagen underweight.

2. Treat abstention as the discriminating evidence. At Józefów, a costless exit was offered and almost no one took it; at My Lai, an outsider crew intervened and some men reportedly did not fire. What does each theory predict about the rate, the distribution, and the character of refusal, and does the My Lai pattern — such as it can be established — favor one of them?

3. Address the problem of the cultural variable without an exterminationist state. There was no elite-orchestrated doctrine that the Vietnamese ought not to exist; official policy pointed the other way, and the army prosecuted the event as a crime, badly. Ambient dehumanization was nonetheless present, in vocabulary and in expected impunity. Does Goldhagen's mechanism require state-sponsored eliminationist ideology to do the work he assigns it, or does diffuse unofficial prejudice suffice? And if diffuse prejudice suffices, does the cultural variable remain distinguishable from the situational one, given that it operates through local unit norms and socialization?

4. Say what each theory implies for the distribution of blame across the platoon leader, the company commander, the brigade and division chain, the institutional design of the operation, and the public that sympathized with the one man convicted. Is the single conviction a vindication of the dispositional account, a refutation of it, or simply an artifact of an adjudicative system that can only individuate?

5. State honestly whether the episode can test this dispute at all. Both accounts were built to explain sustained, bureaucratized participation across years; My Lai was one morning, by conscripts on a twelve-month rotation, in a polity with a functioning press and courts. Is that a difference of degree, a boundary condition on the transfer of the debate, or a category error? If it is a boundary condition, specify precisely what would have to be added or removed for the debate to carry over.

6. Finally, tell me what finding about My Lai would count against the situational account and what would count against the cultural account, so that I can see whether the mapping you have produced is falsifiable or merely accommodating.

Where the record is genuinely disputed, say so and note how the dispute bears on your conclusions rather than resolving it by assertion. Where either theory is simply silent on some feature of the episode, mark the silence. I am not looking for a bibliography or for further cases; I want the mapping and its limits.
