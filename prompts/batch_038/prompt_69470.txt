I am in the third year of a doctorate in formal epistemology at a British university, and I am applying for a one-year research fellowship that requires a two-page proposal outlining a project I could complete within the award period. My supervisor is a decision theorist who has twice suggested, politely but firmly, that my idea is a notational worry rather than a substantive one, which is precisely why I want the proposal to be unusually clear about what would count as a real result. I should say at the outset that I do not want a bibliography or a literature review by citation; I will supply the references myself, and the proposal should instead refer to positions and arguments descriptively.

The half-formed idea concerns Jeffrey conditionalization. Since probability kinematics was introduced in the late 1960s, nearly all of the philosophical pressure has been applied to two places: the rigidity assumption, and the question of what fixes the new probabilities of the cells of the evidential partition, given that the experience does not appear to make the agent certain of any proposition. But I think the deeper unexamined commitment is the partition itself. The kinematic rule requires the agent to have settled, in advance, on a partition that the experience speaks to, and on which the experience's effect is confined; nothing inside the framework says which partition that is, and two partitions can be equally faithful descriptions of one and the same experience while licensing different updates — even when each satisfies rigidity relative to itself. My favourite case is perceptual: a single tasting of a wine can be reported under a coarse division (oaky or not) or a finer one (oaky-and-earthy, oaky-and-fruit-driven, neither), and the coarse description's conditionals are not preserved by the fine description's update. If that is right, then the framework contains an unregulated degree of freedom that is distinct from the familiar complaint about the prior, and — this is the part I most want pinned down — the freedom may leave a permanent residue rather than being washed out by later experience.

Please turn this into the proposal. It should contain: a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer rather than inviting a survey — something like whether there is a norm internal to the Bayesian framework that selects the partition an experience licenses, and whether distinct admissible partitions can diverge in the limit; an account of why the existing responses leave that question open, including why the coherence-style diachronic Dutch book arguments cannot adjudicate between partitions when each is internally coherent, and why Jeffrey's own concession that the numbers are made by the experience is normally read as concerning only the values assigned to the cells, not the choice of cells; the method, set out month by month for twelve months, distinguishing the formal work (defining admissibility, and proving either a uniqueness result or constructing a pair of admissible partitions whose kinematics differ in the limit) from the case work and from one application that would make the project legible outside formal epistemology, for instance systems that must update on evidence delivered under a representational scheme that is itself revised over time; the obvious alternatives I should explain why I am not taking, notably dissolving the issue as pragmatic, or absorbing it into imprecise probability, together with the cost of each; what a successful result would look like; what would count as the project's failing, including the possibility that any two admissible partitions converge and the whole thing collapses into a curiosity, and the possibility that the only selection rule available turns out to be frankly pragmatic, which would make it a negative result; and an honest assessment of scope, flagging where the project threatens to balloon into a general theory of determinates and determinables or of conceptual change. Keep it to about two pages' worth, in a register suitable for a panel that includes at least one reader who is not a formal epistemologist.
