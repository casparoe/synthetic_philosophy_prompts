[1] The Rietdijk–Putnam argument is usually presented as though it were two arguments sharing a premise. Rietdijk, writing in the mid-1960s, took it to establish determinism; Putnam, a year later, took it to establish that future events are just as real as present ones. My first claim is that these are the same conclusion under two names: to say that an event is already real is to say that its occurrence is already settled, and to say that its occurrence is already settled is to say that the future is not open in any sense that could matter metaphysically. My second claim is that the standard objections, though genuinely damaging to Putnam's original formulation, can be repaired.

[2] The repair I propose is a principle I will call the handoff principle. Rather than asserting, as Putnam does, that whatever exists relative to me-here-now exists, full stop, I relativize the claim at every stage of the argument. When observer O and observer P are momentarily at rest with respect to one another, or more generally when O stands to P in whatever relation of equivalence Putnam's argument requires, O's verdict that the events on O's simultaneity hyperplane are determinate licenses P's verdict that the events on P's hyperplane are determinate. Determinateness is passed along one inertial hop at a time, bucket-brigade style, from observer to observer, until every region of Minkowski spacetime has been reached by someone for whom it is determinate.

[3] This is meant to solve two problems at once. It answers the charge of begging the question, because the handoff principle is never asserted simpliciter: at each step the claim is indexed to a particular observer's state of motion, so the presentist cannot complain that I have smuggled the falsity of presentism into a premise. And it answers the objection, pressed by Maxwell among others, that the argument depends on a transitivity of determinateness that special relativity gives us no reason to accept. I do not need transitivity as a global premise, because each hop is licensed locally by the sender. All I need for my conclusion is the weaker claim that for every event in spacetime there is some observer for whom that event is determinate. From there it is a short step to the claim that every event is determinate, since an event's being determinate does not look like the sort of thing that could vary from observer to observer without generating a contradiction.

[4] Two supporting points. First, Malament's result concerning which simultaneity relations are definable from an observer's worldline vindicates the argument rather than undermining it: if the standard frame-relative relation is the unique non-trivial candidate, then the presentist has nowhere to retreat, because no alternative relation of co-presence is available that is both non-trivial and definable from the physical structure of the spacetime. Second, quantum mechanics is no help to my opponent. Even if the fundamental laws are indeterministic, the block contains one specific distribution of outcomes, and an event's having a settled place in that distribution is all that determinism, in the sense relevant to the openness of the future, could require.

[5] Finally, general relativity does not wreck the argument either, and may even help me. Certain relativistic spacetimes admit no global foliation into spacelike hypersurfaces, a fact often advertised as a further difficulty for presentism; but the eternalist needs no preferred foliation, so the loss of one bears asymmetrically on my opponent and not on me. I also want to argue that the variant of the argument built around an accelerated traveler is strictly stronger than Putnam's original, because acceleration supplies an observer-dependent but non-arbitrary way of singling out a simultaneity relation, and I intend to make that variant the one I defend.
