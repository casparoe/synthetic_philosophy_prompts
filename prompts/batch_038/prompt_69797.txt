You are to act as a philosopher who works at the interface between ethics and health economics, equally at home in the contemporary analytic literature on whether death harms the person who dies and in the technical apparatus of cost-effectiveness appraisal. Please write in American English. Think the problem through carefully before you commit to anything: I would like you to begin with a short brainstorm in which you lay out the candidate correspondences between the two vocabularies, including the ones you suspect will fail, and only then produce the finished translation and the report on it.

My own background: I am a health economist, and for roughly a decade I have done cost-effectiveness appraisal for a national health technology assessment body. My working vocabulary is QALYs and DALYs, years of life lost, health-state utilities elicited by time trade-off or standard gamble, discount rates, willingness-to-pay thresholds, cost-per-QALY league tables, counterfactual baselines, expected values, and the value of a statistical life year. I have no graduate philosophy training, only an undergraduate ethics course and some recent self-directed reading, prompted by a long and unsatisfying argument with a philosopher colleague at a workshop. What I could not shake afterward was the sense that my field has already, silently and by construction, taken a side in that debate: the burden-of-disease enterprise books the badness of a death as years of life lost, which looks to me like nothing other than the deprivation account, denominated in person-time. I want the argument translated into my apparatus and then I want to be told what does not survive the trip.

The material to translate is this. First, Epicurus's case in the letter to Menoeceus that death is nothing to us, on the ground that good and evil consist in sensation while death is the privation of sensation, together with his corollaries that the wise person therefore has nothing to fear from death and that a life which has reached the limit of pleasure gains nothing whatever from further duration. Second, the deprivationist reply, in particular Nagel's insistence that death is an evil even though there is no time at which the deceased is thereby made worse off than she otherwise would have been, and the timing problem that insistence generates. Third, the Lucretian symmetry argument, which asks why we mourn the years our death removes from us but not the equally vast stretch of time preceding our birth. Restate each of these as faithfully as you can in my terms: utilities, person-time, discounting, counterfactual baselines, thresholds, expected value.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want, working through it move by move and saying for each claim what translates cleanly, what translates only at the price of distortion, and what has no expression at all in the apparatus of cost-effectiveness analysis. There are four pressure points I would like you to press on specifically. Years of life lost is the one place where my framework books a loss against an interval in which there is no subject to be in any state at all; does that make it the deprivation account in operational form, or does it redefine harm in a way Epicurus would refuse outright? Epicurus anchors good and evil in sensation, whereas my utilities are preference-based and are routinely elicited from respondents who have never occupied the health state being valued; does his experience requirement survive that substitution, or is it quietly replaced by something with different implications? His claim that infinite duration adds nothing to a life that has already attained its limit superficially resembles discounting, and I want to know whether that resemblance holds up or is a false friend, given that discounting encodes impatience rather than saturation. And on symmetry: my accounting convention starts the person-time denominator at birth, which appears to reduce the asymmetry between prenatal and posthumous nonexistence to a bookkeeping artifact — is that the correct diagnosis, or does the artifact point at something real?

Keep the translation honest throughout. Do not smuggle my framework's presuppositions into the rendering and then report them as findings about Epicurus; in particular, do not treat the fact that something is computable in my vocabulary as evidence that the philosophers were confused, and do not treat the fact that they draw a distinction I lack as automatic evidence that I am blind to something important. Close with a verdict on the residue item by item: for each failure of translation, say whether it exposes a genuine limitation of welfare measurement or whether it shows that the original claim was doing less work than its proponents suppose. If you think my suspicion is right — that the deprivation account is years of life lost with extra metaphysics attached — say so plainly and argue for it; if you think I am wrong, identify precisely where the translation breaks my intuition.
