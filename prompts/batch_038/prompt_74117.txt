Setup: the dollar auction. A dollar goes up in five-cent increments. Highest bidder wins the dollar and pays his bid; second-highest bidder also pays his bid and gets nothing.

(1) Rational choice compares the consequences of the acts available to you right now. What you've already spent is gone on every branch, so it can't count for anything.
(2) Take a bidder who's second-highest at 95 cents, the other guy at $1.00. Folding costs him 95 cents. Bidding $1.05 costs ten cents more and, if the other guy folds, hands him the dollar — net loss of 5 cents. That's 90 cents better than folding.
(3) So bidding is the rational move. And the arithmetic doesn't change as the numbers grow: whenever you're second-highest, five more cents buys you a shot at stopping the bleeding. It's always rational to keep bidding.
(4) What holds in the auction holds for anything with sunk costs, because the sunk part is common to both branches and cancels. Finishing the bad PhD, staying in the bad marriage, funding the project that should have been killed two years ago — the marginal comparison never turns.
(5) So the theory that delivers (3) and (4) tells an agent to escalate forever, everywhere, no matter how deep he already is.
(6) No theory can be right that tells everyone never to quit anything. And the theory can't patch itself: to stop the escalation you either let past costs count, which (1) forbids, or you forbid the sequence while permitting every step in it, which means the theory condemns exactly what it recommends.
(7) Therefore node-by-node consequentialist rationality is self-defeating — and since any decision procedure is either node-by-node or it lets sunk costs back in, there's no coherent notion of a rational decision procedure at all.
