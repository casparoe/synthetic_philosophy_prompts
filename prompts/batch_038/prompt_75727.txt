For thirty-four years I directed a cell-biology laboratory. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than my former colleagues would have thought possible, and I find myself unable to settle a dispute that has now recurred at three successive family dinners. I should like a considered verdict rather than reassurance.

I told my grandchildren, aged nineteen and twenty-two, about the department I joined in 1978: the way the senior men addressed the only woman on the faculty, the remarks that passed for comment in journal club, a colleague's observations about the volunteers in a trial he was running. Their conclusion was immediate — those people were bad people. I knew them. Two were conspicuously generous; one misled a funding body in order to shield a junior postdoc; none would have hurt an animal for amusement. My own formulation is that they were not wicked but wrong, and that we have improved since. Under examination, however, the formulation strains. In my old discipline, when one theory supersedes another the successor explains the predecessor: Newtonian mechanics emerges from relativity as a limiting case, and one can say precisely where the earlier practitioners went astray and why they could not have seen it. I can find no analogue for the attitudes of my colleagues. So far as I can tell, the facts available to them in 1978 were very nearly the facts available to us now, and nothing has come to light that they merely failed to notice.

People tell me that each generation's sympathies simply reach further than the last generation's, and that the reaching is the improvement. But reach does not obviously amount to correctness: a wider net can take in what should have stayed outside it, and I cannot see how the bare fact of enlargement settles whether the enlarged view is the better one.

So I put three questions. Is what my grandchildren call progress progress, or a change of standards in which the present merely enjoys the advantage of sitting in judgment? If it is progress, what does it consist in, given that the analogy with scientific supersession seems to fail? And is my line between being wicked and being wrong a real distinction, or special pleading on behalf of my friends? Tell me which side has the better of the argument, and what I might defensibly say to them at the next dinner.
