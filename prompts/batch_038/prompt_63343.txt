I'm a second-year law student volunteering at a clinic on the side, and I'm also sitting in on a seminar about autonomy in private law mostly because it fit my schedule. Last week we covered how the criminal law handles consent to sex when the complainant was deceived, and I've been chewing on it ever since, partly because my roommate is a philosophy PhD student and he kept insisting the doctrine was "just confused," which felt too easy. Here's the pattern as I understand it. If someone lies about the nature of the act itself — the classic hypothetical being a person posing as a doctor who persuades a patient that intercourse is a medical treatment — or if someone impersonates the complainant's spouse or regular partner, the consent is treated as no consent at all. But a whole pile of other lies, ones that plainly drove the decision, don't touch it: lying about being wealthy, about being single, about your intentions or your feelings, about promising to marry. An appellate court not long ago quashed a conviction where the only deception was a false claim to have had a vasectomy. And yet in other places, deception about a condom has been treated as capable of vitiating consent, and the categories seem to wobble around.

The thing that's bothering me is that the same legal system takes almost the opposite attitude in contract. If you misrepresent something material to get me to sign a lease I completely understand I'm signing, I can get out of it — fraudulent inducement is a real defense, and it doesn't matter that I knew exactly what document was in front of me. So structurally identical cases get opposite treatment: I know what act I'm performing, but I'm wrong about a fact that made me want to perform it, and in one domain that destroys my consent while in the other it doesn't. What theory of consent does the criminal law pattern actually presuppose? Is the idea that consent attaches to an act under a description, and only some descriptions are the operative ones — in which case what principle picks them out, and how does the doctrine avoid the obvious problem that any act can be redescribed indefinitely (sex with a married man, sex with someone who won't call back, sex with someone who finds me less attractive than he said)? Or is it not about consent at all, but about bodily harm and risk, with the consent language functioning as a veneer — in which case the stopping points line up suspiciously well with pregnancy, infection, and physical identity, and I'd want to know whether that's a coincidence or a tell. There's also a third candidate I can't fit anywhere: the condom cases look like they're treating consent as a package of conditions the complainant gets to set, which is a different structure again, and it seems to sit uneasily beside the fertility ruling.

What I'm after is a reconstruction of the implicit theory rather than a tour of the labels, so please don't just restate fraud in the factum versus fraud in the inducement and stop there — I want to know what view of consent, or of what the state is protecting, makes that distinction look principled instead of arbitrary. Then separate out what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and be explicit about where the doctrine is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where two or three quite different theories would license exactly the same outcomes, because my roommate's "it's just confused" charge only bites if there's no such overlap. Tell me whether contract and sex law can be brought into consistency without abandoning one of them, and if so which concession you'd make and what it costs. Finally, get concrete about what would change in the courtroom under two rival views — one on which any deception about anything that figured in the complainant's actual decision invalidates consent, and one on which deception only matters when it produces harm in the relevant sense. I mean things like jury instructions, what has to be proved and in what order, whether the defendant's beliefs about what the complainant cared about become legally relevant, and whether the complainant ends up testifying about her own motives. No citations or case names or reading lists please — I can dig that up myself, and I mostly want the reasoning laid out so I could explain it to someone with no legal training. A couple of the sub-questions above are probably more than you can do justice to, so prioritise whichever ones you think carry the most weight, but say which ones you're dropping and why.
