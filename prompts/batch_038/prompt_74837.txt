I am a senior, and for my independent study in philosophy I have been working through the epistemology of testimony with two classmates and our faculty advisor; we meet on Thursdays and argue about a single assignment each week. This term we have hit a wall on the debate between reductionism and anti-reductionism, and I would like to test my own position against someone else's reasoning rather than continue trading intuitions. Here is the proposition I want a number for, stated as tightly as I can manage: anti-reductionism is correct, meaning that when a competent speaker tells me that p, I am thereby prima facie justified in believing p without having to appeal to positive reasons — evidence gathered from perception, memory, and induction about speakers in general — for thinking that this particular speaker is trustworthy on this particular occasion; the presumption in favor of accepting what one is told is not itself something I must have earned. Reductionism denies this, holding that any such presumption must be underwritten by inductive evidence, and that testimony is a channel for justification the speaker already possesses rather than an independent source of it.

Please give me your actual credence that the proposition above is true, and then show me the arithmetic by which you arrive at it. I am less interested in the headline figure than in the decomposition, so I would like a probability and a one-sentence justification for each of the following sub-questions, plus any others you think belong and that I have overlooked: (a) that the child-language objection is decisive — that a child who required antecedent evidence of reliability before trusting any speaker could never acquire the concepts needed to gather such evidence, so some non-inferential entitlement must be in place from the start; (b) that the gullibility worry is fatal to anti-reductionism, or at least that a standing default of trust is dangerous enough to count meaningfully against it; (c) that the distinction between global and local reductionism means the two camps are frequently answering different questions, so that part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive; (d) that testimony transmits warrant the speaker already has rather than generating fresh warrant in the hearer, and that this bears on who wins; (e) that the correct answer flips depending on whether justification is being assessed in internalist or externalist terms; and (f) that testimony is genuinely a basic source, on the model of perception, rather than a parasitic derivative of perception, memory, and inference. Then tell me how you combine these terms: they are plainly not independent, and I suspect several are strongly correlated, so please explain how you are handling that rather than multiplying them naively, and give the final number.

Three further requests. First, identify which single sub-question your total is most sensitive to — that is, which one would move the headline figure most if revised by, say, twenty points — and say what evidence or argument would move that one. Second, my own credence in anti-reductionism is about 0.3, while the classmate I argue with most is at roughly 0.75; given the decomposition you produce, tell me which sub-question the two of us are most likely disagreeing about, and suggest something we could work through in a single meeting, such as a test case or thought experiment, that would separate our positions. Third, say whether your number would change if the proposition were restricted to a single uncorroborated claim made by a stranger, as opposed to a well-documented historical record relayed through many speakers over time, and, if so, what that asymmetry implies about which side of the debate the restricted cases actually favor. You do not need to cite anything or hand me a reading list; what I want is the structure of your reasoning exposed, with its weak points marked.
