I am in the third year of a philosophy doctorate, working on decision theory and its bearing on long-horizon policy; my first degree was in physics. Over the past year I have become aware of a structural pattern that recurs across a family of debates, and I have been unable to locate a term for it. I should like to describe it as concretely as I can, and then ask whether it corresponds to a recognised object with a literature of its own, or whether it needs naming.

The debates in question all concern a quantity defined by an infinite sum that fails to behave. My paradigm is a gamble of the kind discussed under the heading of the Pasadena paradox: a coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the payoff on the toss that ends the game grows without bound while alternating in sign. The terms of the expected-value sum then form an alternating series that converges only conditionally, so the sum has no value except relative to an ordering of its terms — and the ordering is an artefact of how the description of the gamble happened to be written down. By the usual rearrangement result, a different ordering can be made to yield any value one likes. The same shape turns up elsewhere. In my earlier life I met it in quantum field theory, where a divergent integral may be handled by insisting that the divergent quantity is not an observable, or by imposing a momentum cutoff whose scale has to be chosen, or by substituting a summation convention that returns a finite answer, with the standing worry that a different convention would return a different one.

Now the pattern. Confronted with such a case, a theorist appears to have three moves. She may declare that the quantity simply does not exist here, and hold that this is a discovery about the domain rather than a hole in the theory. She may restrict the domain — impose a finite horizon, bound the utility function, require probabilities to decay quickly enough — so that the sum always converges. Or she may replace the aggregation rule with something more permissive that delivers a verdict anyway.

What I keep observing is that each of these moves attracts an objection that is available before anyone has examined the particular repair on offer, and that the objection is selected by the form of the repair rather than by its content. Declare the quantity nonexistent and you are told that you have purchased consistency by withdrawing precisely those claims that were exposed. Restrict the domain and you are told that the boundary is arbitrary: the difference between probabilities decaying at one rate and at a marginally faster one manifests itself only in the limit, so any cutoff drawn between them looks like convenience dressed as principle. Replace the rule and you are told that the verdict in the difficult cases is an artefact of the replacement, since a differently engineered permissive rule would return a differently signed answer.

Two features of this seem to me worth separating out, and I suspect they are what any adequate name would have to capture. First, the objection is generated from a taxonomy rather than from engagement: enumerate the possible repairs, attach one standing complaint to each cell, and the position is disposed of without anyone needing to look closely at the repair actually proposed. Second, the step from the enumeration to the disposal looks to me invalid, and yet it is taken constantly and without remark. That every cell of a partition attracts a familiar complaint does not by itself establish that the partitioned position is untenable; the ready availability of the complaints may be an artefact of the way the options have been carved up in the first place.

So my questions are these. (1) Is there an established term, or a body of work, for the pattern in which the space of repairs to a theory is exhaustively partitioned in advance and each cell is assigned a standing objection whose applicability is fixed by the form of the repair? (2) What are the nearest recognised concepts? My own tentative candidates are trilemma-shaped sceptical arguments, ad hoc rescue, immunising stratagems, self-sealing theories, degenerating research programmes, and the various standing complaints about arbitrary cutoffs — but for each of these I want to know precisely where my pattern comes apart from it, since none of them seems to capture the pre-partitioning. (3) If nothing fits, please coin a term, define it, and above all mark its edges: describe cases that superficially resemble the pattern but are not instances of it, including the case in which an impossibility theorem genuinely demonstrates that the repairs exhaust the options, so that the objection is earned by proof rather than supplied by template. (4) Finally, tell me candidly whether the pattern is real or whether I have merely described ordinary critical discussion at a level of abstraction that makes it look sinister. A bare label would not serve my purposes; what I need is the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where it stops.
