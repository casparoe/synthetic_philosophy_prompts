ok so I'm in a migration ethics seminar this semester and we have to hand in a one-page proposal before the actual paper (7000 words), and my idea is still pretty mushy so I could use some help tightening it up.

everyone in class keeps arguing about which country should take refugees — proximity, capacity, colonial history, who caused the displacement. But it struck me that basically all of those debates assume the person has already managed to get somewhere. Asylum as an institution is built on showing up: you have to reach the territory or the border and claim in person. Which means the whole system ends up sorted by who can travel — young, healthy, cash for smugglers, no dependants — and the people who most obviously need protection (stuck in camps, disabled, elderly, single parents, anyone whose own government won't issue a passport) are exactly the ones the arrival-based route can't reach. Resettlement is supposed to plug that gap but it's tiny, discretionary, and looks more like a gift than a claim.

So my question is roughly: is the presence requirement just an administrative convenience, or is it built into what asylum *is*? And could there be a genuine right to be brought — an obligation on some state to move you — rather than only an obligation not to shove you back once you're there? And if there could, then the allocation problem comes back in a new form: which state owes the fetching, and does the fact that you can't reach anyone make every capable state a candidate?

- the question stated precisely — I'm pretty sure there's two or three seperate questions hiding in there and I need them pulled apart
