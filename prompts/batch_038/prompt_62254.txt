My friend and I burned most of a Saturday night on Adam Smith's impartial spectator and I said something I now have to either defend or eat. He's a lawyer, not a philosopher, and his position was basically that the impartial spectator isn't doing any work: either it's just "what would a reasonable, well-informed bystander think," which is a legal reasonableness standard wearing a moral costume, or it's a polite way of asking you to report your own sentiments and then attributing them to a fictional third party. He offered to bet me a case of wine that if you wrote it down as an actual procedure, it'd collapse into one of those two things inside of ten uses. I told him it wouldn't, becuase the spectator in Smith isn't an average informed onlooker, because the imagination runs in one specific direction — you put yourself into the other person's situation rather than asking them to adopt yours — and because Smith keeps apart two things my friend was running together: approving of the feeling somebody has, and approving of what got done about it. He wasn't buying it and I'm not sure I fully was either.

Here's why it isn't just a bar bet. I chair an internal grievance panel for a mid-size city government, roughly two cases a month, three of us on the panel: me, an HR person, and a union rep. Nobody but me has any philosophy, and I have a bachelor's worth, which is to say none. We get 90 minutes with the file and the parties, then we write a recommendation that goes to the department head and that both sides get to read. We don't decide discipline, we just say whether the conduct was unfair and whether the response to it was proportionate, and that language ends up quoted back at people for years.

So: write me the procedure. I want the questions in the order we'd actually ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each to move on, where the procedure terminates in "no, we can't find unfairness here," and where it stops and hands the thing to a human because what's left is a disagreement about values and not about facts. Tell me which of Smith's distinctions have to survive the translation into language an HR person can use — and which ones can be thrown out, because I suspect that's where my friend's got me. Then handle the two places I think it breaks: cases where the affected person's situation is one nobody on the panel can genuinely imagine their way into, and the second-order problem that when you ask a panelist to consult the impartial spectator they might just be handing you their own gut feeling with a nicer label on it — how would the procedure catch that.

Run it on two real ones. One: a sanitation supervisor chewed out a crew member in front of the whole crew over a missed pickup that turned out to be caused by the supervisor's own scheduling error; he apologized the next day and tore up the written warning, and the employee is still furious six weeks later and says the humiliation was the harm, not the warning. Two: a permitting clerk reported a coworker's falsified timecards straight to HR instead of to her supervisor first, which is against policy, and since then the entire team has gone quiet around her — no shouting, no exclusion from meetings, nothing anyone did that you could point at and sanction — and she says she's miserable and wants a finding of unfair treatment.

And then give it to me straight on the bet. Does what you wrote reduce to a reasonable-person test, or to the three of us reporting our own feelings, or neither? If it partly does, say where, and flag every step that's really a judgment call dressed up as procedure, because that's the part I'd get caught out on in front of him.
