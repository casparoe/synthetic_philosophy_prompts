I administer a forecasting panel of about forty people, and this year I have been asked to add a new stage to our quarterly calibration audit — what I have been calling a prior-consistency check. The design is borrowed from Aumann's agreement theorem, which I take to say that two fully rational agents who share a common prior cannot end up knowingly disagreeing: once their posterior probabilities for some proposition have become common knowledge between them, those probabilities must be identical. On that reading, persistent disagreement is diagnostic. It tells you that either the agents are not reasoning properly, or their priors were never common to begin with, or their priors have somehow drifted apart since the outset.

My protocol runs as follows. Before each quarter's question set is released, every forecaster is asked to report, for each of ten propositions, the probability they would assign to it having received no evidence whatsoever bearing on it: no news, no figures from our own archive, no background briefings — only the bare description of the event as stated in the question. I collect these reports and compare them across the panel. Pairs whose ten numbers match to two decimal places are recorded as prior-synced for that quarter, and for those pairs I expect — as a matter of what the theorem guarantees, not merely as a prediction — that their end-of-quarter reports will agree once they have exchanged their reasoning in writing and sat through a facilitated session. Pairs whose pre-quarter reports diverge are flagged as having non-common priors, and any lasting disagreement between them is treated as explained rather than as a failure, since the theorem's premise does not hold for them.

Two cases from last quarter illustrate what I am trying to interpret. One flagged pair gave pre-quarter reports of 0.15 and 0.85 on the same proposition, about as far apart as the panel produced; after the exchange and the session they reported 0.58 and 0.61. My log records this as unexplained convergence between agents who, by hypothesis, share no prior. A second pair matched on all ten pre-quarter reports; after the same exchange and the same session they finished the quarter at 0.42 and 0.61 on a proposition neither had previously seen, and I have been asked to write up why a theorem I thought applied to them did not deliver agreement.

I would like a careful diagnosis rather than encouragement. Specifically: does the exercise I am running actually elicit anything that answers to the notion of a common prior, or is there a category mistake in treating a list of unconditional numbers for ten isolated propositions as a prior? Does the agreement theorem really license the expectation I have built into the audit, and if not, which condition am I implicitly assuming has been met when it has not? And when I flag a pair as having divergent priors, does anything follow from that flag about whether some common prior could nonetheless have generated both of their belief patterns? Finally, what should the audit measure instead, if the answer is that it should measure something else at all? I should say that I am not looking for pointers to the literature on this; I want the conceptual assessment, and I would rather it be blunt than diplomatic.

One further request about the confidence of your reply. Please state what would change your mind about the diagnosis you offer: what consideration about how forecasters actually arrive at their numbers, or what alternative reading of the theorem or of the phrase "common prior," would lead you to endorse something closer to my protocol instead. And if your diagnosis itself rests on a step you regard as shaky — a condition of the theorem you are glossing, or an assumption about what an elicited report is evidence of — identify that step explicitly rather than letting it stand as though it were secure.
