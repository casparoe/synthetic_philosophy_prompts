My flatmate does theoretical physics and he cornered me in the kitchen last night with this one, and I genuinely couldnt find a way out, though I'm sure there is one. He went:

(1) In the Szilard engine, one molecule in a box plus one bit of information about which half it's in lets you draw kT ln 2 of work out of a heat bath at temperature T.

(2) Landauer's principle says the unavoidable cost of the whole cycle is the erasure of that bit afterwards, which dumps at least kT ln 2 of heat back into the bath, so it cancels exactly.

(3) So the second law only survives because we insist on counting the demon's memory as part of the system. Look at the gas and the piston on their own and you have a net kT ln 2 of work taken from a single bath, which is exactly what Kelvin's statement forbids.

(4) But erasure is only forced on you if the memory is finite. A demon who never wipes anything and just writes each result into the next blank cell of an ever longer tape pays no erasure cost at all, ever.

(5) Nothing in physics puts a cap on how long a tape you can have.

(6) So that demon can run the engine cycle after cycle, each time turning kT ln 2 of heat from one bath into work, with no compensating cost anywhere in the universe.

(7) Which is a perpetual motion machine of the second kind, i.e. precisely the thing the second law says can't exist.

(8) Therefore the second law isn't a law about gases and pistons at all — it's a theorem about how much bookkeeping a finite agent can afford, which he reckons is why he's perfectly happy calling it not really physics.

I want to keep (1) and (2), they're in every textbook and I don't fancy picking a fight with Landauer, and I'm sure (8) is either false or a non sequitur. So the break has to be somewhere in the middle and I can't see it. Which step do you actually reject, why does it look so innocent on a first read, and what does rejecting it cost?
