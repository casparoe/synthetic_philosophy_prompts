I went to a free lecture at the library on tuesday about why ancient societies fall apart, and two people in the audience started arguing during the Q&A and then kept going in the lobby for like twenty minutes while I stood there holding someones coat. I never did figure out who was right. Here's roughly how it went:

1. Nadia said the whole framing of the talk was off. People say the Maya collapsed, but millions of Maya are alive right now and still speak Mayan languages. What actually ended was one particular political arrangement in the southern lowlands — the divine kingship, the courts that commissioned the carved monuments, the tribute system that fed them. Calling that a collapse of "the Maya" makes it sound like a people disappeared.

2. Owen said fine, but that defines away the only interesting question. Cities were emptied, populations dropped hard, long-distance trade networks broke up. If the disintegration of the political and economic order that organized and fed hundreds of thousands of people isn't a collapse, there's nothing left for the word to apply to.

3. Nadia said the word is fine, it just needs a subject — say what collapsed. Same people, same languages, plenty of the same villages, but the palace economy gone and a big chunk of the population relocating north. That's reorganization with a body count, not collapse.

4. Owen said then they don't actually disagree about any facts and are just arguing over a label, which makes her objection verbal rather then substantive. And if it's verbal she can't accuse people who say "collapse" of being wrong, she's just marketing a synonym.

5. Nadia said it isn't verbal, because each label smuggles in a story. Collapse implies a society that failed and ran out of options, that died of its own mistakes. Reorganization implies people making decisions under pressure. Those are different claims about who had agency, and the second one fits the archaeology better.

6. Owen said she'd just changed the subject. Whether they had options is an empirical question — the drought evidence, the settlement surveys, whether people were leaving before conditions got bad or after. If the evidence showed starvation and violence and no exit, she'd call it a collapse herself. So the word is tracking the evidence, and her point 5 was really a claim about evidence, which he's happy to argue, but not a claim about vocabulary.

7. Nadia said not quite, because you still have to fix a metric. Even with perfect evidence you have to decide whether you're measuring population, political complexity, or wellbeing, and those come apart. Farmers who stop paying tribute to a court might eat better than they did under it. Same events, an improvement on one metric and a collapse on another. No amount of evidence picks the metric for you.

8. Owen said then collapse is metric-relative, which is a perfectly normal thing for a technical term to be. Subscript it — use it in Tainter's sense, a rapid loss of sociopolitical structure over a few decades — and every case gets a determinate answer. You don't drop the word, you index it.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right at each step? I mean actually rate them, not just summarize — for each of the eight, say whether it lands, whether it's a fair response to the move before it, and what's weak about it. Then: is there a real disagreement still standing at the end, or did one of them talk the other out of the fight without either of them noticing? And what should one of them have said that neither did?
