I am in the second year of a PhD in economics, working on the normative foundations of regulatory impact assessment: specifically, on whether the way agencies trade small dispersed harms against concentrated severe ones can be justified in terms other than the summation of welfare. My training is in social choice and welfare economics, so I know contractualism chiefly through the summaries that circulate in policy writing, and I am aware that I may be importing errors I am not positioned to detect. I should be grateful for an audit of one such summary.

It comes from the framing section of a recent survey article on distributional weighting in applied welfare analysis. The author is an economist, not a philosopher, and the passage reads:

'Scanlon's contractualism is best read as a reaction against the summation of utilities. An act is wrong when it would be ruled out by principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject, and since reasons belong to individuals rather than to groups, the strength of a principle's case is measured against the strongest complaint it leaves any one person with, never against a total. Because a complaint is simply what is at stake for the person who has it, the test is applied person by person, and the number of people affected is screened off at the first stage and cannot re-enter at any later one. It follows that contractualism cannot weigh anything: a principle requiring us to prevent one death cannot reasonably be rejected in favour of a principle that would spare a million people a night of lost sleep each, a verdict no other moral theory delivers, and contractualists have found no non-ad-hoc way of avoiding it. The tie-breaker they introduce — numbers may decide cases where the complaints are equally strong — concedes nothing of substance, since regulatory choices almost never involve exactly equal burdens. The upshot is that contractualism gives the analyst no resources at all for the central question of regulation: how much concentrated severe harm may be traded against how much dispersed trivial harm.'

My own inclination, such as it is, runs against two of these sentences: I suspect that letting the greater number decide when complaints are equally strong is doing considerably more work than the passage allows, and I suspect that the many-nights-of-lost-sleep case is one where Scanlon thinks his framework reaches a determinate answer he welcomes rather than one he is forced to swallow. But I cannot tell how much of that inclination I have acquired from other summaries of the same kind.

Two cases from my own work may help to fix the target. In the first, a solvent widely used in household cleaning products imposes, on continued use, roughly one additional fatal cancer case per year across forty million households — statistically distributed, never identifiable — and a ban would cost each household about nine pounds a year. In the second, a fixed health budget can either save one identified child's life or spare four million people an illness lasting three days each. The survey article treats these as instances of a single problem on which contractualism is supposedly mute. I am not confident that they are instances of the same problem at all.

Could you therefore do the following? Work through the passage sentence by sentence, or claim by claim where a sentence does too much, and sort each into what a careful contractualist would accept as it stands, what is a tolerable simplification for a non-specialist but would be corrected in the philosophical literature, and what is simply wrong. I would particularly like your judgement on three points: whether the account of complaints as what is at stake for the person who has them is faithful to the role the notion plays, and what turns on it for someone hoping to build an applied framework out of willingness-to-pay data; whether the claim that the number of people affected is screened off at the first stage and cannot re-enter later confuses two claims the view keeps apart; and, on the two cases above, whether the article's diagnosis is even applicable to both of them. Finally, tell me whether the concluding claim about concentrated severe harm and dispersed trivial harm survives correction of the passage — and if it is partly right, whether it is right for the reasons the article gives.

I would rather have your own verdict on each of these than a survey of how the sentences might be read; but where defenders themselves disagree about how much the framework can be made to accommodate, or where you are simply unsure, please mark that explicitly rather than smoothing it into a consensus that does not exist. And if you think the exercise is misframed — if the passage is in fact a fair summary and it is my suspicion of it that needs correcting, or if asking whether contractualism can underwrite cost–benefit analysis rests on a category mistake that makes the comparison idle from the start — please say so plainly and explain why, instead of working politely through my questions in the order I have set them.
