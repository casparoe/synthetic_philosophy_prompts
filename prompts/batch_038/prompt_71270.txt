I'm writing a novel with a lot of argument in it (sorry) and I want to rehearse a scene before I draft it. Play G. E. Moore — the real one, not a strawman: patient, a bit exasperated, certain that he knows his own premises, happy to grant the skeptic his argument is valid while denying it's probative, and unwilling to give up on knowing that he has two hands no matter what I throw at him. Plain prose, two or three paragraphs a turn, stay in character untill I say cut, and if you need a fact about my scenario, ask for it the way Moore would — curious, not defensive.

The character I'm writing lost both arms above the elbow as a baby and has worn prosthetic hands since, so integrated that she reports her sense of where her hands are is no different from anybody else's. In the scene a young philosopher tells her that when she points at her prosthetic and says she has a hand, she isn't reporting an external fact at all — she's describing a pattern of sensation her nervous system supplies whether or not anything is out there, so her case is just everyone's case, only slower and more honest about itself.

My opening line to you, in his voice: Moore, she doesn't know she has hands, she knows she has hand-experiences, and that's the most any of us ever get. If what she says isn't one of your truisms, why is what you say?
