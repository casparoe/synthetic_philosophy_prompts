ok so this is kind of a weird question but I've been going down a rabbit hole lately. In class we did Berkeley as the "matter doesn't exist, it's only minds and ideas" guy, and that was basically all I knew about him. Then I found out he also wrote this huge list of questions about money and the Irish economy — The Querist, I'm pretty sure that's the title — which is literally just hundreds of questions in a row with no answers given. And apparently he wanted Ireland to set up a national bank that the public owned.

Two of the questions stuck with me. One goes: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" And another goes: "Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating?"

So his thing seems to be that money isn't a valuable substance at all, it's a counter whose whole job is to get people working and trading with each other, and if it's sitting still it isn't doing anything. Which is a pretty strange view for 1735.

Anyway — if Berkeley were around now, what would he actually think of Bitcoin? I want more than "he'd probably have mixed feelings." Specifically:

- Would he even count it as money? On one hand it's exactly what he said money should be, a pure ledger entry with zero metal behind it and no intrinsic anything. On the other hand everyone treats it as a thing you hold and never spend, so it fails the only test he gives it. Is something used as a store of value that never circulates a *bad* money, or is it not money at all on his definition? Does he leave any room for a difference between saving and hoarding?
- The hard cap of 21 million. Would he think a fixed supply is a good thing because nobody can debase it, or would he hate it, since the point of a public bank for him seemed to be that the issuer can add or remove tokens to match how much industry there actually is? What would he make of the argument that the cap is the feature, not the bug?
- The whole "digital gold" branding. I feel like he'd say calling money gold-like is definately the exact confusion he was arguing against, but maybe I'm reading too much into that.

And here's the part I most want your take on. My econ teacher once mentioned a town in Austria in the 1930s that printed its own paper money which lost value unless you bought a stamp for it every month, so people spent it fast and the town got a bunch of work done. Now suppose a government today issued a national digital currency that worked like that — programmed to decay if you sit on it, with the issuer able to create more whenever there's idle labor to put to work. Would Berkeley be for it? Is he basically the great-great-grandfather of that idea, or would he draw a line and object, since he also seems to think arbitrarily messing with the denomination of coin is a cheat on the public?

Last one and idk if it's a dumb connection: does his money theory fall out of his immaterialism, or his stuff about words being signs? Money and language both seem to be, for him, signs whose whole being is in what they get people to do rather than what they're made of. Or am I just pattern-matching, and his monetary views are independent of his metaphysics?

But do tell me where you're least sure of yourself and what would change your mind — like which argument or consideration would flip you from "he'd reject Bitcoin" to "he'd embrace it," or from "he'd love the decaying currency" to "he'd think it's tyranny."
