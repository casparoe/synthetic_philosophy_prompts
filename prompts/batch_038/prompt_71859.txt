I'm a phil of biology PhD student working through the units of selection debate and a friend who's an oncologist described a clinical practice to me that I can't get to fit any of the standard boxes — genes vs organisms vs groups — so I want to know what it actually presupposes.

The practice: some groups treating advanced prostate cancer dose adaptively instead of continuously. Rather than hitting the tumour at maximum tolerated dose until it's shrunk as far as it will go, they back off once it's under control and deliberately leave a large population of drug-sensitive cancer cells alive, resuming treatment when the tumour starts growing again. The stated rationale is that resistance carries a fitness cost, so in a lightly treated tumour the sensitive cells outcompete the resistant ones and hold them down; if you eradicate the sensitive population you remove the competition and the resistant clone takes over faster. Early results reportedly gave longer time to progression than continuous dosing.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory here. What notion of fitness, and what level as the unit, does this require? It seems to want two different fitness concepts at once — an intrinsic cost of resistance that you can measure on a cell line in a dish, and a frequency-dependent suppression that only exists across the whole tumour population. Is that coherent, or is one of them doing all the work while the other is decoration? And does it quietly need the tumour to be a Darwinian individual, with heritable variation among its parts, when a lot of the resistance being managed is reversible, non-genetic drug tolerance in persister cells? Tell me what would change in the practice, or in how clinicians talk about selecting for resistance, if you took that plasticity picture seriously instead. Be careful to separate what it strictly presupposes from what it's merely consistent with — I suspect several rival accounts license it — and say where it's philosophically underdetermined.
