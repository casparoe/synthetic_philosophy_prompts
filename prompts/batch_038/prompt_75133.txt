I started volunteering at a small food pantry a couple months back after getting laid off, mostly stocking and intake, and somehow I've ended up being the person who has to write up the minutes *and* a reccomendation for the board. Which is why I'm asking about a fight that broke out last Thursday. It's about whether to keep the client-choice setup (people walk through with a cart and pick whatever they want, like a grocery store) or go back to pre-packed boxes where we decide the contents in advance. Two board members went at it for like forty minutes. Both are smart, neither is a cartoon villain, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Here's the exchange as best I remember it:

(1) Ray said the cart model is obviously right, because you can't go around asking people what's good for them — you watch what they take. His line was: whatever she puts in the cart is, by definition, what she prefers, so the cart data settles the question, and any survey asking families what they need just gets people telling us what they think we want to hear.

(2) Nadia said that's circular. If "prefers" just means "chose," then "the cart model gives people what they prefer" is true by definition and does no work at all, and Ray has left himself no way to say that any arrangement is good or bad for anybody. She said he isn't defending the cart model, he's refusing to ask the question.

(3) Ray said it isn't circular, it's testable: you can catch people being inconsistent, e.g. someone who takes the rice when rice and beans are both on offer and then takes the beans when the same two are offered the other way round. He said his view forbids that pattern, so it has content and can be wrong about someone.

(4) Nadia said okay, but that's a claim about consistency, not about welfare, and she brought up a real case: a woman — call her D — who comes in monthly and takes the processed stuff, boxed mac, canned meat, and who separately told the intake volunteer twice that she wishes she could feed her kids vegetables, and who enrolled in a produce-delivery pilot last spring and then never once used it. She said: now you have D's choices and D's words and they contradict each other, and your framework has no way to weigh them except automatically siding with the choice. And a framework that wins every argument about who someone really is, in advance, isn't a framework.

(5) Ray said the words are cheap talk and the choices are what she actually did, and then he added something that got applause from two other people: if you're going to override her, at least be honest that that's what you're doing — you're claiming you know better than she does what's good for her. He asked whether a room full of volunteers with clipboards really does know D's life better than D does.

(6) Nadia's reply here had two parts and this is where it got confusing for me. First: nobody in this room gets to choose leaving D alone — a pantry is either the box model or the cart model, and the box model is an imposition too, so the real question isn't intervene vs. don't intervene, it's whose values get built into the room. Second: she said D at intake in January, calm and with a plan, is not really the same agent as D on the third Friday at 5pm with a migraine and the kids screaming, and that if you take January-D's side you aren't being paternalistic toward her, you're taking a side in a fight between her selves. But then she conceded that Ray could answer that this just relocates the problem, since you still need a rule for which self's choice counts, and you obviously didn't get that rule from choice itself.

(7) Ray's last move was that step 6 proves his point: Nadia is now openly judging preferences from the outside, and he isn't. He said the only thing that could ever license trusting a food bank's judgment over a family's own is some account of who knows better, and he'd like to hear hers stated rather than smuggled in as "well, the box has vegetables in it."

Nothing got conceded and now the recommendation is mine to draft. So: can you go through this move by move and tell me who's actually right at each of the seven steps — not just who wins overall? For each step I'd like a call on whether the move lands, whether it overreaches, and if it overreaches whether there's a nearby version of it that would work. Then separately: is there something both of them are missing that would actually decide the box-vs-cart question, or does all of this philosophy leave the policy question untouched and it's really just a stalemate about the word "prefer"? And if you think one of them is more right overall, please say so and say why — I'd much rather get a verdict than a diplomatic both-sides summary, since I have to actually recommend something.
