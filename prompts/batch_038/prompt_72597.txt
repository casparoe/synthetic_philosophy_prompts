Retired four years ago from a national lab — most of my career was on phase transitions, neutron scattering, that sort of thing. Since then I've been reading philosophy on my own and I've finally written down where I actually stand on grounding and fundamentality, so the reading group I've joined can attack it. Before I hand it round I'd like a blunt second opinion. Here's the list.

1. The complete physical story — particles or fields or whatever they turn out to be, plus the laws — is the fundamental layer, and anything else that's real is real in virtue of that layer. No extra furniture. My mental picture of it is basically derivational: write down the full fundamental state and the laws, and everything else follows from it the way the rest of a calculation follows from its first line.

2. Grounding is a strict ordering. Irreflexive, asymetric, transitive, and well-founded: every chain of grounds terminates at the fundamental. No loops, no bottomless pits.

3. But I also insist grounding is not mere necessary connection. Two claims that hold together in every possible world can still have different grounds — the old one about Socrates and the set containing him, or on my home turf, the gas having a temperature versus the molecules having the mean kinetic energy they have. The second grounds the first and not the reverse, and that's not a verbal quibble. If someone tells me grounding just is necessitation, I take them to be eliminating the thing rather than analyzing it.

4. Multiple realization is fine by me. One and the same higher-level fact can be grounded by very different physical configurations. Grounding is many-to-one and I don't see a problem with that.

5. Related, though: I won't accept a grounding claim that just gestures at "the physical facts, whatever they turn out to be." A grounding claim has to be able to name the actual facts doing the work. Vague grounds aren't grounds.

6. Emergence: some regularities genuinely only show up at scale and are, for all practical purposes, unpredictable from below. That was my working life in one sentence. But I don't think any of this adds ingredients to the world. Nothing spooky.

7. Structure does real explanatory work, and I'm not willing to treat that as loose talk. The symmetry a system has tells you things about its behaviour that you don't get by cataloguing the parts. I don't want to say mathematical structures are a second fundamental realm sitting alongside physics — but I also don't want to say the symmetry facts are grounded in the arrangement of the parts, because in practice it runs the other way: the structure constrains which arrangements there are at all.

8. One more I want to keep: I don't claim to know what the fundamental layer is. We've been wrong before — caloric, the ether, phlogiston — so I'm not asserting that physics as currently written down is metaphysically correct. I'm saying whatever turns out to be fundamental, everything else hangs off it. Someone in the group said that makes item 1 empty, and it bothers me.

9. And well-foundedness seems to demand a first moment of the universe, which I don't believe in. If time runs back forever with every state having a prior physical cause, that ought to be compatible with everything above.

Sort this out for me. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and how badly — rank them by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, because I've read enough philosophy to know that everything gets called a tension. Which ones only look bad and dissolve once you're careful about what's actually being claimed? I'd like the dissolutions explained properly, not just waved at, because I want to know why I was wrong to worry. For each real problem: the cheapest repair, and what I lose by making it — I want the price, not just the fix. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
