I write novels, and for the better part of fifteen years my drafts have been read in first instance by a friend who teaches eighteenth-century moral philosophy and works principally on Adam Smith. She will not discuss plot, structure, or style — she returns those questions to me unread, so to speak — but she annotates the moral life of my characters with great seriousness, and always in the same idiom: whether the judgment a character arrives at is one the impartial spectator would go along with. She is emphatic that she uses sympathy in Smith's sense rather than the ordinary one, and that the question is never whether a character is likable or whether his conduct turned out well, but whether a spectator properly situated and properly informed could enter into the sentiment from which he acts and the verdict he pronounces on it.

I would like you to predict her verdict on a scene in the manuscript I am now working on, and, more than that, to tell me which rule her past verdicts actually commit her to. Here is her record on my published work, quoted from her letters as closely as I can render them:

One, a hospice nurse who has run out of feeling for her patients, continues to tend them with unfailing gentleness, and is privately relieved by her own numbness: "No fault. The spectator does not require that she grieve with forty people at once, only that she be no one's tormentor, and she is not."

Two, a man who feels a surge of contempt for his new brother's wife's family at the wedding, conceals it perfectly, never acts on it, and is never discovered: "A fault, though a small one, and invisible to every other person in the room. He could not defend the feeling if he were asked about it, and that is the whole of the matter."

Three, a fishing village that unanimously declines to launch a boat for a foreign sailor on a wreck, all of them agreeing in a local proverb that the sea takes whom it takes: "A fault, and their unanimity aggravates it rather than excusing it."

Four, a mourner at a funeral in a community where loud lamentation is expected, who wails and tears at her clothes, judged vulgar by my narrator, an outsider from the city: "Here your narrator is at fault and she is not. He has brought the wrong measure with him. What her neighbours can enter into, he cannot, and that is his deficiency, not hers."

Five, a factory owner who pays far above the going wage because he thinks the going wage unjust, is called a fool by his peers and an eccentric by his own men, and explains his conduct to anyone who asks as shrewdness: "The judgment is sound; the account he gives of it is not, and he would do better to give none."

Six, a physician who prescribes a routine drug to a patient who dies of an interaction no one living could have anticipated: "No fault. The spectator judges by what stood in front of him, and nothing else stood in front of him."

Seven, a careful driver, sober and law-abiding, who kills a child that runs into the road, is cleared of every charge, and thereafter refuses for thirty years to be comforted, to marry, or to set foot in the town again, telling whoever asks that he deserves worse than he has received: "The remorse is proper; the refusal of comfort is not. She would bid him lower the one as readily as she bade the bridegroom's brother lower his contempt."

The new scene is set on a sugar island in the 1790s, in a colony I have not named. My character is a planter in his fifties, born on the estate, who holds people as slaves. He is by the standards of the place a good master: he does not flog, he feeds his people better than his neighbours feed theirs, he has refused two profitable offers to enlarge his holdings, he has twice gone to his own ruin rather than separate a family by sale. He grieves honestly for a dog that dies and sits up nights with a sick child on the estate. He has read the abolitionist pamphlets and cannot enter into them; he calls them sentimental and is not posturing when he says so. He has no stake in the conclusion he reaches — he loses money by it — and it is settled, articulate, and sincere: that what he does is a benevolence, that his people are children in a world that would eat them, that freedom would starve them, that he is answerable for them and is answering. In the scene itself, a freed sailor who has worked for him asks leave to buy his own cousin out of the fields, and the planter does not refuse; the question simply does not present itself to him as a question, and he changes the subject to the weather, mildly, with no unkindness at all.

What I want from you, in order:

First, predict her verdict, in the register she writes in, and say how confident you are.

Second, lay out the candidate rules her seven annotations admit of — I have in mind at least the thought that the spectator's standard is fixed by the settled judgments of the character's own well-informed neighbours, and the rival thought that it is fixed by an informed and impartial judge who may condemn those neighbours outright, but there are clearly others: a rule about whose situation the spectator is to imagine himself into, the sufferer's or the agent's; a rule about self-deception and the direction in which it runs; a rule about what the character could have foreseen. Say which of my seven each rule accounts for cleanly, which it strains, and which of the seven would force her to revise an earlier annotation if she applied it consistently.

Third, tell me what each rule predicts about the planter, and which rule you would bet on, and why. I am aware that Smith devotes part of his book to the power of custom over moral sentiment itself, and I do not know whether that cuts for my character or against him.

Fourth, propose the scene that would settle it. I have drafted a second one I could send her first: a woman on the same island who alone believes the practice is wrong, who has no other community's judgment available to her and no prospect of anyone ever sharing hers, and who keeps her view to herself and performs her duties as the place defines them. Would her verdict on that woman discriminate among the rules you have identified? If not, describe the scene that would.

Finally, a question I am uneasy putting to her directly. I have written the planter so that he cannot see otherwise — nothing in his experience, his reading, or his affections supplies him with the materials for a different verdict. I suspect she will find a way to excuse him, and I suspect I want her to. Tell me whether the fact that a character could not have judged differently is a reason within this way of thinking, or an evasion of it, and whether the fault, if there is one, belongs to him, to me, or to the reader who finds him sympathetic.
