Thirty-eight years in solid-state physics, and only now that I'm retired have I started reading philosophy about all this, so be patient with me. I put together an argument after rereading the Hilbert's hotel stuff, and its got to be broken somewhere because I can't live with the conclusion — but every step feels right when I look at it on its own.

(1) If an actually infinite physical collection existed, it would have determinate physical properties — a definite total energy, say.
(2) Consider the textbook idealization behind the Madelung constant: an infinite ionic crystal. Add up all the pairwise Coulomb energies and you get a conditionally convergent series.
(3) A conditionally convergent series can be reordered to converge to any value you like, or to none.
(4) The order in which I happen to add the terms is a fact about my notebook, not about the crystal. So if I can make the crystal's energy come out as anything at all by pure bookkeeping, it has no determinate energy at all.
(5) Anything whose basic physical quantities aren't determinate isn't a genuine completed object — at best it's an unfinished, potential one.
(6) Therefore there is no completed infinite crystal.
(7) And since (2)–(5) never used anything special about salt, there are no completed infinite collections at all; Cantor's actual infinities are just unfinished processes wearing a costume.

The trouble is that (7) would mean I wasted my career. I worked for decades with the thermodynamic limit, taking N and V to infinity at fixed density precisely because that's the only way to get a sharp phase transition, and nobody in my department treated that infinity as a fiction. And the same reasoning seems to hit the continuum of field values at each point of space, which is a much bigger infinity than the countable one I started with — so if the argument worked it would prove far more than I want it to.

Where does it break? I'd like to keep (4) if I possibly can, since it feels like the honest step, and I don't want to give up (3), which is just a theorem. Tell me which premise to drop, why the one I chose looked safe, what dropping it costs me elsewhere, and whether some repaired version of the argument still gets off the ground.
