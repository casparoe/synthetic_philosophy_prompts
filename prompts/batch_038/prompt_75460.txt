A private foundation is about to commit a fixed endowment to roughly fifteen projects over five years on what it calls the public use of the past: museums, archives, commemorations, curriculum redesign, and memorial design. Its board has taken its framing from Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation and wants the portfolio deliberately spread across monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, on the assumption that each becomes pathological once it escapes the discipline of the others. My job is to tell the board which open questions must be settled before that three-way split means anything, and I want your judgment on the ordering.

Take up four or five candidates — among them: whether the plastic power Nietzsche attributes to a person or a people can be predicated at all of an institution that has trustees, budgets, and no single horizon of its own; whether critical history can ever be more than a condemnation of the past its own judges descend from, and what follows for projects asking communities to sit in judgment on their founders; whether the capacity for forgetting, which Nietzsche treats as a precondition of action, is something a funder can positively support or can only refrain from destroying; and whether monumental history can remain honest about how much of any great episode was accident and mediocrity without ceasing to be monumental. Rank them. For each, explain why it sits upstream of the others rather than alongside them, how tractable it is for people writing grant frameworks rather than running archives, what would count as settling it, and how a negative answer would change the allocation between the three modes. I want the argument for the ordering, not a summary of the essay; assume we have the text in front of us and give no bibliography.
