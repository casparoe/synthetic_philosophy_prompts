I have been asked to deliver the closing address at the final public session of a two-year municipal process in a mid-sized American city, and I would like help writing it.

The dispute, which the audience knows far better than I do: the city's oldest and busiest branch library was named in 1948 for the local mill owner who endowed it. Last year a graduate student's research established from payroll ledgers and the company's own housing records that the mills enforced a strict color line in hiring until 1963, and that the model subdivision built around the library was deed-restricted to white families. A petition to rename drew four thousand signatures; a counter-petition drew more, many of them from descendants of the white mill workers for whom that library was the only public building in the city that ever treated them as citizens. Nineteen public sessions followed. Nothing converged. The council votes next month, and this address is the last thing the room will hear before it does.

The room: petitioners and counter-petitioners; three generations of Black families who were excluded and three generations of white families who were included; branch staff who have been absorbing this for two years; the council; a local reporter; a high-school reading group that meets in the branch on Tuesday nights. Also present, in the third row, is a professor of Jain studies from the state university who served on our panel and who has argued — correctly — that anekāntavāda was historically a metaphysical and polemical instrument deployed in sectarian dispute, and that its recasting as a doctrine of tolerance and pluralism is a modern reinvention. She has told me so twice, to my face. I intend to use the doctrine anyway. The address therefore has to survive her.

My own position is weak in a useful way. I sat on the panel as one of three non-voting community members. My family has run a hardware store here for four generations; my grandparents came from Gujarat; we were never mill people, on either side of the color line. I was raised with the doctrine not as philosophy but as table manners. Two years ago, at our opening session, I gave a short talk about it, and people remember that talk in three incompatible ways: some heard a plea for tolerance, some heard a defense of the mill owner, some heard a shrug. The address must correct all three readings at once without ever referring back to the talk.

What the address must actually do:

- Use the doctrine as the speech's structure rather than its decoration. Specifically, work from the tradition's insistence that every assertion carries the conditions under which it holds — that claims are true in some respect and false in another, and that the respects run along lines like substance, place, time, and mode — and show the room that much of this two-year fight has been a fight over which respect is doing the work. A name is a gift in one respect and an honor in another; a deed restriction is a fact about 1952 and a fact about 2024 in two different senses of "is"; the library is the building, the endowment, the name, and the Tuesday-night reading group, and those are not the same object.
- Refuse the cheap version. The address must not say that everyone is partly right. It must make clear that the doctrine's target is precisely the error of holding a partial view as if it were the whole — one-sidedness is the thing being named, not excused — and that partiality is no alibi. Some claims made in this room are simply false, and the speech must be willing to say so about at least one of them without losing its nerve.
- Neither resolve the vote nor feign neutrality about the history. It may not urge any decision about the name; it must not soften what the ledgers show.
- Not tell the parable of the blind men and the elephant. It has already been told twice in this process, and it is exactly the version of the doctrine I mean to disown.
- Absorb the professor's objection from inside the speech, without naming her and without defensiveness: use the doctrine for something other than agreeableness. Non-violence in speech is available to me as a discipline imposed on the speaker rather than a compliment paid to the audience, and the address should make that visible by being visibly costly to deliver somewhere — a sentence the speaker plainly does not want to say and says anyway.
- Be speakable. Eight minutes, roughly 1,100 words. Plain sentences that one person can say aloud to people who are angry. At most two Sanskrit terms, each introduced once in ordinary language and thereafter never used as jargon. No stage directions or bracketed delivery notes.
- Include one sentence that will sound to the petitioners like a concession and is not, and one that will sound to the counter-petitioners like a refusal and is not — neither flagged, glossed, or explained in the speech itself.
- Close with something the council can actually do next month that is not a verdict on the name — a proposal about how the decision is to be recorded, or how the branch is to carry both of its histories — and make that proposal follow from the argument rather than being stapled to it.

Write the address. Then append a note of no more than two hundred words identifying the two places where you traded doctrinal precision for speakability, and saying what the tradition's own critics would make of each trade: the Buddhist logicians who objected that the sevenfold scheme of conditioned predication violates non-contradiction, and the modern scholars who object to the tolerance reading of the doctrine.
