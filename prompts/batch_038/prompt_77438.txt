I am a military lawyer, and the vocabulary I work in daily is the law of armed conflict: combatant status and the combatant's privilege, prisoner-of-war protections, distinction, military necessity, proportionality in attack, feasible precautions, hors de combat, and direct participation in hostilities — together with the settled doctrine that these rules apply equally to both parties regardless of which side's resort to force was lawful. I have read enough of the philosophy of war to know there is a live argument about the moral equality of combatants: a traditional side holding that soldiers on both sides retain an equal permission to fight and an equal liability to be killed, and a revisionist side holding that liability to defensive harm tracks moral responsibility for an unjust threat, so that killings by soldiers serving an unlawful war stand on the same moral footing as murder; some traditionalists reply that the equality rests instead on a kind of collective agreement among states, or on soldiers' epistemic limits regarding their own side's cause.

Please restate that argument as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary above, and then give me a residue report. Sort the principal claims on each side into three buckets: those that translate cleanly into a legal category; those that translate only with distortion, in which case say precisely what the distortion consists in; and those that have no expression in the law at all. In particular I want to know (a) whether the revisionist's moral claim amounts to nothing more than a denial of the equal-application rule, or is something a status-based legal order cannot even formulate; (b) whether the traditionalist appeals to agreement and to epistemic limitation have any counterpart in devices like the combatant's privilege; and (c) whether the further revisionist conclusion — that even just combatants can rarely be confident enough of an opponent's liability to kill him — has a home among operational constraints on the use of force, or falls outside them entirely.

Do not smuggle my framework's own assumptions in under the label of translation: where a philosophical claim genuinely resists the legal vocabulary, say so rather than recasting it as a rule I already accept. Close by taking a position on what the residue indicates — that the law is deliberately impoverished, bracketing the justice of the cause for institutional reasons it can defend, or that the two philosophical camps are addressing different objects altogether.
