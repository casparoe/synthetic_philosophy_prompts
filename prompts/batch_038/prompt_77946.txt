I work at a small research nonprofit that maintains a public archive of failed projects — not only our own, but anyone's that will file an honest postmortem. Our board votes next month on whether to keep funding it. The executive director's appeal ended with the familiar line about being unable to remember the past and therefore being condemned to repeat it; a board member replied that an organization which keeps a museum of its own failures will eventually stop taking risks at all. I have been rereading Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, and it struck me that the two of them are talking past each other because that slogan carries at least four distinct claims.

Please lay the readings out as a menu I can bring to the meeting rather than merging them into a synthesis. For each: the precise claim it makes (about causation, about base rates and how similar cases have to be for a prior to transfer, about what it takes for a record to actually change anyone's behavior, or about owing something to the people in it regardless of consequences); whether that claim is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false; which of Nietzsche's three modes of history it belongs to — monumental, antiquarian, or critical; and what his specific diagnosis of that mode's failure looks like when applied to an archive like ours. Then answer the question I actually care about: which reading the executive director's sentence required in order to support continued funding, whether that reading survives the board member's objection, and whether there is any single reading under which both of them are right about the same archive.
