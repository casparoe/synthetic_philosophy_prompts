I have just delivered the manuscript of my first monograph — a study in early modern economic history containing, by my own count, something like four thousand discrete factual claims: prices, wages, ship tonnage, dates of office, attributions of letters. The series editor has asked me to add the customary closing sentence to the acknowledgements before it goes to press, the one that concedes in effect that whatever errors remain in the book are the author's own and are to be expected.

A colleague of mine who works on formal epistemology read the final chapters and objected to that sentence in a way I have not been able to answer. His claim is that including it would be dishonest in a precise sense rather than a merely stylistic one: the book asserts each of those four thousand claims flat out, the sentence concedes that at least one of them is false, and so the total package would publish a body of claims whose conjunction I do not believe and, on my own showing, would be a fool to believe. He says I have two honest options — drop the sentence, or hedge the assertions throughout into statements about what is highly probable — and he has repeated this at two seminars now, where it lands as a gotcha rather than as an argument I can see how to engage.

Could you set out, as a list I could take back to him, the distinct precise claims that this stock sentence might actually be making? I can see at least that it might be read as a prediction about the contents of the book, as a statement about who is answerable for them rather than about whether they are true, as an expression of a general attitude toward one's own reliability, and as a piece of genre ritual that no reader takes as an assertion at all; I suspect there are further readings I have not thought of. For each one, please say exactly what it commits me to, whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and whether it can be held together with asserting the book's individual claims without qualification. Then tell me which readings his objection actually requires in order to go through, and which reading the convention ordinarily carries among historians. If your conclusion is that the sentence is harmless, I would rather you did not wave away the tension he identifies: I want to understand why that tension fails to license the consequence he draws from it, and whether there is some narrower residue of his point that is correct and that should lead me to change something in the manuscript or in the way I phrase the sentence.
