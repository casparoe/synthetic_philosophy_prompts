Epistemology seminar, and for three weeks now we've been going in circles about whether any mental state is luminous. The argument I keep hearing runs roughly like this: take a condition that shades in by degrees — gradually getting cold, say — and line up a series of moments from one where it clearly obtains to one where it clearly doesn't, each barely distinguishable from its neighbours. If you were always in a position to know you're in the condition whenever you're in it, then that plus the idea that knowing rules out nearby error lets you step along the series one moment at a time, and you end up knowing you're cold at a moment when you plainly aren't. So no interesting condition is luminous. I buy the shape of it, but our seminar leader says the conclusion is way overextended, and I'd like to see for myself where it bites and where it doesnt.

Rank these from most to least vulnerable to that argument — which are genuinely shown to be non-luminous, which have some way of resisting — and give each a number from 0 to 1, where 1 means the argument goes through cleanly and it's definitely not luminous:

- being in pain right now
- having a headache
- believing that it will rain tomorrow
- knowing that it will rain tomorrow
- it seeming to you that there's a canary on the table in front of you
- intending to raise your arm
- remembering what you had for breakfast
- being over 180cm tall (control case, not mental at all)
- being in a position to know that it will rain tomorrow

For each, a sentence or two on what's doing the work: is it the gradual-shading premise, the no-nearby-error premise, or just the fact that the condition isn't the sort of thing that comes in degrees? Where the ranking and the numbers come apart, say why.

Then the bit I actually care about. Does any of this touch the KK principle — if you know that p, you know that you know that p? Rank these three claims by how much trouble they're each in: knowledge is luminous, pain is luminous, KK holds. And explain whether KK could survive even if knowledge itself fails to be luminous, because on the face of it KK looks like just a luminosity claim about knowledge and I don't see how it escapes. If it doesn't escape, say what's lost: is KK something anyone actually needed, or was it always a philosopher's toy?

Last thing. One guy in my group thinks belief is special because you settle the question of whether you believe it will rain by settling the question of whether it will rain — no introspection, no evidence-gathering, just more first-order deliberation — and so the cold-series argument simply doesn't get a grip on believing. Say where believing should go on the list if he's right about that, and whether the move really dodges the argument or just relocates it to the question of how you're supposed to know that your deliberation is finished.
