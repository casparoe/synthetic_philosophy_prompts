I am at work on a novel about a civil war, and my editor and I have reached an impasse on which I would value an outside judgment.

The book alternates between two first-person narrators, one soldier on each side. I have taken pains to make them twins in what they know: I have stripped out flags, place-names, uniforms, and every allusion to who struck first, and I have given each man evidence of exactly the same quality about how the war began — partial, propagandistic, and sincerely credited. Each also observes the same restraints; neither has fired on anyone he took himself to have no business firing on. In the final third the two meet, and one kills the other. I have written that scene so that nothing in the narration tells the reader which of them to want alive.

My editor calls this a dodge dressed up as even-handedness. Her objection is that by making the men interchangeable I imply their situations are interchangeable — that the soldier serving an aggressor has done nothing different in kind from the soldier resisting him, provided both were careful about their targets and honest about their beliefs. I answer that a man may be doing the only thing he can see to do and be no worse for it, while the two of them are nonetheless not in the same position. She says I cannot have both.

Who is right? Please commit to a verdict and defend it, rather than setting out the case for each of us and leaving me to settle the matter. If your verdict depends on adopting one particular way of resolving the tension I have described, tell me which way you have adopted and why it beats the alternatives.
