The question was:

> **Essay 2.** What makes every 'a' an 'a'? Take account of (i) the printed double-story a, the handwritten single-story one, and the italic one that's somewhere between; (ii) two a's by the same writer on the same page that differ visibly; (iii) the near-misses — the bowl that closes and turns a c into an o, the one that stays open, the half-formed or rotated a. Is a definition in necessary and sufficient features available? If not, what does the work of holding the category together, and does the answer come out differently for handwriting than for print? The student's answer, ~700 words, condensed in a couple of spots where they repeat themself, nothing substantive cut:

> The question assumes there's a shape all a's share, and the first thing to say is that there isn't. A printed a has a closed bowl with a vertical stem and an arm curving over it; a handwritten a is a circle with a stroke down the right side; an italic one leans and can go either way. No list of features — number of strokes, openness of the bowl, angle, proportions — picks out exactly the a's and excludes everything else. The near-misses prove it: close a c and you have an o, open an o and you have something between o and c and e, and the same mark can be an a in one hand and something else in another. So the category is not definable by necessary and sufficient features.
>
> What remains is family resemblance in Wittgenstein's sense: a's resemble one another in overlapping ways with no single common thread. The type/token distinction does some of the work here too — the letter is a type, each mark a token of it — so we shouldn't expect the tokens to look alike, only to be tokens of the same type.
>
> But once we admit that, we have to admit letter identity is a matter of convention. Saussure's principle of the arbitrariness of the sign says there is no natural bond between signifier and signified and that the community is free to settle it otherwise; nothing about the shape of an a compels it to be an a. So the missing definition isn't a defect in our description but the truth of the matter: there is no fact about whether a badly formed or borderline mark is an a. We simply decide, and different scripts decide differently — which is why Latin and Cyrillic can put near-identical shapes to work as different letters.
>
> Where handwriting and print come apart is best spelled out with Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic art. For Goodman what matters is reproducibility: an art is allographic where the work can be mechanically reproduced and autographic where it cannot, which is why forging a painting is possible and forging a Beethoven symphony is not. Printed type is mechanically reproduced, so the printed a is allographic and its identity is fixed by the font design — every instance either complies with the type or doesn't, and the type is its essence. Handwriting is autographic: each a is a unique object whose identity depends on the history that produced it, so beyond what the script's tradition settles there is no fact about which handwritten a's belong to one letterform.
>
> The conclusion is that the printed a has an essence and the handwritten a doesn't. Style — what makes one writer's a's recognizably theirs — is exactly what falls outside the category, since it is what varies among tokens of a type that has no defining features.
