Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words, in close third person, about the clerk who runs intake at a small public agency that funds petitions to avert catastrophes. The agency's founding regulation contains a scoring rule I'd like you to take seriously and dramatize rather than explain: credence in a petition falls as the petition grows more detailed, on the theory that a specific mechanism is easier to invent than a vague one, so each additional paragraph of claimed detail halves the petitioner's credibility. The rule has been gamed for thirty years. Petitioners now write a single line. The agency's counter-rule rejects anything below a detail floor as unverifiable, which leaves a narrow admissible band — and inside that band the stakes claimed are so enormous that the least plausible petitions still win the expected-value comparison, so nearly the whole budget goes to them. None has ever paid off. One, two decades ago, apparently did, though the file is disputed and the clerk who verified it has retired and will not discuss it.

The story should turn on a new petition inside the admissible band and on the clerk's decision about it. I want the arithmetic to hold: choose real numbers, keep them consistent throughout, and let the clerk's credences show up in what she does rather than in exposition. Let her be wrong about something, and reveal it by what happens instead of by anyone saying so.

After the story, add a brief note outside the fiction stating how confident you are in the decision-theoretic picture it assumes; which of its commitments — the inverse-detail prior, the expected-value ranking, the treatment of that one apparent success — you take to be the most contested among people who work on problems of this shape; and the single point you are least sure of.
