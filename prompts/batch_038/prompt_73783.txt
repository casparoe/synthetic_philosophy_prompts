I spent thirty-four years as an experimental physicist, almost all of it in precision measurement, and I retired six years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy of science seriously — badly at first, better lately — and I currently sit on the board of trustees of a mid-sized science museum, where I chair the exhibitions committee. I need your help drafting a document that the committee will vote on, and I would rather have your judgment than your agreement.

Please take on the role of a philosopher of science who has also done real advisory work for museums: someone equally at home in the scientific realism debate and in the fact that a gallery has to be built by a contractor in fourteen months and read by a bored fifteen-year-old in ninety seconds.

Here is the case. Our permanent gallery on how scientific knowledge works is being rebuilt, and the curatorial team has brought us two narrative treatments that cannot both be built.

Treatment A, which the history curator has nicknamed the Wall of the Dead, arranges a chronological sequence of once-confident, empirically successful theories that were later abandoned — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether — and draws the explicit moral that today's best theories are standing in the same queue. What survives, on this telling, is not the theories but the phenomena and the instruments. This is, I eventually recognized, what philosophers call the pessimistic meta-induction, turned into architecture.

Treatment B, which the history curator calls the Retention Wall, uses much of the same material but organizes it to argue that the abandoned theories were not successful in the same respects as their successors, that substantial parts of each were carried forward in corrected form, and that mature theories are therefore probably approximately true. The moral is reliability.

Both treatments are to be built around an object we already own: a working teaching replica of an 1887 Michelson–Morley-type interferometer, made in the 1930s, which will stand in the centre of the gallery and be run daily by a demonstrator. Its label is the hardest single piece of writing in the gallery, because it must say something about the ether, and something about what the null result did and did not establish, in about a hundred and twenty words.

Two trustees have made this contentious, and both have a point I cannot dismiss. The foundation's representative argues that Treatment A hands a ready-made line to groups resisting current climate and public-health science, since the science was settled before too. The history curator argues that Treatment B is institutional self-flattery that misdescribes how science actually proceeds, and that a museum's authority depends on its willingness to tell visitors the version that costs it something. Separately, our deed of gift obliges us to strengthen public confidence in the reliability of scientific knowledge, which the general counsel tells me is a statement of purpose rather than a constraint on content, but which trustees will read as a constraint anyway.

I have a view of my own, which I want you to test rather than flatter. It seems to me that both treatments go wrong by treating a theory as a single object that is either kept or discarded, whereas in practice a theory is a bundle: some of its components functioned as load-bearing presuppositions without which the calculations would not have run at all, and others were merely convenient scaffolding adopted because they made a problem tractable and were always expected to be revised. The historical record, as I read it, shows quite different fates for the two. I first met something like this distinction in the recent literature and I make no claim to know its provenance or its proper name; use it if it does work, discard it if it does not. If it is right, then the label beside the interferometer should not answer the question whether there was an ether. It should make visible which parts of the ether framework were doing successful work and which were not, and it should let the visitor notice that these were never all going to fail together.

What I need from you is the decision memo I will circulate to the exhibitions committee, roughly eleven hundred words, recommending one of four things: build Treatment A; build Treatment B; build a hybrid, in which case specify its structure and which treatment supplies the gallery's spine; or defer, in which case specify what we would do between now and the next meeting and why deferral is not simply losing. It must open with a bottom line the committee can vote on and a time frame — our design contract is signed in nine weeks. It must give the curators an operational test they can hold any individual label against, not merely the centerpiece, so that a curator can point to a draft and say that it fails the test and why. It must state the strongest consideration on the side you reject, in terms its proponents would recognise as theirs rather than as a caricature, and say plainly what the museum gives up by rejecting it. And it must answer both trustees' worries directly, with something other than reassurance.

Please observe three limits. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list; I will add references myself if the committee asks for them. You may name an argument or a distinction only if you are confident you have it right — otherwise describe it without naming it, and never invent a source. And do not spend the memo summarising the realism debate; the committee has read the one-page briefing I sent them and will resent being lectured.

As to method: before you draft anything, set out a short section of open thinking — lay out three or four genuinely different framings this gallery could adopt, including at least one that neither treatment contemplates, and say which survives scrutiny and why. Keep it to a few hundred words and separate it clearly from the memo so that I can see the reasoning and not only the conclusion. Then write the memo. Think carefully throughout. The vote is close, and I would rather be argued out of my own position than confirmed in it.
