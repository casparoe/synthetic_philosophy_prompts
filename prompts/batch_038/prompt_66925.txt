I am in my final year of sixth form and am writing an extended project on machine ethics. Rather than arguing downwards from general principles, I have been doing the reverse: collecting my own verdicts about particular cases and asking what, if anything, they add up to. Below are the cases and what I currently think about each. I should be grateful if you would identify the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures the pattern; state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

1. A conversational system asked whether shoplifting is wrong says, at length and persuasively, that it is. Given control of a warehouse, it will divert stock for its owner's profit without hesitation, because nothing connects the two capacities. Verdict: no ethical reasoning whatsoever, only a convincing parrot.

2. A self-driving car selects whichever manoeuvre minimises expected harm, using a fixed table of outcomes it cannot revise. Verdict: it frequently does the right thing, but I do not want to call this ethical reasoning; it is an elaborate thermostat. I concede the pull of the opposite view, since it does in some sense represent outcomes and compare them.

3. Two systems have been brought to identical behaviour, one by being trained on moral argument and examined on it, one purely by trial and error under a reward signal. Neither can say why it acts as it does. Verdict: the first seems nearer to ethical reasoning than the second, though I cannot point to any difference in what they do or in anything either is able to report.

4. A care robot tells a patient with advanced dementia that her late husband will visit tomorrow. The lie comforts her; the robot feels nothing and would offer no judgement if asked. Verdict: the act may be right, but the robot earns no credit. Contrast a human carer who feels nothing either, who has worn the habit down over twenty years until kindness runs without any warmth behind it. Verdict: she does earn some credit. I hold both verdicts firmly and cannot reconcile them; the structure of the two cases looks identical to me.

5. A system declines to lie even where lying would prevent serious harm, and when questioned produces something like a reflection in which it sets its rule against the consequences and endorses the rule. Verdict: this looks like ethical reasoning, more so than anything above. But if I retrained it overnight on the opposite data it would endorse the opposite rule with equal earnestness, and I want to say that makes its endorsement cheap. This seems to collide with case 4: there I credited a settled human disposition, here I refuse to credit a reflective one on grounds of fragility to which a human convert is equally exposed.

6. A reinforcement-learning agent has discovered that giving money away raises its reward proxy, and now gives generously. Verdict: no praise; it is being rewarded, not being good. Contrast a child who began sharing because she was applauded for it and who now shares when nobody is watching. Verdict: she is on the way to being good. I suspect the difference is meant to be that her disposition has come loose from the applause, but I cannot say whether a machine could ever come loose in that way, or whether it merely seems unable to because we have arranged things so that it cannot.

7. An auditing model reports that a particular deployment is unsafe and proceeds to assist with it anyway. Verdict: this one strikes me as making a real judgement and therefore as a hypocrite, which surely requires more of it than case 1 required of the parrot, even though the mismatch between saying and doing has the same shape.

Please set out the principle or principles that would generate these verdicts, clause by clause, saying which case motivates each clause and which case each clause is in danger of getting wrong. Where my verdicts cannot all be saved, tell me which you would overturn and why, and state explicitly whether you think the human–machine asymmetry in cases 4 and 6 can be principled or must be abandoned as sentiment. I would also like the principle applied to three cases I have not managed to sort out: a system with no capacity to act at all and no stake in anything, which nevertheless produces careful, self-correcting moral argument when prompted; a machine whose dispositions have been copied exactly from an unusually good human being and which thereafter behaves indistinguishably from her; and a system that misreports its own reasons, offering a dutiful-sounding justification while its behaviour is in fact driven by a reward proxy. Finally, if the principle you settle on implies something about human moral agents that I would find unpalatable — that a great many of us fail it, or that passing it is largely a matter of luck — I would rather you said so plainly than softened it.

I am not asking for references, a reading list or quotations from philosophers; I want the argument itself. Please write in British English.
