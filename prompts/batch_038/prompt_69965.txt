I run the design side of a small nonprofit that convenes citizens' assemblies for local councils — maybe fifteen of them so far, team of six, half of us poli-sci trained, nobody who's done any classics. We take 60 to 100 randomly selected residents through a few weekends on something like bus fares, housing density, or whether to close one of two urgent care centres, and the recommendation goes to the council, which often votes on it. I got into reading about Athenian democracy through a podcast years ago and never really stopped, and I keep having this uncomfortable feeling that the three episodes everyone always mentions aren't ancient history to me, they're a list of ways our own things could go wrong, and I have no instrument for telling which. So I want to build one, and I'd rather it come out of those episodes properly than out of a list of deliberative-democracy best practices I could get from any consultancy deck.

Here's what I think I see in each, though I'm aware I'm probably flattening them. Mytilene: the assembly votes one day to kill every adult male in a revolted city, sleeps on it, and reverses itself the next day — but only narrowly, and the argument that won the reversal wasn't "this is unjust," it was "this won't deter anyone and it'll cost us," and then a ship has to race across the Aegean to countermand the order and gets there with almost nothing to spare, while around a thousand people identified as ringleaders are executed anyway without trial. Sicily: they commit a huge force to an island they know essentially nothing about; the man most opposed to it tries to kill the plan by demanding an absurdly large force and the assembly just cheerfully grants it; the loudest enthusiast is pulled off the campaign by a prosecution launched by his rivals and promptly defects and tells the enemy how to hurt Athens; and later the general stuck in the field writes home a letter describing how bad things actually are but hedges it, because he's afraid of what the assembly will do to him if he writes plainly — so the people who most needed the truth got a softened version of it. Socrates: about five hundred jurors, no deliberation between hearing and deciding, convicted 280 to 221, and then a second, separate vote on the penalty in which nobody had any new information and the outcome got worse, partly because of how the defendant answered; and the charges were about impiety and the young, because the thing the city was actually furious about — the guy's association with people who'd destroyed the city and then run an oligarchy — couldn't be charged directly. What I want from you is the failure mechanism in each of those, stripped of the triremes, stated in a way that would let me recognize it in a room in Leeds in 2026.

The deliverable is a screening procedure my team can actually run on a proposed assembly design before we sign it — an hour, six of us, on a call, with the commissioner's project lead in the room. Not an essay about Athenian institutions. I want numbered checks in the order we'd run them; for each, the question we put to the design, what counts as a passing answer versus a shrug, what the red-flag version of the answer sounds like, and whether failing that check means "redesign," "decline the commission," or "proceed and write the risk down." I also want to know where the screen stops and hands the call to the council, because the question is about values or politics rather than procedure, and what we should say when we hand it over. Then run it on three designs we're looking at right now: (1) 45 members, two weekends, reporting three weeks before a council election, one final plenary vote on whether to close one of two urgent care centres, no second reading and no requirement that members say what evidence would change their minds, and the commissioner wants the recommendation published before the vote; (2) a 90-member assembly on housing density running six months with a mid-point report and an explicit right to write back to the council asking for more time or more information — but the brief requires us to seat a designated critic chosen by the council, and the council picked someone who privately supports the proposal; (3) a design where the assembly writes the ballot wording for a binding local referendum on its own recommendation, and the same members are expected to campaign for it afterwards. Be blunt on (1) — we've already signed for it and I'd rather hear that the screen says decline than find out in a year. And please be honest about where the analogy is costume drama: Athens was a slave-owning empire deciding whether to massacre a city, and we're deciding bus fares, so tell me which of these mechanisms are load-bearing at our scale and which ones I'm importing for the aesthetic. No bibliography and no reading list, and don't feel you need to quote Thucydides at me — I want the reasoning, not the sources.

Please use these sections, in this order:

- **Bottom line** — 5 to 8 sentences: whether a screen like this is worth running at all, and which three checks carry most of the weight.
- **Three mechanisms** — roughly 400 words total, one tight passage per episode, each ending with one sentence on what the mechanism is *not*, so I don't confuse it with its neighbors.
- **The screen** — the bulk of it, 800 to 1,200 words: the numbered checks in order, each with the question, the passing answer, the red flag, and the disposition (redesign / decline / proceed-and-document).
- **Not our call** — short, maybe 150 words: where the screen should hand the decision to the commissioner, and the wording to use when we do.
- **Run on my three** — a couple of paragraphs per design, with a verdict and the single most important change for each, and the bad news about (1) stated plainly.
- **Where this breaks** — the disanalogies, the designs the screen would kill that are actually fine, and the failure modes it can't catch at all.
