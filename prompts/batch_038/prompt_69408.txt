I chair the data governance committee for a longitudinal cohort study at our university — some forty thousand participants followed for three decades, with records arriving from clinic visits, wearable devices, and national registries — and I am drafting the committee's methods report. Before I do, I want to understand what our identification practices commit us to. Three features of the system, none of them controversial among our data engineers:

First, every participant is assigned at enrolment a permanent integer, allocated in order of arrival: 1000001, 1000002, and so on. This number is the primary key. Nothing about the person enters into it. It is never reused and never revised; it survives the person's death; and it is the thing to which every observation in the study is attached, including observations recorded before we knew the person's name.

Second, alongside that key the schema declares a small set of substantive attributes to be unique. No two rows may share a national identity number, and no two may share the combination of surname, date of birth, and sex. The database enforces these constraints itself: an insert that violates them simply fails.

Third, each year we run a duplicate-resolution pass. Its standing instruction is that where two records agree on every substantive field we hold, they are to be merged into one, on the presumption that agreement across so many fields cannot be coincidence; a merge may be blocked only by independent evidence that two distinct individuals are involved — a death certificate for one of them, a photograph, a sibling appearing in the registry.

My difficulty is that the first and third features appear to pull in opposite directions. The first says that being the same participant is nothing over and above bearing the same arbitrary integer, that numerical distinctness is recorded rather than earned. The third says that where qualities are indiscernible, distinctness must be earned by evidence, and that in the absence of such evidence two records collapse into one. Two of my engineers, both excellent, disagree about whether this is a real tension: one holds that the integer merely tracks a fact we could not otherwise express, the other that it is a bookkeeping convenience with no metaphysical content whatsoever.

Then came the case that persuaded me the disagreement is not merely verbal. We have enrolled a pair of monozygotic twins who share a surname, a date of birth, a sex, an address, and for most of our purposes a genome; they differ in a national identity number issued to each of them minutes apart by a civil registry that allocates such numbers sequentially. Our uniqueness constraints are satisfied only by that civil number. Strip it away — imagine a jurisdiction that never issued one, or a study conducted before such registries existed — and the twins are, for every descriptive purpose we possess, exactly alike, and yet they must remain two rows with two distinct clinical histories and two distinct outcomes. I find myself reaching for the old thought experiment about a symmetric universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, and I cannot say what my reaching for it commits me to.

So: what account of individuation does this practice presuppose? I should like an answer that does four things.

(a) Reconstruct the view of what makes a thing the thing it is, and of what makes two things two, that the three features jointly require. Be explicit about how the primary key is best read: as recording a primitive thisness, as a stand-in for one, or as something else altogether — a registration event, a pledge about future bookkeeping, a causal-historical thread running through the archive.

(b) Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with. My suspicion is that several rival accounts would license all three features, and I want to know which, if any, are ruled out. In particular: is this practice available to someone who denies that there could be two qualitatively indiscernible individuals at all, and to someone who holds that numerical diversity must always be grounded in some qualitative or relational difference? If it is available to them, say what each of them must say the integer is doing.

(c) Tell me whether the first and third features are inconsistent, consistent but in tension, or merely differently scoped — and, if the last, state the scoping principle in a form an engineer could apply on a Tuesday afternoon. Note that the twins case seems to leave the uniqueness constraints doing work that neither the primary key nor the merge rule can do alone, which may mean the practice embodies not one view but three, badly aligned.

(d) Say what would have to change — in the schema, in the duplicate-resolution instruction, in what we would count as an error rather than as a finding — were we to replace the presupposed view with its most natural rival. I want at least one concrete change I could put to the committee, together with an honest statement of what we would lose by making it.

Please introduce any technical vocabulary you need as you go rather than assuming I already have it, and please resist the temptation to give me the history of the debate or a survey of the positions. What I want is an account of this practice.
