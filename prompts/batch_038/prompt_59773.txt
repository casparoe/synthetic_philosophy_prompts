The question, worth twenty-five points, with a suggested length of four hundred to five hundred words, was this: Kripke holds that some truths are necessary and yet knowable only a posteriori. Using either the identity of Hesperus and Phosphorus or the identity of water and H2O, explain how such a truth is possible. Your answer should (a) say what rigid designation is and explain the work it does in that explanation, (b) explain why the necessity at issue is not merely a necessity about our words or our naming conventions, and (c) say whether Kripke's example of the standard meter bar kept in Paris is a further example of the same kind of truth, giving your reasons.

The candidate wrote: "I will use Hesperus and Phosphorus. An ordinary proper name is a rigid designator: in every possible world in which the object it names exists, it names that object, and its doing so does not depend on the object satisfying any description. Descriptions such as 'the brightest body visible in the western sky at dusk' are non-rigid, since in some possible world something else could have satisfied them — Mars, for instance, could have been brighter. The reference of 'Hesperus' was originally fixed by a description of that sort, and the reference of 'Phosphorus' by 'the brightest body visible in the eastern sky before dawn.' But once the reference is fixed the description drops out of the picture: 'Hesperus' picks out Venus in every world in which Venus exists, because that is the object the name was attached to. In every possible world in which the description picks out anything, it picks out Venus, and this is what makes the name rigid. Necessity then follows from the necessity of identity. Everything is necessarily self-identical, so Venus is necessarily Venus; and since 'Phosphorus' names Venus in every world as well, substituting it into that self-identity yields the result that Hesperus is necessarily Phosphorus. We could nevertheless only learn this by observation, because nobody could have worked it out by reflecting on the two names: they were introduced independently, in different places and at different times, by people who had no idea they were tracking a single object. It therefore took astronomy rather than armchair reflection to establish the identity. This is why the necessity is not a matter of our words. Had human beings never coined either name, Venus would still have been one planet seen twice over, and the fact would still have obtained. Nothing about the truth of the identity depends on the naming convention; the convention only fixes which object we are talking about. Kripke's point is that the two names are not synonyms in any sense that would let someone who merely understands them see the truth straight off — knowing what each name means does not tell you that they co-refer, even though what makes the identity necessary is precisely that they are two names with the same meaning, namely Venus. Water is H2O works in the same way, except that 'water' is not a proper name but a natural kind term; Kripke and Putnam both hold that terms of this kind are rigid, so the identity holds in every world in which water exists. Turning to (c): yes, the meter bar is a further example of the same kind of truth. 'One metre' was fixed by stipulation as the length of that particular bar at a particular time, so 'the standard bar is one metre long' is necessary in the same way that 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is necessary — that length is one metre in every world and at every time, because that is what the word was defined to mean. And it is a posteriori for a parallel reason: anyone other than the person who laid down the definition has to go and measure, and even he had to look at the bar to know what he was stipulating. So it belongs in the same category as the astronomical identity."
