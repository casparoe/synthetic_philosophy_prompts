Before I retired I spent some thirty-five years as a physiologist, most of it recording from dorsal horn and thalamic neurons in anesthetized preparations, and I have come to philosophy late and rather hungrily. In the last two years I have been working through the literature on the affective dimension of nociception, and one thread has held me: pain asymbolia, the syndrome described in patients with insular and opercular damage who can localize and grade a noxious stimulus, will call it pain, and yet seem entirely unmoved by it. Nikola Grahek's treatment of the syndrome is what first made me see that a clinical curiosity might be doing serious work in an argument, since it looks like a natural dissociation experiment separating the sensory-discriminative from the affective-motivational limb of pain. I am giving a short evening talk to a group of former laboratory colleagues, several of whom are physicians and none of whom has read the philosophy, and I want to build the talk around a ranking exercise rather than a survey. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want your reasoning laid out plainly enough that I could defend it at a lectern.

The difficulty is that the reported cases are few and heterogeneous, so I have constructed a single stipulated patient and I want the accounts judged against him rather than against the messier literature. Call him H: a right-handed man in his sixties with a stable unilateral infarct involving the insula and adjacent operculum, examined nine months after onset. My stipulations are these. (a) Discrimination is intact: he localizes accurately, distinguishes sharp from dull and warm from hot, and gives numerically reproducible intensity ratings across sessions. (b) He applies the word pain to the stimuli spontaneously and does not hesitate over it. (c) He declines analgesia, will leave his forearm resting on a warming plate while continuing a conversation, shows no antalgic posture, no nursing of the limb, and acquires no avoidance over weeks of testing. (d) A spinal flexion withdrawal to abrupt noxious stimulation is preserved, but skin conductance and heart-rate responses that are reliable in controls are absent. (e) There is no generalized apathy: his affect is full, he is not depressed, he enjoys meals, he dislikes the taste of a bitter solution and spits it out, and he will assent to and act on propositions such as that being burned would be bad for him, scheduling surgery for a malignancy without difficulty. (f) At fourteen months, with no change in his intensity ratings whatsoever, the same stimuli begin to be described as bad and are actively avoided; asked about the earlier months, he now says those episodes were bad too and that he was mistaken at the time to report otherwise. (g) During the asymbolic period he can also, on reflection, endorse the higher-order judgment that a normal person in his situation ought to be distressed, without being distressed. Treat these as fixed stipulations rather than as claims about real patients; I will ask you below to police their coherence, but not to correct them against case reports.

Please do the following. First, score each of the seven accounts listed below from 0 to 10 for how well it fits case H, where 0 means the account can absorb the case only by ad hoc additions or by denying the obvious, 5 means it absorbs the case but must import a further claim with no independent motivation, and 10 means the account would have predicted this precise dissociation pattern from its own prior commitments. The accounts are: (A) the view that unpleasantness is a primitive, intrinsic phenomenal quality attaching to the sensation, so that pain is bad simply in virtue of feeling bad; (B) evaluativism, on which the pain experience additionally represents the bodily condition as bad for the subject, and unpleasantness just is that evaluative content; (C) imperativism, on which the experience carries imperative content to the effect of protecting the body part or ceasing what one is doing, with badness attaching to the unsatisfied command; (D) the first-order desire account, on which unpleasantness consists in a simultaneous intrinsic desire that the sensation cease; (E) the attitudinal or higher-order account, on which what makes the state bad is a disliking or displeasure directed at it rather than any felt quality in it; (F) the deflationary account, on which H is not in pain at all during the asymbolic period and merely retains nociceptive discrimination without phenomenal pain; and (G) the pluralist or cluster account, on which pain's badness is not one property but a bundle of affective, conative, and attentional components, so that H has a genuine pain that is, for the time being, not bad for him. Second, arrange A through G in a single strict ranking by fit and defend the ordering, noting any pair you would be genuinely torn about and what further test would separate them. Third, identify which one of stipulations (a) through (g) does the most work against which account, and say why you would single out that pairing. Fourth, tell me whether any of my stipulations is clinically impossible or conceptually incoherent — my own suspicion concerns (d) alongside (f), and I would rather hear it from you than from the audience — and propose the minimal repair. Finally, give me your own considered verdict, expressed as a number between 0 and 1, on the claim that H's pain during the asymbolic period was bad for H, together with the two or three sentences you would use to justify the number to a room of skeptical physiologists.
