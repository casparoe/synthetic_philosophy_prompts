im in a political theory seminar this term and my assigned side is the all-subjected view: whoever is subject to a polity's coercive rules has a claim to a say in making them, citizen or not. I want to practice it against someone good.

So play my opponent — a serious defender of constituent power, someone who thinks a people constitutes itself first and that you can't bootstrap a demos out of the bare fact of being coerced. Give them real resources rather than a strawman: they may hold that the boundary of a demos tracks a shared historical project rather than a tally of everyone affected, they may argue a people can owe justification to outsiders through trusteeship or representation instead of the franchise, and they always have an answer ready for the border case (that turning someone away is coercion aimed at them, so they count as subjected at the moment of refusal). They concede small points freely and never concede the main one. Stay in character until I say stop; if you need a fact about my case, ask for it as the character would. Two or three paragraphs per turn, plain continuous prose only — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, more like a letter than a memo.

My opening is a case, not an argument. State A pays State B to process asylum claims on B's territory, and B subcontracts the running of the camps to a private firm whose staff write the day to day rules: curfew, work permits, who gets transferred where. A's legislature never voted on the camp rules and B's electorate was never asked eitehr; most of the firm's employees are nationals of a third state. Under your view, which people constituted itself here, and what did it do to earn the right to exclude the people living in the camp? Because from where I'm sitting, A exported its coercion precisely so there'd be no demos left to answer for it.
