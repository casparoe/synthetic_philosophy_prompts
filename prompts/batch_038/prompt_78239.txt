I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years determining the structures of enzyme active sites by X-ray diffraction, and I have since taken up philosophy with more seriousness than my former colleagues would have predicted. I belong to a reading group that meets monthly in the back room of a bookshop. Last month I proposed the problem of verisimilitude as our topic, on the grounds that in my own field every published structural model is known by its authors to be strictly false, and yet we rank them without embarrassment. Two members of the group — I shall call them Nadia and Emil — ended up in a long argument that I was unable to referee. I have reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can, in the order it occurred.

(1) Nadia opened with a case from my own discipline: a first refinement of an active site and a later one are both false, yet the later is plainly closer to the truth, and this is not loose talk but the working judgment of the field. She concluded that truthlikeness is a real ordering that scientists actually rely on, and that philosophy's job is to define it properly.

(2) Emil replied that the case smuggles in what it needs to prove: a residual measures agreement with diffraction data, not distance from the truth, and one can drive a residual down with a physically absurd model that merely overfits. He added that the history is worse than Nadia suggests — Popper's own definition of verisimilitude was shown in 1974, by Miller, Tichý and Harris, to carry the unintended consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than another, and Popper conceded the point immediately. So the one serious attempt to define the notion failed at its foundations.

(3) Nadia said that is an argument about a definition, not about a concept. The likeness approach — developed by Tichý and Hilpinen and then by Niiniluoto and Oddie — makes closeness a function of the similarity between the worlds in which a claim would be true and the actual world; Miller and Kuipers pursue a content approach on which truthlikeness is something like a product of a truth factor and a content factor; and Schurz and Weingartner restrict which consequences are allowed to count by a relevance condition. A single failed definition, she said, is an ordinary research event, not a refutation of the subject.

(4) Emil said that is precisely the indictment. Three families of definitions that coincide on toy cases and diverge on the hard ones, together with a formal impossibility theorem to the effect that no account can satisfy all the intuitively attractive principles at once, means the ordering is a convention wearing the clothes of a measurement. He added that these accounts are language-relative: re-regiment the very same claim under a different set of predicates and the verdict about which theory is closer can flip, so no such claim has a truth value until a language is fixed — and science does not fix one.

(5) Nadia answered that re-regimentation changes which respects of similarity are being weighted, and that weighting by the world's own structure rather than by an arbitrary vocabulary restores stability. She then pressed an independent point: Emil's alternative cannot explain why severe testing is worth anything. If a theory that has survived a battery of risky tests is no closer to the truth than one that has survived nothing, then survival is mere bookkeeping; corroboration earns its keep only by tracking something.

(6) Emil said survival of risky tests is an epistemic property one can characterize without a metric toward a target nobody can locate. He anticipated Nadia's likely reply — that we need not know the distance to the truth, only the direction, as a climber in fog knows she is ascending without seeing the summit — and objected that the analogy assumes what it needs: on a mountain there is a scale, altitude, that exists independently of the climber and can be sampled locally, whereas with truth there is no such scale, only our own theoretical bookkeeping.

(7) Nadia said the objection destroys Emil's position as much as hers. What counts as content, as risky, and as surviving is framework-relative too. If framework-relativity is fatal to truthlikeness, it is equally fatal to corroboration, and Emil is left with the claim that science has no standards at all — which he does not believe.

(8) Emil accepted the symmetry but drew a distinction. Severe testing makes only a modest claim, that within a practice some claims are better tested than others; truthlikeness makes a grand claim about a relation to the whole truth of the world, which no framework can reach. He said Nadia is entitled to the modest notion and to nothing more, and that talk of approaching the truth is metaphysical decoration.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate each of the eight from clearly mistaken to clearly right on a four-point scale, and justify each rating by reference to what was actually said rather than to the general literature. Then answer three questions directly: does the 1974 collapse of Popper's definition tell against the concept of truthlikeness or only against that definition; is Emil correct that the rival post-Popperian accounts render the ordering conventional, or does Nadia have an adequate answer to the underdetermination and language-relativity worries; and can Emil's notion of severe testing bear the weight he places on it without truthlikeness, or does Nadia's move (7) genuinely demolish it? Finally, tell me what neither of them said — I suspect the exchange left something significant untouched, but I do not wish to suggest what.

I am asking for a committed answer. Where a side can be taken, take it, state it in one sentence, and give your reasons; end by telling me which of the two positions you would defend if you were obliged to defend one. A even-handed survey concluding that the question remains contested would be of no use to me whatsoever — I can obtain that from any introductory treatment. If you believe one of them is simply right about one of the three questions, say so plainly and say why.
