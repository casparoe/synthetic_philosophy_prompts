I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, currently enrolled in a seminar on the metaphysics of probability, and I also produce the interview series for our department's graduate-student podcast. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a deliberately heretical package: he denies that there are objective single-case chances at all, and argues instead that what the Principal Principle really tracks is a norm of deference to whichever statistical model best systematizes an agent's total evidence — chance, on his view, is model-relative, and it shifts as the evidence shifts. He is also on record holding that the admissibility clause in the Principal Principle is not a technical patch but the whole point: the principle governs what evidence an agent is permitted to bring to bear, not what the world is like. He is a gracious and articulate interviewee, but he has two reliable escape hatches. He dismisses standard objections as "frequency talk in disguise," and he says that anyone who wants single-case chances owes an account of chance's authority over belief which his own view delivers for free.

Please draft eight to ten questions for a forty-minute recording that do not let him coast. The following four topics must each be covered, and for each of them include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give:

1. Whether model-relative chance renders the deference principle vacuous — if I am to defer to the model I already take to best systematize my evidence, is the principle doing any normative work beyond "be coherent with what you already believe"?
2. What happens when the agent is uncertain which model is correct: does deferring to a weighted mixture of model-relative chances still deserve to be called chance-deference, and if not, where does the mixture come from?
3. The admissibility clause, pressed for a criterion of admissibility that is not circular — that is, something other than "evidence that does not conflict with the chance."
4. Self-locating evidence, in cases where the agent knows the relevant chance perfectly well but is uncertain which centered possibility she occupies, so that chance and rational credence visibly come apart.

Use the remaining questions to dismantle the two escape hatches one at a time, and close with a question about what observation, or what theoretical cost, would actually make him give the view up.

One request about how you handle this: please commit rather than hedge. Choose the questions you genuinely think will work, and after the list add a short paragraph naming the single question you believe he has no good answer to and explaining why. I would much rather receive a partisan assessment I can argue with than a balanced menu of options that leaves the choice to me.
