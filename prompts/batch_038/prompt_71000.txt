I'm at a four-person political-risk shop and a foundation client has asked us for a single number: the chance that an unwritten two-term convention gets broken in the next ten years, in a country where the constitution says nothing about term limits but every president since the mid-80s has stepped down after two, and where the ruling party has started floating a third term out loud. Our intern assembled a comparison set — 34 unwritten succession conventions across 19 countries, 1870 to 2010, counted from the moment each one had been observed through at least two peaceful successions — and found that 9 of the 34 broke within 25 years of that point. But the moment we tried to turn 9/34 into anything, the whole thing started wobbling: 6 of those 9 breaks occured in a single decade-ish stretch at the end of the window, so they're probably not independent draws; three of the 25 "survivors" were still alive when the window closed, so they're censored, not successes; the underlying archive over-represents countries whose records got digitized, which is not a random sample of anything; and our target case has already run about 38 years, longer than 28 of the 34, and nobody here can agree whether that longevity makes it tougher or more fragile. The two seniors split along the usual lines — one says anchor on the outside view first and adjust only grudgingly, in the spirit of the reference-class-forecasting crowd, the other says a base rate built from cases whose mechanisms (colonial administration, telegraph-era information lag, Cold War patronage) are simply gone is not an anchor, it's a hallucination, and you should be reasoning from how the thing would actually break.

Before we referee that argument I want the spec. Write the desiderata: what a method for putting a probability on a rare, one-off, long-horizon event by way of a small set of historical analogues actually has to deliver in order to count as adequate. I want the requirement stated, then the case or consideration behind it — including the ones that are easy to skip, like how the reference class gets picked and what stops you from defining it by hindsight, how a static "9 out of 34" becomes a hazard over the client's specific ten-year window, what the method owes you when the target case is unlike everything in the sample on the one feature that seems to matter, what it should do about cases clustering in time and therefore not adding up to 34 independent observations, and whether it has to say anything about how the number would move if the client redraws the class. Also mark which of these are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just the house style of one camp or the other, and tell me which single requirement, taken seriously, knocks out the most candidate methods — I suspect that's where the real disagreement between my two seniors lives. And if some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable for events this rare — my guess is that "sample big enough to have a usable standard error," "sample homogeneous enough that the frequency transfers," "class defined only from facts available at forecast time," and "sample drawn from a period whose causal structure still holds" cannot all be satisfied at once — then say that plainly as the result rather than as a caveat at the end.

For context on what counts as adequate: this has to be a protocol four people can actually run in a couple of weeks, hand over as one probability plus an honest interval to a client who will spend money on it, and get scored on a decade from now by someone who wasn't in the room — so a method that only produces qualitative confidence, or that can't be settled by anything short of the heat death of the universe, is out. One extra wrinkle that I think belongs on the list somewhere: the foundation intends to campaign publicly to defend the convention, which means our forecast, if it circulates, changes the thing it's forecasting, and I don't know whether a method has to handle that or just flag it. Finally, briefly and only briefly, run four candidates against the list: the raw base rate from the 34 cases, a duration model with covariates fit to the same data, a structured expert inside-view analysis of how the third term would actually happen, and a no-number option that just presents the closest analogues and their outcomes. A table is fine for that part. The criteria are what I'm after, the scorecard is a courtesy.
