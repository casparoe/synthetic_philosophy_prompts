my citys policy reading group has two people who have been arguing about the world happiness rankings for a year and I want to write the document they should of produced, as if it came out of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Cast one of them as an evaluationist: thinks the ladder question — rate your life against the best possible life, 0 to 10 — picks out what actually matters for policy, and that measures of daily mood are myopic and noisy. Cast the other as a measurement skeptic: thinks ladder scores mostly track income, education and whatever comparison standards are locally normal, so the cross-country ranking is close to a wealth ranking wearing a wellbeing label.

Give me the joint statement they could both sign. Numbered agreed claims first, including what the ladder question is really asking people to do and what the ranking does and doesn't license you to infer. Then a disagreements table where each row is phrased so that the side it belongs to would accept the wording of its own position — no strawmen, no slanted glosses. Then the cruxes, and build them around this case, because it's the one they keep shouting past each other on: suppose a mid-ranked country runs a five-year program that leaves incomes, health, working hours, social contact and reported daily affect measurably unchanged, but successfully narrows the reference group people compare themselves to; afterwards the ladder average is up 0.8 and the country climbs fourteen places. Gain in wellbeing, change in the measuring stick, or both — and can that question even be kept apart from the question of whether a government should be trying to move the number at all? For each crux, name the evidence or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. Finally, say explicitly which parts of the dispute you think turn out to be verbal rather than substantive, and why.
