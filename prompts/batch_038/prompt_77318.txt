I am a novelist, and my third book turns on a rescue scene: a canal lockkeeper has minutes to decide whether to open one gate or the other, drowning either the single diver trapped in the upper chamber or the five workers trapped in the lower one. In every draft, the characters who favor the five defend themselves with some version of the line "the numbers count," and my editor observes that the exchange now reads as a chant rather than a disagreement. I want the phrase to be doing determinate work, and I want my characters to be disagreeing about something.

Could you set out, as a list I can keep beside the manuscript, the distinct precise claims this slogan might be making? For each reading, say what sort of counting it presupposes, whether it is a thesis about reasons, about outcomes, or about procedure, whether it speaks to what the lockkeeper ought to do or to how bad the resulting state of affairs is, and whether it amounts to a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or something simply false. Then tell me which readings a character could sincerely hold while nevertheless opening the gate that drowns the five — because one of them is his brother — and which readings genuinely forbid it. I would like at least one of my defenders of the many to be committed to a reading that cannot accommodate that kind of partiality, so please be explicit about which candidates have that property.

I would prefer the response in American English, and I would rather you not supply citations or a reading list; the analysis should stand on the arguments themselves, and I will attach my own references later.
