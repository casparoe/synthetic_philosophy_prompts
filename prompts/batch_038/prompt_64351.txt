You're a philosopher who works on truthlikeness but who also has a real feel for measurement and psychometrics — basically the person I'd want refereeing a grant proposal about scoring the correctness of open-ended answers. Think carefully, and before you commit to any ranking, start with a short brainstorm (bullets are fine) about what would make one line of work a bottleneck for the others.

Here's the situation. I run a very small group — me (philosophy background), one logician, one person who used to build benchmarks for a living — with two years of funding and a single deliverable: a scoring framework for long-form factual answers where nothing anyone writes is exactly true. Think answers about contested history, or about mechanisms in biology: partly right, partly wrong, right in structure but off in the details. Right now everyone in this space either does exact-match against a hand-written rubric (which throws away everything that isn't binary) or asks a strong model to rate the answer from 1 to 10 (which we all know its noise with a nice interface on it). We want something with an actual account behind it, not a vibe with a leaderboard.

Our current prototype is embarrassingly naive. We decompose an answer into atomic claims and score it as (true claims it makes) minus (false claims it makes), normalised somehow. Two things break immediately. First, once an answer is wrong in enough places the score saturates, and every bad answer ties with every other bad answer — which is the same shape as the 1974 results that sank Popper's original definition of verisimilitude, and I don't find that reassuring. Second, it's gameable: bolt on a pile of true but irrelevant claims and the score goes up while the answer gets worse.

The directions we could spend the two years on, as I currently see them:

1. Similarity/distance route. Define a space of candidate answers for a question and score an answer by its distance from the true point in that space. The hard part is that the distance function has to come from somewhere — and I can't see why it wouldn't be arbitrary, or why any particular one would be privileged.

2. Contrastivist route. Truthlikeness is only ever relative to a contrast class of alternatives, so make the class explicit — say, the set of answers the question licenses, or the set a domain expert would treat as live — and score within that. This might dissolve the gaming problem, since off-topic true claims aren't in the class, but it seems to buy that at the price of scores that don't transfer between tasks or datasets.

3. Probabilist route. Stop targeting truth and use proper scoring rules / expected accuracy over whatever commitments the answer can be read as making. Mathematically well-behaved, cheap to implement, but I worry it quietly assumes every answer is a probability distribution, which long narrative answers are not.

4. Explanation route. Grade the causal or explanatory skeleton seperately from the surface details, on the theory that what human graders are actually rewarding is getting the mechanism right while tolerating wrong specifics. Needs a notion of a correct explanation that we don't have and that nobody else has either, as far as I can tell.

5. Impossibility route. Spend the first year trying to prove an Arrow-style theorem: any ordering over answers that satisfies a short list of plausible constraints (adding a true claim never makes an answer worse, distinct falsehoods can be non-trivially ranked, scores are invariant to how you paraphrase, whatever else you'd add) either collapses or becomes vacuous. No benchmark comes out of it, but it might save two years of flailing.

6. Data-first route. Collect a few thousand human pairwise judgments of which of two answers is closer to the truth, then see which candidate measure predicts them, and let the data pick the concept. My logician's objection is that this just measures which answers people find persuasive, which is a different construct and probably a worse one.

Which two of these would you fund, in what order, and why? I care much more about the reasoning than the list: for each, how much of the other work depends on how it turns out; whether it's tractable for the three of us in year one without any new data collection; and whether settling it would actually change the thing we ship.
