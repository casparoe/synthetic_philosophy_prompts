Third year, metaphysics, dissertation on essentialism, and this week our reading group tried to reconstruct the standard necessity-of-origin argument for the human case -- the one people invoke when they want to back up Kripke's intuition about the table that couldn't have been made from a different block of wood. The goal was to show that you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones you did. What we got instead seems to show that nothing upstream of anybody could ever have been otherwise, and I've spent three days failing to find where it broke.

Fix a case: sperm S, egg E, zygote Z.

(1) Z came from S and E; that fusion is Z's origin.
(2) Origin is essential to a zygote: necessarily, if Z exists, Z came from S and E. There's nothing more to being that zygote than being the product of that particular fusion -- no further fact about it that could survive a swap of gametes.
(3) I am that organism -- Z grown up -- and identity, once it holds, holds necessarily. So necessarily, if I exist, I'm Z.
(4) Hence necessarily, if I exist, I came from S and E. That's the thesis we were after.
(5) But S and E are individuals too, with origins of their own: S is essentially the product of the particular meiotic event that made it, and E likewise. So necessarily, if S exists, that event occurred; same for E.
(6) And what makes any of those events, or their sources, the ones they are is where they came from -- not just the immediate source, but the particular history that produced it. Nothing else could make something that one. So iterating: necessarily, if I exist, every link in my ancestry had the origin it actually had.
(7) Which means the whole list, taken together, pins down everything upstream of me: necessarily, if I exist, the entire actual history of the world up to my conception happened exactly as it did.
(8) I do exist. So that history is necessary. And running the same argument for anyone else gives: every world containing anyone shares our actual pre-conception past.
(9) That's plainly false. My father could have caught a later train and I'd have been conceived anyway, half an hour later; the weather that week could have been different; the coffee could have been tea.
(10) So one of these is false, and the group's verdict was that (2) is the one to go -- the exact opposite of what we set out to show.

I don't accept the verdict and I also can't refute it. So: which single step would you reject? I want to know why that step reads fine on a first pass, not just that it's false -- what makes it look innocuous is the part I care about. And price the other candidates, including just eating (9) and insisting the pre-conception past is fixed. Also: is (3) doing real work, or does the same thing go through if I'm a person who's only contingently the organism I seem to be?

Three wrinkles I couldn't fit in and suspect are load-bearing. (a) The argument assumes there's a determinate answer to "which gametes did Z come from." For a kid born after mitochrondrial replacement -- nuclear DNA from one egg and a sperm, mitochondria from a second donor egg, and in the pronuclear-transfer version the thing put back in the uterus isn't the zygote that got fertilized -- that looks shaky. Which premise even has a subject there? (b) Suppose the clinic splits an early embryo and both halves implant: now two people each came from S and E. Does that break (2), (3), or neither? (c) Kripke's own version of the thesis is tolerant -- what he rules out is coming from a completely different gamete pair, not coming from one that differs in some tiny respect -- and somewhere in our reconstruction the tolerance clause evaporated without anybody noticing. Does putting it back rescue the argument, or does whatever goes wrong go wrong regardless of how big the variation is?

Are (a)-(c) the same bug as the main one or separate ones, and do they make dropping (2) cheaper or dearer? I'd rather keep (2) and (3) than anything else on that list, but tell me what I'm paying.
