I run a little "philosophy for people with day jobs" evening series at a research institute — mostly postdocs in condensed matter, a couple of molecular biologists, one very opinionated statistician — and next month's session is on scientific realism and the pessimistic meta-induction. I want to hand everyone a one-page sorting instrument at the start of the night: a decision tree where each node is a question they can answer honestly without knowing any of the jargon, and each leaf is a named position (constructive empiricism, entity realism, selective or preservative realism, epistemic and ontic structural realism, some flavour of instrumentalism, something like the unconceived-alternatives line — you tell me which of these actually earn a leaf and which don't). Roughly 8 to 10 nodes and no more than maybe 7 leaves; it has to fit on a single sheet and be walkable in ten minutes with a pen.

Two constraints matter more than the size. First, no node may be a question whose answer they've already pre-committed to. My first draft opened with "do you think electrons are real?" and everyone in the pilot said yes instantly, including two people who turned out to hold completely different views about theory change, so the node sorted nobody. I want the discriminating questions to be concrete cases, and I'd rather they come out of the participants' own turf than out of the usual 19th-century museum peices, though use the museum pieces wherever they're genuinely sharper. So things like: if you're a realist about quasiparticles, are phonons and holes real in the same way electrons are, or is that a category confusion? When someone says a quasihole in a fractional quantum Hall system carries charge e/3, is that a claim about the world or bookkeeping inside a model that works? What does a biologist concede who admits "the gene" picks out different things in Mendelian, molecular and developmental contexts? Does a theory that gets the reference of some of its terms badly wrong but preserves the mathematical structure — the old ether-and-partial-drag story, or renormalization-group results that survive a complete change of fundamental picture — count as approximately true, or as true-about-the-wrong-things, or neither? Second, build it from the logical space first and the labels second: I want to see the answer-combinations your questions make available, then which combinations actual defended positions occupy, then the empty cells with a one-line diagnosis of why each is empty — incoherent, unappealing, or just nobody's thought to sit there. And flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree can't separate, saying which extra question would separate them even if it won't fit on the page.

One epistemic ask: please mark, node by node and in the leaf descriptions, which claims are settled among people who actually work on this and which are live disagreements, and don't dress up a contested reading — about what "approximate truth" is even supposed to mean, or about whether the meta-induction is a good argument at all — as though it were the default. If a node only makes sense under one contested account of the debate, put that in a footnote on the handout rather than burying it.
