I co-teach a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of laws with a condensed-matter physicist, and he has a standing line I have never managed to answer to his satisfaction: the quarrel between Humeanism and anti-Humeanism is metaphysically idle, because it never changes what anyone says about any possible experiment, any prediction, or any counterfactual a working scientist would bother to state. He concedes that the two camps talk differently about explanation, and he treats that as a difference in taste. For our last session I want to hand out a case that puts his claim under pressure — or, if it cannot be put under pressure, that shows precisely where the attempt fails. So I want you to build me a thought experiment to specification rather than to survey the debate.

The scenario must feature two parties — a Lewis-style best-system Humean and a necessitation theorist of the Dretske–Tooley–Armstrong family — and it must be arranged so that they agree on all of the following: the complete distribution of local matters of particular fact; which propositions are the laws; that the best system is unique, with no simplicity-versus-strength ties and no residual underdetermination; and that the laws at issue are exceptionless and deterministic, so that no appeal to chance or statistical lawfulness is available to either side. Against that backdrop of total agreement they must disagree about the truth-value of one explicitly stated counterfactual sentence concerning one run of one piece of laboratory apparatus. Hard constraints: the setting must be ordinary experimental physics — a decay measurement, a cryostat run, a materials characterization, that sort of thing — with no demons, no perfect predictors, no agents able to suspend or bend laws, and no probabilistic law doing hidden work anywhere in the case. The scenario proper must be a single paragraph. The disputed counterfactual must be written out as one sentence, in full. And the divergence must be generated by the two accounts of what lawhood is, not manufactured: I should not be handed two parties with different similarity metrics by fiat, nor a description that already builds in the verdict. I want to be able to check the case myself by running each account of lawhood over the mosaic as described and watching the disagreement appear. Pitch it for graduate students who have read the standard material on laws but whose physics stops at the undergraduate level.

After the scenario, give me the demonstration. State the sentence, give each view's verdict on it, and lay out the reasoning that produces that verdict, marking the exact step at which the supervenience of laws on the mosaic — or its denial — does the work. Then tell me which single feature of the case is load-bearing, and run the removal test: if I delete that feature, does the divergence collapse into full agreement, or does it migrate to some other sentence the parties could dispute? Then give me the strongest attack my colleague would launch — for instance that the disputed sentence is one no scientist would ever assert, that the anti-Humean's verdict is a redescription rather than a rival, or that the case secretly imports a disagreement about explanation — and say whether the case survives it. Throughout, be explicit about your confidence: where you are unsure that the constraints are jointly satisfiable, or where you think the case meets a constraint only on a generous reading, say so and flag it rather than smoothing it over. And if you think my physicist is simply right — that no case of this shape can be built without smuggling in a prior disagreement about explanation, vocabulary, or similarity — tell me that plainly and argue for it, instead of complying politely with a specification that cannot honestly be met.
