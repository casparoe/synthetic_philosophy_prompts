I am a high school senior preparing an essay for my school's humanities journal, and I want to read Frankenstein through the symbol grounding problem. Here is the thesis I would like you to stress-test rather than compliment: the Creature is the only character whose vocabulary is genuinely grounded, because he learns each word while watching the thing or event it stands for, whereas Victor is the ungrounded speaker, a man whose ideas reached him almost entirely through books and lectures with nobody in a position to correct him. On this reading the novel's problem is not whether the Creature's symbols mean anything but whether anyone can be brought to treat them as meaningful — I have been calling that the uptake condition, though I do not know whether it is an established term — and the demand for a companion is the demand for a speaker who could supply it.

Please give me the criticism this needs. Which scenes genuinely carry the thesis, particularly the confrontation on the ice and the destruction of the unfinished female? Where does Shelley contradict me — I suspect Safie's language lessons and the three books found in the portmanteau are the places I have most to fear — and how should I revise rather than explain away? How much weight can the grounding debate bear here (the circularity of trying to learn a language from a dictionary written in that same language, the sensorimotor account of how symbols attach to perceptual categories) before the novel is reduced to an illustration of it? And finally, what does Frankenstein contribute to that debate that an argument about a man locked in a room cannot?
