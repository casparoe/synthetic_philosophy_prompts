The students had been assigned the third lecture of Naming and Necessity together with the supplementary section of the Stanford entry on essential and accidental properties that covers the arguments for origin essentialism. The question, as set, is this:

QUESTION (20 marks; 700 words permitted). Kripke asks of Queen Elizabeth II whether she — this very woman — could have been born of different parents, and answers that she could not.

(a) State the thesis Kripke is defending and reconstruct his argument for it, saying what work his account of possible worlds as stipulated rather than discovered is doing in that argument. [8]

(b) It is sometimes objected that this account renders the thesis trivial: if we stipulate, in describing a counterfactual situation, which individual we are speaking of, then a situation in which a woman is born to the Trumans simply is not described as one in which the Queen is born to the Trumans, and nothing substantive has been established. Set out this objection and assess it. [7]

(c) Do cases of monozygotic twinning — one zygote, two developing persons — create a problem for the thesis? Explain. [5]

The submitted answer runs to about 650 words:

(a) The thesis is that origin is essential: necessarily, the Queen originates from the sperm and egg she in fact originated from, so there is no possible situation in which this very woman is born of wholly different parents. Kripke's argument depends on his account of possible worlds. Worlds are not remote countries we peer into through a telescope to see which individuals turn up there under which descriptions; they are stipulated, specified by the descriptions we attach to them. So whether a situation in which a woman is born to the Trumans and grows up in Missouri is a situation in which the Queen is born to the Trumans is not a question for further investigation. It is settled by how we specify the situation, and we specify situations of that kind by origin. The table case runs in parallel: a table made from a completely different block of wood is a different table, however exactly its shape, colour and subsequent history match the original.

Two features deserve emphasis. First, the necessity is de re: it is the woman herself, and not a sentence about her, that could not have had other parents. Second, the thesis is not an independent posit but a corollary of the necessity of identity. Identity statements, where true, are necessary, and there are no contingent identities. Elizabeth is the individual who originated from that particular gamete pair; necessarily, then, she is that individual; and whoever originated from that pair is she. It follows that she could not have originated from any other, and anyone who accepts the necessity of identity is committed to the conclusion. Kripke's own formulation is hedged — a completely different sperm and egg — which shows he is not claiming that an altered nucleotide would have produced someone else. But the hedge raises a tolerance problem, since Kripke never says where the boundary lies, or what principled reason there could be for drawing one there.

(b) The objection is that stipulation empties the thesis out. If we choose, in describing a counterfactual situation, which individual we are speaking of, we can simply decline to call the Truman-born woman the Queen, and the impossibility we arrive at is a fact about our labelling rather than about the world. Its real force is that it leaves the essentialist with no work to do.

The best response is that the objection misconstrues stipulation as arbitrary. Stipulation is answerable to the object stipulated about; we do not get to decide, of any description we please, that it picks out the Queen. On Lewis's alternative there is no transworld identity at all, only counterpart relations, and there the objection takes a different and arguably more damaging form, since what settles which individuals are the Queen's counterparts is overall similarity, which can come apart from origin.

But if that response is right, it changes the status of the thesis. What we are left with is a rule governing the use of the name, not a discovery about the metaphysics of the woman. Understood that way the thesis is immune to counterexample, since no description of a situation can oblige us to apply the name against our own rules of application — but it is also no longer the sort of thing that could have turned out otherwise, and the air of deep metaphysical necessity surrounding it is largely a reflection of how we talk.

(c) Twinning is a genuine problem. In monozygotic twinning one zygote gives rise to two persons with the same gametic origin. Kripke's hedge does not help here, because the twins do not differ slightly in origin: they share it exactly. If sameness of origin were what fixed transworld identity, the two sisters would have to be one individual, which they are not.

The essentialist can retreat to the claim that origin is necessary but not sufficient, and that the twins merely show origin does not by itself determine who is who. The retreat is expensive. A necessary condition on being the Queen that any number of individuals might satisfy does not settle which of them she is, and settling that was the entire point of the thesis. Kripke's own argument, as reconstructed above, needs the move from Elizabeth is the individual who originated from that pair to whoever originated from that pair is Elizabeth; give up that move and what remains is an intuition about hedged cases and no argument. So twinning does not merely complicate the thesis. It removes the reason for holding it.
