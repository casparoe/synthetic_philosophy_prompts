For thirty-six years I directed a laboratory in solid-state chemistry, and since retiring I have been reading in the philosophy of mind with, I confess, the instincts of a person who still expects arguments to terminate in measurements. One line of objection has held my attention. As I have reconstructed it, the reasoning runs: if phenomenal states are causally inert, then the judgment I form and voice when I say that I am in pain is produced entirely by neural events, and the pain itself does no part of the producing; hence the systematic agreement between my judgments and my experiences would be an unexplained coincidence rather than the outcome of any tracking relation. I gather this is what is meant by the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and that it is advanced as an objection to epiphenomenalism rather than as a consequence of it.

A coincidence of that description, however, seems to me to be precisely the sort of thing that shows up in the data. If nothing binds the report to the experience, then on some fraction of trials the two must come apart, and the epiphenomenalist would appear to have no resources for explaining why that fraction is small. I should therefore like to design the experiment, and I would be grateful for guidance on two points.

First, what would serve as an independent measure of the experience itself, against which the verbal report could be compared? I assume one would fall back on a neural signal, but the choice of signal presumably determines the answer, and I do not yet see how one escapes that circle.

Second, and more practically: since introspection must have some finite error rate, much as an instrument does, could it not be calibrated in the ordinary way — a noise floor established under conditions where the quantity of interest is known by construction, as we once used a diffraction standard — and the residual divergence between the calibrated report and the independently measured neural state then compared against what rival theories of mind would predict? If the paradox is, as it appears to be, a quantitative claim about the improbability of agreement, it ought to admit of a quantitative test. Which paradigm would offer the most sensitive one, and what order of magnitude of divergence would constitute confirmation rather than mere measurement noise?
