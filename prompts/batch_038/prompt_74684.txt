I left school at sixteen and have spent thirty-odd years running a small builders' merchant, so I am no scholar, and I would ask you to keep that in mind about the register I want. Two winters ago I bought a second-hand copy of Rousseau's long letter to d'Alembert about the theatre — the one he wrote because a Paris encyclopaedist had proposed that Geneva would be improved by allowing a playhouse — and I have been arguing with it ever since. The difficulty now is that Rousseau supplies both my argument and the objection to it, and I have to give the opening address at the centenary of our village amateur dramatic society.

The occasion: the last Saturday in June, at dusk, on the recreation ground, where we are performing our hundredth pantomime in the open, free of charge, off a generator, with a microphone that cuts out in the wind. I have six minutes.

The audience, in rough numbers: three or four hundred villagers of every age, a third of them children, most of whom have never sat in a theatre; the parish priest, who objected last November to our pantomime as frivolous and to acting as a variety of lying, and who is sincere rather than narrow; an officer from the county arts council who holds the decision on our three-year grant, thinks in terms of reach and audience development, and has twice suggested we would benefit from hosting professional touring companies; and a supply teacher who read philosophy at university and will notice at once if I mangle Rousseau.

What I want the address to argue: ours is not the thing he objected to. He objected to the building, the paid seats, the standing company of professionals whose trade is to feign and to sell themselves, and the audience that sits in the dark being improved at. In our pantomime the cast is drawn from the audience and goes back to it; the script is rebuilt each year out of what actually happened here — the flood, the shut shop, the footbridge nobody will repair — with real neighbours named and played by their own friends; the crowd is expected to hiss the villain, sing the choruses and shout corrections at the Dame; and next year the person who hissed plays the villain. There is no wall there to object to. I have taken to calling it a looking-glass show, because the people on the platform are the people on the benches and they swap. Use that phrase if it earns its place; cut it if it does not.

Where I want to be honest — and this is the part I most need you to get right. I will not claim Rousseau would have approved of us, because two of his points land. He says comedy makes the plain honest man ridiculous and the smooth compromiser lovable, and our Dame routine has done exactly that for years to a parish councillor I respect. He also says an amusement that eats the hours owed to work and to public business is a bad bargain, and this society has eaten more of my hours than I can defend. The speech must carry both, answer them or live with them, and still be warm and funny. It must not mock the priest and must not insult the funder, but it must say plainly and pleasantly why we will not turn into a hall that touring professionals visit while the village buys tickets to watch — without sounding ungrateful for the money.

Finally, the address should do what it argues for. Build in two, and no more than three, moments where the crowd actually performs — a collective hiss, a sung line, getting everyone to their feet for the woman who saved the hall in 1994 — that four hundred people including children and complete strangers can join in without embarrassment or prior knowledge.

Please write the speech. Then, in two or three sentences kept separate from it, tell me where you think the argument is weakest, so that I can cut that part before I stand up.
