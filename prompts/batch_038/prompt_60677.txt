The game. Toss a fair coin until the first head appears. If the first head lands on toss k, the ticket pays 2^k dollars. Each branch has probability 2^-k, so the expected payout is the sum of 2^-k · 2^k, which diverges. I simulated ten million runs; the running average hovered in the low tens of dollars and kept creeping upward. Set that aside — I'm interested in the argument, not the simulation.

The argument.

(1) Rational choice is maximization of expected utility, where utility is a function of total wealth.
(2) Suppose an agent's utility function is unbounded in money. Define a game G\* that pays, on the "first head at toss k" branch, the amount a_k chosen so that the utility of a_k is exactly 2^k utils. That amount exists, since the function is unbounded. G\*'s expected utility is the sum of 2^-k · 2^k, which diverges. So by (1) this agent ought to pay any finite price for a ticket to G\*.
(3) Suppose instead the agent's utility function is bounded above by M — the usual proposed cure, since bounded terms make the series converge. But the ordinary game pays 2^k, so as k grows, the utility of the resulting wealth climbs toward M. The branch probabilities sum to 1 and every branch's utility is heading for the ceiling, so the expected utility of an ordinary ticket is approximately M. This agent ought to pay very nearly their entire net worth for one ticket.
(4) Either way, expected utility maximization sets an absurd price.
(5) A decision theory that tells you to trade your house for a coin-tossing ticket is one no agent can follow, and a requirement no agent can meet is not a requirement of rationality.
(6) So (1) is false — and note that nothing in the argument leaned on repeated play, ruin, insurance, or attitudes to risk beyond what (1) already builds in.
