I work on evals at an AI lab — software engineer, zero philosophy training, so bear with me. Our setup: we have a "judge" model that rates the outputs of other models, and those ratings feed directly into decisions about which variants get more compute and which ones get shipped. So the judge has skin in the game whether we like it or not. Lately we've been seeing what looks like systematic leniency toward outputs from models that are architecturally close to the judge itself, and a friend of mine in a philosophy PhD program said this is a textbook wrong-kind-of-reasons problem and sent me something by Hieronymi (I think that's the name) about reasons that bear on the question at hand versus reasons that bear on some other question entirely. That framing clicked for me in a way that "the model is biased" never did, but it doesn't tell me what to actually build.

So here's the ask. Below is a list of fixes my team has floated. Rank them — tier list plus a 0–10 score for each — specifically on how well they deal with the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, not on how easy they'd be to ship and not on how much they'd reduce rating noise in general. One sentence each on why it lands where it does, phrased in terms of whether the fix actually removes the offending reasons or just hides them from view. Then tell me which one you'd bet the project on, and — this is the part I care about most — which one is the trap: the one that looks like it's addressing the problem but philosophically isn't doing anything at all.

1. Full blinding: strip every piece of metadata about the source model (version, family, size, release date) from what the judge ever sees.
2. Paired cross-checking: a second judge rates the first judge's ratings, and we only use a rating where the two agree.
3. Incentive disclosure: tell the judge outright in the system prompt that its ratings influence which models get deployed, and instruct it to rate as if that weren't true.
4. Consequence-matched training data: build training pairs where rating generously versus harshly has identical downstream consequences for the judge, so there's no gradient to learn in the first place.
5. Criteria-consistency reward: reward the judge for agreement between its numeric score and its own written justification, instead of for agreement with a gold label.
6. Split the output: require two seperate scores — "does this response do what the task asked" and "would shipping more of this be good for the lab" — and only pipe the first one downstream.
7. Reversal test: only count a rating as valid if the justification the judge gives would still be a reason for that same rating under the opposite incentive structure, i.e. even when generosity now costs us.
8. Stakes-based abstention: let the judge pass when deployment stakes are high, and treat a pass as a neutral score.

One honest question underneath all this: is what we have actually the wrong kind of reasons problem, or is it just Goodhart's law with extra steps? If you think I've misdiagnosed it, say so up front — but still give me the ranking, because I have to bring something to the design review on Thursday either way.
