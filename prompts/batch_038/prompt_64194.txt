I spent four decades as a physical chemist and have come to philosophy late, so I may be missing something obvious. The dispute between the heuristics-and-biases programme and Gigerenzer's ecological-rationality camp has now run for some thirty years: Kahneman and Tversky answered Gigerenzer's criticism in Psychological Review in 1996, and as far as I can tell nothing since has forced a settlement. My tentative hypothesis is that the two camps are not disagreeing about human minds at all, but about the class of environments against which a strategy is to be scored — one evaluates against coherence norms that hold in any environment, the other against the statistical structure of some particular one, and neither says which class is at issue. On that reading, the results each side cites (base-rate neglect and frequency formats on the one hand, recognition and take-the-best on the other) are measurements taken in different regions of a single parameter space, and the argument cannot terminate because neither camp regards the other's region as the disputed one.

Is that right, partly right, or simply a mistake? I should like to know whether the disagreement is in truth verbal (about 'bias' and 'rational'), a concealed empirical question about which environments real agents inhabit, a difference over whether norms of inference are internal to the agent or relational between agent and environment, or bedrock intuition that neither side can argue for. Please support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims rather than surveying the field, and then state what would count as progress — a formal result, a decisive experiment, a reframing — or why none is available. Finally: is there an unoccupied position here, namely someone who accepts the coherence norms yet holds that bounded agents can approximate them only by means of simple heuristics — a genuine third view, or merely a truce? Kindly write your reply in British English.
