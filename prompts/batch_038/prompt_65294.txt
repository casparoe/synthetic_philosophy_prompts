I want my students to run headlong into a structural failure in intellectual property before anyone gives it a name. The setting: an upper-division seminar on the philosophy and economics of innovation, 24 students, roughly half philosophy and half economics, one 105-minute weekly session. They have all read some Locke on property and some standard material on the incentive rationale for patents; none of them have seen a formal model of complementary goods, and none are comfortable with more arithmetic than a payoff table requires.

The insight I want them to leave with is this: when the components needed to produce something valuable are owned separately, pricing that is individually rational for each owner can destroy the market for the combined product, and no owner can repair the damage by acting alone. Please design a self-contained in-class game that delivers that experience through its rules and payoffs rather than through anything I say. Withhold the technical labels until the debrief — including the standard names for the complements mechanism, for the anticommons, and for a thicket. I want them to receive those words only after they have hit the wall, and I want the first label to be one they have to be told, because I expect them to have generated the phenomenon without knowing it had a name.

Requirements:

1. Three regimes, run in sequence with the same cohort: an open regime with no exclusive rights; a fragmented regime in which every component is separately owned and separately priced; and a coordinated regime — a pool, a blanket cross-license, or a fixed compulsory rate, your choice, with your justification. Each regime should yield a different total output and a different distribution of payoffs. The middle regime should lose on both dimensions relative to at least one of the others, so that nobody walks away with a one-sided moral about property rights.

2. The open regime must impose a real cost, not merely serve as a baseline: non-disclosure, duplicated effort, or degraded component quality, whichever mechanism you prefer. Strong property should look attractive to them before it looks catastrophic.

3. Payoffs in points or tokens only. Give me a demand schedule simple enough to evaluate at a table in under a minute, and work the arithmetic through for at least one illustrative round so I can verify that the incentives point where you intend. State explicitly whether the fragmented regime's equilibrium is a deadlock at zero trade or a positive but inefficiently small volume, and calibrate the numbers so that the outcome you intend is the one a table of undergraduates will actually land on rather than the one a textbook assumes.

4. Roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and clear rules on the three features that matter most to me: whether downstream improvers can design around a blocking claim, whether owners may bargain with one another or only with buyers, and whether two owners can hold overlapping claims to the same component. At least one student should occupy the position of an improver blocked by a component they did not want and could not avoid.

5. A debrief that maps identifiable moments from the run onto the concepts, and that surfaces the free-rider problem among the owners themselves — why a unilateral price cut by one owner mostly benefits the others, and why that makes voluntary pooling difficult even when pooling would make everyone better off.

6. Failure modes. What will go wrong the first time I run this with 24 people and a single facilitator, and what is your fallback in each case? In particular, tell me what happens if students price generously out of fairness or group solidarity instead of self-interest, and whether the session still teaches anything under that contingency.

7. Limits. Say plainly which feature of the real property system the game cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in words. I suspect the candidates include the multi-decade horizon over which research investment actually responds to incentives, the contested and probabilistic character of real rights, and the fact that in the game the components already exist instead of having been called into being by the prospect of owning them. Tell me which of these genuinely bite, and name anything I have overlooked.

Throughout, be as critical of your own design as you can manage. Identify the single assumption in the payoff structure on which the whole lesson rests, say what you would watch for during the run as evidence that the game has installed a misleading intuition rather than the intended one, and specify what would change your mind: what consideration, objection, or observed outcome would lead you to revise the mechanism or to abandon the claim that this activity can carry the lesson at all. Please write the entire response in American English.
