Two members of my department's law-and-philosophy group — I'll call them Nadia and Ellis — spent most of a seminar dinner arguing about a proposal now before our city council. The ordinance would make it a criminal offence to sell, and to subscribe to, a commercial service that constructs an interactive simulation of a named deceased person from that person's correspondence, photographs, and voice recordings, and licenses it to a surviving relative. Subscribers may configure the simulation to be more agreeable than the person actually was. Surveys in the city show a substantial majority find the practice repellent; on the other side of the ledger, no one has identified an injured party: the relatives are consenting adults, the vendors are licensed, and the activity is confined to the home. Both disputants accept that the Hart–Devlin debate supplies the right frame, and the argument became about which side of it the ordinance falls on.

Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

1. Nadia: the ordinance is legitimate. Prohibiting indignity to the dead is part of what makes a community's dealings with one another intelligible to itself, and a practice that turns the dead into customizable companions degrades that. The loss falls on the shared morality that holds the community together, and that is a proper object of the criminal law.

2. Ellis: that is the disintegration thesis, and it is an empirical claim for which there is no evidence. Changes in shared morality of far greater magnitude — contraception, no-fault divorce, the decriminalization of homosexual conduct — left societies intact, so the inference from "a shared norm has eroded" to "society is threatened" is unsupported. And what the surveys measure is disgust. Disgust is not a form of insight; a reasonable-person test cashed out in terms of revulsion simply entitles the majority's squeamishness to legislate.

3. Nadia: two mistakes there. Devlin's claim was never that any moral change destroys a society; it was that a society is constituted by its morality, so the harm at issue is a change in identity rather than collapse — the question is whether we remain the same community, not whether our institutions survive. And you are in no position to invoke the harm principle, since you yourself defend compulsory helmet and seatbelt legislation. Your objection can only be that this particular harm is unproven, not that only bodily injury counts.

4. Ellis: identity claims are unfalsifiable. Anything can be redescribed as "we are no longer who we were," and a standard that cannot be stated in a form capable of failing is no standard at all. Paternalism differs in kind: it protects an agent from a foreseeable injury to himself. The moralist's harm is the persistence of a belief he considers false.

5. Nadia: that asymmetry begs the question, since you count as injury only what you already recognize as injury. And your position proves too much. On your account there could be no legislation preserving anything — a language, a landscape, a historic quarter — because none of these issues in discrete injuries to identified persons, yet you support several such laws.

6. Ellis: those cases differ because they serve the interests of future persons in ways that admit of measurement. If you want to say the same here, you must say that the dead are harmed by their simulations — at which point you have abandoned Devlin for the harm principle and owe an account of posthumous harm. If instead you say the loss falls on the living, you are back to disgust.

Neither budged, and I have been asked to write the exchange up for our next session. Please adjudicate it move by move: rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification of each rating, and identify any move whose force depends on a claim that a later move quietly drops. Then tell me who is more right overall and why, and point out anything both of them are missing — including whether the dispute turns on reading Devlin as a moralist at all rather than as someone whose objection is really about a species of harm, and whether either of them has taken account of the limits Devlin himself places on the enforcement of morals. If adopting that reading would change any of your ratings, say which ones and how.
