I am at work on a novella set aboard a generation ship whose charter is administered by a machine that performs Harsanyi's impartial-observer calculation in earnest: it gathers each passenger's preferences over lotteries, then ranks outcomes as a chooser would who stands behind a veil of ignorance with an equal chance of being any one of them. I want a scene of roughly a thousand words in which the machine is asked to settle a live dispute — whether to vent a contaminated module, killing eleven to save the ship — and simply stops. The stoppage must not come from the familiar difficulty of comparing one person's wellbeing to another's; my draft has leaned on that too long. It must come from a single passenger whose preferences are lexicographic: she will not trade her brother's life for any number of strangers at any odds, so that no single utility representation of her exists and there is nothing for the veil to average. Everything else should behave — give the remaining passengers ordinary, well-formed preferences and let the machine dispose of them without strain, so the contrast is legible. The machine is not malfunctioning and no component has failed; it is doing exactly what the charter asks, and that is the trouble. Write it as a story rather than an argument: the breakdown should land as a human event — a silence in the chamber, a clerk's slow comprehension, a woman learning that her loyalty has been recorded as an impossibility — and it should end without deciding whether she is unreasonable or whether the charter's requirements are. Let no character lecture about welfare economics; the shape of the problem must be recoverable only from what happens, and from what refuses to happen.
