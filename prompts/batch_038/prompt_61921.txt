I audit a reading group on the Groundwork's universal law formula, led by a professor whose verdicts on maxims have been consistent all term. So far: a maxim of promising to repay a loan one knows one will not repay — forbidden; a maxim of staying silent about a known defect in something one is selling, while never saying anything false — forbidden; a maxim of pocketing a cashier's overpayment and saying nothing — forbidden; a maxim of refusing to aid strangers whenever aid would cost one something one actually cares about, while aiding when it costs nothing — permissible, on the ground that the duty of beneficence is wide and that the latitude concerns how much one does rather than whether one does any; a maxim of skipping tonight's practice of an instrument one is cultivating, without abandoning cultivation altogether — permissible.

The maxim on next week's agenda: whenever a question is hard enough that I would probably get it wrong on my own, I will adopt the judgment of the person I trust most and treat it as my own, as a standing policy. No one is deceived, no one is coerced, and the advisor is glad to be asked.

Predict her verdict. First, which readings of the test survive all five precedents — readings keyed to whether universal adoption would destroy the practice the action depends on, to whether the action would still serve the agent's end if everyone acted on it, to ends a rational being necessarily has, to whether those affected could in principle consent, or to whether the agent is claiming an exception for himself? Then say what each surviving reading implies about the new maxim, which of them genuinely diverge on it, which of her five precedents is the closest analog and whether that analogy helps or misleads, and what you would bet on, with your reasons. Finally, name one further maxim — a redescription or a variant case — that would settle which reading she is running.
