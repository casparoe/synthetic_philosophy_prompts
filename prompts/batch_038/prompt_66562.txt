Ok so I'm in a philosophy of cognitive science seminar and my paper draft got torn apart by my TA in a way I genuinely can't answer, and I want a real diagnosis rather than a shrug. Topic is Dennett's four kinds of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian creatures. I was trying to write a friendly internal critique (I'm on Dennett's side basically, selectionist all the way down) but the TA said it either proves way too much or falls apart at one specific step, and he wouldn't tell me which. Here it is cleaned up from the draft:

(1) The taxonomy sorts minds into kinds by what each level adds: Darwinian creatures have their behaviour fixed by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures can have theirs reshaped after birth by reinforcement; Popperian creatures can run candidate actions off-line against an internal model of the world, so the losing options die instead of the creature; Gregorian creatures get most of their competence from outside themselves, from other designers' work — words, notation, maps, external mind tools — which they can then go on to modify.

(2) But all four are the same abstract process: make variants, keep the ones that do well. Genes are variants kept by survival. Emitted behaviours are variants kept by reinforcement. Imagined actions are variants kept by internal evaluation. Cultural tools are variants kept by whether they get taken up and passed along.

(3) So the taxonomy isn't a hierarchy of kinds of mind, it's one process — selection — running over four different sorts of item.

(4) And once you see that, what separates a higher creature from a lower one isn't anything inside the mind, it's what's lying around it and how long it sticks around. A Gregorian creature is a Popperian creature that happends to be born inside a culture with writing in it. A Popperian creature is a Skinnerian creature that happens to have an internal model rich enough to simulate with. A Skinnerian creature is a Darwinian creature that happens to live long enough to get reinforced.

(5) But "born into a culture with writing in it" and "lives long enough to be reinforced" are facts about habitat and lifespan, not facts about the design of a mind.

(6) So the differences the taxonomy tracks are differences in habitat and lifespan, not differences in kind of mind.

(7) Therefore there aren't four kinds of minds. There's one kind — a selectionist kind — plus four common sorts of surroundings.

Two things I can't live with. First, I dont believe (7): there's plainly a difference between a sea slug and me, and (7) either denies it or downgrades it to a difference in scenery, which feels like a cheat. So my paper would be a reductio of the framework I was defending rather than of Dennett. I can't see the step he's pointing at though. If all four levels really are variation-and-selective-retention, what's left over that could make them different kinds of anything?
