I have been reading the debate over whether habitat destruction is, on balance, good or bad for wild animals, and I keep encountering the same disagreement without having a name for it. Two people examine the same cleared forest. The first says: several hundred thousand insects and small rodents died within a year of the clearing, so the event inflicted a huge quantity of suffering. The second says: that forest would have sustained those populations indefinitely, at a high but steady annual death rate from starvation and predation, so removing it terminates a permanent stream of suffering. Both are working from roughly the same figures, and both believe they have won the argument.

The same move recurs elsewhere. In a dispute over a proposed cull, one side counts the animals killed in a single season, the other counts the offspring those animals would have produced every year for a decade, and the two totals are printed side by side in one paragraph as though they were quantities of the same kind.

What I cannot find a term for is the specific confusion in the comparison itself: one party is reporting something that happened, the other is reporting a rate at which something would have kept happening, and the argument never converts between them, never states the horizon over which the rate is meant to run, and yet each side presents its figure as though it were the net total. Is there an established concept for this, in the wild animal welfare literature, in ethics more broadly, or borrowed from demography, systems modelling, or elsewhere?

Please do not stop at a label. I want to know how it relates to the near neighbours I can think of on my own, such as double counting, the discounting of future welfare, and the total versus average dispute in population ethics: which of these is closest, what each of them actually covers, and where the edges of my case fall outside them. If nothing established covers it, say so plainly and propose a name with a definition, together with the cases that should count as instances and the cases that should not.

Lead with a one-sentence verdict, then give the reasoning, and finish with a table using exactly these columns: term; field_of_origin; what_it_captures; where_it_diverges_from_my_case; fit_rating (best, partial, or poor). If no established term fits, make the final row of the table your proposed term, with the same columns filled in.
