I teach a grad seminar on care ethics in the spring and I've decided to do something slightly perverse: the first assignment is a spec, not a reading. Before anyone opens Gilligan or Noddings or Tronto, I want the class holding a job description — what a theory of care has to be able to do to count as adequate, as opposed to which one is true. I'd like you to write that spec, partly because I want to find out whether it can be written at all.

Purpose first, since I think adequacy is relative to it and I dont want a list that quietly assumes the paradigm case everybody starts from. The theory has to be usable on paid, bureaucratic, industrial-scale care. A home health aide on a 15-minute visit schedule with eleven clients. A care home, 90 residents, six staff overnight. A labour market where the grandmother's care in Manchester is bought with the care someone else's children don't get in Manila. A mother and her toddler is where nearly every account in this literature starts, and my spec should be one that doesn't get to start there.

What I want: the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and under each one a line on what a theory that fails it would actually look like — the failure mode teaches students more than the requirement does. Then say explicitly which are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of a particular camp. Explicitly, not implied, because half my class will walk in assuming "it must be relational" is a requirement when it may well be a partisan commitment doing work in disguise.

Make sure the list covers at least these, though don't let them crowd out anything you think is more basic:

- care competently delivered by someone who feels nothing for the recipient — does the theory need a motivational state in the carer, and if not, what is left of the word 'care'?
- care the recipient refuses or resents, and the recipient's verdict on whether the care landed;
- care that is good for the one cared for and slowly ruins the carer, where the theory has to be able to say both things at once without one cancelling the other;
- who ends up doing the care, and whether a theory that raises the value of care thereby loads more of it onto the same people;
- the distinctions it collapses at its peril — practice vs. disposition, need vs. mere want, caring about vs. actually doing it. Add any I've missed.

And then the part I care about most: tell me which subset can't all be satisfied together. My hunch is that responsiveness to this particular person and any demand that care be distributable as a matter of justice pull against each other — that the second has to generalize and compare in order to allocate, which is precisely the move the first is supposed to resist. If you think that's a genuine bind, say which of the two gives way and why; if you think I've cooked it, say that instead. Same for the whole exercise: if asking for a purpose-relative spec here is confused, or if you think the question can't be answered without smuggling in a substantive theory of care, tell me so plainly rather than handing me a tidy list I then have to distrust.

Finally, briefly — three or four sentences each, no more — how the main contenders do against the list: the relational accounts from the early 80s, the political/institutional turn, and whatever you'd nominate as the most serious attempt to make care a theory of justice rather than an ethic. Flag anywhere you're leaning on a contested reading of one of them rather than something the author would sign.

Format: bottom line in your first sentence — your one-line answer to what the hardest requirement on this list is — then the list and the reasoning behind it. No preamble, don't restate my question back at me. Numbered items with the failure mode underneath each is fine.
