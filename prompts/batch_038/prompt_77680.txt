**Question (25 marks)**

> Rosa is a lifeguard on duty. Absorbed in a daydream, she does not throw the life-ring to a swimmer who is struggling in the surf, and the swimmer drowns. It seems plainly true to say that Rosa's failure to throw the ring caused the swimmer's death. Explain why that claim creates a problem for a theory of causation, and evaluate one proposed response to the problem.

**Candidate's answer (written in roughly fifty minutes)**

> The claim that Rosa's failure caused the drowning is troubling because causation looks like a relation between events, and a failure is not an event. Suppose one holds a process account, on which one event causes another only if some conserved quantity passes between them along a continuous spatio-temporal path. There is no such path here. The only continuous process in the story runs from the cramp in the swimmer's leg to the water filling his lungs, and it does not pass through Rosa at all. So the process theorist must either say that our ordinary claim is false, or say that the physiological sequence is the real cause and that Rosa matters only to explanation and to blame. Neither is comfortable: the first makes a great deal of ordinary causal talk systematically untrue, and the second leaves it unclear in what sense the process theorist still means by "cause" what the rest of us mean by it.
>
> The counterfactual account has an easy time of this, and that is one of the strongest arguments for it. On Lewis's classic version, causation is the ancestral of counterfactual dependence, and the counterfactual we need here is true: if Rosa had thrown the ring, the swimmer would have lived. It is true on a non-backtracking reading, which is what we want. We do not have to suppose that the cramp went away in order to evaluate it; we hold the cramp fixed and change only Rosa, and the swimmer lives. So this account delivers the right verdict in a case where the process account delivers the wrong one, and it does so with no extra machinery.
>
> Notice what that does to the ontological worry. The worry was that there is nothing for the omission to be — no event available to stand in the causal relation. But the counterfactual is about the omission, and it is true. And only something that is an event can be the subject of a true counterfactual about what would otherwise have happened. So the omission is an event after all, and the premise the process theorist started from was simply mistaken. The objection is not so much answered as dissolved.
>
> What is left is the problem of profligacy. Once absences are allowed to cause, everyone on the beach who did not throw a ring caused this death, and so did everyone elsewhere in the world who did not fly in and throw one, since exactly the same counterfactual is true of each of them. That cannot be right: a single drowning does not have a million causes. The obvious repair is to insist that a causal claim of this kind is incomplete until a contrast is supplied. What caused the death was Rosa's failure rather than her throwing the ring; and for the tourist in Oslo there is no relevant contrast to supply, which is why we do not say that he caused anything. This concedes that counterfactual dependence is too cheap to be causation all by itself, but it also disposes of the parallel worry about double prevention. Double prevention is only omission at one remove — a failure to prevent something that would have prevented the outcome — so an account that has already made its peace with absences has nothing further to do about it.
>
> The contrastive repair has a further consequence, and I think it is the most important one. Which failure counts as the cause is settled by which failure was called for, and that is a matter of what Rosa owed the swimmer. A sunbather who failed in exactly the same way, at exactly the same moment, did not cause the death, because nothing was expected of her. So causation by omission is not a natural relation at all but a normative one: it holds in virtue of duties and roles rather than in virtue of what happened. The metaphysics of omission turns out to be the ethics of attention.
