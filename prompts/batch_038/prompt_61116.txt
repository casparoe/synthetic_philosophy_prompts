So next month I'm giving a talk at a philosophy of cognitive science colloquium and I need a Q&A prep sheet, because I reliably freeze up when the questions start.

My thesis is that the free energy principle isn't an empirical hypothesis and shouldn't be defended as one. It's a constitutive constraint on what counts as a bounded system persisting through time, and its whole content is that anything with stable internal and external states relative to some factorization will look, from the outside, like it's approximately minimizing a variational bound on surprisal. I call this the blanket budget view: a Markov blanket isn't something you find in nature, it's something you purchase, and the modeler pays for it in predictive accuracy. Calling something an agent is just the record that some blanket has stayed cheap enough to keep paying for. So active inference explains nothing on its own; the explaining is done by the particular blanket you could afford.

Argument sketch, for the objections to aim at: (1) if a proposed blanket doesn't license predictions that beat a blanket-free model of the same data, there's no fact of the matter that it was the right one; (2) persistence plus a workable factorization is precisely the condition under which the bound is tight enough to be useful; (3) so the principle is true of all and only the systems we could have identified anyway, which makes it a definition wearing a lab coat — and that's fine, since definitions can still discipline inquiry.

The room: about half computational neuroscientists and ML people who suspect this is variational Bayes plus control theory with extra vocabulary, and who will want to know what my reading costs emprically; two philosophers of biology who only care whether the framework buys anything a selectionist story doesn't; and one committed defender of the strong reading — the FEP as a unified, testable theory of life and mind — who is friendly but relentless and will keep asking me what could possibly count against my view. Nobody there does psychiatry, but someone will ask about psychiatry anyway.

Give me exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no wrap-up paragraph at the end. Each item: the objection in the vocabulary that audience would actually use (not mine), then my reply in about two sentences, then one sentence saying where that reply is soft or what I'd have to concede. Order them by how much damage they'd do. One of the five should be the question I'm praying nobody askes — the hole in the blanket budget view I don't have an answer to yet — and label it as such.
