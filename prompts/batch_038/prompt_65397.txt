I sit on a small working group advising on the judicial chapter of a draft constitution, and we have stipulated the polity fairly carefully in order to stop the argument from dissolving into generalities: competitive multiparty elections, a free press, a professional judiciary appointed through a depoliticised commission, and a political culture in which officials take rights claims seriously rather than treating them as decoration. The stipulation that generates most of our internal disagreement is the last one — our modellers expect a single party to hold a stable parliamentary majority of roughly 55 per cent for at least two decades, with unusually high party discipline and near-total backbench loyalty. The live question in front of us is therefore not whether the text should contain rights guarantees, but what should happen institutionally when a disciplined majority declines to honour them.

Here is the proposition on which I want your credence, stated as precisely as I can manage it: for a polity matching that description, adopting strong-form review — a constitutional court empowered to invalidate primary legislation, so that any legislative response requires the far harder path of constitutional amendment — would, over the thirty years following adoption, produce better protection of the fundamental rights of unpopular and poorly represented minorities than adopting weak-form review, under which a court may declare legislation incompatible with guaranteed rights but the majority may respond by re-enacting or expressly overriding at ordinary legislative cost (think of the declaration of incompatibility under the UK's Human Rights Act, or the notwithstanding clause in the Canadian Charter). I want a number, not a hedge about institutional context, and then I want the derivation in the open. Please assign a rough probability to each of the following, with a sentence or two on what drives your figure, and add any further term you think belongs — including one I have failed to think of: (i) whether "better protection of rights" has a determinate meaning against which both institutional forms can be scored at all, or whether the metric is contested in a way that quietly decides the question; (ii) whether judges in such a polity enjoy any epistemic or motivational advantage over legislators on the content of rights, given that disagreement about rights may be no less intractable in a courtroom than in a chamber; (iii) the probability that a disciplined 55-per-cent majority would in practice treat an override as nearly costless — because if it would, weak-form review collapses into legislative supremacy in precisely the cases that matter, and if it would not, the two forms converge; (iv) the ex ante effects, that is, the probability that strong-form review changes how legislation is drafted before any case is ever brought, and the probability that weak-form declarations produce genuine reconsideration rather than symbolic response; (v) the probability that a court holding a power of invalidation becomes more rather than less willing to use it for unpopular minorities over three decades, as against drifting into alignment with the coalition that appointed it. Show me explicitly how you combine the terms — whether you are multiplying conditionals, weighting them, or simply averaging — and then give me the bottom line.

My own figure is about 0.6, while two colleagues on the group are at roughly 0.85 and 0.3, and I suspect they are not really disagreeing about the same sub-question, so tell me which of your terms most plausibly accounts for the gap between them. Then tell me where your number is fragile: which single term, if you were out by twenty points on it, would move your bottom line furthest; which term you are least confident about, and whether that is because you are ignorant of how courts actually behave or because the concept itself is doing dubious work; and what would change your mind — what argument, or what description of evidence you could in principle be handed without needing to look anything up, would push you below 0.5 or above 0.9. If you think my party-discipline stipulation is settling the whole question in a way I have not noticed, say so and say how. Please write your answer in British English.
