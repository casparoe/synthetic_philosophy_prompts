I'm on the awards committee of a mid-size professional association for population and development studies — a few hundred members, one annual meeting, and since the late nineties we've handed out a "foresight prize" for a published long-range prediction that turned out right. Seven of us on the committee: three demographers, an economist, two historians, and me, the philosopher they keep around to sound rigorous, which in practice means I get assigned the messy nominations. This year's is going to be a fight at the March meeting, and I was trying to explain my own position to a friend over the holidays and realised I can't actually articulate it, so I need a document to circulate beforehand that does the work properly.

The nominee died in 2011. In 1974 he published a short book arguing that a particular region's population growth would outrun its food supply and produce mass famine by the year 2000, with a stated mechanism: essentially fixed arable land, yield gains that were already flattening, and a fertility decline arriving too late to matter. No famine happened. Yields kept climbing, fertility fell faster than he expected, and by 2000 the region was a net exporter of staple grains. Three things complicate it. First, the association's own rulebook has an extension clause I'd forgotten existed: a nominee's prediction window may be doubled once, provided the extension is published before the original window closes — and he invoked it, in a 1997 journal letter, moving the famine to "2000–2050, and if not famine then a chronic structural deficit in food and water." Second, his former students argue the mechanism-level claims largely held: the aquifer he warned about is now measurably depleted, and the region has been importing virtual water ever since, so on their telling the prediction was right about the binding constraint even though the headline event never arrived. Third, one committee member has circulated a two-page dissent saying the whole thing is textbook rescue — that "right but early" is unfalsifiable because any window can be extended and any target variable swapped after the fact, and that giving the prize would teach our graduate students that a forecaster never has to lose. He keeps invoking Popper, the economist keeps invoking Brier scores, and neither of them has said what they actually mean by falsified here.

What I need is the written opinion I'll put in front of the committee, call it 800 to 1200 words: award, no award, or award under a reclassification — we do have a seperate, lesser "mechanism citation" that nobody has ever used, which may or may not be a cop-out. It has to settle something we can vote on Tuesday, not survey the debate. So: bottom line up front, then the reasoning, and please be scrupulously fair to the dissent, because I think it's the strongest position in the room and I need to see why it still doesn't carry the vote — or, if you conclude it does, say that instead; I'd rather be convinced than comforted. Deal explicitly with the extension clause, since half the committee will treat it as decisive and half as a loophole, and tell me whether pre-registering an extension makes it a legitimate updating or just an ad hoc hypothesis with better paperwork. Deal with whether "the mechanism was right even though the event didn't occur" is a genuine partial hit or a bait-and-switch on the target variable, and with what a forecasting score would even look like for a claim whose window outlasts the careers of the people scoring it — who is supposed to check the box in 2050. And if you can state a general principle for when a long-run prediction is confirmed rather than merely rescued, put it in a form we could drop straight into the rulebook for next year, because this will come up again and I don't want to be having this argument from scratch every March.
