I am in the third year of a doctoral programme in philosophy of mind, and I help convene a fortnightly reading group that has spent this term on the neuroscientific theories of consciousness. Two weeks ago we turned to Bernard Baars's global workspace theory, and our convenor — a senior colleague who is also advising a local AI lab on a model-welfare protocol — has been leaning hard on the theatre metaphor that Baars developed alongside the theory and set out at length in his 1997 book In the Theater of Consciousness: a stage carrying sensory and abstract information, a spotlight of attention picking out whatever is fully conscious, actors standing for the contents of experience, an audience made up of the brain's unconscious processors receiving the broadcast, and invisible stagehands and directors backstage shaping what gets onto the stage. My colleague uses the metaphor to do real argumentative work. He says it shows that global broadcasting is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness, because on this picture there is simply nothing for experience to be over and above a content's being illuminated and made available to the whole system; that Block's 1995 access/phenomenal distinction therefore collapses in the theory's favour; that any architecture with a genuine broadcast mechanism, the lab's included, is thereby a serious candidate for experience; and that Block's later overflow claim — that perceptual consciousness outruns cognitive access — is not merely false but unintelligible, since the theatre picture leaves no room for a conscious item outside the spotlight.

A friend of mine in the group pushed back with what she has started calling the "empty auditorium" objection, and I cannot work out whether it is decisive or merely pedantic. Her point is this: run through Baars's own cast list and every entry is either a piece of information, a mechanism of selection, or an unconscious recipient — the actors are contents, the spotlight is attention, the audience is explicitly composed of processors that are not conscious, the stagehands are more unconscious machinery — and Baars himself insists there is no little self sitting in the theatre watching the show. So nothing in the mapping is a subject to whom the performance appears. On a strict reading, then, the metaphor describes access-consciousness and is silent about phenomenology; the impression that it explains experience comes from a feature of the source domain that the target is not known to possess, namely that we cannot picture a theatre without picturing someone's felt presence at it. Her proposed repair is to go back to the blackboard architectures of classical AI, which Baars credits as the model for the theory — independent specialists posting to and reading from a shared board, with no audience and no house lights at all — and to ask whether that image is a more honest vehicle for the same claim or a worse one. I can feel the force of her objection, but I suspect my colleague has a reply: the whole point of the metaphor was to avoid a Cartesian theatre, so accusing it of lacking a spectator may be nothing more than the hard problem restated in costume.

Could you audit the analogy for me, in British English? First, give the correspondences explicitly — a table would be welcome — for the stage, the spotlight, the actors, the audience, the backstage context systems, and the theatre itself as a bounded public space observable from outside, saying what in the theory each is meant to carry. Second, separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten my colleague's inference from those that do not; I expect someone will observe that real theatres have buildings, tickets and playwrights, and I would like to know why that is beside the point, if it is. Third, identify what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks, and in particular whether the empty-auditorium objection lands against Baars's own stated use of the metaphor or only against a popular gloss of it. Fourth, tell me whether each of my colleague's four conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the metaphor was carrying them all along; whether the blackboard replacement is better, worse, or merely evasive in a different way; and, if a better analogy for the theory exists, what it is and what work it can and cannot do. Finally, say how much of the theory's credibility depends on the metaphor at all, as against the empirical findings about ignition, non-linear amplification and long-range cortical broadcasting that its defenders also cite — I want to know whether the image is load-bearing or merely presentational. Please be explicit about how confident you are in your central claims, single out the point you are least sure of, and flag anywhere you think I have mischaracterised Baars or Block.
