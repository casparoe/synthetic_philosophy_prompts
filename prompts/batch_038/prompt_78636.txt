Please compose the piece described below, and write it in British English throughout — spelling, punctuation and idiom included.

**The task**

Write a philosophical dialogue of between 1,600 and 2,000 words between two equally skilled philosophers of mind who disagree about whether dreams are experiences. Neither may be made to look foolish, and neither may be allowed to win: the reader should finish genuinely uncertain which of them is right, and clearer than at the start about what exactly turns on the answer.

**The characters and their positions**

The scene is the observation room of a university sleep laboratory, a little after four in the morning, during a run of nights in which both philosophers have been present as observers.

*Dr Vera Lindqvist* defends the sceptical line descended from Norman Malcolm's mid-century argument, which you should present in your own paraphrase rather than as a quotation. Her view has three parts, and she should be made to hold all three distinctly: that a dream is not an experience undergone by a sleeping person, so there is no episode within the sleep of which the waking narrative is a faithful or unfaithful trace; that the only criterion for the claim that someone dreamt thus-and-so is what they say on waking — the impression of having dreamt; and that the supposition that something further went on unreported is not a false hypothesis but an empty one, since nothing could count for or against it. She should also be made to distinguish her conceptual argument from the mechanistic rival on which a dream narrative is composed and delivered at the instant of waking, and to explain why she thinks that rival concedes too much to the other side, since it still posits an event with a time.

*Dr Marcus Oyelaran* defends the experientialist view: that dreaming is a genuine conscious state occurring during sleep, with a subject who is, at the time, in the middle of it; that the waking report is fallible evidence for that state rather than what makes claims about it true; and that the correlation of dream reports with particular sleep stages is a correlation between a measurement and a thing measured, not between two ways of describing one thing. He should concede, early and without prompting, that all of his evidence is third-personal and that he has no private criterion to offer.

**The case they are arguing about**

Treat the following as stipulated fact for the purposes of the dialogue. Do not correct it, hedge it, or attribute it to any real laboratory, researcher or publication.

The subject, R, is a trained lucid dreamer. The agreed signal is three deliberate horizontal eye movements, left–right–left, to be produced from within the dream once R notices that she is dreaming.

- Night 4: the signal appears at 03:12 during verified rapid eye movement sleep. Roused at 03:20, R reports a vivid dream in which she wiped a fogged window with three strokes of the hand, and says she knew at the time that she was dreaming. Trace and report align.
- Night 11: the identical triple signal appears at 02:47 during verified rapid eye movement sleep. Roused at 02:55, R reports nothing whatsoever: she says she was not dreaming and has no impression of having been. Forty minutes later, while completing a questionnaire, she stops and spontaneously produces a long narrative which she says belongs to the earlier period. It contains a tune, which she hums, and a surname. The tune is one the technician had been listening to on headphones in the adjoining control room during the first hour of that night. The surname belongs to a member of staff R has never met and was not told about.
- Night 18: R reports a long, vivid dream on waking, but no signal was emitted at any point in the night. Afterwards, fully awake, she is shown her own recording and asked what she was doing at 04:03, when a burst of rapid eye movements occurred — a period her original report had said nothing about. She confidently describes herself at that moment as brushing hair out of her eyes with three quick strokes, a pattern matching the trace and a detail absent from the report she gave ten minutes earlier.

**What the dialogue must do**

1. Treat Night 11 as the central crux, and have both philosophers return to it at least three times, each time extracting something from it that the other had not anticipated.
2. Include the following device. Before the run began, each of them wrote on a card the result that would count against their own view. Comparing cards, they discover that both nominated Night 11 — but that Vera's card says the case would refute her if the delayed narrative were true *of something*, while Marcus's card says it would refute him if the narrative could be shown to have been composed entirely in the interval between waking and speaking. They must then try, on the spot, to write a single shared card that both would accept in advance as decisive. Show that attempt being made rather than merely announced, and let it either succeed or fail for reasons the reader can see.
3. Have the experientialist press the problem of surprise: if the narrative is built at or after waking, why is it built so as to astonish its own author, as Night 18's brushed hair suggests? The sceptic must give a real answer, not a deflection, and the answer must itself become something the other can use.
4. Have the sceptic press the problem of idleness: what explanatory work is done by positing an experience during sleep over and above the reports, the sleep stages and the environmental traces — and what, on the experientialist's account, is the sleeper's experience *of* when nobody is in any position to notice it? The reply must not simply appeal to the analogy with forgotten waking experiences without addressing the disanalogy that during dreamless sleep there may have been nothing to forget.
5. Include an explicit attempt to determine whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal: the two must search for a single sentence whose truth-value their views genuinely assign differently, and the reader must be able to judge whether they find one.
6. Each must take up a distinction introduced by the other and turn it against its author, at least once apiece. Each must also make one substantive concession — a point on which they say plainly that the other is right and that they have been overstating their case.
7. End without a verdict or a synthesis. The final two turns should consist of each philosopher stating, in one or two sentences, the precise proposition they believe the other cannot coherently accept. Neither replies to the other's statement.

**Form**

Named speakers only, with a short scene-setting paragraph at the head and at most a handful of brief italicised stage directions. No summarising narrator, no reflective coda, no headings within the dialogue. No bibliography, no citations, no references to real studies, and no invented ones either: the only source material available to the characters is the three nights described above and their own reasoning. Do not preface the dialogue with an explanation of your approach, and do not append commentary after it.
