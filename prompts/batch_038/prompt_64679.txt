ok so this turned into like a three week argument at my school and I was the only person keeping notes, so I'm dumping the whole thing on you. It started in psych class. We watched a clip about the famous amnesia patient, the guy who had his hippocampus removed and couldnt form new memories, being asked to trace a star while only seeing it in a mirror. He got visibly better each day, but every single day he was suprised to be doing the task and had no idea he'd ever practiced it.

After class my friend Dev and our debate captain Nina got into it, and it never really stopped.

Dev's opener: the patient obviously knows how to trace the star in a mirror. He also obviously doesn't know that he can, or that he's ever practiced, and he couldn't state one rule for doing it. So knowing how just isn't a species of knowing that — if it were, he'd have to have some fact in his head, and he can't hold onto new facts at all. He said he'd read this is Ryle's old point about abilities.

Nina: bad inference. There's a view — she said it's from two philosophers named Stanley and Williamson — that know-how is knowing-that held under a "practical mode of presentation," where you don't have to be able to report or even recall the fact. On that view the patient does have propositional knowledge, just stored in a form he can't consciously access. So the amnesia case doesn't refute intellectualism, it's literally what intellectualism predicts.

Dev: then the view says nothing. "Practical mode of presentation" is a sticker you put on whatever ability someone happens to have — for anything anyone can do you can cook up some proposition they supposedly know, so the theory comes out true in every possible world. Plus the regress: if knowing how to ride a bike were knowing that you should lean into the turn, you'd need to know how to apply that fact, which needs another fact, forever.

Nina: the regress is a strawman, nobody said you contemplate a rule before acting, the intellectualist says the knowledge gets exercised, not consulted. And your unfalsifiability charge bounces straight back. On Ryle's account know-how is a bundle of dispositions, and when a case breaks it — the pianist who plays perfectly alone but falls apart in front of an audience — you just add another clause about "normal conditions." Both views absorb everything.

Dev: no, there's an asymmetry, because ability comes apart from know-that in both directions and Ryle's view predicts exactly that. Case A: our chess team has a freshman who beats everyone, and when you ask why she made a move she either shrugs or invents an explanation that's usually wrong — one of the coaches checked and like half her post-hoc reasons were nonsense. She knows how to play, and the propositions she'd endorse aren't even true. Case B: a senior on the same team has memorized a mountain of opening theory, can recite true chess facts all day, and loses to the freshman every time. He doesn't know how. Intellectualism has to say the freshman is missing the right mode of presentation and the senior has it, which is just restating the ability facts with fancier words.

Nina: hold on, if know-how is just ability your account is in trouble too. Say the senior gets mono and plays like a beginner for a month — are you really going to say he stopped knowing how and then relearned it? And here's one your view handles worse than mine: a pianist paralyzed from the wrist down. Zero ability. Do you want to say she no longer knows how to play the piano? Most people would say she still knows how, she just can't. So ability and know-how come apart in the other direction too, which is my point.

Dev: fine, maybe "knows how" comes in degrees or can be suspended. That's cheaper than positing a mystery storage format.

Nina: and if know-how is nothing but a reliable disposition, then a thermostat knows how to keep a room at 70 degrees and Stockfish knows how to play chess, and there's nothing left separating the freshman from a machine doing what it's wired to do. Either bite that bullet or admit abilities have to have something representational inside them.

Dev: I'll half-bite it. Maybe a self driving car "knows how" to change lanes in some watered down sense. But that's the problem — stretch the phrase that far and it stops picking out the thing we actually care about, which is the difference between the kid who can play and the kid who read about playing.
