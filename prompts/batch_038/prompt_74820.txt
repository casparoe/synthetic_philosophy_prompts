Last night I got into a two hour argument with my roommate about the seperate persons thing and I'm still not sure who won, and now I have to teach it. Context: I'm TAing intro ethics, 24 students, one 75-minute discussion section on Thursday in a basement room with a whiteboard and no projector. He's an econ PhD student, completely committed to summing, and his line was that the objection is empty — that any time you weigh a policy that helps some people against one that hurts others you're already doing the exact thing Rawls is complaining about, and that there's no unified self doing the compensating inside a single life either, so giving up your Tuesday for your Thursday is just one person-stage getting screwed over by another and nobody finds that mysterious. I couldn't answer him at 1am and I don't want to just play his argument back at my students, but I also don't want to hand them a version where the objection obviously wins, because the three econ majors in the section will smell a rigged game inside ten minutes.

So: design me an activity they can actually play that makes them run into the asymmetry between intrapersonal and interpersonal tradeoffs on their own, before anyone names the objection out loud. No lecture up front, nothing to read beforehand, they shouldn't be able to guess what I'm up to. My half-baked idea is to run the identical payoff structure twice, once framed as one person deciding across their own life and once as a village deciding across its members, and see if their choices diverge — but that is way too on the nose, they'll figure out the point in round two, so either fix it or throw it out and build something better. Whatever you give me needs rules and roles, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in 75 including the debrief, a scoring scheme built on points or the leftover box of department halloween candy rather than real money, and it has to keep all 24 people doing something instead of watching. I'd also really like a second act that turns my roommate's argument around on them: at some point the single life should turn out to have been played by three different students as successive stages, each keeping a private score and forbidden from talking to the others, and the room has to reckon with whether their gut reaction to the within-a-life case survives learning that. If you can rig a mirror-image reveal for the village case going the other direction, even better.

Then the debrief. Map particular moments from the play onto pieces of the objection — where the summing felt fine, where it felt like someone was being used, and whether what actually bothered them was that nobody got compensated or that the person who lost never got a say — and give me the three or four questions to ask in order, with the answers I should expect and what to do if the class just shrugs and sides with my roommate.
