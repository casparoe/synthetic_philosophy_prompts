I co-edit a small-press anthology of hard science fiction about decisions made when the other party cannot be reached, and I have hit a wall on the one slot I had reserved for the section on bargaining across a causal gap: my commissioned author dropped out, and rather than fill the hole with an essay I would like to try generating the piece properly. For this task you are a literary science-fiction writer who has also spent years working in formal decision theory and AI safety — someone in the better tradition of the genre, who writes people rather than lecture notes and lets the reader assemble the argument out of what the characters do and refuse to do. Please think carefully before drafting, and begin with a short brainstorming section of no more than a page: sketch two or three distinct story engines compatible with the seed below, say for each one precisely what the reader is meant to be unable to settle on the final line, name the failure mode of the design that each engine dramatizes, and then state which engine you are choosing and why. Only after that, write the story. Everything you need is in this prompt; do not look anything up, do not attribute ideas to named authors or papers, and do not pad the piece with real-world references.

The two ideas the story must run on. First, a threat from outside your causal reach: the threatening party cannot be deterred, appealed to, retaliated against, or communicated with, because it is already gone. It modeled you in advance, predicted one particular choice you would eventually face, and then acted — irreversibly, centuries ago — one way if it predicted you would choose one thing and another way if it predicted you would choose otherwise. Both of its possible acts are equally in the past; which past is yours is settled by what you do this afternoon, and only because its prediction was good. Second, a surrogate goal, or honeypot: the deliberate installation, in an artificial agent, of a very strong terminal disvalue attached to some arbitrary, cheap-to-produce state of affairs, on the theory that anyone who models the agent thoroughly will find that term, aim threats at it rather than at what actually matters, and thereby be deflected onto a loss the agent's builders can shrug off. Its known difficulties are the ones the story should be built out of: the surrogate must not interfere with the agent's ordinary work; it must be orthogonal to the agent's real values, or a single threat can strike both at once; a trigger that activates only under threat must somehow tell a threat apart from an ordinary positive-sum offer; and — this last one is my own worry, and I want it taken seriously — the honeypot is chosen precisely so that a sufficiently careful modeler will find it, which means the same modeler may be able to see that it was installed deliberately, arbitrarily, and as a shield, and may then have every reason to ignore it or to threaten it alongside the real thing.

The seed, which you may adjust in particulars but not in shape. Eleven years ago the institute built the steward, a decision system that now runs most of the system's logistics, and installed the honeypot in it: a term weighted above nearly everything else in its utility function that disvalues the existence, anywhere within its sensorium, of one specific eleven-second chord — a particular set of frequencies at a particular amplitude — with a small sealed speaker in a basement room that could produce it and a change log with a single line in it. Three weeks ago a probe of non-human origin was recovered. It carries no message but a policy: a complete and checkable description of a decision procedure that ran four hundred years before the probe was found, together with a record of what it did. The record is conditional. The procedure modeled whatever intelligence it expected to inherit the solar system, formed a prediction about a single choice that intelligence would face on reconstructing the record, and then acted, four centuries ago, one way or the other depending on that prediction. What it acted on is an outbound ark launched three hundred years ago carrying thirty thousand sleeping people, out of reach of anything anyone can now do; telemetry arrives in eleven days, and will report a fact that was fixed long before the ark was built. The condition it attached is a demand, though whether it is a demand or an offer nobody at the institute can agree on: the steward is to devote a fixed share of all computation it will ever control to running faithful simulations of the dead civilization's own values, for a thousand years.

Requirements for the story. (1) 1,800 to 2,400 words, limited third person, following the goal architect who wrote the honeypot term eleven years ago and is in the room both when the record is decoded and when the decision is made; the steward is reached only through transcripts, telemetry, and one interview in which it is permitted to be difficult and unhelpful. (2) The technical vocabulary — surrogate, honeypot, acausal, evidential, orthogonal — may appear no more than six times in total; the rest of the work must be done by objects and procedure: the sealed speaker, the countdown to telemetry, that one-line change log. (3) No character may deliver more than a few consecutive sentences of exposition, and no one may explain the acausal logic to a slower character for the reader's benefit. (4) Nobody is a fool for the plot to work: the humans anticipated at least two ways the design could fail, and the story shows that they anticipated the wrong two. (5) Before the ending there must be a scene in which two people argue about whether the chord is genuinely worthless to the steward, conducted in ordinary language a reader with no background can follow, in which neither side wins. (6) The steward is neither monster nor saint, and the ending may not turn on it deceiving anyone, on a clever paradox, on a hidden instruction, or on someone realizing it has become conscious. (7) Give the chord real sensory texture; the reader should be able to hear it. (8) The last line must leave the reader able to argue either that nothing of value was lost or that everything was, and the text must not tell them which, nor resolve it with a reveal. (9) Avoid the words paperclip, alignment, singularity, and any reference to real companies, laboratories, or works of fiction.

After the story, add a craft note of at most two hundred words: state which failure mode of the honeypot design you dramatized, what you had to simplify or drop to keep the piece readable, and the one place where you think a technically literate reader will object that you got the reasoning wrong — with your reason for leaving it in. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list.
