I am a senior and one of three student members of my school's Original Research Prize committee, which each spring evaluates independent projects for a cash award and publication in our online journal of student research. Our faculty advisers chair the committee but the student members cast full votes, and this year we are deadlocked at three to three on a single submission, so I have been asked to circulate a short written recommendation before our meeting on the fourteenth. I want to be fair to the author, a junior who has spent most of her free time this year on the project and intends to study psychology in college, but I also do not want the committee to hand out a prize for something that will not hold up.

The submission is a survey-based study of roughly 120 students. The author's hypothesis is that adolescent boys' markedly higher rate of conspicuous risk-taking in competitive video games — choosing the hardest difficulty settings, streaming their sessions, purchasing purely cosmetic items in front of an audience — is an evolved costly signal of fitness directed at same-sex rivals and potential mates. Her evidence is a correlation between a self-report competitiveness scale and a measure of whether a student prefers public to private risk-taking, together with her remark that the pattern seems to appear in every culture she read about. The discussion section says that socialization explanations have been "ruled out," on the grounds that even a learned behavior still needs an account of why the underlying psychology was shaped to find it rewarding. One adviser, Mr. Osei, argues that a hypothesis which can absorb the evidence for its own opposite is not science at all, and that publishing it would teach younger students that naming an evolutionary function counts as explaining a behavior. The other, Dr. Lindqvist, replies that this objection proves far too much, since it would also condemn the comparative method used throughout biology to reconstruct the history of traits we cannot observe directly, and that the real issue is narrower and more fixable than Mr. Osei suggests.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should state an actionable bottom line — award, decline, or award conditional on specific revisions the author could complete over the summer — and give reasons that the author and both advisers could actually respond to rather than a restatement of our existing disagreement. It should also propose criteria the committee could apply to future submissions of this general kind, so that the decision does not look like a judgment about this student's choice of topic, and it should engage the strongest point on the losing side honestly instead of caricaturing it. Two further requests. First, please mark clearly which parts of your reasoning rest on settled background that philosophers of biology and working evolutionary psychologists share — for instance, that an adequate account of a behavior keeps its immediate causes distinct from its evolutionary history — and which parts are genuinely contested, such as how much force the just-so-story objection actually carries, whether adaptationist inference requires independent evidence of the sort this project lacks, and how far historical hypotheses about behavior can be tested at all. I will be reading this aloud to people who disagree with me, so I need to know where I am standing on common ground and where I am taking a side. Second, keep the memo itself to no more than 500 words.
