Been stuck in this argument in my reading group for about three months now and I honestly can't tell anymore what anyone is disagreeing about. The question: is Buddhist ethics a consequentialism?

Side A says obviously yes. Acts get evaluated by what they produce, karma is essentially a ledger of results ripening across lifetimes, and then there's that past-life case everyone brings up where the bodhisattva, sailing as a ship's captain, kills the man who's about to murder everyone aboard — taking the bad karma onto himself so the passengers and the would-be killer both come out better off. That's just the trolley problem in robes.

Side B says no. The evaluative vocabulary isn't right/wrong at all but kusala/akusala, closer to skillful and unskillful, so what's fundamentally assessed is the state of the agent's mind rather than some state of affairs the act brings about; and nibbana isn't a consequence you produce so much as a condition you cultivate toward, which makes the whole enterprise aretaic.

My hypothesis, and I'd like you to tell me straight if it's wrong: they don't disagree about a single case. Both say the captain is praiseworthy, both say compassion is doing the real work, both say acting from hatred is bad even when the upshot is good. So the entire fight is over the label — specifically over whether a theory can still count as consequentialist when the value it tracks is agent-relative, since your karmic stream and mine aren't fungible, whereas the standard picture assumes one agent-neutral pool of value to maximize.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is there something substantive buried underneath the taxonomy? The candidates I can think of: whether karmic results are even the right sort of thing to evaluate acts by, given that karma is supposed to key on intention and not on how things turn out; whether there's a vicious circle in saying unskillful mental states are bad because they lead to bad results, and bad results are bad because… what, exactly; and whether merit transfer — the practice of handing your accumulated good karma over to someone else, which a lot of these traditions do ritualy — quietly breaks the agent-relativity both sides are assuming.

Then tell me what could actually move this. A piece of textual evidence, a distinction nobody has drawn, a change in the question being asked? Or does every case just get absorbed by both readings, in which case say so plainly and explain what work the debate is doing for the people having it, because "we disagree about whether to apply a term from twentieth-century analytic ethics to a tradition that predates it by a long way" feels like a strange thing to spend a semester on.

One more thing, and it matters to me: be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on it and which parts are live controversy. I'd assumed the philology of kusala/akusala was uncontroversial, but maybe not even that. Don't hand me one camp's reading dressed up as the consensus.
