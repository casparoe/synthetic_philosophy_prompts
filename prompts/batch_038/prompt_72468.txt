You are a philosopher of medicine who has also designed simulation-based teaching for clinical reasoning and ethics programs. Please treat this as a design problem rather than an exposition problem: before writing anything else, sketch two or three candidate mechanics for the session in a few sentences each, say what each would make participants *feel* as opposed to what it would teach them, and then commit to the one that best serves the goals below and say why.

Context: I am a third-year philosophy undergraduate enrolled in a Philosophy of Medicine seminar, and our department's bioethics society has asked me to run one of its Friday evening sessions. The group is about 24 people, mostly second- and third-years, roughly half philosophy majors and half pre-med, nursing, or public-health students; a handful have done hospital volunteering and one works in a pharmacy, and nobody has read any of the relevant literature. I have 75 minutes, a room with moveable tables, index cards, markers, a whiteboard, a printer, and no budget for props or food. What I want them to walk away seeing is not the blunt lesson that health is socially constructed, nor its opposite, but something more specific and more uncomfortable. I want them to try to sort a list of conditions into diseases and non-diseases by consulting biology alone, and to discover that the biology underdetermines the verdict until they make two further choices: which population the trait is being measured against, and which environment counts as the one the organism is supposed to cope with. Then I want them to notice, without being told, that a large share of their arguments were never about whether some internal state impairs a part-function at all, but about whether an impairment is bad enough to warrant medicine's attention and money — so that the debrief can show how a theorist who insists disease is a purely descriptive, value-free category can concede nearly everything said in the room and still refuse to abandon the distinction between disease and illness. Participants should not hear the words naturalism, normativism, or biostatistical, or any philosopher's name, until after the activity is over.

For the mechanics, I have a few requirements and I would like you to improve on them or tell me where they are misguided. I want the frame to be a fictional review board — something like a national coverage-and-formulary panel deciding which conditions get funded as treatment and which get classified as enhancement, variation, or lifestyle — because I suspect a funding decision makes the stakes vivid in a way that "is this a disease?" in the abstract does not. Please build in: (a) sub-panels of four or five that must each commit to a reference class on paper *before* they see the population statistics for a given condition, so that panels choosing different classes get different numbers off the same data sheet and the divergence becomes visible on a public tally; (b) a fixed pot of coverage units so that approving one condition genuinely costs them the ability to approve another, with the tally kept on the whiteboard; (c) a rules-of-order role — one member per panel, rotating — whose only job is to strike any justification that uses evaluative language, and to demand the physiological basis instead; (d) at least two cards where the measured trait is identical but the environment differs (for example, a trait in someone working rotating night shifts versus someone who controls their own schedule), and at least one card where the verdict flips purely on how the population is partitioned; and (e) a couple of historically awkward cases, such as pregnancy, male-pattern baldness, a trait that is protective in one environment and costly in another, intense prolonged grief, and a diagnosis that was dropped from the psychiatric manual after organized campaigning. I would also like your judgment, with reasons, on whether to give panelists secret private stakes — a relative with one of the conditions, or an instruction to protect the budget — and if you include them, how to reveal them without the session turning into a cynicism exercise.

Deliverables, please, in this order: the full rules and the role cards; the case deck as a printable list with exactly what appears on each card, including the data the panels receive in the second half; a materials list I can assemble in an afternoon; a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes plus a compressed 40-minute variant with an honest note on what the compression sacrifices; the debrief, mapping particular moments in the session onto particular claims in the debate and giving the descriptive theorist its strongest rejoinders rather than treating it as a straw man to be knocked over in front of undergraduates; the things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do about each (my worries: the rules-of-order role collapsing into a semantic word game, one confident pre-med student steamrolling a panel into consensus in ninety seconds, panels reverse-engineering the intended lesson from the card selection, and someone reasonably objecting that real panels just apply published clinical guidelines, which is arguably the point); and finally a frank statement of which feature of the positions this activity cannot reproduce and which I will simply have to supply by talking. You may assume I know the literature well enough not to need any of it explained, and please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I will handle sources myself, and I have no way to check anything you cite.
