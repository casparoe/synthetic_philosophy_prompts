I have been asked to write a reader's report for a university press on an anonymous manuscript in trade political economy, and the chapter on Amartya Sen's liberal paradox contains the following passage, which I transcribe as it stands:

"Sen showed something startling: you cannot have both individual rights and Pareto efficiency. His 'minimal liberalism' condition asks only that at least two people each be decisive over one pair of social states — if the Prude prefers that nobody read Lady Chatterley's Lover to the Lewd's reading it, society must rank the outcomes that way, and if the Lewd prefers to read it himself rather than let it go unread, society must rank that way too. Because the domain of admissible preferences is unrestricted, each may take an interest in the other's private conduct, and once you add the Pareto principle — a change that makes at least one person better off and nobody worse off must be ranked an improvement — the social ranking cycles. The result is really Arrow's theorem with rights slotted in as one more set of preferences, so it is unavoidable rather than a quirk of Sen's formalism; and it bites only under near-unanimity, since the strong Pareto condition is what does the work. Gibbard's reply that rights may be waived disposes of the paradox, because Sen had simply assumed that protected rights are always exercised, which nobody believes. What survives is the durable lesson: a society that takes rights seriously must be prepared to pay for them in efficiency."

I was approached as a reader because the manuscript's chapters on distributive justice touch my own area, but I have never taught social choice theory and my formal apparatus is rusty, so I am not confident where the passage is loose and where it is flatly wrong. Please take it sentence by sentence and tell me what a specialist would accept as accurate, what is a permissible simplification for a general readership, and what cannot be defended. In particular: is the Pareto condition invoked in Sen's theorem the same thing as Pareto efficiency in the economist's sense? Is the strong Pareto principle what the impossibility requires, and if not, what does that do to the author's claim about unanimity? Is the theorem a corollary of Arrow's, or does it stand independently of Arrow's other conditions? Does the waiving-rights reply dissolve the paradox or merely relocate it? And are the escapes the author waves away — restricting the domain to rule out meddlesome preferences, or conceiving rights procedurally rather than as decisiveness over outcomes — really as easily dismissed as he suggests?

Finally, tell me whether the closing sentence survives the corrections, and sketch the minimal rewrite of the passage that a referee working in social choice theory could not object to. I need the conceptual audit rather than references, and I should be grateful if you would write in British English.
