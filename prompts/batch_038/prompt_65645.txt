I've been an underwriter at a small life insurance company for about fifteen years. No degree, never taken a philosophy class, but I fell down a rabbit hole a while back on whether dying is actually bad for the person who dies. I ran into the old Greek argument — Epicurus, I'm pretty sure — that goes roughly: when you're here, death isn't, and when death is here, you aren't, so there's never a moment at which you're being harmed by it. And then the pushback, which I think is the mainstream one now, that death is bad because of the deprevation — you lose the years you otherwise would have had.

Here's my thing. I can't stop translating the whole argument into the vocabulary of my job, and I can't tell whether that helps me see it clearly or is quietly wrecking it. In my world, nobody's death is ever a loss booked against the person who dies. It's a liability that lands on our balance sheet and a payout to somebody else — the insured is never the claimant, that's close to a rule of the business. The years you'd otherwise have lived get turned into a number: present value of future earnings, a discount rate, a mortality table, and then a premium. And the table is totally silent about the time before you were born, exactly the way it's silent about after you die — zero exposure on both sides, which to me looks like the symmetry idea is already baked into how I price things whether I want it or not.

The one exception is viaticals, where somebody terminally ill sells their own death benefit to a third party for cash now. As far as I can tell that's the only transaction in my entire line of work where the person whose death is being priced is also the person who recieves the money. So it's the one spot on the ledger where a loss to the dying person is actually recorded somewhere, and I keep going back and forth on whether that's evidence for the deprivation people or evidence that the ledger is confused.

So do the translation for me, honestly — don't just borrow my terms and hand my own assumptions back to me dressed up as philosophy, and don't flatter the analogy past where it holds. I want to know which parts of the Epicurean argument and the deprivation reply map cleanly onto exposure, present value, and who the claimant is; which parts only fit if I distort one side or the other; and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Then tell me which way the residue points — whether the stuff that won't translate shows the philosophers are arguing about something incoherent, or shows my framework is just missing something.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no wrap-up paragraph, each item only a sentence or two. Make them five lines of a translation ledger: name the piece of the argument, give the closest thing in my vocabulary, and mark it clean, distorted, or no counterpart — and use the fifth line to say which way the residue points and why.
