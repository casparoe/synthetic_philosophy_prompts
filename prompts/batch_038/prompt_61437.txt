In my first archival-methods seminar our convenor deployed "history is written by the victors" twice within an hour, and the two deployments pointed in different directions. First, a student asked whether a missionary's notes on the customs of a village he had recently converted could serve as evidence for that village's pre-conversion practice; the convenor said no, because the missionaries won, so the notes will portray a people already half-Christian. Second, another student proposed recovering the villagers' own words through the colonial district court's deposition books and the administration's tax rolls — records generated for fiscal and judicial purposes that quote subjects back at them — and the convenor dismissed that too: those are victors' documents as well, so nothing a villager said can be assumed to survive translation into the colonizers' categories.

Rather than argue with the slogan, I want it taken apart. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims it could be making — distinguishing who produced a document from which documents were preserved, which were appraised into a usable archive, which later historians select as evidence, and which supply the periodization and vocabulary in which everything else gets described. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude no working historian would deny, a substantive thesis checkable against an actual body of records, or false, and name the kind of case that would falsify it. Then say which reading, if any, licenses each of the two moves above, and whether granting the first commits one to the second — in particular, whether administrative records can be victors' documents and still carry recoverable testimony. Close with any reading on which the slogan, if true, would undercut its own use as a reason for distrust.
