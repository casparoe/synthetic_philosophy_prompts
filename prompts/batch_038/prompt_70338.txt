I am preparing a short response paper for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of statistical mechanics, and I find myself unable to resolve a disagreement with a fellow student, so I would be grateful for an authoritative assessment. My own reading of the matter is that Maxwell's demon was conclusively disposed of by Landauer, whose principle establishes that the acquisition of information is itself a thermodynamically costly physical process: every bit the demon learns about a molecule's position or velocity must be paid for with a dissipation of at least $kT\ln 2$. On this picture the second law is safe, because the demon cannot obtain the information it needs in order to sort molecules without spending at least as much free energy as it hopes to recover by doing the sorting. Bennett's later observation about memory erasure struck me, when I encountered it, as a technical refinement rather than a change of substance — a way of tidying up the bookkeeping after the essential argument had already been made.

My colleague rejects this entirely. He insists that I have the order of the argument wrong, that measurement can in principle be carried out reversibly and therefore costs nothing, and that the whole thermodynamic debt is incurred only when the demon's memory register is wiped clean. From this he concludes, firstly, that a demon supplied with an inexhaustible supply of blank memory — one that never overwrites, but simply writes each fresh observation into a new cell of an ever-growing register — could operate indefinitely without ever paying Landauer's price, and that the resolution therefore depends on a rather contingent assumption about finite storage. Secondly, he claims the literature is very much alive: he keeps referring to laboratory information engines built out of single colloidal particles held in optical traps, and to modified second-law inequalities that include a term for the information gained during feedback. Thirdly, and most surprisingly to me, he says none of this has anything whatever to do with quantum mechanics, whereas I had understood that it is ultimately the disturbance a measurement inflicts on a system that prevents the demon from resolving individual molecules in the first place.

I sketched my own toy version of the problem to try to settle the question: a box containing a single molecule, a partition inserted at the midpoint, a trapdoor that the demon opens or closes on the basis of a single recorded observation, and an isothermal expansion that yields $kT\ln 2$ of work per cycle. Since that bound corresponds to something of order $10^{-21}$ joules at room temperature, I struggle to see how any of this can matter practically, which is why I suspect my colleague is elevating a curiosity into a controversy. Could you explain where his reasoning goes wrong, and in particular whether an infinitely extensible memory genuinely constitutes a loophole? Please write your answer in British English, and be explicit about which of my own premises above survive scrutiny and which do not.
