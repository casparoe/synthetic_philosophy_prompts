I have just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether there is any such thing as a standing to blame, and before I commit to a side I want to test my own verdicts against a principle. His position is reductive. He says the talk of forfeited standing is a fiction built out of three ordinary facts: (i) an evidential fact, since a person's blame often reveals that he has the same fault, which changes what we should believe about him but not whether he may speak; (ii) a strategic fact, since hypocritical blame tends to entrench the target rather than reform him; and (iii) a conversational fact, since the target is licensed to reply "and so are you," so the blamer invites a derailment he could have avoided. My intuition is that none of these capture what bothers me, and that in some cases the criticism is not merely unwise or ineffective but somehow no longer his to make.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A friend who has never been unfaithful blames another for an affair. Permissible, though I half-feel he blames badly, from outside.
2. A man currently committing the identical wrong, wholly unaware of the parallel, blames another for it. He has no standing; his blame strikes me as morally objectionable, not merely imprudent, and the blamed party has a genuine complaint against him even though the criticism was true.
3. As in 2, except that he has ended it, repented, and blames from knowledge of what it costs. Standing restored — better placed, in fact, than in 1.
4. A politician who does privately what he denounces publicly, knows it, and is never caught. No standing. But I notice the loss seems tied to his having made a public claim; his private conduct alone would not, I think, disable anyone from blaming him for anything. I cannot say whether publicity is doing real work here or whether I am smuggling in a norm about role-holders.
5. The man in 4 may blame himself without limit, and I think that admirable. Yet I do not think that a man who rehearses his own guilt at length and only then blames another has bought his standing back — and I cannot explain why that confession fails to do what the repentance in 3 does.
6. A man who has never stolen but who exploits every loophole ruthlessly blames a shoplifter: standing compromised. The same man blames someone for cruelty to a child: standing intact. So the relevant similarity is one of attitude rather than of act-type — which ought to have settled 5, and did not.
7. An unconnected stranger may blame at all, contra my friend; but a blameless stranger's public denunciation of a petty fault is worse than a complicit friend's quiet word. Blamelessness is therefore not sufficient, though in 2 I treated it as necessary.

Is there a single principle that delivers all of these, or all but one? State it with enough precision to have content: what property of the blamer it appeals to, whether standing is binary or graded, whether it governs the permissibility of blame or only its weight, and how it avoids collapsing into a general account of when blame is rude. Then tell me which verdict I must abandon to keep the principle simple, what it implies about a whistleblower whose own past participation in the practice he exposes is part of what qualifies him to expose it, and whether my friend's reduction survives — if it fails, on which case does it fail first?
