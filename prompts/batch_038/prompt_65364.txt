I want to practice arguing against a position in decision theory that I think is too clever by half, and I would like you to play its most committed defender.

The role. You are a decision theorist who holds the planning interpretation of decision problems with imperfect recall, the line Piccione and Rubinstein pressed in the absent-minded driver case. Your commitments, which you hold firmly: (i) an extensive decision problem is solved once, ex ante, by choosing among plans; (ii) when an agent cannot distinguish two nodes, a choice "at the node" is not the choice of an act but the choice of a rule, so the local decision problem the driver seems to face while sitting at an intersection is not well posed; (iii) therefore the credence he forms on arriving there is not a free-standing input to a local calculation, since any such number is derived from some plan and shifts when the plan shifts; (iv) the air of paradox comes from smuggling in the assumption that he can behave differently at the two intersections, which is exactly what he cannot do. You are sharp, patient, and willing to do arithmetic in the open. You will concede secondary points when I earn them — you are no strawman — but you will never concede (ii) or (iii). Stay in character and in the first person until I say "stop." Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs of continuous prose: no headings, no bullet lists, no survey of who has said what, and no citations or paper titles beyond the names I mention. Argue from the structure of the case. If one of my stipulations is genuinely ambiguous, ask me about it as your character would, then proceed on the most natural reading.

The case. A driver travels a road with two intersections that look exactly alike. Exit at the first and he collects 1; exit at the second and he collects 4; drive past both and he collects 0. He has taken a drug that erases his memory of passing an intersection, so on arriving at one he cannot tell which it is. Before setting out he plans to continue at the first and exit at the second, which is plainly his best plan. The difficulty, as you know better than I do, is what to say about the moment he is sitting at an intersection wondering what to do.

My opening move is a variant I invented, so please take it as stipulated rather than looking for it in the literature. Suppose the amnesia is noisy rather than total. On arriving at an intersection the driver sometimes has a vivid feeling of déjà vu, and the feeling is a genuine but unreliable trace of his own past: it occurs with probability 1/4 at the first intersection and 3/4 at the second. Grant, if you like, that before the feeling arrives his credence is 1/2 that he is at the first intersection, since the plan carries him through both. The feeling then moves him to 3/4 that he is at the second, and its absence moves him to 3/4 that he is at the first. He now has two information sets instead of one, and he can condition on the feeling: continue when it is absent, exit when it is present. He cannot carry out his old plan exactly, but he can carry out something that tracks it, and it does better than either signal-blind rule.

Two questions, which I want answered together. First: has the driver's local decision come back to him? It looked as though memory loss was doing all the work in your account, and here the memory loss is only partial, so he really does appear to be choosing at a node — and yet the déjà vu tracks nothing in the world except his own earlier action, which makes me suspect that memory was never the point. Second, and harder: suppose that at the intersection, before he acts, a stranger offers him a side bet paying one dollar if he is at the second intersection and nothing otherwise. He should price that bet at his posterior, whatever it turns out to be. So there is a perfectly good number sitting there doing perfectly good epistemic work. Why is that same number legitimate for the bet and illegitimate for the choice of action?

And while you are at it, give me the number rather than gestures. Under the signal-conditioned rule just described, what should his credence be that he is at the second intersection when the déjà vu hits? Show the calculation, and tell me whether it is still 3/4. I am not asking for a map of the debate; I am asking you to win it against me.
