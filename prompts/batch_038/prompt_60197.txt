I am trying to establish whether my verdicts in a family of cases, all of them turning on irrationality that seems to pay, add up to anything coherent. Here they are, in my own terms.

1. My car dies in the desert. The only passing driver will take me home if I promise to pay him on arrival; he is an uncanny judge of whether such a promise is sincere, and he leaves the insincere to die. Once home, he has no way of tracing me and nothing I do can be discovered. I judge that handing over the money then would be irrational — it costs me and buys nothing — yet I also judge that I would have been rational to be someone who hands it over, and I do not blame the payer; if anything I admire him.

2. A large sum lands in my account tomorrow morning provided that at midnight tonight I intend to drink something tomorrow afternoon that will make me wretched for a day and leave no lasting effect. I am free to change my mind afterwards, and nobody will ever learn whether I drank. I judge that I cannot get the money, and that I ought not to try to make myself drink it.

3. A threat that would be ruinous to execute, and that works only because everyone knows executing it would be ruinous. I judge the making of it rational and the execution irrational, and I feel no pressure to revise either verdict in light of the other.

4. A friend cultivates a hot temper because people concede to him. Cultivation: rational. Outbursts: irrational. Blame: none.

5. A colleague takes something that she knows will make her irrationally attached to a project, so that she will see it through. Halfway in, abandoning it would plainly serve her better, and she does not abandon it. Here I say the opposite of case 1: not abandoning it is rational, full stop — not irrational-but-admirable.

Cases 5 and 1 strike me as the same shape — a standing state produces an act that is worse for the agent at the moment of acting — and my verdicts run in opposite directions. The only difference I can offer is that in 5 she installed the state deliberately and knowingly, whereas in 1 the disposition was simply there, selected by other people's ability to see it. I cannot say why that should matter, and I suspect it should not. One further intuition, which may be irrelevant: I think a person who declines to cultivate a payoff-producing state like my friend's temper has made a mistake, but I also think this should not be said out loud, because if everyone believed and announced it, the states would stop paying off.

What principle, or short set of competing principles, delivers the most of this? I would like it stated precisely enough that I can apply it unaided, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on two cases I have not listed — whether payment in case 1 becomes rational if the driver can trace me, and whether it is rational to undergo a painless procedure that installs a costly-promise-keeping disposition visible to everyone I deal with, even if I would end up worse off than if I declined; an explicit statement of which of my five verdicts must go if I want the simplest coherent account, and what that account costs; and whether the distinction between self-installed and merely possessed states has any content at all or should be dropped. Finally, say whether the resulting principle can be recommended publicly, given my last intuition. I want the principle and its reasoning, not a bibliography.
