I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have about three weeks left on a feature whose working premise is that the humblest of our categories — the letters of the alphabet — are the best place to watch categorization happen in the open. The spine of the piece is a question I first ran into in the psychology of letterform perception and in the old Letter Spirit modeling work, which tried to build a program that could restyle a letter endlessly without ever losing track of what letter it was producing: what is the thing that survives when every 'a' ever drawn is laid end to end, from a blackletter manuscript to a geometric sans to a child's crayon scrawl, such that we say it is the same letter? My editor keeps asking what the piece argues, and I have realized I cannot answer until I am honest about what I myself think. So I wrote down my own verdicts on a batch of cases, most of them collected while pestering a type designer I interviewed, a copy editor who sits across from me, and my eight-year-old niece.

Here they are, with the verdict I actually gave rather than the one I think I ought to give. A double-story 'a' from a book face and a single-story 'a' from the italic of the same family: same letter, no hesitation. That same single-story shape when I meet it in a phonetics chart, where it contrasts with the double-story one: two different symbols, and I would not transcribe one as the other. A five-year-old's reversed 'a', stem on the left: still an 'a', just written wrongly — but the identical reversed shape if some script used it consistently as a letter in its own right, in contrast with 'a': not an 'a' at all, and the reversal is exactly what makes it a new letter. The Latin 'H', the Greek eta, and the Cyrillic en, which share a shape precisely: three different letters. Capital 'A' and lowercase 'a', which share almost no visible structure: one letter. A stencil 'a' whose bowl is broken into islands, an 'a' set in hairlines, an 'a' drawn in a single unbroken stroke in an order no writing master would allow, a blackletter 'a', a piece of wildstyle graffiti that I can only read because the tagger told me what it says: all 'a'. The left half of a ligatured 'æ': not an 'a' but a component of one. A single raised dot in the upper position of a braille cell: the letter 'a' — unless a number indicator precedes it, in which case it is the digit one. A three-by-five-pixel bitmap glyph shown to me on its own, which I genuinely cannot resolve among 'a', 'e', and 'o': not determinately anything; the same bitmap in the middle of a word where only 'a' yields an English word: unmistakably 'a', and I am not aware of having reasoned my way there. A double-story 'a' with its upper arc deleted: still an 'a', a slightly odd one; a single-story 'a' with its stem deleted, leaving a closed circle: no longer an 'a', a broken 'o' if anything. An 'a' rotated a quarter turn: not an 'a', though I can recover what it was — and yet an 'O' rotated a quarter turn is still an 'O', so my sensitivity to orientation is not a fixed rule but something that tracks the symmetry of the letter in question. Finally, a glyph produced by interpolating halfway between a double-story 'a' and a lowercase 'e', so that my perception flips between the two readings every few seconds: this is the case I keep returning to, and I cannot tell you whether I think it is an 'a', an 'e', both, or neither — though I notice that if I am shown the rest of the font first, and the rest is unambiguous, I settle on 'a' almost immediately and then cannot see it any other way.

Two things I want from you. First: is there a principle — one, or a short competing set — that delivers all of these verdicts, or nearly all? State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case neither of us has thought of, and tell me, clause by clause, which of my cases forced that clause in. Second: I expect some of these will not survive, and I would rather find that out now than in print. Say which verdict a simpler principle would overturn, what that simpler principle is, and whether the loss is worth paying for the economy; say what your principle predicts about a mark drawn exactly once, in a style nobody else has ever used, by a designer who has since died and left no specimen book, and about a glyph that is determinately an 'a' for everyone who saw it in its original font but becomes ambiguous the moment it is cut out and shown alone; and tell me whether the fact that I can call a genuine 'a' wrong-looking when it is dropped into a typeface it does not belong to — a judgment I am perfectly confident in, and which seems to be about the letter and not about anything else — is something your principle has to absorb or something it is entitled to bracket. I am not after sources, names, or a reading list; the piece will carry its own. What I want is the account.
