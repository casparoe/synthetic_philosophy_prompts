So for about a year I've been in an interdisciplinary working group on shared agency — two social ontologists, a couple of developmental psych people, and me, and I'm the odd one out because I build coordination software for fleets of agents. The group was supposed to produce a joint paper. It hasn't, and honestly it may not, and I've been trying to work out whether the failure is a sign of something interesting or just people being stubborn. I'd rather get a diagnosis than a survey, and I'd rather you push back on my framing than agree with it.

Here's the shape of the standoff as I see it:

One camp is the planning-style individualists — Bratman and the people who've built on him. On their view, shared intention is constructed out of the ordinary planning states of each participant: my intention that we J and your intention that we J interlock, we're each committed to helping the other play their part, and what makes the activity a joint one is that our subplans mesh, so we can each fill in our own bit in ways that fit together. No extra glue needed, no group-level mind, and — this is the part the other camp attacks — nothing in the account commits us to any special obligation to each other. Modest sociality, explained in terms we already understand.

The other camp is the joint commitment people — Gilbert mainly, plus the plural subject tradition. Their claim is that the planning story leaves out the actual explanandum: what's distinctive about walking down the road with someone, as opposed to happening to walk in the same direction, is that you're jointly committed, and that generates a standing to complain. If I just peel off mid-walk you can rebuke me, and I owe you something by way of explanation, and that normative fact isn't a fact about either of our individual intentions. For them an account that dissolves this into interlocking plans plus mutual beliefs has explained the phenomenon away rather than explained it.

Then there's a third strand that doesn't fit either, which is the experimentalists working in the minimal paradigm. They deliberately strip out talk, commitment, shared goals, sometimes even any visible coordination channel, and look at two people lifting a table, or jointly moving a cursor to a target, or lifting in synchrony with a recorded partner, and ask what the smallest thing is that still earns the label joint action. Their measures are things like movement coordination and performance gains. The philosophers in my group basically regard those cases as not joint action proper — no commitment, no obligation, no shared intention, just two causal systems coupled together. The experimentalists regard the philosophers' insistence on obligation as irrelevant to what they're measuring, and sometimes as an unfalsifiable add-on.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to treat as a guess and attack: this isn't one disagreement at all. Each camp picks out its target phenomenon by the kind of breakdown it wants to explain. The planning people are trying to say what goes wrong when coordination fails even though both parties individually intend the very same thing. The commitment people are trying to say what explains the standing to complain when someone walks away. The minimalists are trying to say what survives once you remove everything cultural, linguistic, and normative. If the theories are individuated by their breakdown cases rather than by a shared set of paradigm examples, then they're answering different questions under one heading and there's no fact of the matter about who is right about joint action, full stop.

Is that right, or too deflationary? Concretely:

1. Is the planning-versus-joint-commitment fight substantive, verbal, or a difference in explananda? If it's substantive, say what would have to be true for one side and not the other — what's the actual hinge? I keep thinking it might be a real disagreement about whether normativity is constitutive of shared agency or just a very reliable accompaniment, but I can't tell whether that's a genuine third option or a restatement of the two.

2. Where does the fault line with the minimalists really sit — about which cases count as data, about standards of evidence, about what an explanation in this area has to deliver? Is there a way for their results to bear on the philosophers' dispute at all, or are they incommensurable by construction?

3. Given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress? A distinction someone should draw and that nobody has drawn yet, an experimental design or formal result that could actually discriminate between the accounts, a question that should be dropped, or an argument that the stalemate is the correct steady state and here's why. Please be willing to say the last one if you think it.

One more thing that's made this personally annoying rather than academic: I have to write a spec for our stack saying when a set of our agents plus a human operator counts as acting jointly, for logging and accountability purposes — which agent gets blamed when a joint task goes sideways. The literature has given me nothing operational, and my co-PI thinks I should just use meshing subplans as the criterion while our safety reviewer wants something commitment-flavored with actual obligations attached. If your diagnosis implies anything about that choice, say it, but keep it brief — the diagnosis is the deliverable, the spec is why I care.
