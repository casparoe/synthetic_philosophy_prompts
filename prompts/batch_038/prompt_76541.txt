I hold a chair in aesthetics at a mid-sized university, and for the third year running I am serving as chair of the jury for our magazine's prize for a first or second work of fiction. The jury is six of us: myself, a practising novelist, two reviewers, an independent bookseller, and a retired professor of literature. We receive on the order of 140 submissions. Each juror is assigned roughly thirty and, in practice, reads between fifteen and twenty-five of them; the remainder are abandoned partway through or never opened. I read perhaps twenty myself. Ten days before the shortlist is announced my own remaining reading time is somewhere around eleven hours, and the jury's collective reading time is exhausted. The shortlist has six places and the prize goes to one book. The citation I sign is printed in the magazine and read aloud at the ceremony, which means the jury makes public assertions about works that, in many cases, only one or two of us have actually read. The editor of the magazine clears the wording of the citation but has no say over the award.

Here is my difficulty. I teach and publish on the epistemology of aesthetic testimony — pessimism and optimism about it; the difference between a bare verdict and testimony that arrives with reasons, descriptions, or comparisons; the difference between explaining the badness of aesthetic deference by unavailability (first-hand acquaintance being required for the judgment to be justified, or for appreciation to be had at all) and explaining it by a norm (the justification being available, but forming the judgment on another's word violating a norm of aesthetic agency); and Hume's marks of the true judge, together with the obvious circularity in identifying those marks. None of this has yet become anything I could hand five intelligent people who have read none of it. This year I intend to hand it to them at our first meeting.

Please write that procedure. I do not want a survey of the literature, a bibliography, or named authorities: I know the field and my jurors do not need it. I want the machinery, presented as a document I can print and distribute, running through the following in order.

1. A typology of the reports the procedure will receive — bare verdict; verdict plus identified features; verdict plus a description of what reading it was like; verdict plus a comparison to named works; a report relayed from someone else; a report of what people are saying — stating for each what it may and may not be used for.
2. A weighting rule for the reporter's competence and candour, assessable by jurors who share no canon, and a frank account of how far that assessment escapes circularity.
3. The inclusion/exclusion asymmetry: whether testimony can ever license setting a book aside, and whether it can ever license putting one on the shortlist. State the rule, say which side of the pessimism/optimism dispute it presupposes and whether it assumes an unavailability or a norm explanation, and say what the rule would have to become on the other assumption.
4. An acquaintance budget: if the strict view holds, acquaintance is the only thing that licenses an inclusion, so scarce reading time becomes the resource the procedure must allocate. Give a triage rule for which unread books I must read, in what order, out of eleven hours; and state what happens when the budget runs out — what may be done without acquaintance, and what must then be recorded.
5. Stopping rules and hand-offs: where the procedure terminates in a decision, where it terminates in "read it," and where it hands the question to a vote or to me because what remains is a question about what this prize is for rather than about the books.
6. Output discipline: what the published citation may assert about a work that only some jurors read, and how reliance on others' judgments is to be marked without collapsing into a disclaimer.

Mark, as you go, which thresholds are arbitrary — where any number would serve and the number is doing rhetorical rather than epistemic work — and which steps are judgment wearing procedural costume. Close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do, and a one-page version a juror could keep beside them while writing reports.

Then run it, showing each step, on three cases from this year's pile.

Case A. A novel by an author with two well-regarded previous books. Three jurors hold it: one read sixty pages and reports it fluent but inert with no reason to continue; one read a hundred and reports much the same; the third has not opened it. Nobody has finished it and it is four hundred pages long. May we exclude it, and on whose authority?

Case B. The book I have now read twice and consider the best thing in the pile. Two other jurors were assigned it: one finished and calls it admirable but cold, the other abandoned it at page forty as self-indulgent. Nobody has reading time left and I cannot ask anyone to take it up. Can it be shortlisted, can it win, and what may the citation say about it? If the honest output of your procedure is that my own favourite cannot take the prize, give me that result rather than a loophole; I would rather see where the view bites.

Case C. A submission that is being talked up at a festival by people with no connection to the jury. One juror reports that everyone who has read it calls it extraordinary, having read the first chapter. Does anything from this channel enter the procedure at all, and at what weight?
