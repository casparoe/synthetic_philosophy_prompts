I'm auditing a philosophy of religion seminar this term and one of the grad students has an argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, but I can't find the exact spot where it goes wrong and he's realy good at defending each step individually. It's meant to be a general argument against theodicies rather than against any one of them. He writes it out like this:

(1) If a loving parent permits her child to undergo severe suffering that she could easily prevent, the child is justified in concluding she doesn't love him, unless the child is shown a reason for that suffering that he can understand and accept — he calls this the acknowledgment condition, and he stresses it's weaker than requiring the child to actually be consoled or to come around to endorsing the suffering.
(2) God stands to human beings the way a parent stands to a child; everyone on both sides of this debate grants something like this, since it's what makes divine love the right standard to measure against.
(3) God permits many people to undergo severe suffering he could easily prevent.
(4) No sufferer has in fact been shown a reason of the required kind: the reasons theodicies offer are speculative, and crucially they're generic — each one could be true of absolutely any instance of suffering, so none of them picks out the sufferer's own case in a way he could check or accept.
(5) So everyone who suffers severely is justified in concluding that God doesn't love them.
(6) A being whose love is compatible with leaving the beloved justified in doubting that love isn't loving in the sense at issue.
(7) So nothing satisfies both "God" and "loving," and the God of classical theism doesn't exist.

The reason I say it proves too much is what happens when you run it on ordinary parents. A two-year-old getting a vaccination is in exactly the epistemic position described in (1) and (4): he can't understand or accept the reason, the reason offered is generic ("this keeps you healthy" would be true of any injection whatsoever), and the parent could trivially skip it. So by (1) he's justified in concluding his parents don't love him, and by (6) a parent whose love is compatible with that isn't a loving parent. Every parent who ever vaccinated a kid, or held them down for stitches, or sent them to a school they hated, fails to love them. That's absurd on its face, but I also can't see which step the toddler case is exploiting that the God case isn't. And if I patch (1) to say that justified trust in a parent's competence and track record can substitute for understanding the reason, then (5) collapses and the thing he was attacking walks back in through the window as trust. He also preempts the obvious reply: he says this isn't an evidential argument from evil, so it doesn't matter that we can't think of the relevant goods, and it doesn't require weighing how much evil there is — it only depends on the sufferer's epistemic access, which means skeptical theism and greater-goods moves both miss it entirely.

So, diagnosis please. Which step is invalid or equivocates — I keep circling (1) and (6) but also (4)'s demand that a reason be case-specific, which feels like it's smuggling in something (1) never licensed — or if the argument is actually valid, which premise is cheapest to drop? I'd like to keep (2) and (3), because giving those up feels like changing the subject away from the problem of evil, and I'd rather not be told the parent analogy breaks down somewhere unless you can tell me exactly where and why that's not arbitrary. Then price the exits: what does a theist pay for rejecting (1), what does an atheist pay for accepting (7) given the toddler case, is there a reading where (4) is the weak link (that's my instinct but I can't defend it), and does the whole thing survive if you swap "severe suffering" for ordinary run-of-the-mill bad experiences?
