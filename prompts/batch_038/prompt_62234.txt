Last autumn I was appointed as the lay member of our regional health authority's Scarce Therapeutics Review Panel, and next month we have to decide something I have not managed to think my way through. To prepare, I have been working through the panel's published rulings from the past decade, and I have also been reading Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" (1977) for an evening seminar; the chair refers to Taurek in the minutes exactly once, dismissively, and I would like to know whether that dismissal is earned or whether his view is closer to what we actually do than she supposes. Here is the record as I have summarised it. In a surge year with five patients needing ventilation and four beds, all five judged equally likely to survive with a ventilator and equally certain to die without one, the panel refused to weight the allocation by projected life-years — two of the five were in their twenties, three in their seventies — and ordered an unweighted draw. Faced with a single shipment of antivenom sufficient either for one full high-dose course for a patient with established systemic envenomation who would certainly die without it, or for five divided courses for five patients each facing roughly a one-in-five risk of death and otherwise a good recovery, it concentrated the whole shipment on the one patient, noting that the expected number of deaths was the same either way. It approved splitting a single donor liver into two paediatric segments rather than transplanting it whole into one adult, accepting a modestly higher per-recipient risk of graft failure in order to help two people rather than one. It declined to fund a screen-and-treat programme that would have prevented something like two hundred thousand episodes of a self-limiting but miserable week-long illness, preferring to fund one course of treatment for a single child with a fatal metabolic disorder; the written reasons say only that the panel did not regard the prevented episodes as "adding up" to anything comparable to the child's death. Yet in a later year it divided a regional stock of a thrombolytic among twelve patients, cutting each of their risks of a mild residual deficit from about forty per cent to about five, rather than using the whole stock on one patient facing certain and severe permanent disability. And when a single dose of an orphan drug could go either to a patient who would die within the week without it or to a patient who would die within six months without it, it went to the first, the panel rejecting the argument that the second had more to lose. Running through all of this is the chair's standing phrase in the minutes: that the panel's business is not to do arithmetic on people.

The decision in front of us now has this shape. A single allocation of an antiviral has arrived during an outbreak, and the stock cannot be subdivided below a full course. Protocol A would give one hundred full treatment courses to one hundred patients already in the fulminant stage, each of whom will otherwise die within days and nearly all of whom the treatment is expected to save. Protocol B would give four hundred post-exposure prophylaxis courses to four hundred patients in the early stage, each of whom otherwise has about a thirty-five per cent chance of progressing to the fulminant stage and dying; prophylaxis prevents progression in nearly all who receive it, so roughly one hundred and forty expected deaths are averted instead of one hundred. The two populations do not overlap and there is no hybrid — the stock will not stretch to part of each. One of the newer members has proposed a Protocol C: a single draw across all five hundred claimants, weighted or unweighted, with courses issued as drawn until the stock is exhausted.

What I want is a reconstruction rather than an opinion. Which principles are jointly consistent with those six rulings — genuinely consistent, since at least three seem to fit, including one on which the chair's phrase is meant literally and one on which it is a manner of speaking? For each candidate, say which of the six sits most awkwardly with it and what it delivers on A, B and C. Then give me your best guess at what the panel will actually vote for, with the reason you prefer that reading and an honest confidence level; tell me which of the six is the closest analog to the pending case and how much weight an analog of that sort should carry; and tell me what we ought to decide if coherence with our own record is the governing consideration, together with the principle we would be adopting, and the principle we would be repudiating, on each of the three options. Finally, propose one or two further hypothetical allocations that would separate the leading candidates, stating what each candidate says about them. Where Taurek bears on this — on whether the loss of the hundred is one hundred separate losses rather than a single larger loss, and on whether an equal chance is what fairness requires when we cannot help everyone — engage with the argument rather than using his name as a label, keeping in mind that he allowed one might still save the several while denying that five deaths are a worse thing than one, and tell me whether our record is closer to that view than the chair thinks.
