The question: In "Of the Standard of Taste," Hume sets up a problem about whether a standard of taste is even possible, and then offers one. State the problem in his own terms, explain the standard he proposes, and say whether it answers the problem or just relocates it. 1200 words.

Her answer, in full bar the opening paragraph, which was throat-clearing:

"Hume's problem comes from the fact that judgements of beauty are sentiments, and a sentiment never refers to anything beyond itself, so it can't be mistaken in the way a claim about a matter of fact can be mistaken. Everyone's feeling is right for them. But that makes nonsense of what we actually do: we call some poems bad, we rank critics, we don't treat a toddler's preference as equal to a trained reader's. So Hume can't hold both that taste is sentiment and that some taste is better.

His way out is to stop looking for a rule that defines beauty and look instead for the judges. A true judge needs strong sense, delicate sentiment, practice in a particular art, frequent comparison of works, and freedom from prejudice. The standard is the joint verdict of people with those qualities, and long-run reputation across ages helps us identify who they are. Worth noting the standard is idealised: it's what such judges would say, not a head-count of who has said it.

Hume also needs a way of telling who the delicate judges are without going in a circle — good judges are the ones who judge well. The wine story is supposed to do this. Sancho's kinsmen each detect something wrong in a hogshead, one says iron, one says leather, and everyone laughs at them until the barrel is drained and a key on a leather thong turns up at the bottom. Their delicacy was vindicated by something in the world, not by anyone's opinion.

That move is stronger than people usually allow. The iron and the leather were in the wine before anyone tasted it. So on Hume's own analogy the beauties are in the poem before anyone reads it, and the true judges don't create the standard, they detect it. Which means Hume isn't really a subjectivist at all: judgments of taste are factual claims about properties of works, and they're corrigible in exactly the way the wine critics' claims were. The antinomy doesn't get balanced, it dissolves.

It follows that Hume has no room for legitimate disagreement. He says the general principles of taste are uniform in human nature, so wherever people differ there must be a defect — prejudice, no practice, no delicacy. Every contrary judgment is a symptom of a broken instrument. This is where the theory goes self-entrenching. Take someone raised entirely on heavily compressed modern pop who finds pre-1960s jazz thin and boring: on Hume's account their palate is ruined and their judgment counts for nothing, so the only people able to certify delicacy are the ones already credited with it. The standard turns out to be whatever the group currently called refined happens to like, and the circularity Hume escaped with the wine story comes straight back one level up, at the question of who counts as a judge."
