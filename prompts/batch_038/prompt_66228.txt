**My thesis:** the standard reading treats monumental, antiquarian and critical history as three temperaments, three kinds of historian, maybe three ages of a culture. I want to argue instead that they're three *operations on the contingency of the past* — three ways of fixing or loosening the question of whether things had to come out as they did — and that each mode's pathology is not excess of feeling but a failure to stop the operation.

- **Monumental** de-contingentifies: it lifts the peaks out of the messy tangle of causes and presents them as a chain running across time that the active person can join. That's the point, it's what lets someone act. But it lies about causes, and once you've built the chain you've also built a scale on which the present can only ever lose.
- **Antiquarian** re-contingentifies in the other direction: because I come from *this* town, *this* family, *this* tradition, everything inherited becomes equally precious and nothing can be ranked or thrown out. Piety toward origins. Pathology isn't just conservatism, it's that the reverence detaches from the origin and starts worshipping the old as such, so the curator's pride replaces the sense of rootedness it was supposed to serve.
- **Critical** re-contingentifies by declaring guilt: the past didn't have to happen, so it can be condemned and cut off. But the judge is himself a product of the generation he's sentencing, so the knife keeps turning back, and the standard he judges by can't come from anywhere except life itself.

Then the load-bearing move: I read **plastic power** — the capacity to grow out of yourself, to incorporate what's foreign and turn it into your own blood — as precisely the capacity to *switch register*. So historical health is not a fixed proportion of the three modes and not a dose of forgetting; it's the ability to change mode deliberately and know when to stop. A culture with high archive capacity and low plastic power can't forget, so it can't act.

I then want to claim Nietzsche performs a **fourth mode he never names**, call it symptomatic history: it treats the past not as model, treasure or crime but as symptom, and asks what a given age's way of remembering reveals about what it is sick of. That's what he's doing to historicism itself, to the Hegelian inheritance, and to the whole world-process business he mocks near the end.

Finally an illustration, and this is the part I'm most nervous about. I came from CS before switching to philosophy, so I want to cash the three modes out in how ML research treats its own history: the founder-and-benchmark hero narratives as monumental; the piety toward legacy infrastructure and the ritual resurrection of decades-old ideas as antiquarian; and the periodic demolition of whole prior approaches as critical — which borrows its standards from the tradition it demolishes, exactly the unfairness Nietzsche flags. The malady would be a field where every old paper is one search away, so nothing gets forgotten, and forgetting happens only accidentally.
