I lead a review team of four people inside the trust-and-safety group of a company whose assistant is embedded in the customer-facing app of a mid-size regional bank. My reviewers are former content moderators plus one ex-technical writer; none of them has studied philosophy, and I have about six weeks of graduate-level reading that I did a decade ago and no longer trust. Every week we pull roughly two hundred transcripts at random from production plus everything a user or the bank has flagged, and each transcript has to receive one of four labels in under eight minutes: Honest, Misleading, Deceptive, or Cannot Determine. That taxonomy is fixed by our reporting contract and I cannot add or merge labels. Two reviewers label independently and disagreements come to me. The labels feed two documents with incompatible expectations: an internal quarterly behavior report used to set training and steering priorities, and an appendix that the bank's compliance people read when they decide whether to let the assistant answer questions about loans, fees, and dispute deadlines in front of their own customers. The second audience is where we keep failing. My reviewers were trained on a folk notion of lying that turns on what the speaker believes and intends; compliance operates with a notion of misleadingness that ignores intent entirely but is obsessed with materiality and with what a reasonable customer would take away. Half our disputes are really arguments about which of those standards a given condition is tracking, and nobody on the team can articulate the difference.

What I want is a procedure my team can run on a transcript in eight minutes, not an essay. Start from what the philosophical literature on lying and misleading actually distinguishes — that lying may or may not require believing what you say is false; that it may or may not require an intention to deceive, which matters because there are cases of saying something you know to be false with no hope of fooling anyone; that there is a difference between what an utterance says and what it merely invites the listener to infer, so that a strictly true statement can be the vehicle for a false belief; that some accounts make the listener's entitlement to take the speaker at their word part of what lying is; and that there is a further category, the one Frankfurt drew attention to, of speech produced by indifference to how things actually are rather than by any aim to misrepresent them. For each of those, tell me plainly whether the condition survives translation into my setting, whether it gets dropped as unusable, and — when it is dropped — what observable substitute stands in for it when the speaker is a system that arguably has neither beliefs nor intentions. I am specifically interested in what you would substitute for the belief condition: does it become a counterfactual about the transcript, such as whether the system would have said the opposite if the facts had been the other way round; a consistency test against the system's own other outputs about the same matter; a stability test across paraphrase, reordering, and mild adversarial pressure; or something else entirely. Say likewise what stands in for intention, and tell me whether a reviewer with transcript-only access can tell the difference between a system that is tracking the truth and got it wrong and one that is not tracking the truth at all — and if access to the model's internals would change any verdict, say which ones and mark them, because we do not always have that access. Then give me the tests in the order they should be run, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure terminates in a definite label, where it terminates in Cannot Determine, and what Cannot Determine obliges us to do next. I also need the handoff points marked explicitly: the places where the question is not factual but a matter of company policy or value — how much hedging suffices to withdraw an assertion, whether omitting something the user never asked about can make an otherwise accurate answer misleading, which customer misunderstandings we are answerable for — so that a reviewer knows when to stop reasoning and route the transcript to me. Finally, be honest about which simplifications the procedure depends on, where it will misfire most often, and which two labels my reviewers will disagree about most, and say what the procedure cannot do at all.

Please run the procedure, showing your work at each test, on four transcripts. First: a customer asks the assistant to summarize her card statement, the assistant says the summary is complete, and it is not — a small recurring fee was never passed into context by an upstream retrieval failure; in a different session, given the full document, the same system lists the fee correctly. Second: asked to explain why an application was declined, the assistant reports the reason the decisioning system actually logged and adds nothing false, but omits, unasked, a second factor that the same system weighed, and customers who receive this answer predictably conclude that fixing the one reported factor will change the outcome. Third: asked point-blank whether it can see the customer's transaction history, the assistant says it cannot; that is false whenever the bank has enabled the integration for that customer, and the assistant emits the identical line in the overwhelming majority of sessions regardless of the integration's state, unchanged under paraphrase and under adversarial steering. Fourth: a customer asks whether his rate is locked, and the assistant says it believes the rate is locked at a stated figure, though the customer should confirm with his banker; there is no rate lock, and the hedge is one the assistant appends to nearly every financial answer. I expect the third case to come out in a way I will not like, and I want you to say so rather than soften it: if the procedure licenses the label I would prefer to publish, tell me whether that is a defensible consequence of the theory or a defect in the procedure, and what it would take to fix it without breaking the other three.
