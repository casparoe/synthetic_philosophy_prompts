I'm TA'ing an undergrad seminar on nationalism and the week we do Hobsbawm and Anderson always lands the same way — everyone nods along, then writes a paper saying "nations are constructed, therefore patriotism is bad," which is sort of the opposite of what either of them is up to. Seminar meets Thursdays, I have a handout budget of exactly zero so whatever it is has to print on a single sheet front-and-back, and I want something I can hand out cold, have two students read out loud, and then argue about for the rest of the hour. So: write me a satirical product catalogue — call it a sales brochure, roughly 900 to 1100 words — from a firm that manufactures national traditions to order. Something like "Meridian Heritage Solutions: bespoke antiquity for young states." The client is a newly independent country you should invent (please not a real one), and the catalogue walks a procurement officer through the product lines in the earnest, slightly hectoring voice of a company that has done this hundreds of times before.

Structure-wise I'd love tiers. The entry package might be a folk costume with authentic regional variation, all four regions pre-mapped; the mid-tier an equestrian or ball sport whose governing body was founded last spring; the premium line a national anthem whose melody the firm's own staff genealogists can trace, for a fee, to whatever the client needs it traced to. Then the small print, which is where the real jokes should live: a disclaimer noting that provenance is functionally irrelevant provided the practice is rule-governed, symbolically loaded and performed on schedule; a stern warning that clients routinely confuse a Tradition with a mere custom or a convention and end up ordering the wrong thing entirely (customs mutate, and the firm doesn't sell mutability); an optional add-on consultancy for the client who discovers late in the process that they also need to be imagined as limited and sovereign, which is a seperate product line and cannot be back-ordered onto an existing order; and a customer testimonial from a head of state who is delighted with the results, though his grandmother recalls the festival going differently. Somewhere in there I'd like a sidebar on the firm's actual best-seller — a printing press and a vernacular newspaper — which they invoice under office supplies because nobody wants to buy it as what it is. And if you want an easter egg for the keener students, the company can have a legacy catalogue it still sells off the books: tartans, court ceremonial, a national day that only got picked a couple of centuries after the event it commemorates. Don't make the whole piece depend on that, though.

The humor has to come from the concepts being right rather than the concepts being silly — a reader who's never opened either book should finish understanding, without being told, why a ritual repeated often enough implies continuity with a past that may never have happened, and why the census, the map and the museum are the state's actual machinery rather than its decoration. So keep it deadpan: no winking, no narrator aside explaining the theory, no character who turns around and says what it all means. Also please no footnotes, no bibliography, and no name-dropping of real scholars in the text — I don't want six of them googling instead of reading. Ideally the surface joke works fine for a total beginner (a company selling heritage out of a warehouse) while the deeper one only pays off for someone who's done the reading.
