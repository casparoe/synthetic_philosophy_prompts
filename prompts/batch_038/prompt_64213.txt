I run the long-horizon research desk at a geopolitical risk firm. Clients ask us whether a currently rising power will still be rising in twenty-five years, or whether the security order in a given region will survive the decade, and our analysts answer by writing a narrative history and then attaching a number that tracks whichever story they found most vivid. I want a written procedure that puts the outside view first: an ordered sequence of steps an analyst without statistical training can run in about two days, from framing the question to delivering an interval with its base-rate anchor recorded.

It must confront three difficulties rather than paper over them. First, for questions of this kind the natural reference class may contain only a handful of members; say what the analyst does when no available class yields a usable base rate. Second, the members are not independent draws — powers embedded in the same era or the same system move together — so a headcount of cases overstates how much weight the base rate can bear; give a rule for discounting it. Third, clients routinely argue that the mechanism behind the base rate has stopped operating (nuclear weapons, economic entanglement, changed norms about territorial conquest). The procedure must permit that claim to be made and tested, but must not let it function as a standing escape hatch back to the inside view.

Also specify: how the class is defined before any outcomes are examined, what afterwards counts as a legitimate reason to redefine it, how far case-specific information may move the estimate off the base rate and in what increments, and where the procedure halts and reports the question unforecastable.

Then run it end to end on two client questions of your choosing in this genre, at least one of which yields a verdict the client will resent. Mark all figures as illustrative — I do not want researched statistics. Close with what the procedure cannot do, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic.
