Our interdisciplinary pain group — a neurologist, two philosophers of mind, a clinical psychologist, and me — has spent four sessions on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain and has produced no movement whatsoever. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another attempt to settle it.

The shared starting point, which nobody in the group disputes: a patient with a lesion affecting the insula or its connections to limbic structures reports a sharp, well-localised sensation when pricked, identifies its site and intensity accurately, withdraws the limb, shows little or no distress and no autonomic response, may laugh, and typically will not seek relief. Grahek's argumentative move from this is also accepted by all of us — the sensory-discriminative and the affective-motivational components come apart, so any account on which unpleasantness is simply a matter of how the pain feels is in trouble. Past that point we split three ways. The evaluativist holds that the asymbolic patient's experience still represents the bodily disturbance as bad for him, and that what has come apart is the subject's endorsement of that evaluation; unpleasantness is a property of representational content. The imperativist holds that the pain still issues the command to protect the body part — the withdrawal is the compliance — so asymbolia is not counter-evidence against imperativism at all, but a story about a subject who has stopped caring whether his own commands are obeyed. The internalist holds that a pain which the subject is happy to have continue is simply not unpleasant, that the patients' reports are true as they stand, and that asymbolia therefore severs any necessary connection between feeling pain and being bothered by it; on this view the other two are explaining the datum away rather than accommodating it. Each accuses the other two of changing the subject, and each has a dismissive line about the others' treatment of first-person testimony: the evaluativist treats it as evidence of a content the patient does not report, the imperativist treats it as a report about behaviour, the internalist treats it as authoritative about a property the others locate somewhere else entirely.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion I want defended: the group is not disagreeing about asymbolia, nor even about unpleasantness, but about how finely to carve up the state that 'pain' names — one experience with a hedonic dimension, or a bundle of nociceptive discrimination, hedonic tone, and aversion. Each of the three positions is right about its own carving, there is no single question they are jointly answering, and the syndrome description everyone accepts is doing no argumentative work, because each side has already settled in advance how to slice it.

Tell me whether that is right. Specifically: is this a substantive disagreement about the phenomenology, a verbal one about what 'unpleasant' picks out, three parties answering different questions under a single heading, or is a hidden empirical question doing the real work — for instance whether asymbolia is one syndrome or a heterogeneous family distinguished by lesion site, or whether these patients would choose relief if relief were genuinely costless, a counterfactual our neurologist concedes nobody has tested and which each camp assumes goes its way. Ground the diagnosis in what the three positions actually commit their proponents to, not in a general map of the literature, and then say what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a reformulation, a change of question, or nothing at all — and if nothing, why the impasse is stable rather than merely hard.

Answer with exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction, no summary, and no closing remarks. Do not set out the options neutrally and leave me to choose between them: commit to one diagnosis, state it as your own view, and give your reasons for preferring it to the alternatives you reject.
