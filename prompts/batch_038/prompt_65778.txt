I'm the program chair of a mid-sized CS conference (~900 submissions, 25% accept rate). Every paper gets three reviewers, four or five when there's a split, plus a senior PC member who writes the meta-review. Our written rubric says a paper is accepted iff it is technically sound AND it either makes a significant contribution or is genuinely novel. Reviewers score those three things and then separately give an overall recommendation. We keep seperate tallies and nobody compares them until the meta-review is drafted.

Last cycle we found something embarassing. On one paper: reviewer 1 said not sound but both significant and novel; reviewer 2 said sound but neither significant nor novel; reviewer 3 said yes to all three. Tally the criteria and you get a majority yes on soundness, on significance and on novelty, so the rubric says accept. Tally the recommendations and you get two rejects to one accept. Same ballots, two different outcomes, and none of us could tell the author which one was the committee's decision. It's worse on the best-paper shortlist, where five jurors vote: on one entry all five agreed it was sound, two said significant but not novel, two said novel but not significant, and one said neither. Four of five wanted it in, but no single criterion had majority support — so when the award citation has to state why the committee chose it, we can't write a sentence that's actually true.

The board's policy says every decision must be justifiable by reasons the committee as a whole endorses, which is a phrase nobody has managed to operationalize. One member wants us to always apply the rubric to the majority criterion scores and throw out the overall recommendations; another says that disenfranchises the reviewers who think the rubric has the wrong shape (we have at least two who mark "not sound" purely to sink a paper they dislike); a third suggested randomising between the two tallies so neither method is privileged, which I assume was a joke but nobody said as much. Someone also floated a second vote, in each conflict case, on which tally to use.

Write me a procedure my PC can actually run: the questions to ask in order for each conflicted paper, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just says reject, and where it stops and hands the call to a human because it's a question of values rather than of counting. It has to survive a 90-minute meeting covering maybe 40 conflicted papers, run by volunteers with no formal training, and it has to output a sentence we could put in front of an author who appeals. Then run it on both cases above plus one you invent where the result will annoy me. Mark every step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume, and tell me which of these I can't have all at once: anonymity of reviewers, the same method applied to every paper, reviewers voting only their own view of each criterion, and an outcome that doesn't depend on the order we discuss the criteria in. If that last part is an actual theorem rather than my bad luck, say so plainly and tell me which one I should be first in line to give up. Finish with a short list of what the procedure simply can't handle, so I know when to escalate to the board.
