So I got into a two hour arguement with my roommate last night about Ghazali, and I have to teach the same material Thursday, which feels like a trap. She read the causation discussion of the Incoherence of the Philosophers for her medieval class, I read it years ago, and by midnight we were shouting about whether he's basically Hume with a beard. Her line: both of them say you never observe a cause, only that one thing keeps following another, so it's the same move, and Ghazali just decorates the gap with God. My line: that's sloppy, because they put different stuff in the gap — Hume's filler is a habit in our heads, Ghazali's is a settled practice of God's, and one of those leaves the world genuinely ordered while the other leaves it… I don't know, I lost the thread at 1am and then I was just being stubborn.

Thursday I've got a fifty-minute first-year section, eighteen of them, in a philosophy of science course whose causation unit is Ghazali one week and Hume the next. Whiteboard only, no slides, bad sightlines in that room, and I'd bet real money most of them read a summary instead of the text.

Don't explain Ghazali to me. Plan how I explain him:

- The order the pieces have to go in and what each one depends on. My worry is that if I open with anything God-shaped they file it under theology and stop listening, but if I open with the fire and the cotton I don't know what I'm smuggling in that they won't be able to unpack.
- The one example to build the fifty minutes around. Is the burning cotton really it, or should I construct something else? Tell me what that example has to contain and what it absolutely can't contain, because I suspect the cotton case is quietly doing more work than it should and I want to know what it's hiding.
- What the people he's arguing against actually claimed, in terms a nineteen-year-old can hold onto. They need to see that his target is a claim about necessity and not a claim that the world is chaotic, and I have no idea how to land that without eyes glazing.
- The misconceptions they'll bring in or build halfway through, and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off. I count at least four: he's Hume six centuries early; he thinks we can't know anything so science is dead; occasionalism means God micromanages every event so nothing is predictable; and the one my roommate also raised, that he caused the declin of rational inquiry in the Islamic world. If you think any of those is partly right, say so and tell me how to teach it honestly instead of as a strawman I get to knock over.
- Whether there's a real, felt difference between denying that the fire has to burn the cotton and denying that fire ever burns cotton, and how to get them to feel that difference in under five minutes rather than just nod at it when I state it.
- One question for the last five minutes that tells me whether it landed, where a parroted answer and a real answer come out differently — and tell me what the parroted version sounds like so I can catch it.
- What I should openly refuse to cover at this level and announce that I'm refusing: the Ash'arite atomism sitting behind all of it, Ibn Rushd's comeback, and whether Ghazali is even properly an occasionalist, since I've seen that argued both ways and I don't trust myself to referee it live.

Keep it to something I can actually run in fifty minutes with a whiteboard and eighteen bored students. And if you think my anti-Hume line is just wrong, tell me that first, because otherwise I'll be teaching a position I can't defend the moment someone asks.
