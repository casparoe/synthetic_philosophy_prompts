I have been reading and posting on rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my settled views on free will and responsibility to a working group of people who largely share that background. Before I do, I would like an audit rather than a defense. Here is the package I intend to endorse.

1. The physical world is causally closed and, at the scale of human decision-making, effectively deterministic. Whatever indeterminism there is, is noise; nothing an agent could own lives in it.

2. I accept Frankfurt-style cases. An agent who could not have done otherwise can still be blameworthy, and I take it to have been established that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism.

3. I also accept Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I find it decisive. To be truly morally responsible for what one does, one would have to be responsible for the way one is, in the respects that bear on one's conduct; that would require being the cause of oneself; nothing can be the cause of itself; so true moral responsibility is impossible.

4. When compatibilists appeal to reasons-responsiveness, or to identifying with one's desires, or to the quality of an agent's will, I read them as describing the conditions under which it is useful and fair to hold someone responsible, rather than the conditions under which that person is truly responsible. They are changing the subject. I regard this as a benign and interesting change of subject, not a mistake.

5. I reject retributivism outright. Backward-looking desert has no place in a theory of punishment; what we do to offenders is justified by incapacitation, deterrence, and rehabilitation, and by nothing else.

6. I have no intention of surrendering the reactive attitudes. I resent, I am grateful, I feel indignation on behalf of people I have never met. A life conducted from the objective attitude toward everyone seems to me both impoverished and psychologically unattainable.

7. I deploy praise and blame deliberately and instrumentally — with my children, with colleagues, and with myself, in the sense of cultivating guilt and pride on purpose as motivational technology. I do not consider this dishonest, because in doing so I am not asserting anything about metaphysical desert.

8. I work adjacent to teams that build systems shaping user preferences over long horizons — recommendation stacks, engagement-optimizing assistants, and the like. I hold, and argue internally with some heat, that the designers bear a culpability of a kind the shaped user does not: the person whose tastes were engineered over five years is excused in a way that the engineer is not. My answer to the objection that engineering and ordinary upbringing differ only in degree is that they differ in kind, because in the engineering case there is a party who intended the outcome and built the system in order to produce it.

The two objections I expect to face and believe I can answer are these. First, that (1) is idle: if the Basic Argument needs nothing from determinism, my insistence on causal closure is doing no work in the package. Second, that (5) and (7) cannot be held together — that a person who rejects desert while meting out blame is either confused or a covert retributivist.

Please go through the set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to and dissolve on inspection, and, for each genuine tension, the cheapest revision that would restore consistency together with what that revision costs me. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. If one commitment turns out to be load-bearing for more than a single problem, say so explicitly and tell me what could replace it. And test my stated answer in (8) against the rest of the list instead of granting it; it is the commitment I am least willing to lose, but I would rather find out now that the list cannot support it.
