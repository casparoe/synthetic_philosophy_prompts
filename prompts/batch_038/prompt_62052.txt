I'm a postdoc in an animal welfare lab and I co-run a reading group that's a weird mix: two philosophers of mind, a vet, a couple of behavioral neuroscientists, and me. Last month it turned into a ninety-minute arguement about whether a rat bar-pressing for stimulation of some reward circuit counts as being pleased, and somewhere around the third round it became obvious that we weren't disagreeing about rats at all — we were using "pleasure" for at least four different things and nobody had noticed. So I want to build a sorting instrument, and I want you to write it: a decision tree whose nodes are concrete cases we can all judge and whose leaves are the actual theoretical positions on what pleasure is. The hard constraint is the audience. Half my group doesn't do philosophy, so no node can trade on introspective vocabulary — nothing like "would you say there's a single common quality running through all of these?", because that's just one of the answers wearing a disguise, and the whole point is to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they'd pick. Every node needs to be answerable cold. And since I want to eventualy run the same instrument on the animal cases, the nodes should be framed so they'd still make sense if the subject couldn't report anything at all — behavior, physiology, and third-person description doing the work, with first-person reports treated as just more evidence in the case.

Content-wise: nine to eleven nodes. The cases have to include an acquired taste (bitter espresso, funky cheese, your pick); a pleasure that comes bundled with pain (chili burn, the runner's lungs, a masochist, whatever you think is cleanest); a subject who reports being pleased but shows none of the usual markers — no approach, no smile, no savoring, and sincerely says they don't want it to go on; a nonhuman case, ideally the rat bar-pressing itself, since the neuro people will insist that animals can be made to work furiously for something they visibly don't enjoy; a pleasure taken in something bad, schadenfreude or similar; and at least one case where two subjects have the same episode going on but only one of them is pleased by it. The distinction I care about most, and the one I want the tree to actually be sensitive to, is whether pleasure is something you could have without being in a position to tell that you have it. That's the real split between me and the vet, so the nodes should force people to reveal where they stand on it even though no node asks about it directly.

For leaves I want, at minimum: the single-felt-quality view; the attitude view, where being pleased that such-and-such does the work and the phenomenology is optional; whatever hybrid says the quality is only a pleasure in virtue of the attitude; the view that splits things into a core affective reaction and a seperate motivational pull; pluralism or family resemblance; a functional-role or dispositional account; and the eliminativist who says there's no natural kind here to be found. Attach the standard name to a leaf if there is one, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender has to concede that makes the rest of the table groan. Then flag any pair of leaves my nodes can't genuinely separate, and don't paper over it; I'd rather know the instrument is blunt somewhere than be sold a clean tree. I also want the empty cells: build the tree out of the questions first, then note which combinations of answers no live position occupies, and for each one say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's gotten around to it yet. Start straight in with the tree — no preface, no summary of what I asked for, no "here's your instrument." The unseparable-pairs flags and the empty-cell discussion go after the tree, clearly marked as their own sections.
