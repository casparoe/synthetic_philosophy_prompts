I've spent years reading decision-theoretic rationalist writing and have only recently turned to just war theory, where I've hit a knot I can't untie. The standard story is that Walzer's moral equality of combatants gives soldiers on both sides an equal liberty to kill and an equal immunity from being killed, and that McMahan's revisionism replaces this with liability: one becomes liable to defensive harm through one's moral responsibility for an unjust threat, so combatants on the unjust side are liable and combatants on the just side are not. But the revisionists then say that many unjust combatants — conscripts, people holding reasonable false beliefs about their own cause — are not responsible enough to be liable, and they insist that liability is something distinct from permissibility all things considered, the latter being where lesser-evil reasoning lives. I cannot see what work the second notion is doing that the first isn't already doing. Please explain, for someone fluent in expected-value reasoning but not in rights-and-forfeiture machinery, what liability adds to a case-by-case weighing of harms, and why the obvious consequentialist repair — allowing the killing of non-liable combatants whenever it is the lesser evil — is supposed to fail at restoring Walzer's symmetry.

Answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, covering in order:

1. what liability does that an all-things-considered balancing of harms cannot do on its own;
2. how the conscript and reasonable-false-belief cases drive a wedge between liability and culpability, and why revisionists ground liability in responsibility rather than blameworthiness;
3. the actual reason, not merely the intuition, why a lesser-evil justification for killing non-liable combatants does not put both sides back on equal footing;
4. what a convention or practice of war could and could not supply as a substitute for liability;
5. whether any part of this disagreement is even visible to someone whose only normative tools are expected values plus side constraints on pursuing them.
