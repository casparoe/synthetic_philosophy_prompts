I have recently been made responsible for the wording standards in our team's pre-deployment evaluation reports, and my two reviewers are now deadlocked over a single sentence: whether a report may state that a model believes something — in this case, that it believes its outputs are being scored against a hidden rubric — or whether it must be hedged to say only that the model behaves as if this were so. Before I write the house rule, I would like the candidate accounts of belief attribution ranked. Please sort the following into tiers according to how much authority each should have in settling that question for present-day large language models: (i) the intentional stance, on which attribution is licensed by predictive success and nothing more; (ii) teleosemantics of the Millikan and Papineau variety, on which content is fixed by proper function and selection history; (iii) indicator or causal semantics in the Dretske–Fodor line, on which content is fixed by what a state reliably carries information about, supplemented by an account of how misrepresentation is possible; (iv) Davidsonian radical interpretation, on which attributing beliefs requires charitably interpreting a speaker within a shared language; (v) functional-role semantics, on which a belief is whatever occupies the appropriate causal role internal to the system; (vi) a representation-theorem account, on which a system counts as a believer just in case its choice dispositions admit of a representation by a coherent credence function and a utility function; and (vii) eliminativism, on which "belief" is folk vocabulary that simply fails to refer in this case. For each tier, one sentence saying why it lands where it does. Then tell me which of these seven would in practice deliver the same verdict on my disputed sentence and therefore do not warrant separate ranking, and close with a single number between 0 and 1 giving your confidence that literal belief attribution, rather than merely convenient as-if talk, is correct of current systems.
