I teach philosophy of biology at a mid-size state school, and for nineteen years my office has been across the hall from Renata, who is retiring this spring after forty-one years in the molecular biology faculty. Most of her career went into translation — tRNA, the ribosome, the whole apparatus that actually does the matching — and the thing we have ever really talked about, loudly, in the corridor, at three separate conferences, is the one thing I now can't settle in public: she has always said that DNA is a code and that what her field does is decode it, and I have always wanted to know precisely what that word is doing in her mouth. The department chair has asked me to give the toast at her retirement dinner in May, five minutes at the outside. Roughly ninety people will be there: former students who now run labs of their own, a handful of undergrads, the institute's communications people who may quote it back at us, her sister who teaches high-school chemistry and has never read a paper in her life, and — this is the cruel part — one former PhD student of hers who defected to philosophy of biology about ten years ago and who will catch every fudge I make.

Here's the bind, and I'd like you to write the toast rather than advice about it. I can't retract anything I actually believe, and I can't let her go out of the room having been quietly corrected by the philosopher, which would be unbearable for both of us. But I also can't do the cowardly thing and announce that it's all just metaphor, that the code talk and my scruples are the same poem sung in different keys. She'd hate that, and it isn't true: the codon table is a table, it's real, it was cracked, and the interesting question was never whether it exists but where the meaning is supposed to live. My own view, which you should feel under the whole speech without ever hearing it stated, is that a message requires someone who could have gotten it wrong, that the polymer by itself is just chemistry with a shape, that all the semantic weight in her field gets carried by machinery whose own specificity has to come from somewhere else — and that this is why her favourite story to tell rotation students was the right story all along: the first codon anybody cracked was cracked by putting a synthetic stretch of nothing-but-uracil RNA into a bacterial extract with no cells in it and watching what came out the other end, no sender, no intention, no reader, just an extract that answered. She told that story for forty years. When I once accused her of a category error she said I should first explain why the extract answered at all, and I never did have a good reply. That exchange, or something like it, is the pivot I want; it should carry the affection rather than the argument. There's also a gift being given at the dinner — a framed printout of the whole codon table, which she has said is the only picture of God she'll accept — and one detail I want used honestly rather than decoratively: the table is near-universal but not quite, since in our own mitochondria one of the stop signals gets read as tryptophan. She is famous in the building for banning three words from group meeting: blueprint, recipe, and reading, in that order of hatred. I'd like the speech to notice that she was stricter about metaphors than I ever managed to be.

So: four to five minutes, written to be said out loud, with the sentences short enough that I won't run out of breath. No stage directions, no bracketed instructions, no namedropping of philosophers or schools of thought, and please don't let me say the phrase semantic information aloud — the distinction has to do the work from underneath instead. One joke at most, and it should be about her, affectionately, not about biology as such. It has to land for her sister and for the defector at the same time, and it has to end as a toast that people can actually raise a glass to, not as a conclusion I draw. Nothing in it may be something Renata would consider false — she is dying of nothing and retiring into a vegetable garden, but she will be listening with the same ears she used on grant panels. And if it comes out sounding like a lecture with a punchline bolted on, I'll know, and so will she.
