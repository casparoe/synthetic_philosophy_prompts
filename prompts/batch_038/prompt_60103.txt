So I've become the insufferable person at my Thursday poker game and also in my own investing ever since I went down the rabbit hole on Kelly betting and that whole line of argument about time averages versus ensemble averages. I want to write up a coherent statement of where I've landed for a money blog I contribute to now and then, and before I publish anything I'd like someone to check whether the package actually hangs together. My brother is an actuary and he's told me twice that I'm contradicting myself, but he never says where, and I half suspect he just objects on principle to my anti-utility-function stuff. I'd rather get the real answer than his vibe.

Here's what I actually hold, more or less in the order I came to hold it:

1. Utility functions over wealth are made-up bookkeeping, not a fact about the world. The only defensible way to evaluate a strategy is to ask what happens to your wealth if you actually run it forward through time — the time average along one real trajectory, not an average across imaginary parallel copies of you. I don't want the word "utility" appearing anywhere in my reasoning.
2. Given a repeated bet with known odds, betting the full Kelly fraction is the right answer, and betting less than that is just leaving growth on the table for no reason.
3. In practice I bet around half Kelly, because I never actually know the true probabilities, only my own estimates of them.
4. No bet carrying any nonzero chance of wiping me out completely is ever acceptable, no matter how good the odds or how small the chance of the bad outcome.
5. All else equal I'd rather make a bunch of small bets than one big bet with the same total amount staked.
6. I buy insurance freely — homeowner's, disability, the extended warranty on the car — even though I know each of those is a negative-expectation purchase and even though it obviously drags on my long-run growth.
7. I never borrow money to bet. None. No margin, no loans against the house, no credit card float, no matter how large the edge looks.
8. The Kelly reasoning applies just as much to a one-shot, unrepeatable decision as to a repeated bet. When I decided whether to quit my job and start the business, I treated it as a single big wager and sized it with the same logic, and I still think that was the right way to think about it.
9. I keep my betting money and my safety money in seperate pools and run the growth logic on each pool independently — full Kelly on the trading account, nothing at risk in the safety account.

Go through these and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say exactly what I'd have to give up and what it costs me to give it up — including whether any of the disagreements between my own commitments turn out to be about words rather than substance. If one of these is just false rather than inconsistent with the others, say so plainly; I'd rather be told that than have it folded into a consistency score. Then tell me the cheapest overall repair and, separately, which single revision would clean up the most damage. Please don't spend much space explaining what the Kelly criterion is or rehashing whether expected utility theory is good — assume I know the mechanics cold, that's not where I'm unsure of myself.
