I teach an upper-level philosophy seminar on the ethics and strategic logic of nuclear deterrence: 24 students, one 75-minute session in week six. They will by then have read excerpts from Schelling and one paper on the stability–instability paradox, and we will have spent a week on expected-utility reasoning under low-probability, high-magnitude risks. I want an in-class game that makes two things felt in the body rather than merely assented to on the page.

First, the rationality of irrationality: that a player who credibly removes their own freedom to desist gains, and that a threat which leaves something to chance can be more compelling than one that is certain and fully under control. Second, the stability–instability paradox: that a mutual capacity for catastrophic retaliation, precisely because it is credible, licenses small aggressions below the threshold — the very thing that keeps the peace at the top of the ladder makes the bottom rungs unsafe.

The requirements:

1. Design the game for pairs at tables, with the non-playing students assigned a structured observational task that will matter in the debrief. Tell me what that task should be, so the audience is generating the evidence I will need later rather than waiting.

2. Run it in three acts, with the rules of each act handed out only when that act begins. Act one establishes the ladder and lets a commitment tactic become available; nobody should be told it is available, and I should not have to explain it until after someone finds it. Act two introduces a catastrophe mechanism whose probability the players influence but do not control. Act three must create a provocation of small value to the aggressor and large negative value to the victim, which the victim cannot answer without accepting the act-two risk — and the victim's payoff arithmetic should make forbearance the rational choice. I want someone at that table to say aloud that they have the overwhelming force and cannot use it.

3. Do not let the design depend on telling jokes with the numbers. If the payoffs are to produce the effects in point 2, say explicitly what the incentives are doing at each stage and why a student cannot simply decline to escalate out of temperament rather than out of calculation. Build in whatever asymmetry between the two sides the third act needs.

4. Scoring should cost me nothing and survive a room with no power: tokens, a paper tally, or a scoreboard I can keep on the whiteboard. Give me the materials list.

5. Give me a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the two named ideas and onto Schelling's apparatus, in the order the students encountered them, and that tells me what the observers should have recorded. Then state plainly which features of the real situation the game cannot reproduce — among them the irreversibility of the outcome, the fact that this arrangement has never actually failed, and the moral position of people whose survival is a token on someone else's table. I want to know where the effect risks tipping into flippancy about annihilation and how you would hold the room at that point.

6. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: pairs that deadlock and never climb, a student who refuses to play at all, players who reverse-engineer the arithmetic and stop engaging, and the third act failing to bite because the provocation seems too small to bother with. For each, a fix I can apply in under a minute without breaking the design.

7. Finally, since my room is fixed at 75 minutes, tell me what to cut if act one overruns, and offer a fallback version that works with 8 students in 30 minutes without losing the third act — that is the one part I am not willing to give up.
