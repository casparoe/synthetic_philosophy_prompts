taking an ML ethics seminar, we're on RLHF this week and I keep getting stuck on the reward-model part rather than the ethics-part. Concretely, the standard pipeline: crowdworkers compare pairs of model outputs on a fixed prompt set, we fit a single scalar r with the Bradley-Terry thing, so P(A preferred to B) = sigmoid(r(A) − r(B)), and where annotators disagree we majority-vote down to one label per pair and treat the losers as noise. Then policy training maximizes that scalar minus a KL penalty. My question is what picture of human preference all of that presupposes. It seems to bake in that any two outputs are comparable, that there's a single latent ordering per prompt that each annotator is a noisy measurement of (otherwise why would majority vote be the right move?), and that the ordering is transitive enough to be squeezed into one real number. But I can't tell which of those the practice actually requires vs which it just happens to fit — couldn't a genuinely non-transitive preference still yield a usable scalar? And which rival views does it quietly rule out: incommensurable values, preferences that shift with framing or with what else is on offer, disagreement that's substantive rather than error, the difference between what someone picks and what they'd endorse on reflection? It also strikes me as awkward that the standard complaints — sycophancy, reward hacking, the model gaming verbosity — are complaints about the scalar pointing at the wrong target, when the assumption behind the scalar is never tested anywhere in the pipeline. So: reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what's strictly presupposed from what's optional, and tell me what you'd have to change if you dropped the single-scalar assumption (multiple reward heads, a partial order, deliberative rather than forced-choice elicitation, whatever) — and whether any of those fixes is more than cosmetic given we still have to hand the policy one number to climb.
