I'm a third-year PhD student in political philosophy. In our history-of-economics reading group, a discussion of Lange's trial-and-error socialism was ended by someone observing that the market is a discovery procedure — the implication being that a planning board issuing parametric prices, however carefully instructed about marginal cost, could never come to know what competition comes to know. The slogan clearly carried weight, but I can't yet say how much, and I suspect it was doing at least three different jobs at once.

Could you lay out, as a list I can bring back to the group, the distinct theses this sentence might be asserting? I'd like each reading pinned down along the dimensions that matter: what exactly is discovered (facts already dispersed among agents, or possibilities that did not exist to be known beforehand), discovered by whom and through what mechanism, over what time horizon, and by contrast with which rival institution — since "discovery" means nothing without a baseline. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude that no planning theorist would deny, a substantive and defensible claim, or something closer to false. Then tell me which readings would actually exclude Lange's model rather than merely making it look unappealing, which would remain compatible with a market-socialist economy in which consumer goods and labour are genuinely traded but capital goods are not, and which one the person who deployed it most likely had in mind given the work it was made to do.
