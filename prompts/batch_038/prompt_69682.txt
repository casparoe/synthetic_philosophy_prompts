Thirty-five years running a wet lab, mostly analytical chemistry, and now that I'm retired I've been working through the Bayesian stuff on my own. The maths never bothered me. It's the philosophy around it that's got me stuck.

The thing I'm reading about is updating when you aren't sure what you saw — Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics, I think those are the two names for it. As I've got it: you have a partition of the ways an observation might turn out, the experience shifts your probabilities over those cells, and everything else moves while the conditonals stay put, so P_new(H) = P_new(E1)·P_old(H|E1) + P_new(E2)·P_old(H|E2). The staying-put part has its own name, rigidity.

Toy case I built for myself, from the old job. Litmus strip, and H is the claim that the sample is alkaline. Before I look, the two cells — looks blue, looks red — sit at 0.5 each. I look under bad light and I'd report 0.8 blue, 0.2 red. Long before any of this I'd have said P_old(H|blue) = 0.9 and P_old(H|red) = 0.05. So P_old(H) was 0.475 and after looking it's 0.8·0.9 + 0.2·0.05 = 0.73. That all feels right to me. It's exactly what looking at a strip in a dim room is like.

There's an ex-lawyer in my reading group who argues for sport, and he says the rule is worthless, and he walked me through this. I've been chewing on it a fortnight and I can't find the joint that's loose.

(1) By rigidity, an experience can only move P(H) by moving the numbers over the partition. The conditionals P_old(H|Ei) are inputs to the update, never outputs of it.
(2) So no experience can ever teach you that some sign is a sign of something. The link between evidence and hypothesis is nailed down before you look and is never revised by looking.
(3) And which partition you use is your choice. The very same glance at the strip can be written up coarsely, {blue, red}, or finely, {blue-and-dark, blue-and-pale, red-and-dark, red-and-pale}, and a 0.5/0.5 → 0.8/0.2 shift on the coarse one is not the same update as any shift you might report on the fine one.
(4) The rule also says nothing about where the 0.8/0.2 came from. I could have written 0.99/0.01 or 0.55/0.45 after the identical squint at the identical strip, and the rule would propagate whichever I picked without complaint.
(5) Put (3) and (4) together with the fact that P_new(H) is just a weighted average of the old conditionals: by chosing the partition and choosing the inputs, I can land my posterior anywhere between 0.05 and 0.9 — and with a partition picked cleverly enough, outside that interval as well.
(6) So one and the same experience licenses whatever conclusion I want, as long as I describe the experience suitably.
(7) A rule that licenses everything accounts for nothing. Therefore this is not a theory of how observation bears on hypothesis. Either observation really does confer certainty and we should just condition, or observation does no justificatory work at all.

What I want from you: which step breaks, and what does breaking it cost.

Let me say up front what I'm not giving up. I'm not taking the first horn of (7). My entire working life was making calls under bad light, and I know perfectly well that I was never certain what a strip looked like and yet I did find things out. I can't sign (6) either. But I'd rather be told I'm wrong about (6) than have it assumed away.

So: if the answer is that the partition and the inputs are constrained somehow, tell me constrained by what, and whether that constraint is a fact about the sample and the light, a fact about my eyes, or a rule of honesty about how I report — because those three cost very different amounts. I also want a straight answer on (2). If a kinematic updater genuinely cannot acquire a new evidential connection, ever, that seems like an enormous thing and I've not seen anyone confront it head on; if that's really the price, I want it priced rather than waved at.

And one more. His last clause in (5), that a clever partition gets me outside 0.05–0.9 — I tried to do it on paper and failed, everything I constructed stayed inside. Tell me whether that clause is simply false, or whether I failed for a reason I'd not have spotted, because it changes how much of the rest of his argument I have to swallow.

Finally, if your verdict is that (1) through (6) are more or less correct and the only thing wrong is the jump to (7), say so plainly. In that case I want to know what belongs in (7)'s place — what the rule is actually doing, if not constraining what I'm allowed to end up believing.
