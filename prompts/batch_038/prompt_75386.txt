Take the screwworm eradication campaign — Bushland and Knipling's sterile-insect work, first on Curaçao in 1954, then pushed through the US South, Mexico and Central America over the next few decades until there was a sterile-fly barrier down in Panama — and run it against the scale argument for insect sentience: the thought that even a low probability of sentience, times an astronomically large number of individuals, swamps the expected-welfare calculus.

Map it carefully first. Who are the moral patients here — the irradiated males that get released, the wild adults who mate and come up empty, the larvae that would have hatched inside a calf, the calf itself? The campaign is weird because it barely kills anything: it mostly makes the next generation never exist. Does the scale argument treat never-existed the same as killed, and should it? Then do some arithmetic — give me a break-even probability of larval sentience at which the whole program flips sign, and list exactly what you had to fix to get that number (moral weight per larva vs per calf, whether larval life is net good or net bad, how you count the hosts who never got infested).

Then flip it around. This gets called one of the great triumphs of 20th century entomology: species-specific, no pesticide residue, a World Food Prize. If the scale argument lands against it, is that a reductio or the argument working as intended? And what does the campaign's own justificatory language — cattle-loss dollars start to finish, no fly welfare anywhere that I've seen — tell you about whether the low-probability-of-sentience premise was ever doing real work, or was just nobody's problem?
