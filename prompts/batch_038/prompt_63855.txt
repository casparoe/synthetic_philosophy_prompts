ok, situation. I'm co-writing a chapter with a formal epistemologist who is deeply suspicious of subjunctive conditionals. Her line is that a claim about what someone would believe in a situation where the proposition is false isn't the kind of claim anyone is in a position to evaluate, and she won't sign off on any account whose central machinery rests on it. I lean the other way: I think something like Nozick's tracking idea — belief that varies with the truth of what's believed, where the variation is assessed relative to the method the subject actually used rather than to belief-forming in general — is still the best hope we have, and I'm not ready to abandon it just because the modal idiom is expensive.

We agreed not to argue about accounts until we'd written down what any candidate has to deliver. Thats the part I want from you: the spec, not the scorecard.

So give me the requirements list for an account of knowledge. I want the cases it has to get right, including the ones that get less airtime than the Gettier trio — a belief that is true and would have been true in nearby situations but was formed by a method that was just lucky this time; a belief that tracks the truth in the one situation under discussion but not across the cluster of situations the subject can't tell apart from it; the cases where you know something by inferring it from things you know, and where the inference step itself is such that you could easily have botched it. The distinctions it can't collapse — reliability of a method versus reliability of a particular token belief, closure under known entailment versus closure under competent deduction, a condition genuinely failing versus the subject merely being unlucky, knowing-that versus being in a position to know. The facts about how people actually deploy the word that it has to square with, including the fact that two informed speakers can sincerely disagree about the same case and neither looks like a fool.

And then the requirement nobody usually writes down, which is the one my co-author cares about: checkability. Whatever conditions the account imposes, a competent non-philosopher has to be able to tell whether they hold, using ordinary evidence about the subject's situation and history, without having to run intuitions about far-off counterfactual scenarios. State that as a requirement properly, with the consideration behind it, and then say what a modal-free account has to reproduce that a tracking account gets for free — and whether there's anything tracking gets that simply cannot be reproduced without some subjunctive machinery somewhere.

For each requirement: the case or consideration motivating it, and a tag for whether it's non-negotiable or a camp prejudice wearing a lab coat. My suspicion is that at least one of the closure principles and at least one of the anti-skepticism demands are the latter, and I want you to say which ones and argue it rather than just assert it. Then the part I most want: is some subset of these jointly unsatisfiable? I know the rough shape of the problem — a sensitivity-style condition, some closure principle, and the claim that you don't know you're not a handless brain in a vat can't all be true — but I want it stated cleanly, with exactly which premise each camp drops and what it costs them. And tell me whether relativizing the condition to the method used actually escapes the problem or just relocates it to a new one about how methods get individuated, because if it's the latter I need to know before I stake the chapter on it.

Finish with the single requirement that does the most work ruling out candidates, and a straight answer on whether my co-author could live with it.

Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I'm not checking anything against sources and I'd rather have your reasoning than your guesses about who said what. Work from the cases.
