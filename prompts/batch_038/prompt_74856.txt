Two of my grad students have spent the better part of a year going in circles on the torture-vs-dust-specks case and I've run out of patience with the emails, so I'm making them produce a document. The setup we're all using: one person endures five minutes of what everyone in the room agrees counts as torture, versus some huge number of people each of whom gets a single dust speck in the eye — a few seconds of grit and blinking, no injury, no aftereffect. One student is a committed fine-grained aggregator: she holds that "worse than" is a single transitive and connected ordering with no gaps, so for any nonzero disvalue there's some number of instances of it that outweighs any fixed worse harm. The other is a lexicalist: he holds that some harms sit below a threshhold such that no quantity of them can ever outweigh a harm above it, and he thinks the verdict she's forced to — that enough specks beat the torture — is one he simply can't sign.

Write the joint statement they should have produced. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. Be ambitious: claims about the case itself (what's stipulated and what isn't), about the formal properties of the ordering they both accept, about what each view is actually committed to by way of surprising verdicts, and about which arguments each side concedes the other has going for it. Anything so bland that I'd sign it too doesn't count — I want claims that cost somebody something.

2. A disagreements table. One row per genuine disagreement, with her own acceptable phrasing of her side, his own acceptable phrasing of his, and a column marking the row substantive or merely verbal. Where you call something verbal, say which part of it and why.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. I have suspects of my own but I want yours argued for rather than just listed. Mine: whether his threshold is a claim about the intensity of each individual harm, about how bad off the worst-off person is, or about total disvalue (I think those are three different views and he slides between them); whether the dispute is really about lexicality at all or about interpersonal aggregation, i.e. does his position survive when every speck lands inside one person's life instead of being spread out; whether "worse than" might be vague or incomplete rather than sharp, so that neither of them is wrong; and whether the sequence argument — start at the torture and turn the intensity dial down one notch at a time — forces him to a sharp cutoff, and whether a sharp cutoff is something he can live with.

4. For each crux, a concrete case both would accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance, before the other's reasoning is on the table. I've been using these and you can borrow or replace them: the intrapersonal version (five minutes of torture versus that same person taking a trillion specks spread across a normal lifespan); the dispersion case (a trillion specks distributed one each across a trillion people versus a trillion specks all landing on one person — which is worse, and do they answer differently?); the dial (a machine that can set any number of people to any point on a continuous intensity scale, where you ask for the first notch at which a real difference appears, and whether "there is no first such notch" is a verdict or an admission); and the turning point (his largest number of specks that still loses, her smallest that wins — each should say whether that number is even the kind of thing that could be known, and whats lost if it isn't).

Then close with a paragraph apiece in which each predicts which crux the other will cave on first and why. If you think a year of this argument was a waste of time, you can say that too, but only after the document is done and only if the document shows why. No references — I don't care what's been published, I want the reasoning. Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section, then the table, then cruxes with their cases and predictions.
