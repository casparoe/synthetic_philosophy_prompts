A friend and I argued until two in the morning about what Frege would rule on a case neither of us could find anywhere in his writing, and we have agreed to accept an umpire.

What we both accept is his record of verdicts in the 1892 essay on sense and reference. He rules that an identity between a sign and itself is analytic and knowable a priori, while an identity between two signs for one and the same object can be a substantive astronomical discovery. He rules that the informativeness cannot lie in the reference, since the object is the same, but in the mode of presentation — the sense — as with his two descriptions of the point where the medians of a triangle intersect, and with evening star and morning star. He rejects the account he himself had held in the Begriffsschrift, on which identity is a relation between names, precisely because that makes the statement about the signs instead of about the subject matter. He rules that a sense may lack a reference: the celestial body most distant from the Earth, the least rapidly convergent series, and the sentence about Odysseus being set ashore at Ithaca, which has a sense and expresses a thought even though the whole sentence loses its truth value if the name is empty. He rules that in reported speech words are used indirectly, that their indirect reference is their customary sense, and hence that a report concerns the thought entertained rather than its truth value, so that substituting a co-referential name can change whether the report is true. He insists that a thought is not a private idea requiring a bearer but something objective that many people can grasp; and he concedes that the tidy correspondence of one sign to one sense fails in natural language, where one must be content if the same word carries the same sense in the same context.

The case we are fighting over. An astronomer dubs a bright point twice in one breath, giving one assistant the name Kallos and the other the name Sura, each time by the same ostension and with the same accompanying description — the point visible between those two fainter ones. Neither assistant ever learns anything that distinguishes the names, and each would accept any sentence the other accepts with the name swapped. Six months later it is established that there was one object, not two, and both are told. Three questions follow: did the astronomer confer one sense or two; does the resulting identity stand on a par with a trivial self-identity in cognitive value; and if one assistant sincerely asserts that Sura varies in brightness, is a report to the effect that she believes Kallos varies in brightness thereby true?

My friend's position is that the essay's own definition of sense as mode of presentation allows two names to share a sense, so that on the stipulations the identity collapses into triviality and the report is true; mine is that the whole architecture of the essay requires distinct names to carry distinct senses, so that the identity is informative and the report is not thereby true. We suspect at least one further reading is available that fits every verdict listed above and delivers something else again.

Answer in exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the readings of his view that his verdicts leave open; (2) what each reading rules on my three questions; (3) your best guess at his verdicts and the reason for preferring that reading; (4) the strongest pressure inside his own verdicts against that guess; (5) one further case, of your devising, that would decide between the readings.
