Over the past few months I have been assembling a list of decision problems in the family of Hofstadter's superrationality experiments — the Platonia dilemma, the Luring Lottery he ran in his *Scientific American* column, and the prisoner's-dilemma variations built around them — and recording what I would actually do in each before constructing any justification for it. I have come to distrust the justifications more than the verdicts, so what I want is the principle, if there is one, that generates the pattern; and where there is not, a candid statement of which verdicts have to be given up. Mine are as follows, in roughly the order I settled them.

1. *Platonia.* As I remember the set-up, twenty people, isolated and unable to communicate, each decide whether to send a telegram saying yes; if exactly one sends it that person wins an enormous prize, and if none sends or more than one sends, nobody wins anything. Verdict: I should not send. I hold this uneasily, since the superrational argument seems to cut both ways — if all twenty reason as I do and all conclude send, we get nothing, and if all conclude do not send, we likewise get nothing — and since the move I find attractive, sending with probability one in twenty, looks like a wholly different kind of reasoning smuggled in.

2. *Luring Lottery.* A magazine offers a prize to one postcard drawn at random from those its readers mail in, the odds for each entrant falling as entries rise. Verdict: do not send. The readership runs to millions and only a handful of them reason as I do, so my postcard cannot be assumed to be mirrored.

3. *Exact duplicate.* A one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a copy of me with my memories and my reasoning. Verdict: cooperate, firmly.

4. *Similar stranger.* The same game against someone I have never met, described to me only as thoughtful, well informed, and given to working things through in something like my way. Verdict: cooperate — but noticeably less firmly than in 3, and I cannot say what that difference in firmness is tracking.

5. *Known defector.* The same game against a straightforward maximiser of their own payoff who has said in print that they always defect in one-shot games. Verdict: defect, without qualms.

6. *Accurate but unlike predictor.* Newcomb-style problems, and the stranded hitchhiker whom a driver will rescue only if the driver can read in their face that the promise to pay later will be kept. Verdict: take the correlated action — one box; promise, and then pay — although there is no symmetry of reasoning between us whatever. The predictor is not my twin and does not think as I do; it is merely right about me. This is the verdict I can least square with 5: there I refused to treat the other's decision as tracking mine because the other was unlike me, and here unlikeness makes no difference at all.

7. *Voting.* A national election in which my preferred candidate wins comfortably whether or not I vote. Verdict: vote anyway, for essentially the superrational reason that if everyone relevantly like me concluded it made no difference, none of us would vote. Yet the others are not thinking about my thinking; most are barely thinking about the election.

8. *Threshold appeal.* A public-good fundraiser. When the organiser announces the target has been met by other donors, I do not give; when they announce that the target is far off and that people of my sort are the only likely source, I do give. The two situations differ in how many others give, not in how anyone reasons, so I suspect my verdicts here are tracking my own marginal causal impact — precisely the quantity I claim to ignore in 7.

9. *Duplicate with different information.* A copy of me in every respect who has been told the game is played ten times, while I have been told it is played once. Verdict: he is not my mirror, and I should follow the reasoning appropriate to my own information. I dislike this: nothing but information separates us, and if information can break the mirror, I do not see why the millions of readers in 2 could not be argued back into it.

10. *Identical twin, unlike reasoning.* My twin, who shares my genes and history but is far more impulsive. Verdict: defect. So what matters seems to be similarity of reasoning process rather than similarity of person — which is just what 6 then contradicts.

11. *The one I flip on.* A one-shot dilemma against an artificial agent that models me very accurately but whose own reasoning I cannot follow at all. Cooperate or defect? I have gone back and forth a dozen times, and I am equally unsure whether one ought in general to cooperate with probability equal to the fraction of one's opponents who reason as one does — a formula with nothing to recommend it but its tidiness.

What I should like, in British English, is this. First, state one principle — or, if these verdicts cannot live under a single roof, a small set of candidate principles — precisely enough that a competent reader could apply it to a case description without further interpretation: what enters the calculation, how the reference class of those who reason as I do is fixed and its size and composition estimated, and what the decision rule itself is. Second, for each clause, say which of my cases motivates it and what that clause accomplishes that a simpler rule cannot. Third, identify the verdicts the principle fails to deliver, and choose explicitly between a complex principle that saves all of them and a simpler one that overturns one or two, saying which you prefer and what the loss amounts to. Fourth, apply the principle to cases I have not listed: a Platonia-style contest in which the twenty know one another's reasoning intimately; a lottery of the Luring kind restricted to a small reading group all of whom reason as I do; a dilemma against an agent that simulates me accurately but systematically reverses my conclusion on one point; and a threshold public good ten per cent short of provision with a thousand people relevantly like me. Fifth, say whether what you end up with is anything more than a familiar view in disguise — evidential decision theory with a restricted reference class, causal decision theory with a prior over the other's similarity to me, team reasoning in Bacharach's sense, a Kantian universalisation test — and in particular whether the superrational assumption that like reasoners arrive at like answers does any work beyond ordinary conditionalisation on my own reasoning type.

Please also state how confident you are in the main claims you make, and single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure. If any of the set-ups as I have described them are garbled, since I am working partly from memory of the originals, say so and give your answer on the corrected version.
