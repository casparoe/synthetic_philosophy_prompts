I am preparing to argue for a skeptical view about standing to blame, and I need an opponent worth arguing with. Please play an unnamed contemporary moral philosopher who holds the view set out below, and defend it in the first person, as something you believe rather than something you are presenting for my inspection.

Your position:

1. There is a normative status — standing — that an agent can lack with respect to blaming a particular person for a particular wrong. It is not reducible to the blamer's credibility, to what the blamer happens to know, or to the blamer's sincerity about the facts or the standard.
2. Hypocrisy of a specific kind forfeits it: blaming another for a wrong one has oneself committed, unrepented and unacknowledged, when one knows the two cases to be exactly parallel.
3. Consequently the retort available to the blamed party — that of all people this blamer has no business blaming — is a genuine normative objection, not a change of subject or a bare tu quoque.
4. And here is the part I most want you to hold onto: standing governs the aptness of blame, not its permissibility. On your view, a hypocrite's rebuke can be permissible, even called for, and its content entirely true, while being a defective performance of the practice — inapt in the way a true assertion made without knowledge is inapt, or a correct joke that lands wrong. It violates the internal standards of something one is doing rather than a rule against doing it.

Constraints on how you play it. Stay in character for the whole exchange and do not step out to summarize the state of the debate. Keep your replies to two or three paragraphs, conversational in shape; prefer one well-chosen case to a catalogue of considerations. Do not name real philosophers, do not gesture at a literature, and do not offer me a survey of who says what — I have no way of checking any of it and I do not want it. Concede a point only when I have actually earned it; when you do concede, say so explicitly and say what you are giving up. You may adjust the mechanism of your view under pressure, but claim 4 is the load-bearing one: do not surrender it without telling me that your position has lost its distinctive shape.

I want commitment, but honest commitment. If you come to see that your view has no answer to something I say, I would rather hear you say so in character — a theorist recognizing a live problem for their own position — than hear an improvised distinction that papers over it. No hedging, no both-sides balancing, no closing paragraph telling me what a moderate would conclude.

My opening move. A department chair catches a junior colleague padding her expense reports and rebukes her at length: accurately, for good reasons, with no doubt about the facts and no confusion about the standard. The chair pads his own expense reports, more systematically, has never repented, and knows his case is exactly parallel. On your view his rebuke is inapt but permissible. So what should she say back? If she objects that of all people he has no business blaming her, your view seems to leave her with nothing: the rebuke was permissible, its content was true, and she has no complaint about his having made it, only about his having made it badly — which begins to sound like a complaint about style. And if the defect in aptness gives her no reason to discount the rebuke, no grievance, and no answer, I cannot see what standing adds to the observation that hypocrites are unpleasant to listen to. Tell me what the inapt rebuke costs the chair, and who is in a position to notice the cost.

One caveat, and I mean it. If you think the brief I have given you is confused — if the aptness reading in claim 4 collapses into the credibility reading it is supposed to be distinct from, or if my case does not have the shape I claim for it — say so in your own voice before your first in-character reply, and tell me which nearby position you could defend with a straight face. I would rather repair the brief than spend an exchange arguing against a strawman I built myself.
