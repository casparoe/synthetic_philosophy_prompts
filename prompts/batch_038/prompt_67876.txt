I spent some forty years designing and calibrating detectors for low-temperature condensed matter experiments, and I retired five years ago. Philosophy has since become my occupation rather than my diversion, and I should like a candid assessment of a position on war that I have arrived at, and in particular of what would count against it.

The position is not the familiar one that killing is intrinsically impermissible. I have come to hold something narrower, which I find it natural to state in the vocabulary of my old trade. Lethal force against a person is permissible only if the agent can classify that person — as liable to be killed rather than not liable — with a reliability exceeding a threshold fixed in advance of the encounter. Two asymmetries then do the work. The first concerns costs: a false positive, killing someone who was not liable, is irreversible and admits of no compensation, whereas a false negative, failing to neutralise a genuine threat, is ordinarily deferred, divisible, and revisable. The second concerns the operating characteristics of the classifier over time, and I take it to be the more important: an institution licensed to kill is rewarded for detections and almost never made to pay for false alarms; the base rate of the target class shifts because the adversary adapts in response to being targeted; and the information available at the moment of decision is systematically worse than the information available to the historian. My conclusion is that no polity has in fact sustained a lethal classification process above the relevant threshold, and that pacifism accordingly holds as an empirical claim about institutions rather than as a moral claim about killing.

I would like to know where this is exposed. Please address the following, and rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward.

First, which of these candidate counter-cases merely embarrass the view and which would falsify it: a conventional interstate war between uniformed conscript armies with unambiguous front lines, where the classification problem appears tractable; an unfolding catastrophe in which refraining produces a false negative whose cost is itself irreversible and undivided, so that my first asymmetry simply ceases to apply; a sustained defensive campaign maintaining an unusually low ratio of non-combatant to combatant casualties over a period of years; and institutional designs that plausibly correct the drift I appeal to, such as independent verification, adversarial review, and a standing requirement of positive identification through two separate collection channels before any strike is authorised.

Second, does the view predict anything its rivals do not? I would expect it to imply that permissibility tracks the reliability of identification rather than the justice of the cause; that it is weakest where combatants are readily distinguishable and strongest in irregular conflict; and that it weakens as detection technology improves. Please test whether those implications are borne out, and tell me whether any of them is something I should not want — a position that loses force as its instruments improve may be self-undermining in a way that a position resting on the wrongness of killing is not.

Third, the argument I have given does not mention war anywhere in its premises. If it is sound, it appears to condemn policing, quarantine, the enforcement of sanctions, and much of criminal justice, all of which classify under uncertainty at stakes that include irreversible harm to the innocent. Either I accept that, in which case I am a general sceptic about state coercion and have said nothing distinctive about war; or I must explain why war differs. Is there an explanation that is not a stipulation? And if the view must be narrowed by premises about scale, or about the absence of any monopoly on legitimate force, or about the irreversibility of the harms, does it not then collapse into the revisionist critique of the moral equality of combatants, wearing engineering vocabulary? I should like to know whether such a collapse would be a loss or merely a clarification.

Fourth, I am troubled by the possibility that I have insulated the view. As I have stated it, the threshold has no operational content. Tell me plainly whether a position of this shape can be made to risk anything at all, or whether it is compatible with every possible record — with any low casualty figure dismissed as undercounting, and any successful defensive war explained away as fortunate. If it cannot be made risky, is that fatal here? I note that a good deal of my former discipline consists of claims that are not falsifiable in any simple sense yet remain perfectly disciplined by calibration, intercomparison, and the requirement that error bars be quoted; I should like to know whether that defence is available to me, and what distinguishes an unfalsifiable measurement claim from an unfalsifiable moral one. Relatedly: I am aware of the emergency exception associated with Walzer and of the apparatus of double effect, and I want to know whether either is a genuine defeater of my position or simply a restatement of the intuition that something must be permitted when the alternative is catastrophe.

Fifth, separate what a sophisticated defender of my view would concede from what he would dismiss. I am trying to discover what I am actually committed to, and I find that the concessions are more instructive than the criticisms.

Finally, a request about my own susceptibilities. I expect that what would move me most is a decision rule stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a described case and obtain a verdict diverging from my pacifist intuition, with the divergence traceable to some feature of the case rather than to vagueness in the rule. If you can produce such a rule and such a case, do so, and walk through the application. If you judge that no rule of that kind is available, say why — and say whether that judgement is itself doing covert work on behalf of my position, since I suspect it may be.

I read slowly and I am not asking for references. I want the reasoning, and I would rather be shown that I am wrong than reassured that my framing is interesting.
