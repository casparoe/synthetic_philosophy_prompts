I cover artificial-intelligence research for a general-interest science desk. Last week I sat in on a closed workshop whose stated purpose was to settle what a safety claim about a deployed model should actually consist of. Two participants — call them A, who works on formal guarantees, and B, who runs adversarial evaluation at a lab — argued for the better part of an hour, and I could not tell which of them was right. I would like a referee's report rather than a summary.

The exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) A opened by arguing that a guarantee stated as average performance over a test distribution is worthless for alignment purposes, because deployment is not sampling from that distribution: a single input, however improbable, can produce a catastrophic and irreversible outcome, so the only meaningful claim is one that holds for every input, including the cleverest one an attacker can construct.

(2) B replied that a worst-case bound over an unrestricted input space is either so loose that it licenses nothing or so tight that it forces the system to refuse nearly everything, and that what protects users in practice is a sharp statement about typical behavior plus an honest accounting of the residual tail; insisting otherwise, B said, drives teams to substitute private judgment while claiming rigor.

(3) A said the tail is exactly where adversaries live: an average-case guarantee is a guarantee only when nobody is trying to break it, which is the one situation in which you least need one, and it silently assumes the deployment distribution resembles the evaluation distribution — the assumption a jailbreak exists to violate.

(4) B conceded part of this and proposed a middle route: specify the adversary explicitly — bounded compute, bounded query access, a stated number of attempts — certify against that class, and publish the specification so that others can attack it. That, B said, is worst-case reasoning done honestly rather than aspirationally.

(5) A's closing move was that this quietly reintroduces the reasoning B had just rejected, since the adversary class will be drawn so that the attacks the lab already knows how to generate fall inside it, and the threat worth worrying about — an attacker more ingenious than the model of attackers — is precisely the case assumed away. Publishing an assumption, A said, protects no one from its failure.

Neither budged, and the workshop report ended up containing both positions side by side. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, and say who is right about which specific point rather than only who wins overall. Then tell me which of the two is more right on the whole, and identify any assumption or distinction that both of them are treating as settled but that a referee ought not let pass.
