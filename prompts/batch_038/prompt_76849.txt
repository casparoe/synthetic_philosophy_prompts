I am working out a puzzle about the badness of pain, and I want your judgment on how one particular philosopher would have resolved it. Treat what follows as a self-contained brief: everything you need is stated here, and I would much rather you reason hard over this material than reach for the secondary literature. No bibliography, please, and no invented textual references — if you think a point turns on a passage you cannot recall, say so and argue from the doctrine instead.

**The phenomenon.** There is a well-documented condition, usually called pain asymbolia, in which a person goes on feeling pain but is no longer bothered by it. Lesions — particularly of the insular cortex and of its connections with limbic structures such as the cingulate — can abolish the aversive quality of noxious stimulation while leaving intact the ability to say where the pain is, what sort of pain it is, and how intense it is. Comparable dissociations are produced by cingulotomy and, in a milder and more familiar form, by opioids: the patient reports the same intensity as before and reports that it simply no longer matters. Asymbolic patients do not withdraw from or guard against noxious stimuli in the ordinary way; some reportedly smile at them; over time they accumulate burns, cuts and joint damage that aversion would have prevented. For the sake of argument, stipulate one further feature: such a patient retains every intellectual judgment about the situation — knows the stimulus is damaging, knows he should move his arm, can do so if he attends to it — and what is missing is precisely the affective push that normally makes that knowledge move him without deliberation.

**The commitments.** Consider Descartes. In the Sixth Meditation he treats pain, hunger and thirst as modes of thinking that arise from the mind's union with the body and are confused rather than clear and distinct; their function, on his account of the institution of nature, is to signify to the soul what is beneficial or harmful to the composite, and they are reliable enough for the conduct of life without being infallible. His own illustration of the failure case is the dropsical man who is thirsty when drinking will harm him: nature's teaching misleads because the body-machine is disordered, as a badly made clock still tells the wrong time by its own mechanism, and this does not impugn the general order of nature. In the Passions he holds that the passions are perceptions caused and sustained by movements of the animal spirits, that their use is to dispose the soul to will what nature indicates as useful and to persist in that volition, that sadness is the passion attendant on the representation of some harm, and that while the will cannot switch a passion on or off directly, it can divert attention, reattach thoughts, and — through firm and determinate judgments — govern what the passions move it to do.

**What I want from you.** Where would Descartes locate the badness of pain: in the confused sensation itself, or in the sadness and aversion that the sensation normally recruits? Then answer the harder question that his own system makes available. On the face of it, the asymbolic patient has achieved by brain damage what Descartes recommends by resolution: he judges that the harm is not the evil he takes it for, and the judgment no longer meets any resistance from below. Call this, for short, the unintended sage. Does Descartes' philosophy entitle him to welcome the case as a confirmation — showing that pain as sensation and pain as evil really are separable, exactly as his account of the passions requires — or must he treat it as a reductio, showing that the aversion is not an optional add-on but part of what makes the sensation a sign at all, so that a wholly unbothered pain has lost its place in the institution of nature? Note that the dropsy precedent cuts both ways, and say which way he turns it. Finally: on this reading, is the asymbolic patient still harmed by his pain, and what becomes of the claim that pain is bad? If the badness is wholly in the aversion, then abolishing the aversion abolishes the badness, and the condition starts to look like a cure rather than a deficit — tell me whether Descartes accepts that consequence or blocks it, and how.

I want a definite verdict, not a survey of what his texts might permit. Commit to one answer on the central question — sensation or aversion — state it in a single sentence up front, and argue for it from the commitments I have set out. Where his system genuinely admits both readings, do not leave me to choose: tell me which you would bet on, what the bet costs you, and what would have to be true for the opposite verdict. Close with a short note on the one or two places where your reconstruction is most exposed, including any place where you think my summary of his position is itself contestable.
