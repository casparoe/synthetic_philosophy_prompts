I write novels — literary fiction, mostly about ordinary people behaving badly in emergencies — and the book I'm finishing has a scene that's going to get me in trouble. A ferry goes down off a coast that isn't mine. My narrator is in a small boat that will swamp with six people in it, and she pulls her brother aboard and leaves five strangers in the water. Later she defends herself, and she defends herself well: she's read the anti-aggregation literature, she knows the argument that five deaths aren't one big death and there's no single subject anywhere who loses five times over, she knows its a claim about what she was permitted to do rather than about which outcome was better. My problem is that right now she sounds like a clever person rationalising, and I dont want that. I want a reader to close the book unable to shake her argument.

In three weeks I'm on a festival stage interviewing the philosopher who's probably the most eloquent living defender of this Taurekian line — forty minutes, me asking the questions, audience half novel-readers half grad students. I'd rather the interview do double duty as research, so I want questions that are actually about the content of his view and that also hand me material I can put in her mouth.

Give me ten, in three batches. (a) Three that pin down what he's actually committed to: is the thesis axiological, or about permission, or both — and what happens when the numbers get huge, does a million deaths really fail to be worse than one? (b) Four that go where I think it hurts: the no-subject-of-the-aggregate-loss premise and what it does to the case of five of my own fingers versus one; the "you'd want to be saved if you were one of the five" symmetry objection; the coin-flip-as-equal-concern move and whether weighting that coin is just letting the numbers count in disguise; and where the line sits between saving your brother and letting five die to avoid ruining your shoes. (c) Three that drag the view out of the lifeboat and into institutions — a coast guard, a hospital triage protocol, a firm writing the decision rules for a fleet of rescue drones — and ask whether it goes quiet exactly where it would matter, and if so, what could account for the difference.

For every question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, not the one I'd like him to give. He's charming and he deflects beautifully, so a follow-up beginning "but surely..." is useless to me; I need the second move already loaded.

No softballs. Nothing about what drew him to philosophy, nothing a utilitarian could answer identically, and nothing whose only job is to let him restate the view in nicer words.

Last thing, and I mean it: after the questions, tell me where you think you've got this wrong. Which of your predicted replies are you least sure he'd actually give, which of my framings caricature the position, and what would change your mind about whether the view is coherent rather than just a good paradox? If you think one of my pressure points doesn't bite at all — if he has a decent answer to the five-fingers thing, say — tell me straight, because I'd much rather find that out now than in print.
