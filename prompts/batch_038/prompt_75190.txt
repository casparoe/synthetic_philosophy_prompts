I've spent the last year writing software for an inertial navigation unit — ring laser gyros and accelerometers, nothing external — and the hardware keeps raising a question I have not been able to settle. The unit reports its own rotation without reference to anything: it senses a turn as a rate to be integrated, and that rate is a rate with respect to no body at all, not the ground, not the sea, not the stars. Its velocity, by contrast, is only ever meaningful relative to a chosen frame, and every handbook we issue to new engineers says so explicitly. Yet when we characterize drift we compare the unit's zero against a celestial frame defined by distant quasars, and among the relativistic corrections we apply is a term for the local standard of non-rotation being dragged along, by a few parts in a trillion, by the rotation of the Earth itself — which makes the zero point sound like a fact about where the matter is, after all.

What metaphysics does this practice presuppose? Is an inertial navigation system essentially substantivalist hardware — a technological descendant of Newton's bucket, intelligible only if space or spacetime is something rotation can be measured against — or is it equally licensed by a relational view on which all motion is motion relative to the total distribution of matter, and the gyro reading is merely an extraordinarily stable proxy for that? Reconstruct the implicit commitment, distinguishing what the instrument strictly requires from what it merely coheres comfortably with, and say where in the practice the metaphysics is genuinely underdetermined — where several incompatible accounts of space would license exactly the same calibration procedure, the same drift budget, the same confidence in a position fix. Then tell me whether the substantivalist and relational readings diverge anywhere an engineer could actually detect. If they never do, is the choice between them empty, or does something in how we specify, calibrate, and trust the instrument still commit us to one side?
