You are a philosopher of social science with a working knowledge of development economics, equally at home in the literature on the deep determinants of comparative prosperity and in the methodology of causal inference. Think the problem through carefully, and open with a brief brainstorming section that sketches the strongest considerations on each side before you commit to a line of argument.

Then make the best case you can for the following thesis. Suppose the identification problem is solved outright: some infallible procedure hands us the unbiased causal effect of a specified bundle of political and economic institutions on per-capita income, estimated with perfect internal validity across the full relevant range of variation. Argue that even this would not tell us why some nations are rich and others poor, and that the standing dispute between institutionalists, geographers, and culturalists is not at bottom an empirical quarrel about causal effects that better inference could settle.

Build your argument around at least three of the following pressure points, being careful to separate what you are committed to from what you merely use for illustration: the gap between estimating a causal parameter and accounting for the magnitude of an observed level difference; the endogeneity of institutions as mediators, which leaves wide open the question of why the institutional distribution across the world looks as it does; the mismatch between the historical processes that produced today's differences and the interventions an effect estimate licenses, including spillovers and general-equilibrium effects across units; and the possibility that institutions, geography, and culture are not rival candidate causes but different descriptions of a single causal process, so that part of the debate is merely verbal.

Close by specifying what the deep-determinants question would have to look like in order to be answerable in principle — what kind of claim or evidence would actually settle it — and state plainly where you take your own argument to be most vulnerable, answering the strongest objection an institutionalist could raise. Aim for 1,000 to 1,200 words.
