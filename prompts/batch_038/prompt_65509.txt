I am four years into a novel set in a small state that, after a currency collapse, handed most of its consequential decisions to conditional prediction markets — the arrangement usually credited to Robin Hanson under the name futarchy, in which a legislature agrees on the measure of national welfare it wants and then lets traders' odds on what would happen under each proposed policy determine which policy is adopted. To get the book right I have spent a long time with the proposal and with the objections to it, and I have a friend, an economist who reads drafts of my worst chapters, who keeps telling me that the objection I care most about is old and has a name, but she will not say which one, and I have begun to suspect she is wrong that it is old at all. What I cannot find a label for goes like this.

The device is supposed to earn its authority from being right, but the only way it can be shown to be right is by having its advice taken, and having its advice taken changes what the advice was about. Concretely: the market prices a bank's failure at thirty per cent, that price becomes public as the state's official estimate, depositors act on it, the bank fails, and the market is scored accurate — though the accuracy was manufactured by the scorekeeping. Or the reverse, which interests me more: the market prices an insurgency's ceasefire at eighty per cent, the peace is treated as nearly settled, the parties who had been paying to sustain it stop paying, and the ceasefire collapses. The estimate was credible, and being credible is precisely what destroyed it. And in the governance version there is a third twist, the one my third act lives on: the market is asked what would happen under the policy it recommends, but if the recommendation is followed, the counterfactual branch never occurs, so the single judgment that would test the device is the one judgment the device forecloses by being obeyed. What I seem to have is a configuration in which an instrument's reliability is a decreasing function of its own credibility — the more completely it is trusted, the less able it is to be tested, and the less able it is to be tested, the more it must be trusted.

My instinct is that this is Goodhart's law, or a self-fulfilling prophecy, or what people mean when they say that an economic theory performs the economy it describes, and I have tried each of these in the margin and each feels wrong by a small amount. Goodhart concerns a measure that is optimized until it comes apart from the thing it measured, but in my cases nobody is gaming the measure; it corrupts itself simply by being believed. A self-fulfilling prophecy is standardly a false belief that becomes true, whereas here the belief may have been perfectly calibrated at the moment it was formed, and it is the publication that ruins the calibration. And the performativity idea, as I understand it, treats a model's shaping of its object as something that can run in either direction and does not imply that a model becomes less accurate the more authoritative it gets. So: does the configuration I have described have an established name, in the prediction-market literature, in social epistemology, or anywhere else? If it has a family of near-names, I want to know which one actually fits and exactly where each of the others stops fitting — I will not be satisfied with a bare term, because the carving is what I need for the book. If nothing fits, coin one, with necessary conditions, explicit exclusions, and at least one boundary case on each side so I can see the edges. And please commit rather than hand me a menu: tell me which single diagnosis you think is right, give your reasons, and say what you give up by saying so. If you think the configuration is incoherent — that what I have described cannot happen, or that the third twist dissolves once you notice how conditional markets are supposed to be settled — say that plainly instead of humouring the novelist.
