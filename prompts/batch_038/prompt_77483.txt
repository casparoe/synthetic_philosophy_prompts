My father is seventy-one. In March he had a stroke, and though he recovered most of his speech and his walking, something shifted in the way he meets injury. Last month he rested his hand against the rim of the stove and kept it there long enough to blister; he noticed only because my sister pointed at it. He has a pressure sore on his hip from sitting too long in one position, and he will describe it to you with perfect accuracy — its location, its strength on a scale of one to ten, whether it throbs or burns — and then return to his crossword. Ask him if it hurts and he says yes, without hesitation or complaint. Ask him whether it bothers him and he says no, and he means it. He does not grimace, does not favor the hip, does not ask for anything, and he declined the analgesics offered to him after a minor procedure on the grounds that he saw no reason to take them.

His neurologist was matter-of-fact about all this, said the pattern had a name and that it fit the location of the stroke. So I am not asking a medical question — he is monitored, the wound is dressed, and we have been told what to watch for. I am asking about something I have been turning over for weeks and that nobody else in the family seems willing to sit with.

I keep sensing a contradiction in the description of my own father, and I cannot find where it is. On one hand it seems obvious that pain is bad in virtue of what it is like: no one avoids it because of an opinion they hold about it. On the other hand, my father appears to have the entire feeling and none of the not-wanting, and the thing we would have called his suffering has quietly vanished. So which of those two was the badness all along? If it was the feeling, then he is being badly hurt and simply cannot register that he is, and I ought to be far more alarmed now than I was in April when he was complaining. If it was the not-wanting, then he is plausibly better off than he was, which is not a sentence I can say aloud without feeling like a monster.

I would like help working through this, and I would rather you be direct with me than agreeable:

1. Is a report like his — it hurts, but I do not mind — actually coherent, or is one of its two halves getting something wrong? What would have to be true of pain for both halves to be honest at once, and what would have to be true for one of them to be a kind of self-deception or a failure of introspection?

2. What does his case tell us about where the badness of pain lives: in the sensation, in the reaction to it, or in some relation between the two? Take the main available accounts seriously, and tell me which his case supports, which it embarrasses, and which it fails to decide between. If some of these accounts make different predictions about what he is like when no one is watching him, say so, because that is testable in a household.

3. There is a wrinkle I cannot stop thinking about. I once read — and I genuinely do not know how reliable it was, so please treat it as a rumor and tell me whether it survives scrutiny — that opioid analgesics work largely not by making pain feel less intense but by making it cease to matter, so that patients report the pain is still there and simply stop caring about it. If that is even roughly right, is my father in an accidental version of the state the drugs were meant to produce? And does that make such drugs a treatment for suffering, or a way of manufacturing indifference while the damage continues unchecked?

4. Give me the practical upshot, because decisions fall on me here. When a person reports pain and also reports not minding it, what exactly should his family and his doctors be treating? Is there anything left to relieve? Does his assurance settle the question of whether he is harmed, and if not, what could settle it?

5. Finally, a question about me rather than him. I find I am more disturbed by his indifference than I ever was by his complaints — more disturbed, in fact, than I am when my brother describes the same facts without any distress. Is that squeamishness, an inability to tolerate an unfamiliar picture, or am I tracking something real about his condition that the calm response misses? I want to know whether my unease counts as evidence for anything.

I am not looking for reassurance, and I am not looking for a checklist of questions to bring to his physician. I want the underlying issue argued through, with whatever distinctions turn out to be necessary to make it come apart cleanly, and I want to be told plainly if some part of what I have written here is confused.
