I have no academic training in philosophy. I spent thirty-one years as a freight dispatcher, and since retiring I have been reading on my own and trying to write down, as precisely as I can manage, what I actually believe about where a mind stops and the world begins. I read the 1998 Clark and Chalmers paper on the extended mind years ago and have been arguing with it ever since. What follows is my current position. I suspect not all of it fits together, but I cannot tell where the damage is, and I have no idea which piece I would give up — so please do not be polite about it and do not tell me the set is basically coherent if it is not.

1. I accept the parity idea in full. If a process would obviously count as thinking when it happens inside a skull, then it counts as thinking when it happens outside one, and the only honest difference between the two is location.

2. I accept the notebook case. The man with the failing memory who writes everything down and consults it without hesitation really does believe, in the dispositional sense, that the museum is where his notebook says it is. His belief is not a second-rate imitation of the intact-memory woman's belief.

3. But it counts only because of how the notebook is hooked up to him: always there, invoked automatically, its contents taken on trust without further checking, endorsed by him back when he started keeping it. Merely being within reach of useful information does not make that information part of anyone's mind.

4. Here is a condition of my own, which I have not seen anyone state this way. Call it the scarcity condition: an external resource is part of my mind only if a stranger could not get the same result by walking up to the same resource. If anybody can have it for the looking, it belongs to the world rather than to any of us. This is why I refuse to say a search engine is part of anyone's mind, and why I am happy to say it of my own paper ledger of fuel surcharges, whose abbreviations are mine alone and useless to anyone else.

5. My wife and I have kept one shared calendar on the refrigerator door for forty years. Neither of us could get through a week without it. I want to say it is part of both of our minds.

6. I see no inconsistency in holding all of the above while denying that the notebook, the ledger, or the calendar has any experience whatsoever. Extending the mind does not extend consciousness, and I cannot see why the paper would feel anything.

7. Knowing is more than being reliably right. If someone never once checks whether their source has gone bad, and the source does go bad, and they end up correct anyway by luck, I do not want to call that knowledge. Keeping some sort of eye on your sources is part of what separates knowing from a fortunate guess.

8. Even so, I want to say the man with the notebook knows where the museum is, in exactly the sense in which the woman with the intact memory knows it.

Please audit this set. Lead with a one-sentence verdict on whether it is jointly tenable. Then list the tensions in order of severity — worst first, not all presented as equally bad — and for each say which commitment has to go or be weakened, and what the cheapest repair would cost me. Where two items only look like they conflict, say so explicitly and say why, rather than padding the list. Do not recommend further reading. Hard limit: 450 words for the entire response.
