I run a small podcast that grew out of a rationalist meetup, and next week's guest is a working New Testament scholar — mainstream critical, not a mythicist and not an apologist — who thinks the criteria of authenticity, used carefully, actually deliver a defensible minimal portrait of Jesus. He has a go-to deflection. Press him on circularity or on the bias baked into dissimilarity and he says the criteria are heuristics, not algorithms, and that they're just a formalized version of the weighing that every historian does anyway. I want a run of eight to ten questions that makes that move progressively harder to keep using.

Coverage I want, one question each at minimum:

- The criterion of embarrassment read as a likelihood ratio. You can't estimate how likely an awkward detail is to be preserved without knowing what the redactors found awkward, and you infer that from what they preserved. Also worth noting that they usually didn't delete awkward material, they kept it and explained it — the baptism by John is retained but reframed so that John protests it should be the other way round, and the cry of abandonment on the cross survives in the earlier gospels and gets replaced in Luke and John. Does retain-and-reframe count for or against authenticity, and does his answer change depending which case he's looking at?
- Double disimilarity. If you only admit material that looks like neither first-century Judaism nor the early church, your output is by construction a pile of exceptions. What kind of person can that method produce, and can a portrait made entirely of residues support any positive claim about what he was mainly about?
- Multiple attestation. Independent in what sense — sources, or forms? And is a pass on this criterion evidence about Jesus, or evidence about how far back a tradition stream goes?
- The background-model problem. The criteria are calibrated against a picture of first-century Judaism and of early Christian tendencies that was itself built largely out of the same texts. I want him to give one case where a change in that background picture flipped a verdict, and say whether that's a feature.
- Asymmetry. As far as I can tell the criteria only ever license inclusion. Is there a saying he used to count as authentic and has since ruled out? If not, what's the mechanism by which his method could be wrong?
- The social-memory objection, that there's no clean line between a stable core and later interpretation — but press his likely reply, which will be that the memory people are doing criteria-shaped inference with the serial numbers filed off.

Audience note: half my listeners are Bayes-brained and will think this is trivially a likelihood-ratio problem, and I want the episode to be fair rather than a cage match. I've been reading the same blogs since before I had a beard and my reflex is epistemic learned helplessness about anything Jesus-shaped, so make the good questions genuinely answerable by someone who knows the field. Every question gets a follow-up keyed to the specific answer he's most likely to give, with the next move already written out. Don't build anything on a citation I'd have to look up — names are fine as gestures, but the substance should be the argument. End with one that makes him name the single most load-bearing assumption in the whole approach and say what happens to his conclusions if it's false. No softballs about how he got into the field.
