**Question 3 (20 points), as it appeared on the paper:**

State the distinction that the doctrine of double effect draws between what an agent intends and what she merely foresees, and give the further conditions that standard formulations of the doctrine add. Then apply the doctrine to each of the following:

(i) A runaway trolley is heading toward five workers on the main track. The driver can steer onto a siding, where one worker will be killed. He steers onto the siding; the five are saved.

(ii) As in (i), except that the siding rejoins the main track downstream of the five. Unless the trolley strikes the one worker on the siding, it will continue on and kill the five. The driver steers onto the siding; the trolley strikes and kills the one worker and stops; the five are saved.

Explain why defenders of the doctrine hold that it yields different verdicts on (i) and (ii). Then set out one objection that this pair of cases is standardly said to raise for the doctrine, identifying who pressed the objection and what the conceptual difficulty is supposed to be.

**The candidate's answer:**

The doctrine of double effect is, strictly speaking, a doctrine about the agent rather than about the act: it tells us when a person escapes blame for harm she has brought about. It is applied, as I shall apply it here, to the permissibility of acts, which is why it is usually described as a constraint on action.

The distinction is between what the agent intends — what she aims at, either as her end or as a means to her end — and what she merely foresees, namely those consequences of her action that she takes to be certain or highly likely but that play no part in her plan. Traditional formulations add three further conditions to the requirement that the bad effect be merely foreseen. The act itself must be good or at least indifferent in kind. The good effect must not be produced by means of the bad effect, but must follow from the action at least as immediately as the bad effect does. And there must be a proportionately grave reason for permitting the bad effect: the good must be worth the harm. The doctrine is generally traced to Aquinas's discussion of whether it is permissible to kill an assailant in self-defence.

The standard illustration is the pair of bombers. The strategic bomber who attacks a munitions plant, foreseeing that children nearby will be killed, does not intend their deaths; they lie outside his plan, and he would gladly bomb the plant without them. The terror bomber who attacks a civilian population in order to break the enemy's morale does intend those deaths, since the demoralisation works through their suffering. Both produce the same harm for the same military benefit, but the doctrine permits only the first.

Foot introduced the runaway trolley in order to give the doctrine a clean illustration of exactly this structure. The driver who steers onto the siding foresees but does not intend the death of the one worker: the death plays no part in a plan that consists simply in getting the trolley off the main track. Compare the judge who frames an innocent man in order to appease a threatening mob; there the death of the innocent man is precisely what the plan requires. So the doctrine permits the driver's steering and forbids the judge's framing, and the trolley case has served ever since as the standard exhibit of the doctrine's explanatory power.

The loop version is meant to break that. Notice, though, that the loop driver has exactly the same intention as the driver in the first case. What he aims at in both is that the trolley not reach the five; in neither case does he want anyone to die, and in both he would be delighted if the one worker were to step clear at the last moment. The only difference between the two cases is a fact about the track layout downstream of the switch, and it cannot be that a moral distinction rides on that. Quinn's treatment of these cases is generally taken to concede the point: he presses what is called the closeness problem, and, finding no non-arbitrary way to separate what the loop driver intends from what the first driver intends, he gives up the intention-based doctrine in favour of a straightforwardly consequentialist assessment of cases of this kind. If that is right, then the pair (i)–(ii) does not merely embarrass the doctrine; it shows the doctrine to be an unworkable attempt to make permissibility depend on a distinction — between intending and foreseeing — that cannot be drawn in the very cases where it is supposed to matter most.
