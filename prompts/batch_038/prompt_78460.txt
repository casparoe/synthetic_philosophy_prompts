Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning over a series of choices he wrote out for me on the back of an envelope, and I have not managed to settle the matter in my own head since. He is a graduate student in economics; I am not, and I have no formal training in decision theory. His position is that my answers are jointly incoherent and that I should simply withdraw one or two of them. My suspicion is that there is a pattern to what I chose, but I cannot state it, and his account of what I must be doing seems to me to leave out something obvious. I would like a careful assessment of whether my verdicts add up to a principle.

Here are the choices and what I chose. In each case the sums are real, the choice is made once, and I would be choosing for myself.

1. Option A: $1,000,000 with certainty. Option B: a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. I chose A, without much hesitation.

2. Option C: an 11% chance of $1,000,000 and an 89% chance of nothing. Option D: a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and a 90% chance of nothing. I chose D, also without hesitation.

3. He then ran the same shapes with the sums scaled down by a factor of five thousand. $200 with certainty against a 10% chance of $1,000, an 89% chance of $200, and a 1% chance of nothing: I take the gamble. And an 11% chance of $200 against a 10% chance of $1,000, otherwise nothing: I take the 10% chance of $1,000. At this scale I choose the riskier option in both pairs and nothing reverses. I could not say why $1,000 felt like play money and the larger figures did not.

4. He restated choice 1 as a drawing of tickets: one hundred numbered tickets in a hat, and under A every ticket pays $1,000,000, while under B tickets 1 through 89 pay $1,000,000, tickets 90 through 99 pay $5,000,000, and ticket 100 pays nothing. I still chose A. Being shown precisely which ticket does the damage did not move me at all.

5. He then restated B as a two-stage affair: a ticket is drawn from the hundred; if it falls in 1 through 89 I am handed $1,000,000 and sent home; if it falls in 90 through 100, a second drawing from an urn of eleven balls decides, ten of which pay $5,000,000 and one of which pays nothing. Set against A, the certain million, I chose B. I want to be explicit that I take this to be the very same lottery as the B of choices 1 and 4; only the description changed, and my choice flipped with it. I am not proud of this verdict, but I am confident I would make it again.

6. Option A: $1,000,000 with certainty. Option B: a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and a 90% chance of $900,000, so that no branch pays nothing. I chose B.

7. $3,000 with certainty against an 80% chance of $4,000: I take the certain $3,000. But a 25% chance of $3,000 against a 20% chance of $4,000: I take the 20% chance of $4,000.

My own guess at what I am doing is that I refuse any chance of walking away empty-handed when a substantial sum is on offer with certainty, and that where both options risk nothing I simply compare the money. That cannot be right, because in choice 2 I do precisely what it forbids, and in choice 5 the same prospect is acceptable or unacceptable depending on how it is narrated to me. So I am offering it only as a starting point to be repaired or discarded.

What I would like from you is the following.

First: is there a principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that delivers all seven verdicts? State each precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to a pair I have not seen, and identify which of my verdicts motivates each clause of it. Where a candidate needs machinery beyond a straightforward trade of money against probability — a constraint or floor rather than a weighting, a treatment of the two-stage description in choice 5 as a genuinely different object from the reduced lottery, a value curve whose shape depends on the scale of the sums at stake — say what that machinery is, why it is needed, and what it costs me to accept it.

Second: if the only route to keeping all seven is complexity, tell me instead what the simplest principle that keeps most of them says, which verdicts it forces me to abandon, and how much credence I should give the intuitions I reported about those. I suspect that verdicts 5 and 7 cannot both survive, though I cannot see why they would not.

Third: apply your preferred account to two choices on which I have no verdict to report and have not thought things through. In the first, A is $1,000,000 with certainty and B is a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of owing $50,000. In the second, one option is a 99.9% chance of $1,000,000 with a 0.1% chance of nothing, and the other is a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89.9% chance of $1,000,000, and a 0.1% chance of nothing. Then propose one further choice of your own devising that would most sharply discriminate between the accounts you are weighing, and state what each of them predicts for it.

Fourth, and separately: my friend advanced two claims that I would like evaluated on their own terms. One is that the assumption my choices offend — he called it independence at one point and the sure-thing principle at another, and I could not tell whether he took these to be the same claim — is not a substantive assumption at all but something evident the moment it is stated, so that a violation is not a disagreement about how much risk to bear but a failure of arithmetic. The other is that anyone who chooses as I did in 1 and 2 can be walked through a sequence of small paid exchanges that leaves him steadily poorer, indefinitely, provided only that he keeps answering consistently. Are those claims correct as stated? And if my verdicts do turn out to be captured by some coherent principle, does his charge of irrationality retain any content, or does it amount to a preference for a rival principle dressed up as a requirement of thought?
