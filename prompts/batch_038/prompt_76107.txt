I am preparing a position paper on the foundations of formal epistemology, and before I commit it to print I would like an audit of the package of views I have settled on. I should say at the outset that I do not know where the weak points are: I arrived at these commitments one at a time over several years and have never systematically checked them against each other, so please do not assume that any of them is more expendable than any other, and please do not simply return the whole list to me as jointly questionable.

Here is the package.

(1) Accuracy monism. The only fundamental source of epistemic normative significance is the accuracy of one's credences — their closeness to the truth, as measured by a strictly proper scoring rule such as the Brier score. Nothing else does independent normative work here: not coherence valued for its own sake, not reliability, not practical success, not any consideration concerning how one's credences would guide action.

(2) Probabilism, on dominance grounds. It is irrational to hold a credence function that violates the probability axioms, and the correct argument for this is the accuracy-dominance argument: any incoherent credence function is strictly less accurate, at every possible world, than some coherent alternative, relative to any strictly proper scoring rule. Given (1), that settles the matter.

(3) Conditionalization, on the same kind of grounds. On acquiring total evidence E, one is required to move to one's prior conditionalized on E. The argument I take to establish this is again an accuracy-based dominance argument, not a pragmatic one.

(4) Uniqueness. For any total body of evidence, exactly one credal state is rationally permitted; permissivism is false. Moreover, the route by which evidence pins down that unique state must itself be accuracy-based — I want no residue of evidential constraint that does not ultimately reduce to accuracy as characterized in (1).

(5) Required imprecision in evidential voids. Where one has literally no relevant evidence — the bias of a coin about which one knows nothing whatever, or whether some unobservable parameter falls in a given interval — rationality does not merely permit but requires an imprecise credal state: a set of probability functions rather than a single one. Any sharp assignment in such a case manufactures a precision one does not possess and so misrepresents one's evidential situation.

(6) Doxastic involuntarism. Credences are not under direct voluntary control; one cannot adopt or abandon them by decision.

(7) The irrelevance of pragmatic arguments. Money-pump and sure-loss reasoning tells us nothing about epistemic rationality. Showing that a doxastic state would license self-defeating betting behaviour has not shown that the state is epistemically defective, and no such argument can establish an epistemic norm.

What I would like from you:

- Work through the package pair by pair and, where relevant, three at a time. Tell me which commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do, and keep the two categories strictly separate: I would rather receive a clean bill of health on an apparent tension than have it listed indiscriminately alongside the real ones.
- Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each, say what has to give, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package.
- Flag any commitment that is doing silent work — cases where the argument for one of them presupposes something not stated in any of the others, or where the force of an argument depends on a choice I have not registered as a choice at all (a way of scoring, a way of aggregating, an assumption about which alternatives are available for comparison).
- Tell me, finally, which single revision would resolve the largest number of problems at once, and whether anything recognizable as accuracy-first epistemology survives it.

I am not asking for names, references, or a survey of the literature. I am asking for the analysis, and for it to be specific enough that I could tell whether you have actually found a problem or merely gestured at one.
