I sit on the hospital committee rewriting our donation-after-circulatory-death protocol, and we have been deadlocked for fourteen months in a way that strikes me as structurally odd rather than merely hard. Three positions are on the table. The first holds that circulatory criteria license a determination of death only when the loss of function is irreversible in the strict sense; since our protocol contemplates patients whose hearts could be restarted by resuscitation or extracorporeal support within the stand-off window, declaring them dead either abandons the requirement that donors be dead before procurement or trades on a silent equivocation. The second holds that this objection proves too much: irreversibility has always been indexed to what intervention is available and to the decision not to intervene, so a patient declared whole-brain dead is no more strictly irreversible than ours, and the only workable notion is permanence — function will not return because nothing will be done to restore it. The third thinks both are fussing over the wrong organ system: what matters is the permanent loss of the capacity for consciousness, after which the heart's status is a technicality.

Everyone on the committee agrees about the physiology — the stand-off interval, the risk of autoresuscitation, what resuscitation could and could not achieve in these patients. My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than assumed, is that the deadlock is therefore not empirical at all. It is a disagreement about what kind of concept death is: a tracking concept whose extension is fixed by biological facts and which we merely try to detect, versus a licensing concept whose extension is fixed by what we need it to authorize, from procurement to inheritance to withdrawal of support. On that hypothesis the permanence-versus-irreversibility quarrel is where a normative choice is doing its work while dressed as physiology, since choosing between them is choosing who holds the permission slip.

Diagnose the disagreement rather than ruling on who is right about death. Tell me which of the clashes are verbal, which are substantive, which are normative decisions disguised as factual ones, and whether any side is answering a different question under the same word. Say plainly whether progress is available and what it would consist in — a distinction, a reframing, an institutional decision, an empirical result — or why the impasse should be expected to persist.

Please use these headings, in this order: "Bottom line" (two or three sentences stating the diagnosis flatly); "The three positions as their proponents would state them" (about 300 words, no strawmen); "Where the fault line actually is" (about 450 words, assigning each clash to a category); "What would count as progress" (about 300 words, concrete); and "What would not" (about 150 words, naming the moves that look like progress and are not). Assume I know the clinical details and do not re-explain the death criteria. No citations or reading lists.
