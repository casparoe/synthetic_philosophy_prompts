I run communications for a seven-person nonprofit that does emergency home repairs for elderly and disabled homeowners in a rural county — wheelchair ramps, furnaces, roofs. Last year we raised about $180,000, nearly all of it in small gifts from people living within forty miles. The board has asked for $300,000 this year and has told me, gently, that the increase is expected to come from better writing rather than from new staff. I should mention that I have no training in any of this: I taught high school English for eleven years and took this job six years ago, so what follows is the reasoning of an amateur who has been turning the same problem over for two months.

Over the winter I read a great deal of marketing material and studied what larger organizations send, and I built a four-email sequence. It worked. Our open-to-give rate went from roughly 4 percent to roughly 11 percent in the first test. But the better it works, the less comfortable I am, and I can no longer tell whether my discomfort is conscience or squeamishness about doing my job well. I would like a candid assessment from someone who treats the details as the point rather than offering generalities.

Here is the sequence, described as honestly and completely as I can.

Email 1, Monday: a story of about four hundred words concerning one household — the name, the town, and the particular thing wrong with the house. In the case I used, a furnace out since November and a family sleeping in a single room with a space heater. There is no ask, no button, and no donation link, only the story and a line saying we will write again in a few days.

Email 2, Wednesday: a short note referring back to the story, then a giving ladder — $40 covers a grab-bar installation, $120 covers a furnace inspection and parts, $400 covers a ramp section. In the donation form $120 is pre-selected, and the box marked "make this monthly" arrives already checked; the donor must uncheck it. Testing this roughly tripled our monthly-giver rate.

Email 3, Thursday: sent from the development director's individual address in the first person, so that it lands as a personal message and people reply to her directly. In substance it says: "I could see that you opened our note about the K family but didn't get a chance to respond — no pressure at all, I just wanted you to know the county's match ends Friday." The opening claim is true. Our platform reports who opened what, and we do look.

Email 4, Friday: a merge field inserts a first line that has no connection to the reader's actual history with us and was chosen only because it tested well — "You're one of those people who follows through." It goes to the entire list, including people who have never given us anything.

Two further things, and the second is where I am genuinely stuck.

First, I built myself a rule and I want to know whether it is any good. The rule is that I should be willing to print, at the top of the email, a plain statement of what the email is doing to the reader and why. Running the sequence through it: Email 1 passes, Email 2 passes and I would happily explain the ladder aloud, Email 3 I would rather not print, Email 4 I certainly would not print. So far the rule tracks my stomach. Then it went wrong in two directions.

It passes Email 1 without complaint, because I would readily disclose that we lead with one vivid household since one household is what people respond to. Yet I am the one who chooses the household, and I choose the ones with a good photograph and a sympathetic story rather than the ones with the worst houses. There are around two hundred open cases on our list at any time, and the ones I write about are not the most urgent ones. The rule notices nothing here, since saying so out loud would not weaken the email in the slightest.

And it passes the first sentence of Email 3 in a way I find alarming: if I printed "we can tell whether you opened our email," I suspect a fair number of readers would find that reassuring rather than repellent — evidence that we pay attention — and the sentence would go on working exactly as before. So the rule says fine. My stomach says not fine.

Second, I know the obvious reply to all of my worry. We are raising money for people whose furnaces are out; donors give freely and can close the window; nobody is defrauded; and if I decline to do this, the additional $120,000 does not materialize and the repairs do not happen. I find that argument convincing in the aggregate and completely unconvincing when I read Email 4. I have not been able to explain the gap to myself.

So, concretely:

1. Take the four emails one at a time. Are they the same sort of thing, or are some of them ordinary good writing and others something else entirely? If a line is crossed, where exactly, and what is the line made of?

2. Is my disclosure rule worth keeping? It appears to fail by passing Email 1 and by passing the tracking sentence in Email 3. Is there something to add that repairs both failures without making the rule so strict that I cannot write anything at all — and if so, why is that addition the right one rather than a patch I have contrived in order to reach the verdict I already wanted?

3. Which elements should I change or cut before Monday's send, and what should replace them that you would expect to raise something close to the same amount of money? I would rather have three concrete substitutions than a warning.

4. Is the "the repairs do not happen" argument as strong as I think it is, or am I right that it does not reach Email 4?

On format: after your reasoning, please give me a single table with one row for each of the four emails plus one row for each of the two changes you recommend most strongly. Use these columns, in this order — item (email number, or the proposed change); what it actually does to the reader, stated as a mechanism in plain language rather than as a label; whether it would survive being disclosed at the top of the email (yes, no, or partly, with one sentence on why); verdict (keep, keep with a change, or cut); the specific change or the reason to cut; and confidence (high, medium, or low). Please make the table self-contained, as I want to forward it to my director without the rest of the message.

One more request, and I mean it. Tell me where you are least sure. Identify the row you would bet on getting wrong and say why, and say what argument would change your verdict — something a colleague of mine could put to you on Tuesday that would move Email 3 or Email 4 to a different verdict. And if your view is that my discomfort is a failure of nerve rather than a moral perception, say so plainly, and then say what evidence would show you were wrong about that as well.
