Thirty-eight years in a lab doing trace-element work on lake and estuary sediments, so I have personally counted varves and argued about whether a peak was real or a smear from the corer. Retired now, reading a lot of philosophy of science, and running a small monthly group out of the university here: two actual stratigraphers, a historian of science, an environmental historian who has never held a core in her life, and me. Next month it's my turn to set the session and the topic is the Anthropocene. I want to hand them a case they cannot all agree about beforehand, and I want it built properly rather than found.

So: construct me a thought experiment to specification. Not an illustration of the debate — an engineered case where I've told you in advance what it has to do, and you show me it does it.

The specs:

1. One realistic sedimentary archive somewhere on Earth. No sci-fi, no magic preservation, no perfect record of everything. A paragraph or two is plenty.

2. Inside it, two candidate markers for the base of the Anthropocene. Both must genuinely satisfy the technical conditions stratigraphers impose on a boundary — sharp, datable, resistant to reworking, correlatable across basins — and the two must imply start dates that are decades to a century apart. If one of your markers is just bad stratigraphy, the case is void, because then the geologists in my group win in five minutes and there's nothing to discuss.

3. The disagreement must not be closable by more data. Hold that fixed and say so: assume every core on the planet is perfectly recovered and perfectly dated. The conflict has to survive that.

4. This is the load-bearing bit. The marker that correlates best globally has to be the one that says least about human agency, and the marker that says most about human agency has to be the one that correlates worst. And it has to come out of the structure of your case, not be a coincidence of where you happened to put things. Show me why that trade-off is forced.

5. Put two people in the story: a stratigrapher who is simply right on the formal criteria, and a historian who is simply right about something the formal criteria cannot see. Neither one confused, neither one overreaching. If either comes out looking like a fool, the case has failed.

I know the formal proposal got batted down a couple of years back, so please don't just re-run that vote — I want a case that would still bite even if the working group had won and everyone had accepted their spike.

After the scenario, do the demonstration: state where each discipline's own standards put the boundary and why, name the feature of the case that is doing the work, and then tell me what happens if I delete it — specifically, if I let the stratigrapher choose either marker freely, does the whole thing collapse back into the ordinary pick-the-best-spike argument, and if not, why not. Finish with the best attack each of your two characters can make on the other's reading, at full strength, no straw men.

And then a short answer to the thing actually bugging me, which I'll get asked at the end of the night: is what's left over a gap in the evidence, or is it a decision about what a geological time unit is for? I want you to commit. Both-sides mush will get me laughed out of the room.

Under nine hundred words for everything. And no reading list, no bibliography — I'll go and find papers myself, I still have my library card, and I know perfectly well that you'd be guessing at half of them anyway.
