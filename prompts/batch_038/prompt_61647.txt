An online essay in a philosophy magazine introduces Molinism to general readers in the following terms:

'Molina, a sixteenth-century Jesuit, held that God has three kinds of knowledge: natural knowledge of all that is necessary or possible, free knowledge of the world He actually creates, and, standing between them, middle knowledge — His knowledge of what any possible free creature would freely do in any circumstances He might place it in. God therefore surveys the counterfactuals, settles which circumstances to create, and so knows in advance precisely what will happen; those counterfactuals are true because God, in His wisdom, sees what each creature's character would lead it to do. This is also Molina's answer to the problem of evil, since God creates the best world that human freedom allows. Critics reply that the whole scheme is determinism in disguise: if the counterfactuals are settled before God's decision, the creatures never had an alternative, and Molina has merely relabelled divine decree as foreknowledge.'

I came away persuaded that the critics have the better of it, but a friend who works in this area says the paragraph makes God's knowledge depend on something Molinists insist it cannot depend on, and that the closing sentence is not the objection anybody actually presses — she would not elaborate. Could you take the paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a Molinist would endorse without qualification, which are fair simplifications, and which misrepresent the position? Then: does the determinism charge survive once the summary is corrected, and if it does not, what is the strongest genuine objection to middle knowledge, and how does it differ from the one quoted?
