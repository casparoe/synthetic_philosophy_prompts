I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and off the back of a piece I wrote last spring I've been talked into giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop provisionaly called Legible Games. Mornings are game theorists — people who've proved theorems about program equilibrium. Afternoons are mutual-aid and legal-defense organizers, most of whom have never heard of a Nash equilibrium and dont intend to start now. Same room all day, I go first: six minutes, no slides.

The workshop's spine is program equilibrium. Players submit programs instead of moves, each program gets to read the other's source code before acting, and suddenly unconditional cooperation isnt naive anymore — you can run something that cooperates only with programs it can check will cooperate back. Defection stops being free.

Three things I need it to do. Land that idea for the organizers without turning into a pop-sci explainer that the theorists squirm through. Face the objection the organizers already have, which I think is basically right: "just show everyone your code" is a clean equilibrium concept and a dangerous political demand, because the people most exposed by transparency are usually the ones least protected by it. And raise something I've been calling the read-only problem — in the standard setup inspection is free, symmetric, and costless, but in any real game reading someone's program is itself a move: it costs you something, it tells the other side what you were looking for, and it hands them your reasoning to mimic or to exploit.

If you can, hang the whole thing on one image: the fragility result where two cooperating programs both defect because one player added a single extra space to their source code. End on a question for the room, not a takeaway. My editor has banned the phrase "AI alignment."
