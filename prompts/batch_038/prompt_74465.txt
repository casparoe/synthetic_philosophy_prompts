I convene a non-specialist reading group in the philosophy of language, and this month we are working through a popular introduction to twentieth-century analytic philosophy by an accomplished intellectual historian who is not a specialist in this area. Its chapter on meaning contains the following passage on Quine's radical-translation argument:

> Quine asks us to imagine a field linguist confronting a language for which there is no interpreter and no prior contact, so that the only evidence available is what the speakers say in the presence of what both parties can observe. A rabbit scurries across the clearing, a native utterance occurs, and the linguist tentatively records gavagai as meaning rabbit. But that same stimulation would equally license recording it as meaning undetached rabbit part, or momentary rabbit stage, and no further interrogation of the natives can discriminate among these options, because each of them implies exactly the same pattern of assent and dissent to whatever else the linguist might prompt. Even the natural next move — asking whether this gavagai is the same gavagai as the one from a moment ago — is unavailable, since the apparatus of sameness and individuation the question depends on is itself what is in dispute. So reference, Quine concluded, is radically inscrutable. There is a fact of the matter about whether the natives' word picks out rabbits, parts of rabbits, or stages of rabbits, but it lies beyond anything the linguist could in principle uncover: a limit on knowledge, not unlike the thing-in-itself behind the appearances. In this respect the difficulty is simply the underdetermination of theory by evidence, the thesis Quine pressed against physics, transplanted into semantics: empirically equivalent rivals, settled by convenience rather than by truth. Quine accordingly rejected meanings as entities — no museum of ideas for words to be correlated with — and held that synonymy has no behavioral criterion, so that translation is a practical accomplishment rather than the discovery of correspondences. His later talk of ontological relativity extended the lesson: because reference can be shifted wholesale by a systematic reassignment that leaves the truth value of every sentence untouched, there is no such thing as what an expression refers to except relative to a scheme we settle on, and our objects turn out to be useful fictions. The upshot is a thoroughgoing deflationism: nobody's words ever determinately mean anything, and mutual understanding is a fortunate accident rather than an achievement.
