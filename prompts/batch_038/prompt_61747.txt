A multiplayer world I designed for a small studio is being switched off in six weeks, and the studio has asked me to give the closing address at the in-game farewell, which is also being streamed to the press. Perhaps forty thousand players will be there: some who have logged in daily for nineteen years, a few journalists who have already written the whole thing off as mass delusion, and at least two people the world genuinely hurt — one who lost a substantial sum buying items from a fraudster operating inside it, and one who was harassed for months while the moderation team, which included me, did nothing.

Write the address, eight to ten minutes spoken. It may not comfort anyone by saying the world was only a game and none of it really happened, and it may not flatter anyone by saying it was all just as real as anything outside. It has to be able to say, without embarrassment, that the friendships and the grief were real while the items and the wrongs had a different standing from their offline counterparts, and to make that difference intelligible to somebody in the crowd who has never thought about it. It should not use the vocabulary of philosophy — no talk of ontology, no named philosophers — and it should come to terms with the fact that when the servers stop, nothing here survives in the way a book or a building does. End with something for the two people I failed that does not turn into a statement of corporate regret.
