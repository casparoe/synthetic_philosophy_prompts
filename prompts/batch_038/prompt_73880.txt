I am drafting a section of a paper on Anscombe's 1956 campaign against the honorary degree Oxford proposed to confer on Truman, and in the course of it I have built a reductio that I did not intend and cannot discharge. It is designed to run against her position from the inside: it grants her own criterion and then claims to show that the criterion cannot leave her where she wants to stand. I would be grateful for a diagnosis. Please write your answer in British English.

The argument, as I have set it out for my readers:

(1) On Anscombe's account, what makes Truman's authorisation of the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki murder rather than tragic necessity is that the deaths of the inhabitants were not merely foreseen accompaniments of something else he chose. The destruction of a populated city was the means by which Japan was to be brought to surrender; the deaths were accordingly chosen.

(2) A death is chosen as a means rather than merely foreseen when the good the agent aims at could not be secured without it: when the benefit is realised by that death, and not by some further event that merely happens to occur alongside it.

(3) Now take a case Anscombe would want to treat differently. A bomber crew is ordered to destroy a marshalling yard on the edge of a city in order to shorten the war by paralysing rail traffic; the crew knows that a housing estate adjoins the yard and that many of its residents will be killed. The benefit aimed at is produced by one physical event — the explosions over that part of the city — and that same event is what kills the residents. There is no further, wholly beneficial event that could have occurred in its place.

(4) The case therefore satisfies (2): the good could not have been secured without those deaths, so the deaths are means and not side effects.

(5) Generalising, any act of war that yields a military benefit in a populated place yields it through the destruction of that place, and the destruction of that place is the destruction of the people in it. So (3) and (4) hold of every such act.

(6) Hence no killing of non-combatants in war is merely foreseen; all of it is intentional killing of the innocent, and by Anscombe's own principle all of it is murder.

(7) But Anscombe is no pacifist of that stamp. Her protest presupposes that it matters whether a man chooses to kill the innocent or whether his act, directed at something else, kills them obliquely, and that the line is not crossed by every act of war.

(8) To keep (7) one must deny (3)–(4), and the denial on offer looks like this: the crew's good is the destruction of the railway, not the destruction of the houses, and the former description excludes the deaths while the latter does not. Yet if that is the reply, whether a killing is murder turns on which description of his act the agent adopts — and descriptions are cheap. Any commander can render his killings merely foreseen by describing his aim narrowly enough.

(9) So either (6) holds, and Anscombe's condemnation of Truman collapses into a blanket condemnation of war that she does not hold and cannot, on her own premises, distinguish from it; or (8) holds, and the line between murder and permissible side effect is drawn by the agent's choice of words, which is no line at all.

Three things I want from you. First: which step should be rejected, and why did that step look innocuous when I wrote it down — what feature of it invited the move? Second: whatever your diagnosis, tell me what fact about the marshalling-yard case, if any, does the work that the description of the crew's aim apparently cannot, and whether Anscombe is entitled to appeal to it given her own account of intentional action, on which an act is intentional under a description insofar as a certain kind of answer to the question "Why?" applies to it. Third: price the alternatives. Say what it costs to give up (2), or (5), or (7) instead, and which of those costs is bearable for someone who wants to preserve the claim that Truman's decision differed in kind from an ordinary bombing run rather than merely in degree.

Two clarifications, so that we do not talk past one another. I am not asking whether the bombings in fact shortened the war, nor whether the historical record supports the Allied account of Truman's reasoning; the argument is meant to bite even if everything in (1) about his aims is exactly as Anscombe described it. And I am not looking for a defence of the bombings or for a survey of the literature on double effect — I want the failure located.

I should say where my own sympathies lie, since you may want to weigh them: I would much rather keep (1) and (7) than (6). But I will not insist on that preference if the price of keeping them is that (8) is left standing, so please tell me plainly whether it can be answered.
