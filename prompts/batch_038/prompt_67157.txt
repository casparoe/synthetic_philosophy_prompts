Read a pop-philosophy paperback on the train last week, one of those "big ideas made simple" things, and its chapter on faith and reason summed up the Clifford–James argument in a way that felt a bit to tidy. Here's the gist, roughly in its own words:

"Clifford's case is that beliefs are contagious — a belief you hold badly eventually gets acted on and hurts somebody, which is why the shipowner who talks himself into thinking his rotten emigrant ship is seaworthy is a murderer in all but name. To his credit Clifford doesn't dodge the awkward version: if the ship had actually made it across, he admits, the owner's error would have been harmless, but since we can never count on that, the rule has to be absolute — it is wrong always and everywhere, for anyone, to believe anything on insufficient evidence. James, writing a generation later, basically accepts the rule and then adds a loophole. He notices that some decisions are live, forced, and momentous: you can't sit them out, and the stakes are huge. And he points out, with his example of the climber who has to leap a chasm in the Alps, that believing you can make the jump sometimes helps you make it. So James's conclusion is that where the evidence runs out and the choice matters, you're entitled to believe whichever option is more inspiring. Strip away the Victorian prose and the two men aren't really disagreeing at all: James is Clifford with a safety valve, and the quarrel is about tone rather than substance."

That last sentence is what I dont buy, but I'm a reader and not a philosopher, so I can't tell whether the book has quietly rigged the deck earlier on. Can you go through it claim by claim and sort what's fair from what's a legitimate simplification for a general audience from what Clifford or James would flatly reject?

Specifically: is the shipowner business being handled honestly, given that Clifford runs a second case involving some political agitators who publicly accuse respected citizens of kidnapping children? What work is that second case doing that the first one can't? And is James's move really a loophole he grants to Clifford's rule, or is he objecting to something more basic about how that rule is supposed to apply — including to the person who declines to choose? Also, what does James actually mean by a live hypothesis, since the book seems to be using the word the way you'd use it about a radio broadcast, and is "whichever option is more inspiring" James or a strawman? Finally, once the errors are corrected, does the "just a disagreement about tone" verdict survive, or is there a real substantive dispute left standing — and if there is, state it as sharply as you can and tell me which side has the better argument.
