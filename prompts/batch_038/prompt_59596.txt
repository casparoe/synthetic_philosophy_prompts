I am revising a chapter of my dissertation on probability kinematics, and I have come to think that the whole debate over van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin case (1981) is aimed at the wrong target. I would like you to argue for that claim — not to survey the literature on it, but to make the strongest case you can for it, on the working assumption that I am right and that the burden is to show why. Everything you need is stated below; I have computed the numbers by hand and would rather you spent your effort on the argument than on re-deriving them, though you are welcome to check them.

The case, in the labeling I will use throughout. Judy is dropped into an area divided into Red (R) and Blue (¬R), and independently into Second Company territory (S) and Headquarters territory (¬S). Her prior is uniform over the four quadrants A = R∧S, B = R∧¬S, C = ¬R∧S, D = ¬R∧¬S, each 1/4. A radio message from a source she takes to be reliable tells her that if she is in the Red area, the probability that she is in Second Company territory is 3/4 — that is, her posterior ought to satisfy q(S|R) = 3/4, equivalently q(A) = 3q(B). Because that constrains a ratio rather than a partition, neither Bayes's rule nor Jeffrey's rule applies as it stands, and the literature fills the gap with distance-minimizing rules. Two of them, on this case: Rule A, which minimizes Σ p·ln(p/q) subject to the constraint, yields q = (0.375, 0.125, 0.25, 0.25) for (A, B, C, D) — exactly what Jeffrey conditionalization on the partition {A, B, ¬R} gives if you hold P(R) fixed — so Judy's probability of being in Blue stays at 1/2. Rule B, which minimizes Σ q·ln(q/p) subject to the same constraint, yields q ≈ (0.3505, 0.1168, 0.2663, 0.2663), so her probability of being in Blue rises to ≈ 0.533.

Now the variant I built, which is what actually convinced me. Suppose two messages arrive, from different sources. The first is as above, q(A) = 3q(B). The second says that if she is in Second Company territory, the probability that she is in the Red area is 3/4, so q(A) = 3q(C). Quadrant D is mentioned in neither message, and neither ¬R nor ¬S is mentioned as such in both. Imposing the two constraints simultaneously, Rule A gives (0.45, 0.15, 0.15, 0.25) and Rule B gives ≈ (0.4327, 0.1442, 0.1442, 0.2788). Imposing them one at a time, Rule A gives (0.46875, 0.125, 0.15625, 0.25) if the first message precedes the second and (0.46875, 0.15625, 0.125, 0.25) if the order is reversed; in both sequential runs the earlier message's constraint ends up violated (q(S|R) comes out at 0.789 rather than 0.75 in the first run), so the second update partly undoes the first. Worse, the no-news intuition that drives the single-message discussion is ambiguous here in a way that changes the answer. If it means that Judy's probability for each region she was not told about should be untouched, then q(¬R) = q(¬S) = 1/2, which together with the two constraints forces q = (0.375, 0.125, 0.125, 0.375) and raises D — mentioned in neither message — from 1/4 to 3/8. If it means instead that the quadrant mentioned in neither message should be untouched, then q(D) = 1/4, which forces q = (0.45, 0.15, 0.15, 0.25) and drops q(¬R) to 0.4. Those two readings are jointly unsatisfiable.

The thesis I want defended is this: a report of a conditional probability is not a constraint on the posterior at all, and treating it as one is the original sin of this literature. It is an ordinary piece of evidence — the event that a particular informant, in a particular epistemic situation, produced a particular report — and the correct response is plain conditionalization on that event, with likelihoods supplied by an explicit model of how the informant came to be in a position to say what she said. On this view Rule A, Rule B, and the two readings of no-news are not rival logics of belief revision but different tacit, and generally false, assumptions about the informant's situation; the order-dependence above is an artifact of the constraint picture, since conditionalizing on the conjunction of two report-events is commutative by construction; and the no-news intuition is not a primitive to be honored but a derivative result that comes out right exactly when the report is genuinely silent about the unmentioned region and wrong when it is not.

Please make the case. Concretely, I want (a) the positive argument, stated as an argument rather than as a comparison of intuitions; (b) a worked informant model for the single-message case that delivers a determinate verdict on whether Judy's probability of being in Blue should stay at 1/2, rise to ≈ 0.533, or do something else, with the likelihoods written out rather than gestured at; (c) a treatment of the two-message variant showing how the view dissolves both the order-dependence and the collision between the two readings of no-news; and (d) replies to the three objections I expect from my committee: that the view merely relocates the arbitrariness from the choice of a distance metric into the choice of a likelihood model, which is if anything less disciplined; that when the source is stipulated infallible and the report stipulated to be a direct readout of the true conditional probability, the likelihood model collapses back into the constraint, so the thesis has no bite precisely where the puzzle was posed; and that the view changes the subject, since one can acquire a conditional probability with no informant at all — by calculation, or from an instrument's readout — in which case there is no psychology to model. Finally, price the view for me. It implies that two agents with identical priors who receive numerically identical reports may rationally diverge, because their models of the source differ, and it makes the logic of belief change hostage to empirical facts about informants. Tell me why that is worth paying, or concede that it is not.
