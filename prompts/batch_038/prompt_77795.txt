Final-year philosophy of AI module here, and I've been lumbered with making the handout for our last seminar, which is on Dennett's counterfeit people. Trouble is about half the group will tell you they're signed up to the counterfeit-people worry and then can't agree on anything it's supposed to imply, so I don't want another summary of the reading. I want one of those 'which position do you actually hold' quizzes.

What I'm after: nine-ish questions, each a concrete case with two or three options, plus a scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto the positions. The cases should be things like a system that writes letters in the voice of someone's dead father, a companion app that says it doesn't want to be switched off, a chatbot that gives legal advice and happens to be right, a game NPC, a model that volunteers that it's a machine whenever anyone asks. Nothing may turn on whether the thing is conscious, feels anything, or understands, because our tutor has banned the whole inner-life question from the handout on the grounds that half the group thinks it's unanswerable and it swamps every other disagreement. You have to sort people purely on what they'd say about behaviour, design, disclosure, trust and what we should actually do.

The key has to be able to land on at least these: the Dennettian line, where nothing in there is understanding anything and the real damage is what happens to our practice of treating each other as persons; the view that the intentional stance goes all the way down, so a system that genuinely earns belief and desire attributions just is a person and calling it a counterfeit is special pleading; the phenomenology-first view, on which all this person talk is a distraction from whether anything can be harmed; the pure deception view, where building person-shaped systems is fine and the only wrong is letting them deny what they are; the status-conventionalist view, where personhood is a legal and social standing we confer and there's no fact to track; and a relational view on which personhood is constituted by being embedded in relationships, so asking about a machine simpliciter is malformed. If you reckon some of those are one position wearing two hats, say so and merge them in the key rather than pretending to separate them.

Then there's my own view, which I've been calling the counterfeit-interlocutor view and which nobody in the seminar has heard of because I made it up on the bus: the wrong isn't imitating a person, its taking up a conversational role you've no standing to occupy — being treated as a witness, a mourner, an advisor, a party to an agreement, where the standing is what carries the weight. On that view a companion app is harmless and a system whose testimony gets believed is not. Put it on the tree as a leaf, then tell me plainly whether it survives or collapses into one of the others, and if it collapses, into which one and why. I'd much rather you killed it than padded it out to make me feel clever.

Also flag which pairs of leaves the quiz genuinely can't separate, and note any combination of answers the questions permit that no real position occupies, with a line on whether each empty slot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's just unappealing, or because nobody's thought of it yet.

Start with your bottom line in one sentence: if we had to write a disclosure rule for person-shaped systems tomorrow, which leaf should it be built on. Commit to one and give your reasons — I don't want the menu handed straight back to me. Then go right into the quiz, no preface, no restating any of this, and save the notes on merged positions, unseparable pairs and empty slots for the end.
