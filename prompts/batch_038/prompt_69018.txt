I spent 32 years doing coupled-system modeling for a federal lab — some of it defense-adjacent, back when that was just what the work was — and I retired four years ago. Since then I've fallen into reading history and philosophy, badly and out of order, and I'm enjoying it more than I expected. Lately it's the old argument about who's responsible for the Cold War: the orthodox line about Soviet expansion, the revisionist line about American markets and open doors, the post-revisionist "both sides, mutually misread" line, and whatever happened to the debate after the Soviet-side archives opened up in the 90s. What bothers me isn't which camp is right. Its whether the question is even the kind of question that can be right or wrong.

So here's what I want. Give me an actual number, 0 to 1, your credence on this proposition, which I've tried to state as tightly as I can:

"The question 'who started the Cold War?' has a determinate true answer — meaning there is a fact of the matter about which party, or which parties in what proportions, bears primary responsibility for the onset of the conflict, such that historians with full access to the relevant evidence would converge on it, and two historians who disagree are making a factual error rather than adopting different but equally legitimate framings."

Then take it apart for me. I want the sub-questions you think the number actually depends on, a rough probability for each with a sentence or two of justification, and then how you combine them into the final figure. Some candidates, though I'd rather you propose your own and tell me if these are bad ones:

- whether "start" can be pinned to anything at all, given that you can date the onset to 1945, 1946, 1947, or 1948 depending on what you're counting as the thing that started;
- whether responsibility aggregates across dozens of actors and institutions in a way that licenses a "primary" cause, or whether primacy is a storytelling device;
- whether the counterfactuals needed here — would Stalin have consolidated an Eastern bloc absent American choices, would Truman have made his absent Soviet behavior — are in principle recoverable from documents, or unknowable even in principle;
- whether convergence among professional historians is evidence of a fact out there, or just evidence of shared training and shared norms about what counts as a good explanation.

Now the wrinkle I keep coming back to from my old life, and I'd like you to grapple with it rather than be polite about it. In a coupled system you have to seperate forcing from initial conditions, and which one you call "the cause" of some outcome depends entirely on the baseline you hold fixed. Perturb one variable against a fixed background and you get a clean attribution. Ask which of two variables that co-vary and feed back on each other "caused" the regime shift, and you get an answer that's a function of the decomposition you chose, not a feature of the world. If that's the right analogy, my proposition isn't vague so much as ill-posed. But I can see the objection too: human actors, unlike sea surface temperature, can be shown to have chosen, can be held to standards they themselves endorsed, and leave memos about their reasons — so the analogy leaks somewhere. Tell me how much of your credence survives that objection, and where exactly the leak is.

Two last things. Which term in your decomposition are you least confident about, and what would move the overall number most — a big new archival release, a better worked-out account of counterfactuals in history, or something about how "responsibility" itself works? And if you think my proposition is confused as stated, don't humor me: say so, tell me what's wrong with it, and give me your number on the closest well-posed thing nearby instead.
