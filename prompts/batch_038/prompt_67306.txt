The setup: our crossing module decides whether to proceed through an intersection or stop and wait, and by design it's stateless. Every call gets the same local sensor snapshot and returns an action, it carries no memory of how many intersections it already handled on this route. That isn't laziness — the module lives on an isolated partition that the watchdog can restart at any time, so after a restart any counter we kept would be garbage anyway, and our verification story assumes no cross-call state. The same shape shows up in our job scheduler's retry path, which also can't tell which attempt it's on. I ran into the absent-minded driver example (the Piccione and Rubinstein one: two exits that look identical, best payoff at the second, and the guy can't remember which one he's at) and it is uncomfortably close to what our system actually is. My thesis: given a genuinely memoryless module, the only implementable policies are ones that behave identically at every intersection, i.e. one randomized proceed-probability, and that parameter should be chosen ex ante by maximizing route-level expected cost and then frozen — the module must not re-decide locally against its own guess about where it is, because that makes the system time-inconsistent (each local re-decision looks defensible in isolation, the composition is worse than the committed policy) and because the local choice leans on self-locating beliefs I can't put in a safety case. Audience is maybe fifteen people: mostly planning and control engineers who live in MDP/POMDP land and whose reflex will be that this is a solved problem, just carry a belief state and the paradox evaporates; one ML researcher who trains end-to-end learned policies and will think hand-tuned coin flips are embarrassing and that RL already handles partial observability; our safety lead who owns the certification artifacts and cares about determinism, reproducibility after an incident, and auditability, for whom "you want me to sign off on a randomized controller?" is very much the brand; and a new hire with a game theory PhD who will actually know the literature and could catch me misstating it, which is my real fear, because I half-remember the paper arguing that under the right beliefs the driver's local preference lines back up with his plan, and if that's right it cuts against basically everything I'm saying and I don't know enough to tell.
