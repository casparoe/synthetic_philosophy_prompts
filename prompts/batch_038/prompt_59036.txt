You are a moral philosopher who has also spent time designing fair allocation procedures for small organisations — housing panels, mutual-aid committees, visiting rotas — and who is equally at home in plain language and in technical argument. Please think carefully before writing anything, and treat the specifications below as binding rather than advisory.

Some context about where I am coming from. I have no academic training and have never taken a philosophy course; I have been working on this problem by myself for a couple of years, because I help run a small volunteer fund that pays for things like funerals, boiler repairs and emergency dental work for people in our area. Requests reach us by email and by word of mouth, and there is never enough money to cover everything that arrives. What has become impossible for me is telling, in my own head, which of my pulls toward one request over another is a preference I am entitled to keep and which is a bias I ought to correct.

The distinction I have been using, for want of a better one, is this. Call a pull *load-bearing* when it would still be there if the feature that triggered it were stripped away: I attend more to requests from people I have met, and I would still attend to them if I learned that I had met those people quite by accident. Call it *borrowed* when it exists only because it is riding along on something with no standing at all: whether the email was fluently written, whether the file happened to be on top of the pile, whether I had received good news that morning. There may already be proper names for these two things. I have never looked them up, so please do not assume I know the literature.

The argument I need help with is with a friend who sits on the committee with me. Her position, roughly, is that the distinction always collapses into the inside view: if I know precisely how a judgment of mine was produced, including the unflattering parts of the causal chain, and I still endorse it after thinking it over calmly and with all the facts in front of me, then it is a preference and there is nothing left to correct. My suspicion is that endorsement does not settle the question. I want a case that shows her why, and I emphatically do not want a case that assumes my conclusion, because she will spot that immediately.

So: construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

**Scenario requirements**

1. One decision, one decision-maker, ordinary and contemporary — a committee, a household, a workplace, a rota. No neuroscience, no scanning, no stipulated brain states, no perfectly introspective characters, no demons, no science fiction.
2. The decision must impose a real, identifiable cost on a third party. If nobody is worse off, calling the pull a bias is too cheap and the case proves nothing.
3. The judgment must be produced jointly by (a) a partiality of the load-bearing kind that is contested but defensible — most obviously partiality toward people one knows or has standing commitments to — and (b) a sensitivity to a feature with no moral standing whatsoever. Do not reuse fluency of writing, position in a pile, or mood from good news; find a feature that fits your setting and that no plausible moral view makes relevant.
4. Now run these four tests on the case, and have them come out in this pattern. The *swap test*: reverse the standing-less feature while holding everything else fixed, and ask whether the judgment moves. The *endorsement test*: tell the decision-maker exactly how her judgment arose, then let her reflect calmly and fully informed on whether she wants to keep it. The *provenance test*: ask whether a person with the same values, in a slightly different but equally ordinary situation, would have judged differently. The *pattern test*: look across a run of her decisions in formally similar cases and ask whether the variation tracks the standing-less feature. The swap and endorsement tests must both return "preference — leave it alone." The provenance and pattern tests must both return "bias — correct it." These verdicts must be earned by the construction, not announced.
5. Irresolvability: no further information — more honesty, better introspection, a longer conversation, a fuller description — should let anyone, including the reader, determine whether the pull is load-bearing or borrowed. Make this visible by showing what each natural next question would and would not settle.
6. Keep the scenario itself to no more than two paragraphs.

**After the scenario, deliver**

- each of the four tests run explicitly, with what it returns and what in the case produces that return;
- the single feature doing the entangling work;
- what happens if that feature is removed or weakened — does the case collapse into an obvious bias, into an obvious preference, or into something else;
- the strongest reply available to my friend, including whether she should say the case is mis-described, that one of my four tests is bogus, or that my two terms cannot bear the work I am asking of them;
- one or two sentences on what a person actually running such a fund should do, if the diagnosis is right.

**What counts as cheating:** stipulating in the narration that the pull is a bias or a preference; making the decision-maker self-deceived, dim, or in bad faith in any way the reader is meant to catch (she should be as honest and self-aware as a person can be); choosing a standing-less feature that some respectable moral view could argue matters after all; or resolving the entanglement at the end with a clever piece of information.

**Process:** before writing the scenario, brainstorm three or four candidate settings and standing-less features, saying in a sentence each why the weaker ones fail my specifications, and only then commit to one. If you conclude that any two of my specifications cannot be satisfied together, say which, explain why, and tell me what you changed rather than changing it silently.
