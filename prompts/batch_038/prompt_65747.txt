I am the instructor for an upper-level course on aesthetics and political value, and I need to grade the following examination response. I have deliberately not marked it yet and would prefer an independent assessment before I commit to a grade. Please assign a mark out of 100 — our departmental convention is that 70 represents a competent but unremarkable answer and 80 or above a genuinely strong one — and supply the point-by-point comments that justify that mark. I want the comments anchored to identifiable claims in the response rather than to a general impression of it.

The question as set to the students was in two parts.

Part (a). In his 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art," written for delivery at an NAACP conference and published that year in The Crisis, Du Bois argues that art cannot be separated from propaganda. Reconstruct that argument. Say specifically what he takes himself to be rejecting, and how he answers the objection that making art serve a political end corrupts it.

Part (b). Consider the following case. It is stipulated for the purposes of the question and is not drawn from Du Bois. A streaming platform commissions a six-part drama about the eviction of a Black neighborhood to make way for a logistics hub. The writers' room delivers two cuts. Cut A is the showrunner's preferred version: sympathetic to the residents and, on the showrunner's own account, the more beautiful of the two as television. Cut B portrays the neighborhood's internal compromises unsparingly — the resident who becomes the landlord's agent, the family that takes the buyout early and then pressures others to follow — and the showrunner regards it as harsher, less accomplished, and truer. The platform tests both against the other and adopts whichever maximizes completion rates in its target demographic; that turns out to be Cut B. No one at the platform has any interest, hostile or friendly, in the neighborhood's political fortunes; the selection procedure is purely commercial. On Du Bois's criteria, which cut is the better work of art? And what does the case show about whether those criteria are indexed to the artist's intentions, to the work's effects on its audience, or to something else?

The response I received:

Du Bois's 1926 essay, delivered at an NAACP gathering and published in The Crisis, is a rejection of art for art's sake. He argues that the Negro artist cannot afford the luxury of disinterestedness, because the world his work circulates in is already hostile to him: white authors and the popular culture they feed have for generations represented Black people as comic or as menacing, and those representations do political work whether or not anyone intends them to. Du Bois's response is to accept the instrumentalization rather than deny it. Art is propaganda, and it may as well be honest propaganda.

The cost of this is acknowledged. Du Bois does not pretend the propagandist's obligation comes free. He subordinates beauty to truth and to the needs of the struggle, and where they conflict he takes the side of truth: he will not accept a beautiful falsehood about his people, however well made it is. On his account beauty is essentially the honest presentation of a people's real condition, so the conflict is more apparent than real once the artist stops flattering. What he gives up is the claim that form alone can carry value.

This is where the standard objection bites. If a work is judged by what it does politically, then it is good insofar as it advances the cause, and Du Bois has no principled way to refuse a flattering falsehood — an uplifting novel full of spotless heroes — provided it mobilizes effectively. He wants to escape this by insisting on truthfulness, but truthfulness is then doing all of the work and the aesthetic vocabulary is decorative.

Turning to the case. Cut B is the better work on Du Bois's criteria, because it is the truer one, and his central complaint against his opponents is precisely that they prefer the agreeable image to the accurate one. Cut A is what the defenders of respectability have always wanted: sympathetic, handsome, and false about the compromises people actually make under pressure. The platform's indifference is beside the point. What matters is the work, not the accounting department that selected it.

That said, the criteria are indexed to the artist's intentions. Du Bois is not offering a theory of audience effects; he is telling makers what they owe. A cut chosen by a completion-rate test is not propaganda in the sense he means, because nobody made it as propaganda. So the right answer is that Cut B is the better art and also not strictly an instance of what Du Bois prescribes, since the platform was prescribing nothing. The case shows the limits of an ethics addressed to artists in a world where selection has been automated.
