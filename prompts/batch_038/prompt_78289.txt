I am assembling a methods session for an advanced undergraduate seminar in historical theory, and I have put together what I intend to present as a reductio of the standing demand that historians avoid judging the past by the standards of the present. My difficulty is that I have come to think the reductio succeeds, and I very much do not want it to. Below is the argument exactly as I hand it to the students. The steps are numbered so that you can point at one of them rather than at the whole.

(1) Any judgment a historian passes on the past is formulated in concepts the historian possesses, and the historian possesses only the conceptual and normative resources of the present.
(2) Those resources were not selected for tracking moral truth. They were inherited from the controversies, institutions, and vocabularies of the historian's own time, and they carry that provenance with them.
(3) So when a historian condemns a past practice, what the condemnation reports is a relation between the practice and the historian's inherited standards — not a fact about the practice considered on its own.
(4) The obvious escape is to judge past actors by the standards of their own society instead. But identifying what those standards were is itself a task of selection and description, and selection and description require the historian's present concepts; the escape therefore reproduces the original problem one level up.
(5) Hence there is no standpoint from which a historian's moral judgment is anything other than the present speaking in the past's name.
(6) It is illegitimate to hold a person responsible for failing a standard they could not reasonably have recognized; an agent's obligations extend no further than the reasons available to that agent.
(7) By (5), no standard a historian applies is one the past actor could reasonably have recognized as a standard; from the actor's position it arrives as a foreign rule.
(8) Therefore no historical condemnation is ever legitimate, and the only fair posture toward the past is the suspension of moral judgment.
(9) Yet historians and their readers do condemn the past — chattel slavery, the Atlantic trade in enslaved people, the burning of heretics — and those condemnations seem to be among the most secure things historical writing delivers, not among its most doubtful.
(10) So something in (1) through (7) must go; and since (9) is the most secure item in the list, what goes must be the demand itself: judging the past by present standards is not an error to be avoided but a condition that cannot be avoided.

Two features of (10) are what I cannot live with. It dissolves the entire methodological debate it was meant to settle, since if the charge of presentism can never be made out, the injunction against it is empty advice; and it is symmetrical in a way that embarrasses me, because if I substitute future historians for present ones, the same steps appear to show that our own conduct is immune in principle to the appraisal of our successors, and that my own conclusion is no better grounded than the practices it condemns. I would most like to keep (9), and I would also like to keep (1), which strikes me as close to a truism; I suspect (6) is where the pressure should go, but I am reluctant to abandon it outright, because a good deal of ordinary fairness depends on something like it. Colleagues have offered me at least three different exits, and they are mutually incompatible.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on whether historians should moralize. Specifically: identify the step or steps that fail and explain why each of them looked acceptable at first pass; say whether the failure is a false premise, an invalid inference, or an inference that is valid only on a reading the earlier steps do not actually supply. If you think the argument is in fact valid, say instead which premise is cheapest to surrender and price that surrender explicitly. Then tell me what your diagnosis commits one to — among the contextualist, the moralist, and the interpretive positions on this question — and whether any non-trivial version of the injunction against presentism survives your repair, and if so, what it would actually forbid a historian to do. Please adjudicate the three exits rather than surveying the literature; I do not want a bibliography, and you may name positions without citing them.

Write the answer in American English. Give me the diagnosis in prose first.
