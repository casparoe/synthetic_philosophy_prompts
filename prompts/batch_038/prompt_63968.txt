I run a monthly bioethics journal club at a teaching hospital — mostly residents, a couple of attendings, a pharmacist, and two of us from the philosophy department. Next month is the therapy/enhancement line and I want to hand out something better than the usual "restoring normal function vs. going beyond it" diagram, since everybody in the room already knows that slogan and it settles nothing.

Can you build me a decision tree that sorts someone's actual position on the distinction? Here's what I care about:

- Every branch node has to be a question someone could answer by looking at a case file, not by declaring a metaphysics. "Is the shortfall measured against the species, against the patient's own earlier self, against the demands of their job, or against their peers?" is fine. "Is health intrinsically valuable?" is not.
- Build the logical space first, then locate the views. I want to see the questions and which answer-combinations they permit, and then the named positions dropped into that space — not a list of positions each followed by a paragraph.
- Work at least these four axes in somewhere: what the baseline is relative to; restoration of a lost capacity versus a capacity the person never had; whether the gain is positional (worth having mostly because others lack it) or absolute; and whether the intervention changes the patient or instead changes the environment and other people's expectations. Add more axes if you think they're doing real separating work, and tell me which candidates you dropped as redundant.
- Leaves I need: the normal-functioning / medical-necessity views (both the Boorse-and-Daniels theoretical version and the blunt insurance-committee version of it), the positional-goods or arms-race view, the giftedness/mastery objection, the transhumanist "there's no line, only better and worse states" view, the disability-studies critique that the line smuggles in ableism, and the deflationary view that the line is administrative convenience carrying no moral weight. Add anything important I left out. And if two of these turn out to be one view wearing a hat, say so rather than printing it twice.
- Flag every pair of leaves your questions genuinely can't seperate. Don't fudge it — if the giftedness objection and a virtue-ethics objection to self-perfection land in the same leaf, tell me and either give the extra question that would split them or admit none would.
- Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment, plus one case from my list that it calls differently from at least two other leaves.
- Note the answer-combinations your axes make room for that nobody occupies. For each, say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but unattractive, or because nobody's just gotten around to it.

Cases to sort with:
1. 68-year-old with plain age-related hearing loss wants a cochlear implant whose processor gives him better-than-young-normal frequency resolution.
2. Soldier on night watch, no sleep disorder, wants modafinil to hold vigilance for twenty hours.
3. Boy at the 3rd percentile for height, no endocrine abnormality found, wants growth hormone.
4. A Deaf couple deliberately selecting for a deaf child.
5. Med student wants a beta-blocker before a procedure she's fully competent at but shaky-handed on.
6. Propranolol given shortly after a car crash to blunt the emotional intensity of the memory before it consolidates.
7. Person blind from birth gets a retinal implant that also handles near-infrared.
8. Long-term SSRI user who wants to taper off and do a supervised psychedelic-assisted program, aiming at well-being above her pre-depression baseline.

Keep it to six or eight questions and no more than ten leaves — it's a handout, not a monograph. Please write the whole thing in American English, since it's getting printed for a room of Americans who will complain about spellings.
