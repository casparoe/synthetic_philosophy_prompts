I am the instructor for a second-year course on the epistemic foundations of democratic institutions, and I am grading the midterm tonight: sixty scripts, one essay question worth twenty-five of the hundred available points, with a strict five-hundred-word limit. I have graded about half of them and I want a second, independent read on the script below before I fix its mark, since it is longer and more confident than most and I would rather not reward fluency by accident. I am not telling you what I made of it, because I want to see whether your reading converges with mine. The question was:

"State the Condorcet jury theorem, spelling out each assumption it requires. A colleague in a government department tells you: 'The jury theorem proves that larger committees decide better than smaller ones, so we should simply make our advisory committees as large as we can afford.' Evaluate that claim, saying what the theorem does and does not license."

The student wrote, in roughly 485 words:

"The jury theorem was proved by Condorcet in his 1785 essay on applying mathematical analysis to the probability of majority decisions, and it is the formal basis of what people now call the wisdom of crowds: the famous county-fair case, in which the crowd's pooled guesses at the weight of an ox came out almost exactly right, is the theorem in action. In its simplest form the theorem says that if a group of n people each answers the same two-option question on which there is a right answer, and each has probability p of answering correctly, where p is greater than one half, and each votes independently of the others, then the probability that the majority answers correctly is greater than p, increases as n increases, and approaches one in the limit. So the majority is a better judge than the typical individual member, and a larger majority is a better judge still.

The colleague's claim therefore looks well supported, but three qualifications limit it. First, the theorem needs both a fact of the matter and independence, and real committees deliberate. Deliberation is usually presented as a threat to the second condition, but I think the colleague is right to discount that worry: discussion exposes members to information they did not have, which raises each member's p, and since the theorem's conclusion strengthens as p rises, a larger deliberative committee gains more from the rise in p than it loses from the loss of independence. Second, the theorem tells us nothing about what happens when p is one half or below; in that case the group's accuracy simply stays where it was, at chance, however many members are added, so enlargement is neither harmful nor helpful. Third, the colleague ignores Condorcet's own paradox: with three or more options rather than two, majority preference can cycle, so a larger committee can be less stable rather than more reliable. That is why the theorem is properly applied to yes-or-no questions and to elections, where a majority can be treated as tracking a fact about which candidate is better.

None of this overturns the colleague's advice. Because the probability of a correct majority rises so quickly with n, most of the epistemic benefit is captured at a modest size, after which the practical costs of a very large committee — time, coordination, free-riding — dominate. Enlarging committees is worth doing up to a point, and the theorem tells us where that point is."

What I need from you is an assessment, not a summary. Give me a mark out of twenty-five, with our bands in mind (twenty-two to twenty-five: excellent; eighteen to twenty-one: good; fourteen to seventeen: adequate; ten to thirteen: weak; below ten: poor), and then a point-by-point justification that works through the script in order, saying of each substantive claim whether it is right, wrong, or defensible but unsupported — I want that third category kept clearly distinct from the second, since I do not deduct for thin argument where the claim itself would survive scrutiny. Where you identify an error, say what the correct position is and how much it costs: whether it is a slip in passing, a misconception that would mislead the student in later work, or a mistake that damages the evaluation the question actually asked for. Pay particular attention to whether each of the three qualifications does the work the student thinks it does, to the historical and conceptual comparisons the student draws, and to what the final paragraph claims the theorem settles about committee size. Finish with two or three marginal comments I could paste into the script, each attached to the sentence they concern, and one line for the grade sheet telling the student the single thing to work on before the final.
