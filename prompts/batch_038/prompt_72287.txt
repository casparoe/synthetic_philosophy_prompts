I practice corporate governance for a living: board composition, allocation of control rights, agency costs and monitoring, dual-class and golden-share structures, staggered boards, poison pills, entrenchment, fiduciary duties owed to a residual claimant, and the market for corporate control. I have essentially no training in political theory. I would like the case for constitutional monarchy — the version actually defended in Britain, Scandinavia, and Japan — restated in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage.

Please cover at least these four pieces: Bagehot's distinction between the dignified and the efficient parts of a constitution, and his claim that the dignified part is what earns the deference the efficient part then spends; the argument that a hereditary, non-partisan head of state lowers the stakes of electoral competition by placing the ceremonial core of the state beyond the reach of whoever wins it; the reserve powers of the crown, using the 1926 Canadian refusal of a prime minister's request for a dissolution and the 1975 Australian dismissal of a sitting prime minister by his governor-general as the two cases where those powers were actually exercised; and the Japanese settlement, in which the emperor occupies a purely symbolic office with no powers of government at all.

Then give me the residue report, in three parts: what translates cleanly (I suspect the reserve powers look something like a poison pill and the crown something like a golden share carrying no cash-flow rights, but tell me whether those guesses hold); what translates only with real distortion, and what the distortion consists in; and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary whatsoever. For that third category I particularly want to know how to handle the fact that a monarch is fiduciary for nobody — there is no principal, no residual claimant, and no party with standing to complain — and how to handle the possibility that the office's value depends on the population not conceiving of it as a governance device at all. If that self-effacement is itself a functional requirement, does a successful translation into governance terms destroy the thing it translates, and does that count against the translation, against the monarchy, or against neither?

Two constraints on your answer. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and present them as translation results: flag every point at which you had to supply a principal, a claim, or a standard of efficiency that the monarchist argument does not itself contain. And close with your own verdict on which of two readings the residue supports — that corporate governance simply has no term for legitimacy that is not instrumental to someone's returns, or that the talk of dignity, unity, and political theatre is doing work a competently designed governance structure could do without the theatre. I want to know which, and where the untranslatable remainder does the most damage.
