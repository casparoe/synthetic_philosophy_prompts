You're a historian of economic thought who's read both the philosophical and the economics literature on Hume's political essays. Think it through carefully: brainstorm the candidate diagnoses first, then commit to one.

There's a fight about Hume on money and trade that I've never seen get anywhere, and I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it is — not a survey, not a verdict on who's right. Camp one reads him as a quantity theorist and monetary neutralist: money isn't one of the wheels of trade but the oil that makes them run smoothly, so considering a single kingdom by itself the plenty of money is of no consequence because prices adjust in proportion; and the balance-of-trade argument supplies an automatic adjustment that stops any nation gaining permanently by hoarding specie. On that reading his remark that the magistrate's good policy is to keep the money increasing is only about the transition, and his distrust of banks and paper credit is about political risk — paper foreigners won't take, paper that a domestic disorder reduces to nothing — not about non-neutrality.

Camp two says that launders him into a mid-20th-century monetarist: Hume thinks a growing stock of money keeps alive a spirit of industry and enlarges the stock of labour, in which real power and riches consist, and that the public gains from plenty of money in war and foreign negotiation, which is a claim about relative power rather than a veil. So the transition isn't a one-off, it's a lever.

My hypothesis, which may be wrong: a lot of this is two questions under one heading — long-run equilibrium versus transition dynamics — and money matters means at least three different things here (affects prices, affects real activity for a while, can be used as a lever). Plus a buried methodological dispute about wether interpretive charity requires making Hume consistent.
