For a seminar on the foundations of consumer theory I have assembled an argument against the revealed preference programme, and I now suspect it proves far more than I can defend. The steps:

1. An operational definition of a theoretical term is exhausted by the operations that fix its application: whatever those operations record is the whole of the term's content.
2. Revealed preference theory defines preference operationally, in terms of the bundles an agent selects from the budget sets open to them.
3. So a preference ordering is nothing over and above the record of which bundle was taken from which budget set.
4. For any choice from any budget set, there is an ordering of the available bundles that ranks the chosen bundle first: the ordering recorded by the choice itself.
5. So no choice an agent actually makes can fail to maximize some preference ordering.
6. So no possible observation of consumer behaviour can conflict with the claim that consumers maximize.
7. A claim that no observation can conflict with is untestable, and hence empirically empty.
8. Therefore utility maximization, as this framework understands it, is empty; and since utility is defined through choice, utility has no content beyond a redescription of behaviour.
9. Note that nothing in 1–8 depends on economics. Applied elsewhere: whatever sugar dissolves when immersed reveals a disposition to dissolve, and whatever fails to dissolve reveals the absence of one, so by 4–7 solubility is empty too; likewise whatever organism leaves more offspring thereby reveals greater fitness, so by 4–7 fitness is empty too.

I cannot accept 8, and I am no more willing to accept 9's consequences, since solubility is about as uncontroversial a dispositional property as science possesses. My instinct is that 1 is the rotten plank — operationalism is disreputable independently of this argument — but if that is the only exit, then what I have written is not an objection to revealed preference theory at all, merely a general attack on definitional reduction, and my seminar draws no economic lesson from it.

Identify the single step at which the argument first goes wrong. Then explain what made that step look unobjectionable when read in isolation, and price the two main alternatives: what is lost by giving up 1, and what is lost by conceding 8. If you think that defenders of the framework who reject 8 owe an account of how it can describe an agent who selects against their own ordering, say briefly what they owe. Open with one sentence naming the step you reject, and keep the whole response under 400 words.
