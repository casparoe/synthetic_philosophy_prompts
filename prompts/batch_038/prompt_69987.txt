I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long feature on the quarrel between Thomas Hobbes and John Wallis, the Savilian professor of geometry at Oxford, which opened in 1655 when Wallis took apart the mathematics of Hobbes's De Corpore and was still generating pamphlets two decades later, with Hobbes publishing attempted squarings of the circle into his eighties. My editor has agreed to let the piece be substantially about philosophy rather than about the spectacle of an old man being humiliated, so I need to be sure I understand the knot inside Hobbes's own position before I write a word of it. Here is what I would like explained.

Hobbes seems committed to three claims that pull against one another. First, that philosophy is reasoning and that reasoning is nothing but computation: the adding and subtracting of the consequences of names that we have ourselves stipulated. Second, that geometry was, on his telling, the only genuine science yet in existence, because we can know the causes of a thing only where the thing is of our own making — the geometer defines a circle by specifying the motion that generates it, and can then demonstrate its properties from that generation. Third, that the symbolic algebra and the methods of indivisibles being cultivated at Oxford were not science at all but notation-mongering: marks manipulated cleverly enough to yield answers while leaving the manipulator with no conception of what has been established.

Please work through the conceptual problem that results. In particular:

1. If reasoning just is reckoning with names, what principled line can Hobbes draw between legitimate computation and the empty symbol-shuffling he attributes to the algebraists? Is the difference that the geometer's names stay tethered to conceptions of the things they mark while the algebraist's do not — he says elsewhere that words are counters for the wise and ready money for fools — and if so, where does that tethering come from? It looks as though it has to be smuggled in against his nominalism, on which names are arbitrary marks and truth is a matter of their right ordering, not of their hooking onto anything.

2. His constructivist criterion looks as though it ought to favour algebra above everything else, since the algebraist stipulates symbols and rules and builds results out of them, which is precisely the making-from-our-own-power that Hobbes says demonstration requires. Explain why he thinks stipulating notation is not the same as generating a magnitude, what work motion does in his account of definition, and whether the operative demand is causal — a definition must say how the thing comes to be — or something narrower still.

3. Does the dispute with Wallis reduce, on this reading, to a disagreement about what counts as a proof? I want to know whether Hobbes's string of failures at classical construction problems is best read as incompetent mathematics, or as the predictable output of a coherent but idiosyncratic theory of geometrical knowledge — and, if the latter, exactly where that theory comes apart, or whether it simply cannot govern the practice it purports to describe.

4. Hobbes's claim to have founded politics as a demonstrative science rests on the same criterion: we make the commonwealth, so we can know its causes. Tell me what happens to that claim if the criterion cannot do the work he wants it to do in mathematics.

Write for an intelligent lay reader who has never studied seventeenth-century mathematics: no symbolic notation, and gloss any of Hobbes's technical vocabulary — ratiocination, names of names, resolution and composition — in a sentence or two on first use. I need the reasoning, not the sources, so please give me no bibliography, no list of secondary literature, and no invented citations. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, and close with a short paragraph saying whether there is a live version of Hobbes's worry in mathematics today — the sense that a proof can be valid and still fail to show why the result holds — or whether that analogy is merely decorative.
