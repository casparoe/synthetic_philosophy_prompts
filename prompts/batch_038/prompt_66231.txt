Our little reading group has been meeting in Dana's kitchen every Tuesday for about four months and we've completely stalled. Six of us are philosophy grad students, two are from science and technology studies, and one guy (Ray) only shows up because he's writing about Dewey and tolerates the rest of us. We started with Peirce's two 1878 papers in the Popular Science Monthly series and then read James's Pragmatism lectures, and the whole thing has turned into an argument not about truth but about what the argument is even about. Half of us think Peirce and James gave incompatible answers to the same question; the other half think they were answering different questions and Peirce's later renaming of his view was mostly a turf thing; Ray thinks both of them are just doing something to the word "truth" that we'd now call deflationary; the STS people think the interesting disagreement is downstream, about whether an account of truth has to be usable by somebody who can't wait around for inquiry to finish. Next week I've volunteered to bring a one-pager that sorts us, because we keep re-litigating the same thing under different labels.

So: can you write me a decision tree for this? Seven to nine branch questions, each one phrased as something a member of the group could answer honestly without knowing which way it pushes them -- nobody should be able to tell they're being routed toward "you're basically a Peircean" or "you're a crude instrumentalist" from the wording of the question, and no question may contain a person's name or the name of a position. The spine of the tree should be whether the 1878-vs-Pragmatism dispute is substantive or merely verbal, but I need certain distinctions to actually show up as branch questions rather than being smuggled into the leaves: whether truth is a property a belief has timelessly or one it acquires in the course of being verified; whether the account needs an actual final consensus at the end of inquiry or only a conditional about what would be believed if inquiry went far enough (Peirce himself seems to shift between these, and we can't agree whether that's a revision or a clarification); what happens to the account if inquiry gets cut off -- the race dies out, the library burns -- and whether that sinks it or whether there's a reply; what you say about a belief that pays off handsomely right now but would be overturned by further evidence; whether "warranted" and "true" name one property or two, which is where Ray's Dewey stuff has to be able to land as a real leaf and not get absorbed into James; whether the pragmatic maxim is a theory of meaning, a theory of truth, or a device for dissolving pseudo-problems; and whether moral and religious hypotheses get the same treatment as empirical ones or are special cases.

Each leaf gets a name -- coin one if nobody's ever used it -- plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment, the thing its defender will least want read aloud in the kitchen. I want at least the two historical positions, at least two modern descendants of the Peircean line (including the contemporary verificationist revival, and I want the standard objection that defining truth by what ideal inquiry would conclude is either trivial or circular to show up somewhere the tree can route a person to), and at least one leaf that says the whole quarrel was verbal. Then tell me which combinations of answers your tree permits but nobody actually occupies -- give me at least two -- and say for each whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered. And if there are two leaves your questions can't really separate, say so plainly and tell me what question would separate them if you had one; Ray will absolutely notice if you paper over it. One more thing: no bibliography, no citations, don't tell me to go read anything -- this has to be usable by people who are arguing on Tuesday and won't have time to check a single source.
