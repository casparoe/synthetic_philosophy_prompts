I am the recording secretary of the Ashfell Valley Rescue Association, a volunteer mountain rescue body of nine elected members covering one valley and the ridges above it. The county pays for nothing after dark, so every night helicopter sortie comes out of a discretionary reserve of about $190,000 that takes years of dues and appeals to rebuild. The duty officer handles routine calls; the board convenes only when a call is flagged as hard. Ten years of minutes have left me with a record that feels coherent but that I cannot write down as a single rule, and I have been reading Singer's pond argument — the one about wading in after a drowning child rather than ruining your clothes — with the sinking sense that we have been litigating a much more expensive pond for a decade without ever naming it. Everything you need is below; treat my figures as given rather than trying to check them against the world.

The record, in order:

1. Nine years ago: a solo climber in an unforecast whiteout, hypothermic on a ledge, in voice contact with dispatch, on our ground. A night sortie would consume that year's entire reserve and put a four-person crew at real risk. Launched, 8–1. The whole of the recorded reasoning is one line, proposed by a member who has since left the board: "There is a man on a ledge." He lived.
2. Eight years ago: a proposal to build and staff a winter waypoint hut on the same ridge, $150,000 out of the reserve, which our own incident data suggested might prevent one death every twenty years. Declined, 7–2, the minute reading "not a rescue."
3. Same budget cycle: instead we funded $6,000 of printed route cards and trailhead briefings from the operating budget, which our medical adviser estimated would avert roughly one serious injury every three seasons. Approved unanimously.
4. Six years ago: three riders buried in an avalanche in the next valley, ground belonging to another association that had already launched its own dogs and asked us only to relay radio traffic. A member moved to send our team anyway; the chair ruled the motion out of order because the other association had not requested it. Our adviser's marginal note says that by the time our dogs could have reached the site the survival curve for a fully buried victim would have flattened. Stood down, 6–3.
5. Five years ago: two teenagers swept into a culvert on our ground after climbing a posted closure fence. A shore-based throw-bag rescue, no reserve money, no risk to the team. Ratified unanimously, with the observation minuted that recklessness bears on who pays, not on whether we go.
6. Five years ago, autumn: a woman reported missing on day nine, on our ground, a full search costing most of the reserve. Stood down, 9–0 — the minute says there was no one left to save — and the members paid for the recovery of her remains out of their own pockets.
7. Four years ago, which is also when our treasurer first compiled the coverage figures described below: a climber alive and in voice contact on our ground, our ground team forty minutes out, when the state air ambulance, already tasked elsewhere, announced it was diverting to him and would be on scene in fifteen minutes. Recalled our team, 7–2, the chair minuting that we could not have reached him before the state crew and that he was being rescued.
8. Three years ago: a caver trapped behind a flooded sump on our ground, alive, talking to us, and the only cave-diving capability within two hundred miles was ours. Freeing him meant a pumping operation costing more than the whole reserve and leaving us unable to answer any call for the rest of the season. We sat with it all night; at four in the morning the water dropped on its own and he walked out. No motion was ever put, and no vote exists.
9. Two years ago: a night call for a party of four on our ground, reachable only by a $40,000 sortie that would leave the valley uncovered for six hours while our second team drove out. Launched, 9–0. A low-acuity skier's call came in during the gap and the neighbouring association reached her.

Tonight's call, on which the board votes at 02:00. A man is pinned by a rockfall on our ground, alive, awake, in voice contact with dispatch, his wife at the base station. He had left the marked trail to cut twenty minutes off a switchback, which under the line minuted in item 5 bears only on billing. Our surgeon puts his chance of surviving a textbook night extrication at about two in five, and at close to zero if we wait for daylight. No other agency can reach him in the window: the state crew is four hours out on another tasking. The sortie costs $185,000, effectively the whole reserve, and the treasurer's standing estimate is fourteen months of dues and appeals to rebuild it, during which we could fund no night sortie at all. The treasurer's figures, kept since item 7, say we field about one and a half night sorties a year that nobody else in the region can answer, and that over the past decade calls we answered late or not at all ended fatally about half the time; she cautions that the two recent rebuilds which came in unusually fast, after well-publicised night rescues, were exceptional and that fourteen months is the base case. On membership: the same two members dissented in item 7 and were among the three dissenters in item 4, both times wanting to go; three members have never voted reserve money for anything but a live call; one votes with the chair as a rule; the two newest members have no record; and the chair, who has kept the coverage figures on every agenda since they existed, wrote the minutes in items 4, 6 and 7.

Here is what I want. First, sort my candidate rules against the record. I can see four: a salience rule (a determinate living person within our reach on our own ground commands the reserve whatever the cost, while statistical lives command only the cheap programs); a stewardship rule (spend only where the expected lives saved exceed the expected lives the spent reserve would have saved later, counting identifiability and proximity for nothing); a mandate rule (we are a rescue body and not a public-health body, and we act only where we are the agency that can make the difference); and a fair-share rule (with the county, the state and the neighbouring associations all underfunding night cover, we are already doing far more than our part, and owe no more). Say which survive every item above, which are killed and by which item, and whether any rule you would write fits the record better than mine — in particular, whether the salience rule needs a reachability clause to survive item 7, and what is left of it once that clause is added. Second, say what each surviving rule requires tonight, and give me your best guess at the actual vote: direction and margin, with reasons, identifying the single closest precedent and saying exactly where that analogy breaks. Third, if the record genuinely underdetermines tonight's call, state the principle we would be adopting, and would find hard to disown in the next ten years of minutes, by voting each way — and name the kind of future call that would settle which rule we have been running, given that the caver's night did not. Please end with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of LAUNCH or STAND DOWN, giving your prediction of the board's decision rather than your own preference if the two come apart.
