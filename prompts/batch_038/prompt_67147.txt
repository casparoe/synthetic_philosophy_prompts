For the better part of two decades I have read and posted in the rationalist ecosystem: the sequences, the forecasting tournaments, the recurring threads on Dutch books and calibration. Across all of it the community's working picture of updating has stayed essentially classical. You observe E, you condition on E, you move on. Jeffrey's alternative, on which an experience shifts your probabilities across a partition without ever making any cell certain, gets a respectful nod and is then ignored in practice, because it seems to add machinery without adding guidance. I have come to think that is a mistake, and that the mistake is costing us something real, particularly now that so much of what we update on is a number or a sentence produced by another agent rather than a sensory certainty.

I want to take probability kinematics seriously, and I have assembled a small group to do it: four people, roughly eighteen months of part-time effort, call it five hours a week each. One of us is a graduate student in formal epistemology; the other three are comfortable with probability and decision theory but have no measure theory and no professional philosophy training. We have no publication obligation and no institutional pressure. What we want at the end is a set of positions we actually hold, plus at most two short position papers. Assume we are already subjectivists and already persuaded that credences obey the probability axioms; please do not spend space defending probabilism, the old requirement that something be certain if anything is to be probable, or the value of calibration.

What I want from you is an agenda, argued for rather than surveyed. Not a bibliography, not a reading list, not citations of any kind: we cannot check them, and I would rather have your reasoning about the structure of the problems than your recall of who said what. Where you rely on a technical result, state it plainly enough that three of us could verify it with pen and paper.

My own starting hunch, offered as a target to attack. I think the discussion has concentrated on the wrong end of the rule. Jeffrey conditionalization tells you how to propagate an input: given new probabilities on the cells of a partition, and given that your conditional probabilities on those cells stay rigid, everything else follows mechanically. What it does not tell you is where the input comes from. In practice that is the whole difficulty. You glance at a gauge, read a forecaster's number, or receive a probability from a model, and you are supposed to arrive unaided at both a partition and a complete distribution over it. Call this the input problem. My suspicion is that it sits upstream of nearly everything else, and that a group with our resources should spend most of its eighteen months there rather than on the internal properties of the rule.

Argue with that hunch, and rank the following candidates against it. You may add problems I have not listed and you should discard ones you think are not real.

1. The input problem, as just described: what constraints, if any, govern the formation of the new partition probabilities themselves.
2. Partition dependence. The same experience can be described by different partitions, and different partitions can yield different posteriors even when the rule is applied correctly. Is there a principled way to choose, or is the non-uniqueness benign?
3. Order dependence. Successive kinematic updates need not commute. Which conditions restore commutativity, and does the failure matter for an agent accumulating evidence over time?
4. Failure of rigidity. When the experience shifts your conditional probabilities on the cells as well as your probabilities of the cells, kinematics is simply silent. What should take its place?
5. Old evidence. Does kinematics help with the confirmation of a hypothesis by evidence the agent already fully believed, or does it make that problem harder?
6. Indexical and self-locating evidence: uncertain evidence about where you are in a sequence, of the sort that generates the standard sleeping-beauty controversies.
7. Testimony and machine output. When your evidence is another agent's probability report, you are uncertain both about what the report means and about the reliability of the reporter. Does this require a rule of its own, and how does it relate to Jeffrey's own remarks on the testimony of the senses?
8. Competition among rules. Kinematics is a special case of maximum-entropy updating, and reflection-style constraints relate your present credences to your expected future ones. When these prescriptions conflict, which governs, and is the conflict substantive?
9. Justification. The dynamic Dutch book argument that disciplines classical conditionalization depends on conditioning on certainties. Is there an analogue for kinematics, or does kinematics enjoy only a permissive justification, being sufficient to avoid a book without being necessary?
10. The descriptive question: whether people update even approximately kinematically, and whether it would matter if they did not.

For each item you keep, tell me what makes it genuinely open rather than merely unsettled by temperament; how many of the others depend on its answer; whether it is tractable for four people in eighteen months with only one of us able to do real formal work; and what a satisfying outcome would look like at the end, given that we are unlikely to prove anything anyone cites. Then say what evidence over the coming year would move each item up or down your ordering.

Next, name the crux. Which single disagreement does most of your ordering silently depend on, such that resolving it would force a re-ranking? I suspect it is whether diachronic coherence has any normative force at all once certainty is given up, but tell me if I am wrong and say what you would put in its place.

Finally, tell me what to cut, and do not be diplomatic about it. If you think some of these are pseudo-problems, artifacts of notation or disputes about how to describe a case rather than about what to believe, say so and explain what the people arguing about them are really arguing about. A short agenda I trust is worth considerably more to us than a comprehensive one.
