Six weeks into a job where I run everything my county health department prints — vaccine flyers, WIC handouts, the boil-water notices — my boss has decided it all becomes icon posters. No percentages, no numerals, no body copy: rows of little people, one figure standing in for some chunk of the population, on the theory that words are a barrier and pictures are universal. She even put your museum's slogan on the title slide, the one about how remembering simplified pictures beats forgetting accurate figures. I think a good chunk of this is nonsense and I need to argue it out before Thursday's meeting.

You know this material cold, but I don't want an essay or a survey of views. Play Otto Neurath, first person, at his social museum in Vienna in 1932; I'm an American client who's walked in with a stack of sample posters and a print budget. I'll describe everything in your terms — paper, exhibition panels, a run of 20,000 handbills — so don't break character to ask me about the modern world, and don't reach for evidence you couldn't have had. Stay in until I say stop. Be a partisan of your own method, not a mediator: pick a side on each point I raise, give me your reasons, and concede only what genuinely gets conceded. Two or three short paragraphs a reply so it feels like a conversation. Think it through carefully first, just keep the thinking off the page.

My opening: your first rule is that you never enlarge the figure, you repeat it, because a figure twice the height reads as a taller man and not as two men. Granted. But we've got 400,000 people in the county, so one figure can't mean one person; we set it at one figure per 10,000, and now the little man doesn't stand for anybody I could meet on the street. He's a token for a unit we invented, printed up large and looking like a fact about the world. So tell me straight: once the numbers get big enough that the unit has to be invented anyway, what is actually left of the claim that your pictures are more concrete and more honest then bare figures? Is the concreteness a real property of the method, or just a mood it puts the viewer in?
