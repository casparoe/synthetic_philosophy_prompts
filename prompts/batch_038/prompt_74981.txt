I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and I have picked up what I think is a useful habit from it: before publishing a position, treat it as a set of priors and check whether the set is jointly coherent, rather than checking whether each item is defensible in isolation. I am applying that habit to a long draft I have been sitting on about Kant's 1797 reply to Benjamin Constant and the murderer-at-the-door case. I co-host a monthly philosophy meetup here, and my own package got picked apart in a way I could not answer, so I would like a proper consistency audit of it before I put it in front of strangers. To be clear about the deliverable: I am not asking for exegesis, for a survey of what commentators have said, or for a defense or refutation of Kant. I want to know which of my own commitments pull against which others, how badly, and what the cheapest repair costs.

Here is the package, as I would state it in the post.

1. **Strictness.** The duty of truthfulness is a perfect duty: no consideration of good outcomes can ever override it. Where lying turns out to be permissible at all, that can only be because the duty fails to engage in the first place, not because it has been outweighed.

2. **Ground.** What makes a lie wrong is structural rather than consequential: the liar takes the very vehicle by which he makes his mind available to another person — his declaration — and converts it into a tool for steering that person, thereby using him as a mere means. No harm need result for the wrong to be complete.

3. **Generality.** A lie that harms nobody in particular still wrongs humanity generally, because it destroys the credibility of declarations as such. This is the strand of the Constant reply I find most persuasive; the polite white lie is wrong on exactly this ground.

4. **Forfeiture.** In the doorstep case the pursuer has no claim on my truth. His wrongful purpose forfeits it, the duty of truthfulness never engages toward him, and lying to him is therefore permissible. Strictness survives intact, because strictness governs only where the hearer retains the claim.

5. **Evasion.** Silence and literally true but misleading answers are permissible, and not only at the door: we practice benign evasion constantly, as in the enthusiastic proposal to get lunch sometime with someone you have no intention of seeing. I also hold that there is no morally relevant difference between lying and misleading — both aim at installing a belief the speaker knows to be false, and implicature is simply a slower delivery mechanism. Whether Kant himself would have licensed evasion is a textual question I have deliberately bracketed; it does no work in my package.

6. **Method.** No appeal to consequences may enter the grounding of the duty. Relatedly, I reject the remark in the Constant reply to the effect that the liar is answerable for whatever unforeseeable results follow from his lie, as a point about legal liability smuggled into moral argument. The item my group pressed hardest, and the one I am least secure about, is whether 3 is compatible with 6: "destroys the credibility of declarations" sounds suspiciously like a claim about aggregate downstream effects, which is precisely what 6 rules out.

7. **Case verdict, my own construction.** Call it the Reluctant Intermediary. The person knocking is not the killer but a courier the killer has paid to knock and ask whether anyone is at home; the courier has been told nothing, means no harm, and would be appalled if he understood what he is being used for. My verdict is that lying to the courier is permissible, and permissible for exactly the same reason as lying to the killer: the questioner's character does no work in my account, and I want the post to make that a selling point rather than an embarrassment.

Please audit this as a set. Identify the pairs that genuinely conflict and the pairs that only look as though they do, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. For each real tension, state the minimal revision that restores coherence, what that revision forces me to retract elsewhere in the list, and whether any single change would resolve more than one tension at once. Tell me explicitly whether 7 is entailed by the rest of the package or orphaned by it, and if orphaned, which of 1 through 6 was supposed to deliver it and why it cannot. Finally, say whether my worry about 3 and 6 is the actual structural problem or a red herring — I have no prior about which commitment I ought to give up, and I would rather not be told that all seven are equally troubled. Argue the case directly, without a bibliography, reading list, or quoted passages, and please write in American English.
