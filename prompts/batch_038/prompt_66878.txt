In the spring I am delivering the dedicatory address for a new theater building at the university where I teach: a four-hundred-seat house with an adjoining scene shop, funded almost entirely by the bequest of an alumna who died two years ago. I am a political philosopher who has published on Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert on the theater, and everyone in the theater department knows it. Several of them are expecting either an ambush or a capitulation, and I intend to give them neither.

Some facts about the occasion you will need. The donor spent eleven years as a working actor before leaving the stage, going back to school, and building a career in municipal finance; she was unsentimental about the profession she had left and said so plainly. Her bequest carries one condition, which the provost has asked me to acknowledge from the podium: the house is endowed so that it need never go dark, with admission free or nominal for students and for the surrounding neighborhood, and with a standing requirement that most roles in each season be played by people who are not being paid to act. The program will seat the donor's family in the second row; the trustees and two substantial gift officers in the first; roughly eighty undergraduates who will perform in the building; the theater faculty, including a Molière scholar who has read my work closely; and a scattering of townspeople who have never set foot on campus.

Write the address. Six to seven minutes, about nine hundred words, delivered from a lectern in the empty house before the first performance. Here is what constrains it. I hold the substance of Rousseau's case: that a theater does not reform manners but follows and flatters them; that pity spent in a darkened auditorium is pity not acted upon; that the commercial playhouse is a levy on those who can least afford it and an occasion for vanity, and for the separation of a silent, paying public from what happens on the stage; and that his objection is indexed to scale, so that what he takes to corrupt a small republic that still knows itself is not what he takes to be happening in a capital that has given up on knowing itself. I will not say anything in this speech that I take to be false, which closes off the standard dedicatory move — that this building will make our students better citizens, or measurably more empathetic people. Nor will I be a scold at someone else's celebration: the address must be a gift to the department, and the theater people in the room must be able to hear themselves honored by it rather than tolerated. The chair has asked me, gently, not to bring up the Misanthrope.

So the philosophical work has to be done somewhere other than in the praise of spectatorship. My own view, which I want you to commit to rather than balance against alternatives, is that the donor's condition changes the object under discussion: a house that never goes dark, that charges nothing, and that fills its casts out of its own audience stands much closer to the festival Rousseau proposes as the alternative to the theater — the gathering in which the people are at once the spectators and the spectacle — than to the Parisian playhouse he was actually afraid of. Make that the spine of the address. Argue for it in the speech's own rhetoric rather than announcing it as a thesis, and let the donor's history of leaving the stage do some of the work without turning her into an illustration of Rousseau's point.

Do not quote the letter or any other text of Rousseau's; use his ideas, not his sentences. Do not name-drop beyond him and, at most, one play. Do not hand me options, outlines, or a menu of strategies: make the definite choices, and then append a short afterword of no more than three hundred words saying what you committed to and why — including where the speech leans on a contestable reading of the letter (whether the festival passage is programmatic or merely rhetorical, whether Rousseau's objection is ultimately moral or political, whether his indexing to scale is a principled limit or a convenient one), and where you have taken liberties with him for the sake of the room. Please write in American English.
