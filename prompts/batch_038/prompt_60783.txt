I'm writing a novel about a philosophy grad student who starts to suspect she might be a philosophical zombie, and in chapter 8 she's supposed to lose the argument — shaken, not triumphant. A philosopher friend gave me the chain below to put in her opponent's mouth, and the trouble is it seems to prove way more then he wants it to:

(1) If a scenario is conceivable, it's metaphysically possible.
(2) It's conceivable that water isn't H2O — you can coherently picture a world where the clear, drinkable stuff filling the oceans and coming out of the taps is XYZ, and nothing in what you've pictured is contradictory.
(3) So it's possible that water isn't H2O.
(4) But water is necessarily H2O; that's the whole point of a posteriori necessity.
(5) So (1) is false.
(6) And once (1) goes, the zombie argument loses its engine: it's conceivable that there are zombies, but that no longer licenses the claim that zombies are possible.
(7) Besides, if conceivability can't establish possibility, then nothing done from an armchair can establish any modal claim — including (4). She can assert the necessity of water-is-H2O, but assertion isn't argument.
(8) So the two sides are at a standoff, and no amount of imagining settles anything either way.

(7) is the one that makes my head hurt. It looks like he's using (5) to saw off the branch (4) is sitting on, but (4) was supposed to be the fixed point that made (5) come out true. And honestly I'm not even sure (2) is fair — when you picture the XYZ world, is the stuff in the oceans water, or is it something that merely plays water's role? Those feel like different things to imagine.

So: which step do I tell him to drop, why did that step look innocent before you stared at it, and what does dropping it cost each side? I'd rather keep (1) for my plot than give up the zombie premise, so I want to know what I'd be paying, and what her other exits look like if (1) can't be saved.
