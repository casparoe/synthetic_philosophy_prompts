I'm in a reading group working our way through the machinery chapter of Capital vol. 1 and we've gotten completly stuck on the opening section ("The Development of Machinery") — not on the history, but on what kind of claim Marx thinks he's making in it. I'm an engineer by training and the only non-humanist in the group, so I may be misreading things that are obvious to everyone else. Here's the chunk we're arguing over, from the old English translation:

> "Mathematicians and mechanicians, and in this they are followed by a few English economists, call a tool a simple machine, and a machine a complex tool. They see no essential difference between them... From the economic standpoint this explanation is worth nothing, because the historical element is wanting. Another explanation of the difference between tool and machine is that in the case of a tool, man is the motive power, while the motive power of a machine is something different from man, as, for instance, an animal, water, wind, and so on. According to this, a plough drawn by oxen, which is a contrivance common to the most different epochs, would be a machine, while Claussen's circular loom, which, worked by a single labourer, weaves 96,000 picks per minute, would be a mere tool. Nay, this very loom, though a tool when worked by hand, would, if worked by steam, be a machine... All fully developed machinery consists of three essentially different parts, the motor mechanism, the transmitting mechanism, and finally the tool or working machine... These two first parts of the whole mechanism are there, solely for putting the working machines in motion, by means of which motion the subject of labour is seized upon and modified as desired. The tool or working machine is that part of the machinery with which the industrial revolution of the 18th century started. And to this day it constantly serves as such a starting-point, whenever a handicraft, or a manufacture, is turned into an industry carried on by machinery... The machine proper is therefore a mechanism that, after being set in motion, performs with its tools the same operations that were formerly done by the workman with similar tools."

Here's what I want help with, roughly in order of how stuck we are. (1) He rejects the first definition because "the historical element is wanting" — what historical element, exactly, and does the three-parts analysis actually supply it, or is that just a third candidate definition wearing a different hat? The last sentence I quoted ("performs with its tools the same operations that were formerly done by the workman") looks to me like it could be a straight-up definition, in which case what did he gain by trashing the other two? (2) The Claussen's-loom example seems to me to undercut him: he says that same loom is a tool when worked by hand and a machine when worked by steam, which is precisely the motive-power criterion he rejected two sentences earlier. One of my friends says the point is that he's doing a reductio of the criterion and the loom remark is part of the reductio, but then "Nay, this very loom..." reads like he's endorsing it. Is that just me being dense? (3) When he says the working machine is the part with which the industrial revolution started, and that "to this day it constantly serves as such a starting-point" — is that a claim about 18th-century England, a claim about the logical order of his own analysis, or both? And if both, what licenses moving between them, because the whole section seems to trade on that slide. (4) A couple of paragraphs above the pasted bit he says machinery is "a means for producing surplus-value," but machinery is constant capital and can only hand over to the product the value it loses through wear, so it creates nothing. How is this section supposed to hook into that? Is the answer entirely relative surplus value — cheapening the wage goods and so shortening necessary labour time — or does the other mechanism, the early adopter whose individual value undercuts the social value of the commodity, do more of the work here, and does the three-parts analysis matter differently for the two? (5) People keep dragging the Grundrisse notebooks into this section, especially the passage where he says the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, and they read that as Marx already seeing automation dissolve the law of value. I'd like to know whether anything in *this* Capital section points in that direction or whether Capital has deliberately pulled back from it — and if it has pulled back, what the pullback costs him.

On the epistemics: be as definite as you can about what the text itself commits him to, but please flag clearly wherever you're leaning on a contested reading or wherever the passage just doesn't settle the question — I'd much rather you say "this is underdetermined, here are the two ways to take it and what would decide between them" than give me a tidy verdict you don't actually hold. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I can't chase sources down and I don't want them; I want the argument, not the scholarship around it. And one more thing: if any of my five questions is a fake problem — say, if (2) dissolves once you notice something about how the reductio is set up, or if you think I've misread what "starting-point" is doing in (3), or if you think (5) is just anachronistic smuggling — tell me that plainly instead of dutifully answering all five. Same if you think the whole framing assumes Marx has a consistent account of the tool/machine boundary here when the text is better read as deliberately not offering one.
