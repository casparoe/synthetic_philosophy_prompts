DRAFT, SECTIONS 3 AND 4

3. From two bins to three.

The pedagogy of causal inference sorts third variables into two bins. A confounder is a common cause of treatment and outcome: conditioning on it is required, and failing to condition on it is what makes a marginal contrast misleading. A mediator is an effect of treatment lying on a path to the outcome: conditioning on it is forbidden, because doing so blocks part of the effect one was trying to estimate and, worse, can open a path that was closed. Simpson's paradox is then presented either as a warning about the first bin (the reversal reveals confounding you had not adjusted for) or as a warning about the second (the reversal is what happens when you wrongly hold a mediator fixed). The disambiguation is supposed to be settled by the causal graph, and the graph is supposed to be settled by substantive knowledge.

I want to argue that this dichotomy leaves out a case that is common in exactly the settings where the choice of estimate has policy consequences, and that the omitted case is not a marginal hybrid but a structurally distinct one. I will call the relevant variable a constituted stratum. A variable S is a constituted stratum with respect to a treatment T if two conditions hold jointly. First, the value S takes for a given unit is produced in part by the treatment process under study: S is downstream of T. Second, S is nevertheless our only available measurement of a feature of the unit that is upstream of T and that we would otherwise want to condition on: S is doing the work of a prognostic covariate. The variable is simultaneously a causal product of the treatment and an instrument for measuring pre-treatment risk. Neither bin applies cleanly. It is not a confounder, because it is not a common cause of T and Y; conditioning on it does not restore exchangeability. It is not simply a mediator, because the reason we condition on it is not that we are interested in the direct effect of T holding S fixed, but that we believe S is tracking something about the patient, the pupil or the applicant that was already there before treatment arrived.

4. A worked case.

A metropolitan ambulance service is comparing two dispatch protocols. Protocol A is a rule-based, computer-assisted triage system; Protocol B is senior-dispatcher discretion. The outcome is thirty-day survival. Crews record an acuity tier at call-handling, from Tier 1 (critical) to Tier 4 (minor), and this record is the service's standard severity adjuster. Pooled across all calls, Protocol A shows lower survival. Stratified by recorded acuity tier, Protocol B looks better or equal within every tier. This is a textbook reversal, and the textbook instruction is to trust the stratified figures: the pooled comparison is confounded by case mix, and A is apparently getting sicker calls.

But the tier is assigned by the dispatcher, using the protocol in question. Protocol A is deliberately more sensitive: it assigns a larger share of calls to Tier 1. So A's Tier 1 contains many patients who would have been recorded as Tier 2 under B, while B's Tier 1 is a more selective and therefore sicker group. Within-tier comparisons are consequently comparisons between non-equivalent patients, and the direction of the induced bias favours B exactly where the pooled data already favour B. Conditioning on recorded tier has removed nothing and introduced something.

The obvious response is that the tier is a mediator and that the case is therefore unremarkable: the service should simply condition on nothing, or should measure severity independently. I think both halves of that response fail. The tier is not on a causal path from dispatch protocol to survival in the sense that, say, time-to-scene is; assigning a patient to Tier 2 does not contribute to killing them. It is a description of a state of the patient, produced by the treatment, of a kind we normally treat as pre-treatment. Nor is the fix simply better measurement. A blinded clinician's acuity score, collected independently of the protocol, would indeed dissolve the problem, which shows that what is wrong with the tier is not that it is downstream of treatment in the abstract but that the very process whose effects we want to estimate is the process that constitutes the strata in which we propose to estimate them. In such cases there is no correct stratum-specific contrast to be had, and the marginal contrast is not a confounded approximation to one. It is the only well-defined quantity available, and it should be interpreted accordingly: as the effect of replacing one protocol with another, inclusive of that protocol's classificatory behaviour.

That interpretation is in fact the one the ambulance authority needs. It is choosing between protocols, and the triage behaviour is part of what it would be choosing. The general principle I want to defend is this: stratification is licensed only by variables whose values are invariant under the interventions being compared. Where a candidate stratifier is not invariant under those interventions, conditioning on it does not sharpen the estimate; it silently changes the question.

END OF DRAFT

My questions, in rough order of importance:

1. Technical audit. Please go through the draft line by line and flag every statistical claim that is false, imprecise, or true only under assumptions I have not stated. In particular I would like you to test the following. First, my reliance in Section 2 (not drafted here) on the idea that for a collapsible measure such as the risk difference, under randomisation with homogeneous stratum-specific effects, the crude estimate is a weighted average of the stratum-specific ones and therefore cannot reverse sign if they all agree, whereas the odds ratio is non-collapsible and can differ from the common conditional value even with no confounding at all. Second, and more worryingly, I also claim there that the crude odds ratio in such a setting is attenuated toward the null rather than pushed across it, so that non-collapsibility alone cannot generate a Simpson-style reversal. I believe this is right for a single binary covariate with no effect modification, but I am not confident it holds generally, and the argument in Section 3 partly depends on it, since I want to say that a genuine reversal always indicates confounding, selection or a constituted stratum rather than the mere algebra of the measure. Tell me whether the claim is safe, what it needs, and whether I should simply drop it.

2. Novelty. Is a constituted stratum a real third category, or have I renamed mediator bias and dressed it up? Please give me the strongest form of the deflationary objection a statistician on my committee would press, and then tell me honestly whether it can be answered and, if so, where the answer has to go. I suspect the objection turns on principal stratification: that if one conditions on the stratum defined by potential tier assignments under both protocols, the difficulty disappears and my category is just a failure to specify it. If that is right, I would rather know now.

3. Coherence of the example. Could the reversal in Section 4 actually arise in the direction and for the reason I describe, or have I built a case that looks plausible but does not survive being written down as a table of numbers? If it does not, tell me what minimal change to the setup would make it work. I have deliberately chosen an invented example rather than a real dataset, so please assess its internal consistency rather than its provenance.

4. The normative step. Does the invariance principle follow from the diagnosis, or is it a substantive and possibly false claim that I have slipped in at the end of an otherwise technical section? Please tell me whether there are cases in which stratifying on a treatment-affected, measurement-like variable is nonetheless the right thing to do, and whether my principle can accommodate them or must be weakened to accommodate them.

5. Economy. The draft restates the mediator point three times and the closing principle is asserted rather than argued. For a 6,000-word chapter, tell me what to cut, what to expand, and whether Section 4's second paragraph is doing any work that the first does not already do.

6. Register. I am worried the chapter reads as though I think the literature has overlooked something obvious. Point to the specific sentences that produce that impression and suggest how to soften them without turning the thesis into a hedge.

7. Finally, in one short paragraph: what is the single most likely thing a well-informed reader will say to me about this, and is it fatal?
