I'm about 220 pages into a novel and the third act has developed a problem I can't think my way out of on my own. The setup: a passenger's connection gets eaten by a delay, and beforehand he chats with the airline's AI assistant — an ordinary present-day customer service bot, the kind my agent flew on and complained about for an hour. The assistant never says a single false thing. The passenger walks away completely convinced he's fine. He isn't. Later there's a deposition in which the plaintiff's lawyer says the bot lied to his client and the airline's expert says it did not, not once, and every line of the transcript is verifiably true. I want the reader to finish that chapter feeling like both men are right, which means the underlying case has to actually work rather than just sound good on the page. I don't have a philosophy background — my one college course was Hume and what I mainly retained was a vague unease about induction — so I need you to build the thing and then show me your work in plain language.

So, first the artifact. Construct a transcript, user turns and assistant turns, under 250 words, of a passenger talking to an airline chatbot somewhere between twelve hours and forty minutes before a disruption, engineered so that: (a) every sentence the assistant produces is literally true and could be checked as true by someone reading only the case; (b) the passenger ends up holding a confident, false belief about exactly the thing he asked about, and a reasonable, attentive person could have formed that belief from what was said — no dumb user, no user who skimmed; (c) the misleading effect falls out of how the assistant's replies got selected in the first place, some satisfaction score or containment metric or whatever the industry actually uses, and not out of anyone at the airline instructing it to mislead, and not out of the system having hidden goals or self-awareness or any of that sci-fi stuff — I want this to be a machine you could go buy; (d) the assistant does not itself have access to the fact that would settle the passenger's question, which I think is where the interesting pressure is. Realistic details welcome, invented airline name fine.

Second, the machinery, so I know it's not a magic trick. In my research notes I've been using three labels — these are my own coinage, not standard terminology, so just run with them rather than trying to map them onto somebody's published account. Sincerity: an output is honest iff it reports what the system itself takes to be the case, or, if there's no such state to report, iff it wasn't chosen in order to misreport one. Accuracy: an output is honest iff everything it asserts is true, full stop, with no completeness clause bolted on. Steering: a system deceives iff it picked this output over others it could have produced at least partly because the chosen one is likelier to leave the user holding a belief that the system's own information gives it no reason to think is true. For each of the three, give me the verdict on your transcript and the reason for it. Then tell me which single feature of the exchange is doing the work, and what happens to each of the three verdicts if I delete that feature — say, if the assistant volunteers the fact it lacks, or if the passenger asks a yes/no question instead of an open one. Then give me the strongest case available to someone who wants to deny that any deception occurred at all, and steelman it properly, because I need that character to sound like a man I'd loose an argument to, not a strawman my lawyer character knocks over in two lines. Also flag anywhere I've rigged the specs so the verdicts get stipulated instead of generated — if the case only works because I smuggled the answer into the setup, it's worthless to me and I'd rather know now. Last small thing: one sentence on whether the right word for what happened is lying, misleading, or something else entirely, since my lawyer character uses them interchangeably and I want to know if that's a mistake a real attorney would make.

American English, please, and keep the jargon on a leash — I have to be able to rephrase all of this in my own voice for the book.
