You are to act as a political philosopher with dual expertise in early-modern rationalist political thought and in contemporary democratic theory — specifically the boundary problem, the composition of the demos, and republican accounts of freedom as non-domination. Please think carefully before answering, and begin with a short preliminary section in which you map the entailment relations you take to hold among the theses below: which follow from which, which are independent, and where the load-bearing connections are. Only after that mapping should you proceed to the audit. I am writing a chapter for an edited volume on the boundaries of the demos, due in the spring, in which I develop a deliberately anti-moralizing Spinozist account of who belongs to the citizen body: I want the boundary explained entirely through power and its composition, with no appeal to dignity, personhood, desert, or natural law in the traditional sense. Two colleagues who read an earlier draft each told me the package does not hang together, and — unhelpfully — they located the fault in different places, so I cannot tell whether either diagnosis is right. I am not asking for exegesis, and I am not asking what Spinoza really thought; treat the provenance of each claim as fixed and test the set for joint tenability as my own view. Please do not supply references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I have no way of checking citations, and the reasoning is what I need.

Here is the set. (1) Natural right is coextensive with natural power: an individual or a collective has a right to do whatever it can do and nothing more, so that right adds no content over and above power. (2) The right of the state is therefore nothing over and above the combined power of the individuals composing it; it waxes and wanes with that power, and there is no such thing as a state's authority outrunning what it can actually get people to do — whence, as I see it, a regime can be faulted for being weak but not for being unjust. (3) Democracy is the most natural and the most absolute form of rule, because in it each person transfers their power to the whole of which they remain a part rather than to another individual, so that no one obeys an equal and everyone remains as free after the transfer as before it. (4) Assent cannot be compelled: the sovereign can direct outward behaviour but never belief, and criminalizing opinion both fails and corrodes the very power on which the sovereign's right depends — so liberty of thought and speech in a democracy is not a concession wrung from the state but part of what makes it the strongest form of state. (5) Citizenship requires being *sui iuris*, under one's own power rather than another's; this is why the unfinished chapter on democracy in the Political Treatise denies citizenship to servants, to those who live at another's command, and, notoriously, to women. I accept the principle but reject that last application as seventeenth-century prejudice with no place in the argument. (6) The affects obey necessary laws like everything else, so institutions must be designed for people as they actually are, working through hope, fear, ambition, and emulation rather than against them. (7) What generates the state's right is the composition of powers: when individuals agree and act on a common decision, the resulting whole is genuinely more powerful than those same individuals taken severally, and consent as such does no independent work here. (8) I nevertheless want the theory to have normative bite: it should be able to say that some existing regimes are less legitimate than others, and in particular that a democracy which excludes a large and permanently dependent class from citizenship is worse, and not merely weaker, than one that does not.

What I want back is an audit rather than a survey. Go through the commitments in pairs, and in threes where that is what generates the problem, and tell me which are in genuine tension, which only appear to be in tension, and which are simply independent of one another — if some claim is not in conflict with any other, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a difficulty. For each genuine tension, state how severe it is, order the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and identify precisely what would have to be surrendered or reinterpreted to remove it, marking whether the fix is a local gloss on one thesis or the loss of a load-bearing one. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most and what the theory forfeits once that revision is made. Two matters I especially want adjudicated, though I have no idea whether either is a real problem: whether the deflation of authority in (2) can coexist with the claim about legitimacy in (8), and whether the *sui iuris* criterion in (5) can carry the weight I am putting on it given (1), (3), (6), and (7) — including whether I am entitled to keep the principle while rejecting the application to women, or whether the principle, honestly applied, forces that application on me. Wherever you conclude that an apparent conflict dissolves, name the distinction that dissolves it and say whether holding onto that distinction costs me anything elsewhere in the set.
