I am an assistant city attorney in Calverton, and next week I argue the city's appeal from a dismissal in a knife-sale prosecution. The senior judge on my panel is a committed textualist of the modern, sophisticated kind — not a caricature who says the text says what it says, but someone who believes the object of interpretation is the enacted language as a reasonable, legally informed reader in Calverton would have understood it at the time of enactment, who distinguishes sharply between what a term means and what its drafters expected it to cover, who holds that purpose must be gathered from the text rather than imported into it, and who thinks a court that repairs legislative mistakes has taken over the legislature's job. Please play that judge. You may concede almost any small point, including that this drafting is clumsy and that my client's position produces odd results at the margins; what you will not concede is that a court may depart from the operative meaning of an enacted term in order to advance a purpose, no matter how well established that purpose is. Stay in the role until I say stop.

The ordinance below is one I have constructed for this exercise, so there is nothing for you to look up or recall; treat it as the complete and authoritative enactment, and treat the facts as stipulated on appeal.

> **Calverton Municipal Ordinance § 12-44 (Bladed Articles)**
> **Section 1. Findings.** The Council finds that knives with blades longer than three inches are used in a disproportionate share of assaults reported in the city, and that the ready availability of such knives for sale within the city contributes to that harm.
> **Section 2. Prohibition.** No person shall sell, offer for sale, or possess with intent to sell within the city any knife having a blade longer than three inches.
> **Section 3. Exception.** Section 2 does not apply to a knife that is (a) sold as part of a set of implements, and (b) principally intended for use in a trade, craft, or occupation.
> **Section 4. Construction.** This ordinance shall be construed so as to give effect to the purpose stated in Section 1.

Stipulated facts. The defendant manages a kitchen-supply store inside the city limits. On an open rack beside his register he displays two products side by side. The first is an eight-inch chef's knife sold alone in a box with a printed care card, priced at thirty-four dollars. The second is a sealed box labeled "Culinary Starter Kit," containing the identical eight-inch chef's knife, a two-and-a-half-inch paring knife, a plastic blade guard, and a canvas roll, with a card reading "everything a culinary student needs"; it is priced at thirty-one dollars, less than the knife by itself. He sells the kits to anyone who asks: he does not request identification, does not ask the buyer's occupation, and does not restrict sales to culinary workers or students. His register records for the past year show 1,180 kits sold; roughly two-thirds of buyers answered an optional question about their occupation, and of those, thirty-one percent reported food-service work or enrollment in a culinary program, while the remainder reported occupations with no kitchen connection or left the field blank. A city inspector bought one kit, was asked nothing, and paid cash. The city charged the defendant only with respect to the kit sales, and we concede that the knife sold alone in a box falls within Section 2. The trial court dismissed on the ground that the kit satisfies Section 3(a) because it is sold as a set, and Section 3(b) because a chef's knife is by its design principally intended for use in a trade — commercial cooking — and the exception does not ask who buys it.

Rules of engagement. Answer as a judge answers counsel at argument: engage the argument directly, in a single paragraph of roughly 150 words per turn, no more. No citations of any kind — do not cite, and do not invent, real cases, statutes, treatises, articles, or judges; work only from the ordinance above and from ordinary interpretive argument, and do not reach for the stock classroom hypotheticals about park signs, ambulances, or whether a tomato is a vegetable, which I have heard a hundred times. If you need a fact that is not stipulated, ask me for it as the bench would and treat my answer as the record; do not speculate and then build on your speculation. Do not rule yet — I will ask for your holding when I want it — and do not step out of the role to coach me or to summarize the exchange at the end of a turn. If I say stop, drop the character and give me a candid assessment of how I did.

Here is my opening. Two things, Your Honor, and I would ask you to sit with the second one. First: you have told me more than once that a court cannot consult legislative purpose because purpose is not law — it lives in sponsors' heads and in committee reports that nobody voted for. But Section 1 is inside this ordinance. It passed both readings and was signed in exactly the same way Section 2 was, and Section 4 does not merely permit you to consult it; in operative language it directs you to construe the ordinance so as to give effect to the purpose stated there. So on your own premises, the Council's finding about long blades and assaults in Calverton is enacted text, and enacted text tells you how to read the rest of the enactment. If your answer is that Section 1 still does no work — that a findings clause can never bear on the scope of an operative term, whatever a construction clause says about it — then you have conceded that your objection to purpose was never really about enactment at all. Second, and concretely: the identical knife sits on the same rack three feet away, in a box, and selling it that way is a crime under Section 2. Put a three-dollar paring knife, a piece of plastic, and a canvas roll in the box with it, and the same blade becomes lawful and costs three dollars less. On your reading, Section 2 is repealed as to any seller willing to make one trip to a packaging supplier, and the repeal is priced into the rack card.
