I'm a third-year criminology PhD student working on criminalisation theory — basically, when the state may turn conduct into a crime — and the Hart/Devlin exchange is adjacent enough that I'm supposed to have a grip on it. I don't. I've only ever met it through other people. So: I picked up a trade-press history of the great law-and-liberty arguments at the airport, and it does the Wolfenden stuff in a couple of pages. I typed the Devlin paragraph out:

"Devlin's 1959 Maccabaean lecture was his answer to the Wolfenden Report, which had recommended two years earlier that homosexual acts between consenting adults in private should cease to be crimes. His case opens from a sociological claim rather than a moral one: a society is not merely a collection of people who happen to share a government, it is held together by a common set of moral ideas, and if too many of those ideas go the society stops being a society. On that basis the law may criminalise conduct that is immoral even where it harms nobody, for much the same reason that it may criminalise sedition. Devlin's test for when this is appropriate is refreshingly democratic: consult the ordinary reasonable person, and if that person responds with disgust, indignation and intolerance, the state is entitled to act. Note that Devlin was no relativist — he thought the law should enforce true morality rather than the conventions of the moment, which is why he took the Wolfenden recommendation to be wrong in principle and not merely imprudent. Hart's reply demolished the argument on its own terms: he showed that societies absorb enormous moral change without coming apart, so Devlin's factual premise was false and nothing survived of the case for legal moralism."
