Ok so I'm a junior and for my ethics class we're doing this semester-long thing where you have to actually practice some virtue framework instead of just writing an essay about it, and I picked Franklin's project of arriving at moral perfection out of his autobiography. I've been running my own version since like October: I cut his thirteen virtues down to six (dropped Cleanliness and Chastity, and folded Silence into Sincerity), and I do the weekly rotation with a little notebook, one page per virtue, black dot every time I break the precept, which is basically his setup. The reason I picked it is that I actually bought the underlying claim, which he states pretty directly: "It was about this time that I conceiv'd the bold and arduous Project of arriving at moral Perfection." And his account of why it's hard is a habit story — "the contrary Habits must be broken and good ones acquired and established, before we can have any Dependence on a steady uniform Rectitude of Conduct." So the view I've landed on is: character is a bunch of seperable habits, you attack them one at a time in a fixed order, and the daily marking of lapses is what does the work; if somebody falls short of perfection that's a failure of patience and bookkeeping, not of the method. On my reading the method can't lose, it can only get abandoned too early.

Tell me what would have to be true for that to be wrong. I want real defeaters, not just "you're being naive about self-improvement," and I want them sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Some things I keep chewing on: does the whole thing collapse if the virtues aren't actually independent, i.e. if working on one damages another? Franklin says Order was the one that gave him the most trouble and he partly blames it on having work where his time wasn't really his own, which is kind of my life between school and a shift at a grocery store. Also the Humility precept is just "Imitate Jesus and Socrates," which sits weirdly next to Sincerity's "Use no hurtful Deceit" — is imitating somebody a habit or a performance, and does it matter that Humility got added last on a friend's suggestion and that, as I read him, he half-admits part of what he wanted was to look humble? Would that count against the project or only against him as a person? And then there's the hatchet thing at the end, where he compares himself to a guy who got tired of turning the grindstone to make his hatchet bright all over and just took it as it was, speckled — and he says he was still better off for having tried. If the guy who invented the method is satisfied stopping there, what is the position even claiming anymore, and what is it risking?

Second thing: is my version of the claim already insulated by qualification? If every failure gets explained away as not enough rotations, or not enough attention, or the wrong ordering of the list, does it predict anything a rival wouldn't — say somebody who thinks character is mostly situational and that the dots in my notebook measure my self-image more than my conduct? I'd like to know what evidence would actually move me: which findings about habit formation and self-monitoring a committed Franklinian would have to take seriously and which they'd wave off; whether the daily marking could make things worse instead of better, like if it only trains you to notice lapses, or turns one bad afternoon into a written-off week; and what an observable difference would look like between the method working and me just getting older and more conscientious. No need to name studies or give me a reading list — just say what kind of result would do it. And be straight with me about where you think a defender of Franklin's project should dig in rather than concede.
