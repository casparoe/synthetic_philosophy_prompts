I convene an upper-level undergraduate seminar on blame and second-personal ethics (twelve students, one three-hour final exam, closed book). Each year I build the essay section around a stipulated framework of my own devising, so that the question is fully answerable from the page in front of the student and no credit accrues to whoever happens to have read the most literature. This year's framework is a three-clause account of standing to blame, and I need you to produce the assessment materials for it.

Please write:

1. **The exam question.** Present the following account, in this form or a tightened version of it, under a name of your choosing:

   *The Reciprocity Account.* S has standing to blame P for P's violation of norm N only if all three conditions hold.
   - **R1 (compliance).** S complied with N throughout the window that begins when S first came to believe that P's violation had occurred and ends with S's act of blaming.
   - **R2 (non-selectivity).** S is disposed to blame violations of N by anyone, including S's intimates and S herself, where the circumstances of those violations are relevantly similar to P's.
   - **R3 (motive).** S's act of blaming issues from S's judgement that P violated N together with S's acceptance of N, rather than from the benefits S expects the blaming to bring her.

   Then present two cases, which you should flesh out into concrete vignettes with named agents and enough stipulated detail that no further facts are needed:

   **Case A.** A departmental chair publicly and accurately calls out a colleague for taking credit for a student's work. She has never done anything of the kind herself, she has a settled record of calling out such conduct in her friends and in her own former students, and she applies the norm impartially. She is also, as she would readily admit if asked, doing it now largely because the episode gives her a platform she has wanted for some time and because the colleague is a rival; her recognition of the wrong is present but is not what is carrying the act.

   **Case B.** A woman reproaches her brother for an affair that is wrecking his marriage. Her own affair, which her brother knows about and which ended thirteen years earlier, preceded the window entirely; she has been faithful throughout the window and remains disposed to blame infidelity impartially, including in her own family. But she maintains that her affair was not a violation at all, since her marriage was already over in all but name, whereas his is not. Her brother accepts the norm, accepts that he violated it, and does not resent her reproach.

   The question must ask the student to determine, for each case, whether the account yields the intuitively right verdict, and which clause or combination of clauses delivers it, including where the verdict depends on the stipulation of the window in R1 and on the phrase *relevantly similar* in R2; to construct one further case of their own on which the account comes out wrong, identifying whether it is over- or under-inclusive and what mechanism produces the error; and to evaluate whether the objection that the account cannot explain what is wrong with hypocritical blame, even in cases where it does not strip the blamer of standing, is fatal to it. Set it for a 75-minute slot worth 25 marks, and tell the student explicitly that no marks are available for attributing views to particular authors and that the account is to be treated as a self-contained object.

2. **A model answer of approximately 700 words**, written as a strong undergraduate would write it in the time, not as a journal article.

3. **A marking scheme totalling 25 marks**, allocated to named moves rather than to topics, that states for each move how many marks it carries and how candidates typically botch it. It should distinguish clearly between a first-class answer and a merely competent one, and it must make the discriminating insights explicit rather than leaving them implicit in the model answer. I would expect those to include: that R1 cannot do any work in Case B because the window opens long after the sister's affair, so the intuition has to be located in R2 or R3 or nowhere; that R2 is either trivially satisfied by the blamer's own account of relevant similarity or else must be read as assessed against the correct account of it, in which case the clause restates the fact that her self-exemption is unjustified rather than explaining it; that the fixed-indexing of the account to a single norm N leaves it structurally unable to handle the paradigm case of hypocrisy, which is a dispute about the norm's reach rather than a refusal to comply with an agreed norm; that R3 can carry Case A only if *issues from* is given a dominance or sufficiency reading, which then strips standing from blame that is partly moved by satisfaction, by therapeutic advice, or by a desire to repair a relationship; and that these pressures together point toward collapsing the account onto R3, with R1 and R2 redundant. You may substitute better moves if you find them, but the scheme should say what a candidate must do to earn them.

Finally, include a short note on two marking situations I always have to adjudicate: what to award an answer that spends its time rejecting the framework rather than working within it, and what to award an answer that reaches the right verdicts by an obviously lucky route.
