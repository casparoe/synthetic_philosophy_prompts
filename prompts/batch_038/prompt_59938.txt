I spent forty-odd years in comparative physiology — mostly measuring things in fish — and since retiring I've taken up philosophy, which is turning out to be considerably more work than I expected. I also sit on the trustee board of a small animal welfare charity, and the argument we can never finish is which animals our campaigns should actually count: cephalopods, decapods, insects, farmed fish, and how far to push it. I've landed on a fairly definite position that I want stress-tested rather than flattered, so please be as unkind to it as the evidence allows.

The position: raw brain size is worthless as a guide to morally relevant mental capacity — a large cetacean brain tells you almost nothing on its own — and what really matters is neuron numbers in the parts doing the interesting work, i.e. the pallium or cortex rather than the cerebellum. What sold me on it was the cell-counting literature that came out of the "brain soup" style methods and the unbiased stereology they compete with: once you count cells instead of weighing tissue, the ordering changes completely, because packing density varies enormously, some small brains are extraordinarily dense, and huge brains turn out to be mostly cerebellar granule cells that plausibly contribute very little to anything you'd care about morally. So restricted neuron count is my best available proxy, and it's the thing I want our policy to key on when we decide whether to spend money on prawns or on pigs.

What would count against this? Give me the full map of its exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which tier each item sits in and why. I want the measurement-level threats separated from the conceptual ones: how much of the position rests on counting methods that disagree with each other or are sensitive to fixation and dissection choices; whether the restriction to "the relevant" neurons is doing illegitimate work, i.e. whether I've gerrymandered the metric so that elephants and whales can be dismissed by definition and nothing could ever surprise me; whether neuron number is even the right currency at all, given glia, cell size, connectivity, myelination, conduction velocity and metabolic rate; and whether the inference from cognitive capacity to moral status is a seperate and much shakier step, since the capacity to suffer might live in subcortical affective systems that don't scale with pallial neuron counts at all. Tell me what the view predicts that its rivals don't — behaviour-first views, affective-systems views, whatever you think the real competitors are — and name two or three concrete organisms that would actually discriminate between them, rather than cases everyone can reinterpret. I'd like to know which of these a committed defender of my position should concede as genuinely counting against it and which they'd be entitled to wave off, and finish with a straight answer on whether this view risks anything at all or has been insulated by qualifications until it's unfalsifiable — and if it has, whether that's fatal for a claim of this kind or just normal for a proxy measure. I'd find it most persuasive if you pointed at the sort of empirical result that would make me rewrite my own trustee paper next month. One practical thing: no reading list and no citations, I don't need them and you'd probably get them wrong anyway — just describe the findings, methods and arguments in your own words, and flag anything you're not sure about. British English throughout, please.
