I'm auditing a graduate philosophy-of-mind seminar this term and I've been asked to lead the discussion section on Dennett's "Quining Qualia," in front of people who know the material considerably better than I do. The part I follow is the general shape of the coffee-taster case: two tasters, years into the same blend, both reporting that the coffee no longer pleases them the way it did, but locating the change in different places — one in the coffee as it is for him, the other in himself. What I don't follow is how much work the argument is entitled to do, because I've become convinced that the report "it tastes the same to me" is being asked to carry more than one kind of claim, and that the conclusion slides between them. I'd like you to explain the conceptual geography here rather than give me another exegesis of the paper; I've read it three times and I can recite the dialectic.

To sharpen what I mean, here are three tasters of my own devising. All are twenty years into the same blend, and all report that something has changed. **Aldous** says the coffee tastes precisely as it always did — same body, same finish — but he no longer likes it, and he explains this as a change in his own standards: two decades of tasting everything else has spoiled him for this one. The liking moved; the taste did not. **Beatrix** says her liking, her descriptions of the cup, her ability to pick it out blind, and her willingness to keep drinking it are all exactly what they were — she'd still choose it over anything on the shelf — but that the coffee now tastes different to her. Pressed to say how it differs, she can't; she only insists that the difference is in the tasting itself and not in anything she does with it. **Cosmo** says the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and that he likes it exactly as much, but he has noticed that he can no longer reliably distinguish this blend from a much cheaper supermarket brand in a blind trial. Nothing has changed in how the cup strikes him; what has changed is that the striking is no longer finely enough tuned to track what is actually in it.

Please explain what the real conceptual differences are among these three, and in particular: whether Cosmo's case is a genuine third category or a disguised version of one of the other two; whether "it tastes the same to me" expresses the same claim in each of their mouths, given that in Cosmo's it looks less like a report about his experience than a report about the resolution of his discriminatory capacities — and if that's right, whether Beatrix's contrary claim is also partly about resolution, which would seem to pull the rug out from under the idea that she is reporting a change in a quale at all. Then tell me what to make of the authority asymmetry: we take Aldous at his word without argument, while Beatrix's report invites reinterpretation, and I can't tell whether that asymmetry is a discovery the argument earns or a prejudice it smuggles in. Finally, and most importantly for my purposes, say which of the three the anti-qualia argument actually needs to eliminate, and whether it can eliminate that one without also eliminating something plainly unobjectionable — Cosmo, for instance, who surely has undergone a real change in himself even if nobody would call it a change in how the coffee tastes. I'm not after a bibliography or a map of who has defended what; I want the distinctions laid out so that I can defend them at a whiteboard.
