I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I have been assigned a substantial feature on a dispute that resurfaces whenever a large conservation project is announced: the claim, pressed mainly by writers in the effective-altruism tradition, that degrading or destroying habitat may reduce total wild animal suffering, on the grounds that the animals who would otherwise have been created into a landscape of predation, parasitism, and starvation never exist at all. The field ecologists I have interviewed find the claim repellent; the philosophers who advance it find that repellence question-begging. I have roughly seven thousand words and a single reporting cycle, and I need help deciding what the spine of the piece should be.

Here is the menu of candidate cruxes as I currently see it. Please tell me which three deserve the most space, in order, and defend the ordering rather than surveying the field:

1. The scope question: whether the invertebrates who constitute the overwhelming majority of animal individuals are sentient at all, and whether any present method could settle it.

2. The baseline question: whether the claim that habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering is well-formed at all until one has fixed a population ethics. My working suspicion is that competing theories differ not merely in the magnitude of the answer but in its sign, so that person-affecting views, total views, and critical-level views each return a different verdict on identical ecological facts. If that is right, then the empirical work everyone is arguing over sits downstream of a choice nobody is making explicitly.

3. The measurement question: whether the distribution of causes of death in a given habitat, and the duration of each, can be estimated accurately enough to sign that habitat's expected welfare contribution.

4. The reductio question: on a purely suffering-minimizing metric, paving a wetland scores better than restoring it, and a lifeless landscape scores best of all. Does that outcome discredit the metric, or discredit the intuition that recoils from it?

5. The discourse question: whether publishing the claim measurably erodes public support for habitat protection, and whether that consequence may legitimately enter into what a journalist decides to report.

6. The institutional question: whether welfare-inclusive impact assessment is something an agency could actually operate, or a hook for opponents of regulation to demand yet another study.

Apply these criteria explicitly and weigh them against one another: how much of the real practical disagreement turns on the answer; whether the crux could plausibly be moved within a few years by empirical work, or only by argument, or not at all; whether resolving it would change any decision that someone is actually positioned to make; and, for non-specialist readers, whether it can be made vivid without collapsing into a slogan. Where two criteria pull in opposite directions, tell me which you are trading against which and why.

Two further requests. Be aggressive about the implications people assert that do not follow, and equally attentive to the ones that are real but routinely understated: which standard moves in this debate are dead ends, and where am I most likely to be misled by whichever camp has been loudest online? Then tell me what would change your ranking, what evidence or argument appearing in the next few years would move an item up or down, and what development should prompt you to tell me to abandon this frame entirely and write about something adjacent instead.

One loose end to fold in. The argument that most wild lives are dominated by early mortality is usually built on r-K selection vocabulary, and my understanding is that ecologists themselves largely set that vocabulary aside in the early nineties in favor of life-history theory, with fast and slow life histories taking the place of r and K. Is that objection as damaging as I suspect it is, is it an objection to the welfare conclusion or only to the way the conclusion is packaged, and does it belong in the top three?

Please do not send me a bibliography or a list of papers to read. I have my own contacts and my fact-checking is handled separately. What I want is the agenda and the reasoning behind it.
