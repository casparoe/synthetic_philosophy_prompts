I chair a forty-five-minute author-meets-critics session at our department's spring colloquium, and I need to hand the respondent a set of questions in advance. The author's book argues that the paradox of fiction rests on a category confusion between two tiers of emotional life: a lower tier of stimulus-driven, belief-free response systems — startle, flinch, disgust at a wound — and an upper tier of cognitively mediated emotions in the ordinary sense, such as grief, indignation, and admiration, which do require belief-like commitments about their objects. On her account, fiction reliably recruits the lower tier plus an imaginative gloss that subjects then misreport as the upper tier; where readers insist they are grieving for a character, the grief is in fact directed at something they genuinely believe in — their own losses, their own mortality — with the novel serving as its occasion rather than its object. She extends the account to suspense on re-viewing, which she treats as lower-tier arousal shaped by pacing, score, and cutting rather than by any uncertainty about the outcome. When pressed, her habitual reply is that 'the objection already assumes the folk taxonomy my account is meant to replace.'

Write exactly five numbered questions for the respondent. Four must target the following, in any order: first, the scope of her explanation — the responses she can accommodate without belief are precisely the ones that never generated the paradox, while the ones that did are the ones she redescribes as something else, so ask her to identify a single fiction-directed emotion her account preserves as genuine and still explains; second, the belief-freeness of the lower tier itself, given that even startle and flinch are visibly dampened by what the subject knows — the snake behind glass, the transparent floor over a long drop, the shrunken jump once a safety cue is trusted — and given that if the lower tier is cognitively penetrated, the boundary between the tiers is no longer a belief boundary and her dissolution loses its engine; third, the redirection claim, pressed through the fact that readers report being consoled by a sequel in which a dead character survives and re-devastated by one in which she dies again, which is hard to square with the grief's being about the reader's own life; fourth, the suspense claim, which needs to explain why lower-tier arousal is nonetheless experienced as being about whether the character gets out, and why some re-viewers lose it entirely while others do not.

The fifth question is yours to choose, subject to one condition: it must be the question most likely to make her revise or abandon a particular commitment, and it must say which commitment you take to be at risk. Each item should be two sentences — the question, then the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give — and at least two of the follow-ups must be built so that her appeal to the folk taxonomy is not an available escape. Nothing generic about her career or her influences, and no question she can dismiss by declaring first-person reports unreliable or by deferring the issue to future neuroscience. Return the five numbered items only: no introduction, no headings, no closing note.
