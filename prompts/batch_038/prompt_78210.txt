I've lived in this town my whole life except two years in the army, and I run a small engine repair shop off the state road. About three years back I let myself get talked onto the county historical commission, which is how I ended up in the middle of something that has eaten two years of my evenings. I don't have any training in any of this, so I might be describing the fight badly, but here it is.

In October of 1931 the impoundment up on the ridge behind the old slate works let go, and the water came down through the bottom of town, which is where the boarders and the seasonal hands and the men brought in by the labor contractor lived. Every October the church on Water Street holds a remembrance and reads names off a brass plaque, and the plaque says twenty-seven, and under the names it says and others whose names we do not know. For maybe ten years now a guy who used to be the plant engineer, plus a couple of people from the historical society, have been saying the number is eleven, because the county coroner's ledger says eleven and the company payroll books say eleven, and that the plaque inflates it and that inflating it is its own kind of insult to the eleven who are actually documented. The descendants' association and the church say the ledger was kept in a company town, by the company that built the impoundment; that nobody in the flats was in the payroll books anyway because they were boarders; and that the women who washed the bodies and walked door to door asking after missing brothers knew more than a clerk did. Both sides are decent people and both sides think the other is doing something close to a sin.

Here's the forcing problem. There's a state marker coming, the money is already approved, the review board wants a figure on it, and they will not accept between eleven and twenty-seven. Our commission has to send the wording up in the spring. I've been reading about these arrangements where two camps that can't stand each other sit down and produce one single document together — adversarial collaboration, I think its called — and I want to try it on us. I dont want a referee ruling on who's right, and I don't want a script of the two of them talking past each other. I want the document itself, the one both sides could actually sign.

So write that joint statement. Begin with the claims both camps genuinely accept, and I mean genuinely — not watered down to where it says nothing anybody would dispute. There's more shared ground than either side admits, and some of it is awkward. The earliest place anyone has found twenty-seven in writing is a newspaper piece from 1953 and a church fundraising letter from 1955; before that the church's own language was more than twenty, and the oral histories were mostly recorded in the seventies by a folklorist, forty years on. On the other side, part of the company's records burned in 1954; one of the eleven in the coroner's ledger is a man whose granddaughter will tell you he came out of the water and died of pneumonia in 1941; and last summer a college class ran ground-penetrating radar over the pauper's section of the cemetery and came up with something like nineteen or twenty plots, though a few of them look older than 1931.

Then name the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody. My hunch is that one crux is whether the silence of an archive kept by the responsible party counts as evidence about who died, and another is whether the number on a marker is a claim about bodies or a claim about who the town is answerable for. If you think I've got those wrong, or that one of them isn't a real disagreement at all but two words for the same thing, say so and say why. For each crux, give me something concrete that both sides would agree bears on it — the radar survey seems the obvious one — and write down in advance what each side predicts about it, so we can check later who blinked first. Where they still differ, phrase each difference in wording that side would accept as a description of its own view: I want the plant engineer to read his paragraph without feeling caricatured, and the same for the woman who runs the descendants' association.

No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, none of that. Continuous prose front to back, the way you'd write a letter. I know a table is the tidy way to lay out the disagreements and I'm telling you not to use one, because this has to be read aloud at a public meeting and I want sentences.

Last thing, and it matters to me: tell me what would change your mind here. Which consideration would make you hand me a different document, or make you decide that one of the two camps can't honestly put its name to this? And tell me where you think the draft is most likely to fail, and whether it has quietly taken a side without admitting it.
