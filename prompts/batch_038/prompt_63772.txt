Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about drug policy, and the frustrating part was that we could not locate our disagreement. He favors Portugal-style decriminalization but is appalled by commercial cannabis retail; I favor a regulated legal market for cannabis and think Portugal was too timid about supply. We agree that the drug war has been a disaster, that drug use can be self-destructive, and that children should be kept away from all of it. Somewhere in there the two positions come apart, and every time we tried to name the split we ended up trading labels — harm reduction, legalization, neo-prohibitionism — that told us nothing. I want to replace that argument with an instrument, and I would like you to build it.

Write a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a named position on drug policy, beginning from no commitment other than general moral and political competence, and ending at a leaf. I want roughly ten branch nodes and between ten and fourteen leaves, and I want it built out of the logical space rather than out of a list of familiar camps: first the questions and the answer combinations they permit, then the positions located in that space.

The tree should, at minimum, keep the following apart, because these are the axes my friend and I kept collapsing into one another:

- Whom the law targets (the user, the supplier, or both) as distinct from how severe the sanction is (criminal prosecution, administrative penalty with no criminal record, or nothing at all).
- Decriminalization with a real administrative apparatus attached — fines, mandatory appearances, the option of treatment in place of a penalty — as distinct from full de jure tolerance.
- Someone who thinks recreational intoxication is morally wrong and nonetheless wants criminal penalties against users repealed, as distinct from someone who thinks such use is morally neutral.
- Whether the state may restrict a substance solely because using it badly harms the user, as distinct from only because it harms third parties or imposes costs on them.
- Which lever a respondent thinks actually moves consumption: supply-side enforcement, price, potency limits, advertising bans, treatment capacity, or the social meaning of the drug.
- Whether the respondent would accept a legal commercial market with advertising, branding, and profit motives, or prefers a state monopoly or a non-profit club model that keeps the drug legal but commercially inert.

Please also include at least one node that turns on a concrete historical episode rather than an abstraction: Portugal from 2001 onward, with possession referred to administrative commissions rather than prosecutors; Oregon's 2020 ballot measure decriminalizing small amounts and the legislature's partial reversal four years later; or the retail cannabis regimes that opened in the American West in the mid-2010s. Nodes of that kind should ask the respondent what they take the record to show — direction of overdose deaths, of blood-borne infection among people who inject, of adolescent use, of traffic safety, of the revenues and power of illicit suppliers — and not whether they approve of the policy.

Phrase every node as a question a respondent could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. No node may ask whether the respondent supports decriminalization or legalization; those are the outputs, not the inputs. Where a node concerns evidence, do not lean on statistics I would have to verify: ask for direction and for the respondent's confidence, and let the tree branch on the combination of the two.

Three further deliverables:

1. Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what question would be needed to separate them. My friend and I may well answer all ten nodes identically; if so, that is the most useful thing the instrument can tell us, and I want it stated rather than papered over.
2. For each leaf, give the position's name if it has one, or mark it unnamed, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
3. List the answer combinations the space permits but that appear to have no occupants, and diagnose each: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored?

Finally, because the point is to sort people by what they actually believe rather than by what they call themselves, append a short key locating the familiar camps — Portugal-style reform, full commercial legalization, prohibition concentrated on supply rather than use, and the abolitionist view that criminal enforcement should end without a legal market being erected in its place — at the nodes where they diverge from one another.
