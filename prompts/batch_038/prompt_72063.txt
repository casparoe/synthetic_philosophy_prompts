I am in the final year of a BFA in film at a small school of art and design, and in June I will give the graduating student address at our diploma ceremony. I would like you to write that address for me — specifically, the version of it that I have spent six weeks failing to write myself.

The occasion and the room, since almost everything turns on them. The ceremony is held in a hall named after the collector who endowed our school's prize for experimental practice, an award given each year to the most conceptually ambitious graduate work; this year's recipient is a classmate of mine, and the man whose name is on the building is also receiving an honorary degree that morning, largely for a piece best known for consisting of an unlit room and a wall text you read in the corridor afterwards in order to know what happened inside. The audience will be about six hundred: parents and grandparents, some younger siblings, the trustees and donors, and faculty from five departments. My grandmother, who raised me, will be in the second row, and she has never once told me she understood anything I have made. Perhaps a fifth of the room teaches or studies art theory and will recognize every move I make; the rest will not know who Tolstoy is, let alone what he argued about art.

The reason I am asking rather than writing it. I am in a philosophy of art seminar that spent a month on Tolstoy's 1897 book on art, and the month has done something to me I cannot undo. I now find myself substantially convinced by his account: that art is the deliberate communication of a feeling the artist has himself experienced, that it succeeds to the degree that others are infected by that feeling, that the strength of the infection depends on the individuality of the feeling, its clarity, and above all the sincerity of the person transmitting it, and that the worth of art depends on which feelings it transmits and how many people are capable of catching them. I am convinced by his rejection of beauty as the criterion, by his impatience with obscurity and with allusion as a substitute for communication, and by his claim that an art addressed to a small cultivated class is a corrupted art. I am also aware that he applied these standards without mercy, dismissing Shakespeare, Beethoven, Wagner, and Dante along with most of his own fiction, while praising Uncle Tom's Cabin, Les Misérables, Dickens, and Millet's paintings of people at work.

My own thesis film is the sort of thing he would have called a counterfeit: twenty-two minutes of borrowed iconography, legible mainly to people who have already seen the work it quotes, dependent on a programme note, and made — I said this aloud in seminar — largely in order to be admired by eleven people. Two professors whose work I love, and whose kindness to me I cannot repay, would fare no better under his criteria. So the address I have been asked to give is, on the account I have come to hold, a ceremony honoring a great deal of bad art, funded by a collector whose prize rewards precisely the vices Tolstoy thought most damaging, and delivered by someone guilty of them.

What I need from you, concretely:

An address of seven to eight minutes, roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words, written to be spoken aloud rather than read.

The theory has to be load-bearing but invisible. The address must itself be an attempt at infection — it has to transmit a feeling this particular room can actually catch — and not an argument addressed to it. If it turns into a lecture about Tolstoy it fails by his own standard. A listener who has never read him should feel the pressure of his criteria without needing his name, while the fifth of the room that knows the book should be able to see exactly where each condition is doing work.

It may not justify anything on the grounds of beauty. The usual ceremonial consolation — you have made beautiful things, and the world needs beauty — is closed to me, and I do not want it smuggled back in by way of words like luminous or ravishing.

It must confront the reflexive problem, which is the real reason I cannot write this myself. Tolstoy's sincerity condition appears to condemn the ceremonial address as a genre: a piece produced to order, on a fixed date, for a captive and largely paying audience, engineered to produce a predetermined effect, looks very much like what he means by art made for effect rather than from feeling. I want the address to face that squarely. I do not want it to dissolve the problem with a wink or with a joke about how Tolstoy would have hated the occasion.

It must decide — and I want a decision, not a survey of the considerations — whether to say the cruel verdicts out loud in front of the people whose life's work they would exclude, including the honorary degree recipient sitting on the platform twenty feet from where I will be standing. There is a genuine question whether an honest Tolstoyan address can be delivered at this occasion at all. If you conclude it cannot, say so plainly and give me the address you can defend instead, explaining what you gave up.

It should be willing to be uncomfortable, but it must not become a public humiliation of my classmates on the one day that belongs to them, and it must not turn my grandmother into a rhetorical prop. Those two constraints are in tension and I want you to work inside the tension rather than announce that it exists.

After the address, give me three or four paragraphs of your own reasoning: what position the address takes and why you took it; which of Tolstoy's conditions you deliberately aimed to satisfy and which you knowingly failed; whether Tolstoy himself would have classified this speech as art or as a counterfeit; and where you have leaned on a contestable reading of him — in particular, whether the sincerity condition really does exclude commissioned ceremonial work, and whether his standard for the highest art should be read narrowly as Christian or more broadly as whatever unites people in the best consciousness available to their age. Please paraphrase him rather than quote him; I cannot check a quotation, and I would rather have an honest paraphrase than a plausible-sounding one.

Be committed throughout. I do not want a balanced appraisal of Tolstoy's merits and errors, and I do not want the address to hedge between his standards and the standards of the room in order to be safe with both. Take a side, make the definite choices, and give me your reasons for them.
