I am preparing a methods seminar for beginning doctoral students in history on the problem of silence in the sources, and I want a purpose-built thought experiment rather than another lecture on how archives are products of power. Please construct one to the following specifications.

**Setting.** Invent two surviving bodies of record concerning a single event in a plausible fictional jurisdiction roughly a century ago — a strike, a forced removal, an epidemic quarantine, a tax revolt, your choice — and describe each in one paragraph. Do not use a real archive, a real institution, or a real event, and do not quote the wording of any invented document: describe what the files and registers contain at the level of their series, categories, and coverage. These two bodies of record are exhaustive. There is no third collection, no missing ledger that turns up in an attic, and no body of survivor testimony waiting to settle the question.

**Specification 1.** The first archive must be the better one by every ordinary standard, and must be shown to be better on at least the following: volume; continuity of coverage across the whole period; proximity in time to the events; internal consistency; quality of preservation; quality of arrangement and description (a working finding aid, coherent series, stable units of description); and absence of known false statements. Nothing in it has been destroyed, withdrawn, or falsified, and nobody who made it intended to mislead anyone.

**Specification 2.** Nevertheless, a careful historian confined to it must assert something false about the event — a claim of the kind historians actually make, such as when the event ended, how many people were involved, or who acted, rather than anything metaphysical. She must be led there by ordinary, defensible source criticism, including the perfectly reasonable inference from the absence of later entries to the absence of later events.

**Specification 3.** A second historian, confined to a fragmentary and much worse-documented rival body of record — thin, later, poorly arranged, with a finding aid that barely works — must withhold that claim or contradict it, and must be right. She must arrive at her conclusion by ordinary criticism of her own material, not by luck, intuition, or prior knowledge of the truth.

**Specification 4.** The mechanism linking 1 through 3 must be classificatory rather than mendacious. The false belief must arise from what the first archive's creators counted as an event worth a file, from the unit of description they adopted, and from the way the resulting arrangement guides a researcher's eye — not from lying, suppression, or loss. It must be possible to say, truly, that the first archive records everything its own categories recognize, and that this is precisely why it misleads.

After the two paragraphs, run these checks:

(a) Itemize how the first archive satisfies each metric in Specification 1, one line per metric, so that a reader can confirm the case has not been rigged by quietly making it a bad archive after all.

(b) Locate the silencing within the familiar four-site account of how power enters the production of history — the making of the record, the assembly of records into a collection, their retrieval by a researcher, and the retrospective assignment of significance (the framework Michel-Rolph Trouillot set out in *Silencing the Past*, 1995). Say which site is doing the work in your case, and argue why it is that one and not the narrative or retrospective stage, which is where students almost always put it.

(c) State, for each historian, the rule of inference by which silence is being treated as evidence, and make explicit the assumption about the recorder's coverage — its sampling rule, in effect — that the first historian needs and does not have.

(d) Name the single feature of the case that makes the asymmetry go. Then remove it and say what remains: if what remains is only the familiar complaint that the powerful kept the records, say so plainly, and say what would have to be added back for the case to work again.

(e) Give the strongest available objection from a defender of the first archive's epistemic superiority. It must be a genuine methodological argument — for instance, that the rival body of record has categories of its own and licenses inferences at least as questionable, that the seminar's verdict that the second historian is right already presumes the independent access to the past the whole exercise denies, or that a well-arranged archive is corrigible in ways that fragments are not. The objector may not simply complain about power, and the objection may not be a straw man: it should be one a good student could make and one I would have to answer in the room.

(f) Close with two or three sentences on what this case teaches that a general talk about archives and power does not.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,100 words in total. The case must generate the verdicts from the material you describe; a scenario that stipulates that the first historian is wrong and the second is right, without making that follow from the coverage and arrangement you have specified, does not count.
