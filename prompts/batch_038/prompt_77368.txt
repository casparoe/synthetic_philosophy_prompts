A friend and I argued for two hours last night about the statue and the clay and got nowhere, so I would like to see the dispute run properly. Write a dialogue of roughly a thousand words between two metaphysicians standing in a conservation studio: one who holds that the statue and the clay are numerically distinct objects, each wholly present in the same place at the same time, and one who holds that coincidence is a mistake, to be replaced by distinct four-dimensional wholes that merely share temporal parts.

Give them a case that is not the familiar one. The figure was knocked to the floor and shattered; it has now been reassembled from all of the original matter, in the original arrangement, by a conservator who intended nothing else from the outset. The usual asymmetry runs backwards here: it is far from clear that the lump persisted through being reduced to scattered pieces, whereas the artifact, taken apart and put back together with that intention in place throughout, has a serious claim to have persisted the whole time.

Have them argue about that case rather than retreating to the standard smashing example. Each should press the other on what their own ontology commits them to about the fragments, on how many things were on the pedestal before the fall, and on whether the advantage the statue enjoys here is something their view can explain or must simply swallow. Neither may be written as having missed something obvious, and each should concede at least one substantive point to the other.

Close with a brief note saying how confident you are that each side's central moves are ones its defenders would actually make, and identify the single claim you are least sure of.
