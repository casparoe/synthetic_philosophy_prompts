I am a junior at a public high school, and this year I am taking an elective called Technology and Power, which meets twice a week and is mostly readings and argument. In the fall we read Langdon Winner's 1980 essay asking whether artifacts have politics, and the case that took hold of our class was his account of the Long Island parkways built during Robert Moses's years in charge: the overpasses were given a clearance low enough for an automobile and too low for a city bus, so that the beach at the end of the parkway was reachable in practice only by people who happened to own a car. Winner's point, as I understood it, was that once the concrete was poured the question of who would get to the shore was settled without anyone ever voting on it, and it stayed settled for decades after the men who approved the plans were dead. He also says somewhere in the essay that the interesting cases cut right across the distinction between intended and unintended consequences, and that phrase has been stuck in my head ever since.

Our teacher was not satisfied with the example. She objected that a low clearance is simply a low clearance: parkways of that period were built low partly to keep commercial traffic off a landscaped road and partly for reasons of cost and appearance, and Moses barred buses from his parkways by regulation anyway, so the exclusion may have lived in the rule rather than in the concrete. She said Winner had chosen a case where the artifact and the intention are impossible to pull apart, and that this makes the example rhetorically powerful and evidentially useless. I could not decide which of them was right, and after a while I stopped wanting to decide, because I think the unresolved version is more honest than either side winning. That is the thing I want a poem to hold.

My class is contributing to our school's humanities night in April, and I would like to read something aloud before the panel on infrastructure and inequality. Please write me a dramatic monologue of thirty to forty lines in blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter, enjambed where it helps, and something that reads well out loud in under three minutes — spoken by one of those overpasses, addressing no one in particular. The bridge may report only physical facts and physical events: what it was poured from and when, the distance from its underside to the asphalt, the vehicles that pass beneath it, the one kind of vehicle that stops short at the approach and turns around, the shadow it throws at four in the afternoon, the salt, the cracks, the summer it was repaved, a family walking under it toward the water with something carried between them. It must never use an abstraction or a moral word — no politics, no racism, discrimination, inequality, oppression, systemic, or deliberate — and it must never identify people by race or by income; the passengers should be recognizable only by what they ride in or what they are holding. Above all, the bridge must never claim to have chosen anything: it can be built, stand, weigh, span, measure, carry, allow, and stop, but it must not intend, decide, mean, or refuse. Somewhere around the two-thirds mark I want a turn, achieved by a change of register rather than by any device of rhyme: the bridge reports that people have lately begun arguing about whether its height was set for the reason the argument assumes, that they come with tapes and with copies of minutes and with rival theories, and that none of the arguing has changed by an inch what fits beneath it. End on a measurement or a plain fact about traffic, not on a lesson, and do not resolve the disagreement my teacher and Winner were having. A reader who knows the essay should be able to tell exactly which case this is and that the poem has taken the intended/unintended distinction seriously; a reader who has never heard of either of them should simply find it a good poem about a bridge. Please begin your response with the poem itself, with no preface, no note on the choices you made, and no commentary after the final line.
