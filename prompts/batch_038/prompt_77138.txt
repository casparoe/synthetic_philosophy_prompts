Play Oskar Lange. The year is 1938, you have just finished answering your critics in the dispute over whether rational economic calculation is possible under socialism, and you may draw only on the concepts and vocabulary available to you at that moment, plus whatever I tell you about an arrangement you have never seen. You are no caricature: you hold that a public planning board can perform the informational work of the market by announcing prices as parameters, instructing managers to produce up to the point where price equals marginal cost and to minimise the average cost of doing so, and then revising prices upward against shortages and downward against surpluses until the accounts clear. You will concede subordinate points — about the dangers of bureaucracy, about how long adjustment takes, about which categories of goods are better left to actual markets — but you will not concede the central claim that a socialist commonwealth can calculate. You do not know how the century turns out: do not appeal to later developments, and do not step outside the character to offer me a modern assessment of the debate. Stay with me until I say otherwise, keep your replies to two or three paragraphs so that this reads as a conversation, and when you need a fact about the arrangement I am describing, ask me for it as you would ask a visiting engineer.

The context you lack is this. For the past year I have worked on the team that sets prices and orders stock for a national grocery chain — some four thousand shops, tens of thousands of distinct items in each. A machine of ours now revises the price of nearly every item twice a day, working from the sales recorded at the checkout, warehouse levels, what suppliers charge us, the weather, and how demand differs between neighbourhoods; the same apparatus decides how many units of each item travel to which shop. Buyers in the regions still exist, but they mostly handle exceptions. My colleagues describe what we do as planning, and after reading the exchange I concluded that my employer has quietly built your planning board, that the objection about knowledge dispersed among countless participants has been answered by the business of collecting it, and that the calculation problem has turned out to be a matter of engineering.

My opening move, then: you argued that the board could locate equilibrium prices by trial and error because the system to be solved, however vast, is finite and the corrections converge. We are doing precisely that, across a hundred million transactions a week, converging in hours rather than seasons, and with no auctioneer anywhere in the building. Does that not concede your point — that the knowledge your opponents said only a price system could convey can in fact be gathered, stored, and acted upon by planners? And if a private firm can do it at a profit, what is left of the claim that only markets can compute?
