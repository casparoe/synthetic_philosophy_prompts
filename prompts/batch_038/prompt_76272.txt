Last night I lost the better part of four hours to an argument with a friend that ended with neither of us moved, and left me holding a question I cannot answer, which is why I am writing to you.

Her opening move was unremarkable: how do I know the Earth existed more than five minutes ago, rather than having come into being complete with all its apparent past? I played the obvious hand — memory, tree rings, starlight in transit, the geological record — and she played the obvious replies: memories can be implanted, the fossils and the starlight can have been installed along with everything else. But somewhere in the third round the shape of the exchange changed, and it is that change I want identified.

What I noticed was this. Every test I offered had to be trusted *on precisely the point under dispute*: to use the tree rings as evidence I must already assume that traces of the past are traces of the past. So the doubt had no route by which it could be resolved — not a difficult route, but no route at all. Yet nothing my friend said was incoherent, and the proposition she doubted was not necessary or true by definition; it was, so far as I could tell, perfectly possible that she was right. And still her doubt did not behave like a doubt. It never touched what either of us did next: we went on talking, drinking tea, referring to last week, exactly as before.

Then it spread, which is the part that struck me most. Having raised the five-minute question, she immediately wanted to press a second, ordinary question about whether I remembered a conversation correctly — and I found that I had no answer to the second one that did not depend on the material I had just conceded was unusable. Her own doubts lost their footing along with my claims. She could not keep the local question and press the global one; she had to choose, and she wanted both.

The same configuration turned up a week later at work, in a meeting, without any scepticism in sight: a colleague argued that a certain dataset could not be trusted because it was produced by people with incentives to shape it, and then, three minutes later, cited a different output of the same pipeline as evidence for his own point.

Now, here is what I think the thing is not, and I would like each of these near-misses dealt with rather than waved at. It is not self-refutation: my friend never contradicted herself. It is not question-begging, which seems to describe my answers rather than her question. It is not unfalsifiability in the usual sense, because the item under doubt was not a hypothesis competing with rivals — it was closer to something my other claims are measured against, so that dropping it would not mean adopting a competitor but losing the ability to have competitors. It is not a presupposition of the conversation in the sense of something the participants have granted and could retract. It is not the trilemma of infinite regress, circularity, and arbitrary stopping: the trilemma treats all three outcomes as failures, whereas here the stopping does not feel like a failure at all. And I know there is a famous gesture of holding up one's hands in response to this kind of challenge; it struck me as changing the subject, and I would like to know whether that is the right diagnosis of it.

So: does this configuration have an established name, in epistemology or in the theory of argument? Which of the near-misses above is genuinely the closest, and on exactly what point does the described thing come apart from it? If there is no established name, coin one and define it, and then give me its edges. In particular: does the phenomenon attach to the proposition doubted, to the practice it sits inside, or to the act of doubting itself — and why does that choice matter? Must revision be impossible in principle, or does it also cover background items that could in fact be revised but never are (I spent years assuming a colleague was honest and was eventually forced to abandon that; the structure looked identical)? And what is the one test I can apply mid-argument, without philosophy, to tell this kind of doubt from an ordinary unresolvable one?

If the concept belongs to a particular author or book, mention it in passing, but I am not after references — I want the carving. Please also tell me how confident you are in the identification you land on, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, the one I should distrust if I repeat it back to her.

Your final line must be of the form \answer{...} containing only the name you settle on: an established term if one fits, otherwise "none established" followed by your coinage.
