I'm an ML engineer doing a part-time philosophy MA, and last weekend I was at a reading group where we got onto Jeffrey conditionalization — the rule for updating when your evidence isn't a proposition you come to believe outright but a shift in your probabilities across a partition, so Q(A) = Σ P(A|Eᵢ)Q(Eᵢ) with all the old conditionals P(A|Eᵢ) held fixed. One of the other students, who does RL for a living, spent most of the session insisting the whole thing is a non-problem. His pitch: perception is just a noisy channel, Jeffrey's rule is what you get when you propagate a soft label instead of a hard one, and if you want to be pedantic you can always add an extra variable for what the sensor said and recover the entire update as ordinary conditioning in a slightly bigger space. Therefore there's no such thing as genuinely uncertain evidence, the input problem is a philosopher's artifact, and the classical Bayesian story is fine exactly as it stands. He also said this is why nobody in engineering ever felt the need for a special rule — you just carry distributions around and move on. Two of us pushed back and got nowhere, partly becuase none of us could say crisply what we were objecting to.

So I want the analogy audited rather than summarized. Give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: what plays the channel, the transmitted symbol, the received symbol, the confusion matrix, the prior over inputs, and where the agent's perceptual judgment is supposed to sit in that picture. Then tell me which disanalogies actually threaten his use of it and which ones are cosmetic. A few I can half-see and want pressure-tested: the channel picture seems to assume there is a definite output symbol that gets received, even if the receiver is unsure what it means, whereas the Jeffrey case is supposed to be one where no definite proposition about the world gets learned at all; the confusion matrix is meant to be given from outside the agent's own probability assignments, but Q(Eᵢ) is supposed to be *the* deliverance of perception, so I can't see where a likelihood would come from without just re-posing the problem; and rigidity looks like it's doing real work that the channel story gets for free. Also — does his "just condition on an extra variable" rewrite rescue the analogy or gut it? If every Jeffrey update is secretly ordinary conditioning on some virtual observation, then either perception always does deliver something certain, in which case he's right and the deflation stands, or the virtual observation is bookkeeping fiction, in which case the analogy isn't explaining anything. I want you to pick a horn and defend it, not just lay both out neatly. And seperately: I think two Jeffrey updates on different partitions don't commute, while plain conditioning is order-insensitive in the obvious cases — if that's right, does the channel picture have anything to say about it, or is that a spot where the analogy has already run out of road?

One more thing. Be straight with me about what's actually settled here versus what people who work on this still argue about — whether rigidity ever genuinely fails, whether Jeffrey's rule is the unique way to do what it does, whether the input problem is fatal or just unfinished business. Don't present his deflationary conclusion as the consensus if it isn't, but don't present the opposite as consensus either: I've got no stake in Jeffrey winning this. If you think the analogy is broadly sound and his conclusion follows, say so, and say where the residual unease is nonetheless legitimate. And if you can offer a better analogy for what a Jeffrey update is — one that doesn't smuggle in a definite received symbol — I'd take it, including an honest note on what that replacement costs you and which features of the case it still fails to capture.
