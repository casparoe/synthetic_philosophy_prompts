I co-edit a small annual called *Forms of Argument*, in which each commissioned piece parodies a genre of nonfiction prose in order to expose how a particular discipline actually reasons — the genre does the argumentative work, and the parody makes it visible. This year's section concerns evolutionary explanations of human conduct, and I would like to commission an advice column about costly signaling and the handicap principle. Not the cocktail-party version ("peacocks have tails, therefore people buy Rolexes"), but one whose jokes only land if the technical machinery is being handled correctly. The piece should be a complete agony-aunt column, roughly 950 to 1,100 words, written by an invented columnist who holds that every human act is a signal and that a signal is worth precisely what it would cost a worse person to fake it. Give the column a name, a one-line bio for the columnist, and a brief editor's note; then four reader letters with replies; and finish with two or three lines of small classified advertising in the column's own commercial idiom. Invent all names. Do not cite anything, do not name real researchers or real papers, and include no footnotes or bibliography: the columnist may gesture vaguely at "the biologists" or "the models," and that vagueness should itself read as part of what is being satirized.

Please seed the mailbag as follows, and treat the signaling theory in each letter as a constraint on the joke rather than as decoration. First, a wealthy man who bought an expensive electric car to demonstrate his environmental commitments and is furious that everyone reads it as a display of income; the reply has to explain, correctly and without condescension, that the receiver rather than the sender fixes what a signal means, and that a cost which barely bites the sender certifies nothing, since honesty depends on the cost falling harder on the low-quality type. Second, a woman whose rival has sent extravagant flowers weekly for months on a credit line, and who asks whether the display can be trusted; here the columnist should discover, with visible discomfort, that financed, recoverable, or third-party-borne cost severs exactly the correlation his whole framework rests on, and he should fail to close the loophole with anything better than a procedural fix — an audit, a bonding requirement, a waiting period. Third, a man with a deep voice and a square jaw who keeps being promoted over better candidates and wants advice on making his signals more authentic; the reply must keep an unfakeable, physically constrained index sharply distinct from a strategically produced handicap, and the writer must end up more aggrieved than he began. Fourth, a reader whose partner says he loves her every night at no cost to anyone and whose friends insist this therefore means nothing; the columnist must try to dismiss it as cheap talk and be unable to, because where interests genuinely coincide a costless message can be perfectly informative — let him paper over the difficulty and let the reader see the paper. At least one of the four should also turn on the distinction between a signal and a mere cue, that is, involuntary leakage the sender never chose, and in that case the columnist should be entirely right, which matters for the next paragraph.

Two further requirements. The target of the satire is an explanatory style — the reduction of all human conduct to honest-signaling equilibria, and its resale as self-help — not any person, institution, or school, and the columnist must not be a strawman: give him at least two letters in which he is simply, technically correct, so that the reader is obliged to grant the framework real explanatory purchase before watching it overreach. The standard criticisms must never be stated. No character may say that the theory is unfalsifiable, that the handicap principle is contested, or that honesty need not be costly; a reader should have to reconstruct those objections from what happens in the letters — from a display that has been adopted by so many senders that it no longer discriminates at all (a wedding photographer, a graduate applicant, a monastery's fasting rule, your choice), with the columnist's advice making the inflation worse; from a moment where the line between an unfakeable index and a producible handicap starts to look like a convenience of the modelling rather than a fact about the world; and from the cheap-talk letter he cannot answer. Then the closing move: the last item should reveal, from inside and without emphasis, that the column sits in the same economy as everything it diagnoses — the columnist turns out to be unpaid, or writing to a house style he did not choose, or answering to an editor whose demands cost him nothing — so that by his own differential-cost criterion his authority to advise anyone is exactly zero. Deliver this in the final line or two, deadpan, with no wink, no authorial aside, and no explanation of what the reader has just been shown.
