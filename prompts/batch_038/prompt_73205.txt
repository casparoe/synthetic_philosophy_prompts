Midway through the autumn term I took over a second-year module in moral psychology from a colleague who went on unexpected leave, so I am now marking essays for students whose seminars I have only just begun to lead. The subject has also become awkwardly personal this year: since the election, the same argument has been running, with variations, through my own household, and reading these papers at the kitchen table has not been entirely comfortable. I want to mark this one on its merits alone and be able to defend the mark to an external examiner, without importing my own commitments into the feedback.

The essay question I set was:

"Moral foundations theory reports a systematic difference between political liberals and political conservatives in which moral concerns they treat as relevant. Does that difference tell us anything about which of the two outlooks is morally correct? Say what would have to be true for the answer to be yes, and what would have to be true for it to be no."

Our department's scheme is out of twenty: 17–20 for a distinction-level paper that engages the normative question head-on and is accurate about the psychology; 13–16 for a competent paper with minor gaps or slips; 9–12 for a paper that describes the theory well but partly answers a different question; below 9 for serious inaccuracy or failure to engage.

Here is the submission.

---

Moral foundations theory, developed by Jonathan Haidt and his collaborators, holds that human morality rests on a small number of innate psychological foundations which cultures then elaborate in different ways. The original five are care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion and sanctity/degradation; a sixth, liberty/oppression, was proposed later, largely to accommodate libertarians, who fit awkwardly into a simple left–right scheme. Haidt compares the foundations to taste receptors: everyone is born with the same receptors, but different cuisines bring out different ones, and individuals differ in how strongly each receptor fires.

The theory grew out of Lawrence Kohlberg's stage theory of moral development. Kohlberg held that moral reasoning progresses through a sequence of stages, the highest of which treats justice as the whole of morality. Haidt's contribution was to add the remaining foundations as further stages beyond justice, so that a fully developed moral reasoner passes through justice and arrives at a broader palette of concerns. This is why the theory can be used to compare political outlooks: it measures how far along that sequence a person has got.

The central empirical finding is that liberals and conservatives use the receptors differently. On the Moral Foundations Questionnaire, liberals weight care and fairness very heavily and treat loyalty, authority and sanctity as comparatively unimportant, whereas conservatives distribute their weight more evenly across the foundations. In one of the researchers' own studies, participants were asked to complete the questionnaire as a typical liberal or a typical conservative would answer it; accuracy varied with the respondent's own ideology, and the self-described very liberal participants were the least accurate overall. That is suggestive: people whose morality is concentrated in fewer foundations seem to have more difficulty seeing the moral world from outside it.

Does any of this show that one side is correct? The obvious objection is that it does not, because the theory is descriptive. Haidt's social intuitionist model holds that moral judgments are caused by fast intuitions, with reasoning arriving afterwards to defend them, so the foundations are not conclusions anyone argued their way to. And if the foundations are products of cultural evolution rather than of rational insight, then establishing which foundations a person relies on tells us about the causal history of their judgment, not about its truth. Moving from conservatives register more foundations to conservatism is correct would commit a naturalistic fallacy.

There is a stronger version of this objection, which I think is the best one available. Haidt's taste analogy can be pushed further than he pushes it. A person born without bitter receptors is not wrong about food; they are simply missing something. Likewise a person unmoved by flags, or by concerns about ritual purity, is not thereby in error — they have one fewer input, and inputs are not claims. Missing a claim cannot make you mistaken, because you never asserted anything. On this reading the theory describes variation in moral experience the way colour-vision research describes variation in visual experience, and neither tells us which perceiver is right.

The same analogy, though, appears to cut the other way in practice. Haidt says that a moral matrix binds and blinds: a matrix drawing on more of the foundations gives its members a wider view of social reality, and the accuracy study shows that this wider view has measurable consequences for how well one can predict other people's judgments. If liberals systematically fail to register considerations — the value of institutions, the moral weight of loyalty — that genuinely carry moral weight, then their outlook is incomplete in a way that conservatism is not. Incompleteness of that kind does seem to count against a moral view, even if it does not refute it.

So the descriptive asymmetry is not proof, but it is evidence: the more foundations an outlook tracks, the more likely it is to be tracking something real. On balance moral foundations theory gives us a defeasible reason to prefer the broader moral palette, without ever settling the question outright.
