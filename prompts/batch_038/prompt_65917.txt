I am the elected chair of a central labor council, a regional federation of some forty affiliated locals from a dozen different international unions. Our constitution describes the council's role as "coordinating, not commanding": affiliates pay per-capita dues to the council, which in turn runs joint organizing, political, and mobilization programs, but each local retains exclusive authority over its own bargaining and strike decisions. I need a decision memo for the council's executive board, which meets Tuesday.

The case is as follows. Local 1180, our smallest affiliate at roughly 340 members, represents laundry, linen-distribution, and housekeeping workers employed by a private facilities contractor that services a large nonprofit hospital system in our region. Three weeks ago the local walked out after the contractor began transferring hospital-route work to a non-union firm, dissolving the bargaining unit route by route rather than discharging anyone outright. The local's own executive board authorized the strike; its membership was never balloted, and about a third of the unit, mostly the housekeeping crews, has continued working. The local has now set a picket line at the hospital's main staff entrance and is asking the council to call on all affiliates to honor it — in particular the roughly 4,000 hospital employees in three other locals who must cross that line daily to reach their shifts. Honoring the line would be secondary action: the hospital is not the employer of the striking workers. Our general counsel advises that a council-wide call could expose those three affiliates to unfair-labor-practice charges and damages liability, and could jeopardize their own contracts, two of which expire next spring.

Two members of the executive board have framed the decision in ways I cannot reconcile, and I want the memo to engage both on their own terms rather than averaging them:

- The first advocates what she calls the status view. A labor council is not a vendor of services to its affiliates but a standing commitment to treat any affiliate's fight as the federation's own. On this view the council either occupies that status or forfeits it; there is no version of the council that declines a picket-line request because the costs fall on other locals. Refusing on grounds of expense is not a prudent exercise of solidarity but its abandonment, and the legal exposure is simply the price of the thing the council is.

- The second advocates what he calls the mandate view. The council is an agent of forty principals, with authority defined by what they have actually delegated. Solidarity that spends other locals' members' jobs, contracts, and legal exposure without their consent is not solidarity but the disposition of stakes that are not the council's to dispose of. A federation that routinely exceeds its mandate will lose it, and it will lose it precisely when the stakes are highest.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting, roughly 900 to 1,200 words. It should:

1. Recommend one concrete course of action — endorse and call, decline, or endorse in some qualified form you specify precisely — with the actual steps, the sequence, and the dates attached, so that the board can vote on it rather than on a mood.
2. Identify the single consideration doing the decisive work in your recommendation, and state what would have to be true for the opposite recommendation to be correct.
3. Give the losing view its strongest case in its own vocabulary, and then explain why it nevertheless loses, without caricature and without appealing to a compromise that simply splits the two.
4. Address the procedural defect squarely: what weight, if any, the council should give to the fact that the strike was authorized without a membership ballot and that a third of the local is working. This cuts both ways, and the memo should show it — the worry that conditioning solidarity on internal procedural purity hands the council a permanent excuse for refusal, and the countervailing worry that endorsing an unballoted strike asks four thousand uninvolved workers to bear costs for a decision they had no part in making. Say how the council can hold both concerns without letting either dissolve.
5. Propose a standing rule, or a short set of criteria, that the council could apply to the next request for secondary action — written in terms a board could actually vote on — and show how that rule would have decided this case.

Two constraints. I do not want a survey of labor law or a history of secondary boycott doctrine; treat the legal exposure exactly as the general counsel has characterized it above and reason from there. And I do not want the memo to resolve the tension between the status and mandate views by declaring it a false dichotomy. Take a side, and pay for it.
