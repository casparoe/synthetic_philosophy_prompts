Last winter my brother was on second shift in a distribution warehouse. He noticed that one of the forklift straps had frayed, mentioned it to no one, and nine days later the strap gave way and a load came down across another man's leg. When the investigation wrapped up, my brother said the only thing he felt he could honestly say, which was that he hadn't done anything. Nearly everyone in my family answered him with the same line — not choosing is still a choice — and treated that as settling whether he caused the injury. I've been turning the line over for months, because it feels both obviously right and clearly not enough, and I suspect it is several different claims wearing one coat.

Could you take it apart for me? I'd like a menu of the precise propositions that sentence could be expressing, running from the ones that are true but empty through to the ones that would be a substantive claim about how the world is put together. For each, say what a person is committed to by accepting it, whether it is plausible on its own terms, and — this is what I care about most — whether it is strong enough to carry the weight my family put on it.

One thing keeps nagging at me that I hope you'll address directly: when my brother did nothing, there doesn't seem to have been anything there. No movement, no event, nothing that happened. So if the slogan is being used to say something caused a broken leg, I can't see what the supposed cause is made of, and I'd like to know whether that worry is serious or just a trick of grammar. I also can't tell whether "he caused it" and "he is to blame for it" are the same question, because the sentence seems to slide between the two without anyone noticing.

Some details of the case seem to matter and I don't know in which direction. Three other employees had also seen the frayed strap and also said nothing. A safety inspection was booked for that month regardless of whether anyone reported anything. My brother's supervisor had told the shift not to stop the line for anything short of an emergency. And the injured man had already decided to use that forklift for that load that shift. My sense is that some of these facts make things worse for my brother and some make them better, and that the slogan gives me no way of telling which is which — so please say, for each reading on your menu, whether these details change its verdict.

Lastly, tell me which reading the people who repeat this line usually mean, whether it's the reading that was doing the work in the argument I heard, and if not, what the argument would have needed instead.
