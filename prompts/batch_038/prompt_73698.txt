You are a specialist in the philosophy of mind whose work focuses on the temporal structure of consciousness. Please think carefully, and before rendering any verdict, spend a short opening section reconstructing what is genuinely at stake and listing the possible resolutions of the dispute — including resolutions neither participant considered.

For context, so that we are working from a shared description: in the color phi illusion, reported by Kolers and von Grünau, two stationary dots of different colors are flashed a short time apart, and observers report a single dot moving between the two locations whose color changes abruptly somewhere near the middle of its apparent path. Since the second color cannot yet have been registered when the change is apparently witnessed, Dennett distinguished two families of explanation. A Stalinesque explanation holds that the brain stalls, waits for the second flash, and then produces a single experience of motion-with-color-change, so that what is later reported is what was actually experienced. An Orwellian explanation holds that the observer really does experience the first color traveling the whole path, and that the arrival of the second flash then rewrites the record, so that memory and report are edited after the fact. The two explanations predict the same reports, and Dennett draws the conclusion that there is no principled way to choose between them, and hence no determinate point at which the experience happens.

At a reading group, two members argued about that conclusion. (1) Nadia said it is exactly right: the two stories agree on every input, every output, every behavior, and even on the underlying neural sequence, differing only in whether a given location in that sequence is called pre-experiential or post-experiential, and a difference in labeling is not a difference about the world. (2) Owen replied that Nadia has slid from an epistemic premise to a metaphysical conclusion: that we could never tell which happened does not show that nothing happened either way, unless having an experience just is being disposed to report it — which is precisely what is in question. (3) Nadia said that assumption is not smuggled in but is the only account of what an experience could be; without it, Owen's fact is a difference that makes no difference to anything, anywhere, ever. (4) Owen answered that reports cannot settle the order for a reason deeper than ordinary underdetermination: on the Orwellian story the report is caused by the edited record rather than by the experience it purports to describe, so anyone who appeals to what subjects say in order to determine what they experienced has already assumed that no such editing took place. (5) Nadia said this makes Owen's position self-sealing — he has constructed an account of experience that is immune in principle to every kind of evidence, which is a defect rather than a virtue. (6) Owen's final move was that Nadia's operationalism, applied consistently, would dissolve not only the question of when the experience occurred but the question whether the subject had any experience at all, and that if she is willing to accept that she should say so plainly instead of presenting it as a discovery about color phi.
