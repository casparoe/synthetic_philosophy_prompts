Retired instrumentation engineer here — spent thirty-odd years designing control loops for chemical plants, and only started reading political philosophy properly since I stopped working. I keep comming back to Libya in 2011 and I want help sorting out what went wrong in a framework I can actually trust.

Here's my frame. In my old job we distinguished open-loop from closed-loop control. Open-loop: you compute an input, apply it, walk away — no sensor reporting what the process is really doing, so you can't correct, and every modelling error just compounds. Closed-loop: you measure the output continuously and feed it back, so the controller adapts, and if your model of the plant was wrong you find out early and cheaply. What killed you in practice was almost never a bad model; it was a missing or slow feedback path.

Now map that onto the intervention. My understanding of the facts: Security Council Resolution 1973 in March 2011 authorised force to protect civilians and explicitly ruled out any occupying force; the African Union had a mediation roadmap running in parallel that got overtaken; the operation slid from shielding Benghazi to enabling the rebel advance to Gaddafi's death at Sirte in October; and afterwards Libya fragmented, weapons spilled into the Sahel, and Russia and China — who had abstained rather than vetoed — used the whole episode to block Syria resolutions for years.

So, going one direction: does R2P, or just war thinking generally, have a closed-loop requirement hidden inside it? The jus ad bellum criterion of a reasonable prospect of success looks like a single prediction made up front, before you have any data, which in my book is an open-loop bet dressed up as a calculation. Is last resort a feedback statement or a one-shot one? And who is supposed to be the sensor — the AU roadmap, a UN panel, Libyans themselves — and at what point in 2011 was it disconnected?

Then run it back the other way: what does Libya reveal about the theory that the theory doesn't already say? Where is R2P simply silent — I'd guess the day after, and any ongoing duty to keep correcting — and please don't force it to speak there. Also tell me where my open/closed-loop analogy is doing sneaky work or just breaks: a plant has no interests, a population does, and I know that's not a small difference.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which consideration, if I raised it, should make you reverse or seriously qualify the judgement you've just given, and whereabouts is your reasoning most likely to be wrong?
