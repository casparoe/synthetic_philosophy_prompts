I sit on the editorial standards board of a mid-size science magazine — three of us, plus the editor-in-chief who doesn't vote. We're meeting Wednesday morning and I've been asked to circulate something beforehand, because we've hit a case that our disclosure policy basically doesn't cover. Here's the situation. One of our staff reporters used an AI tool to work through about forty technical papers for a feature on groundwater measurement. The tool pulled out the methods and the headline numbers, flagged where papers disagreed, and produced a summary doc the reporter worked from. She didn't read maybe a dozen of those papers end to end. She did go back to the originals for every direct claim that ended up in the copy, she says, and she checked the numbers. But there's one sentence in the draft — a finding attributed to a specific study — that our fact-checker couldn't locate in the study at all. When we asked, the reporter said the tool had reported it and it was consistent with two other papers she had read, so she let it stand on the strength of the pattern rather than the primary source. Nobody on the piece thinks she was careless; she's one of our best, and honestly I believe her account of her own process.

The policy we wrote two years ago says, in so many words, that anything a machine wrote has to be labelled, and that a byline means a human is accountable for the accuracy of the copy. Both halves are useless here. Nothing in the piece was written by a machine, and the reporter is perfectly willing to be accountable — she just can't tell us what she'd have caught, or what she'd have missed, if she'd done it the old way. Meanwhile the reporter's union rep has pointed out that a lot of freelancers read papers using exactly the same shortcut, i.e. skim the abstract, google the key figure, move on, and we've never policed that. Our board is split. One member wants the piece held until she reads all forty papers, on the principle that we can't publish what we can't trace. The other wants it to run with a one-line disclosure and a policy review in the fall. Both of them have asked me for the write-up.

So: can you draft the memo I'd circulate Tuesday night? It needs a clear bottom line — publish as is, publish with a specific disclosure, hold for a specific fix, or kill it — plus a concrete time frame that works against a Monday publication date. And it needs to actually engage the two strongest arguments on the other side of wherever you land, not strawman them: first, that refusing these tools just makes us slower and less thorough than outlets that use them, so the "pure" choice costs readers coverage they'd otherwise get; second, that the whole magazine already runs on readers trusting people they can't check — the copy editor, the fact-checker, the stringer in another country — so singling out this one link in the chain as the place where understanding dies looks arbitrary. That last one is the version I actually want answered, because if there's a real difference between outsourcing your checking and outsourcing your reading, the memo has to say what it is and where the line sits, in terms a newsroom could apply to the next case without convening a philosophy seminar. Roughly 900 to 1200 words, plain language, no jargon dumps, and put the recommendation up top in its own section so people can skip to it. I'd rather you reason it through than send me a list of what other publications have done.
