I am in the third week of a senior seminar on indexical and self-locating belief, and the six of us in the reading group are supposed to arrive at next week's session having taken a position on the Sleeping Beauty problem. The difficulty is that three of us say one-third and two of us say one-half, and when we actually argue it turns out that we are offering different reasons for the same number, and would offer the same number for quite different reasons. Our seminar leader has suggested that we sort ourselves before we debate. Could you build us the instrument for doing that?

What I have in mind is a decision tree — roughly nine branch questions and seven or eight leaves — that takes someone from "I have been told the setup of the original problem" to a named position. The branch questions must not be about the answer to the original problem. None of them may ask which camp a person belongs to, use the labels halfer or thirder, or be answerable only by someone who has already read the literature on this. Every node should be either a short concrete case on which the positions genuinely come apart, or a precise claim that a person can accept or reject on reflection.

Three of the nodes should be cases I have constructed for this purpose, and for each you should tell us what every position your tree terminates in is committed to saying:

**Rehearsal.** Beauty is put to sleep Sunday and a fair coin is flipped. If heads, she is awakened Monday, interviewed, and never awakened again. If tails, she is awakened Monday and interviewed, and then awakened Tuesday — but on Tuesday she is given back her full memory of Monday's interview and is explicitly told that this is her second awakening. On Monday she remembers nothing after Sunday. What credence in heads should she have on Monday here, and is it the same as or different from her Monday credence in the original problem? If a group member's answer differs between the two cases, what does the difference turn on?

**The Broker.** On Sunday, before the coin is flipped, Beauty must fix a single number: the price per ticket she will pay for a ticket that pays one dollar if the coin landed heads. She knows that on every awakening she will be offered as many such tickets as she wants at exactly the price she fixed, that every purchase is honored and settled on Wednesday, and that she will not remember her earlier purchases. Is the price she should fix on Sunday the same as the price she should be willing to pay when she is awake on Monday? If not, which of the two numbers is her credence, and what makes it the credence rather than the other?

**The Duplicated Form.** Ada and Bea share an office; neither is ever drugged, and neither is ever confused about who she is or what day it is. A fair coin is flipped Sunday. If heads, one copy of a form is placed in the shared inbox; if tails, two indistinguishable copies. Both clerks know the rule. On Monday Ada takes a form from the inbox and files it. Bea watches from the next desk and knows that Ada is filing a form but has not touched the inbox herself. What credence in heads should Ada have? What credence should Bea have? Can those two answers rationally differ, and if they can, what does that say about whose perspective the original problem is even asking from?

Beyond those three, the tree should probe at least the following, each in whichever form — case or claim — you judge more diagnostic: whether a proposition containing an implicit "I" or "now" is the kind of thing that can bear a credence at all; whether Beauty's total evidence on Monday is exhausted by propositions statable without indexicals; whether a rational agent can shift her credence without having learned anything she did not already know she would learn; whether a number deserves the name of credence if it fails to match the price the agent would pay for a bet that recurs at every awakening; and whether the puzzle simply evaporates once one insists that credence is only defined relative to a specified decision problem.

The leaves must include: the halfer position associated with Lewis; the double-halfer position, on which the credence remains one-half both before and after the awakening; the thirder route that runs through self-locating evidence; the distinct thirder route that runs through a general principle about counting observers or observer-moments; the view that no unique rational credence is determined, whether via imprecise credences or permissivism; the deflationist view that the question is ill-posed; and at least one hybrid that answers different variants differently, with the hybrid's governing rule stated explicitly. If a branch-counting view tied to Everettian quantum mechanics genuinely earns a leaf rather than a parenthetical remark, give it one; if it does not, say why.

Then please do four further things. First, lay out the logical space before you locate anyone in it: give the questions, and a table of the answer combinations they permit. Second, for every combination no named position occupies, say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is consistent but unattractive, or because no one has simply thought to defend it. Third, flag every pair of leaves that nine questions cannot in fact separate, and name the further case that would separate them — do not paper over these. Fourth, close with a one-page scoring sheet we can print, so that someone who answers the nodes in prose can still be placed.

Please attach no citations, no bibliography, and no suggested readings, and do not attribute a view to a particular author beyond labels that are already standard currency in the debate. Two of us will be working through this on a train with no signal, so it has to stand entirely on its own.
