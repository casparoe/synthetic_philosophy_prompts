Here's the problem. I sit on a five-person research-integrity panel at my university. Retraction requires all three of: (i) the data were fabricated, (ii) the corresponding author knew or should have known, (iii) it can't be fixed by a correction. Last month we had a case where the votes came out 3–2 on (i), 3–2 on (ii), and 4–1 on (iii) — and then when the chair put the retraction question itself, it went 0–5 against. Everyone on the panel is individually consistant, and everyone accepts the three-part rule. A colleague pointed at this and said the panel had just "found" that the paper ought to be retracted while not one of us thought so, and it kind of wrecked me, because I've spent years arguing that we should always vote premise-by-premise. So I wrote out why I now think the panel shouldn't exist:

1. Whatever a committee judges is fixed entirely by what its members judge plus the voting rule. There's nothing over and above that.
2. For any voting rule you like, there's some possible pattern of member judgments on which the rule hands the committee a conclusion that not a single member accepts — that's literally what happened to us, and I'm told there's a theorem saying you can't design your way out of it in general.
3. When that happens, the committee's conclusion isn't explained by anyone's accepting it. It falls out of the arithmetic.
4. A conclusion nobody accepts isnt a judgment. It's a bookkeeping artifact.
5. So in cases like ours the committee doesn't really judge the conclusion; it only looks like it does.
6. And by (2) that can happen for any rule, on any issue with premise-structure.
7. So committee judgments on structured issues are a useful fiction.
8. If they're a fiction, our findings have no authority — we're generating noise on letterhead.

I'd really like to keep (1), because I'm no fan of spooky group minds, and I'd really like to deny (8). Which step do I have to give up, and what exactly do I lose by giving it up?
