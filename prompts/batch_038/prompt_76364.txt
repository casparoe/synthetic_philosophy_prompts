I'm teaching Bolt's A Man for All Seasons in a philosophy of language seminar alongside Austin, and I want to frame it as a play about performatives rather than about conscience, which is how it always gets taught. The obvious hook is the oath: More won't say the words, and Cromwell's whole game is to get him to speak — the state treats his not-saying as if it had content, and can't convict him until there's an overt act. So can Austin actually adjudicate that? Is Henry's declaration of himself Supreme Head of the Church a misfire (no such procedure exists, wrong person, coerced uptake) or an abuse (procedure intact, insincere)? The bit I most want pushed on: Austin's felicity conditions all presupose a convention that's already in place, but Henry is trying to create the convention in the very act of invoking it, with Parliament and the bishops ratifying after the fact. Does the play see that bootstrapping problem, or does it just assume the king's words fail because God isn't behind them? And second — More seems to hold that whether an oath binds turns on the swearer's private intention, which looks like exactly the casuistry he'd condemn in anyone else. Does Bolt notice? Does the Common Man's ending, keep alive and don't make trouble, amount to the play's verdict that what matters about an utterance is its effects rather than its sincerity conditions? Tell me where the reading breaks down; I don't want a piece that just uses the play to illustrate How to Do Things with Words.
