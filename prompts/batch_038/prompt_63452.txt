I don't want to describe my own read of it because I'd rather get yours cold. Here's the question as it was set:

QUESTION 2. Nora has read a great deal of literary criticism and has, we stipulate, an excellent long-run record of being right about novels. You have never read the novel in question. Nora tells you it is beautifully paced but emotionally cold, and on that basis you come to believe it is beautifully paced but emotionally cold. Take her reliability as given and not up for grabs. (a) Is your belief justified? (b) Could you come to know that the novel is beautifully paced? (c) Suppose the answers to (a) and (b) are both yes — does it follow that you are in a position to appreciate the novel's pacing? If not, say precisely what is missing and why it isn't already supplied by a justified true belief. In the course of your answer, explain the acquaintance principle and say whether it is best read as a claim about (i) justification, (ii) knowledge, or (iii) appreciation, and defend your reading against at least one rival. Then say whether Hume's account of the true judges supports or undermines the principle, keeping in mind that the answer to that last part cannot just be a report of how much confidence you'd rationally place in a good critic.

And here's the answer, roughly 40 minutes' worth by the look of it:

The acquaintance principle is the claim, going back through Sibley and Wollheim to Kant, that aesthetic judgements have to be grounded in first-hand experience of the object. You cannot judge a novel beautiful on the strength of someone else's description of it; you have to read it. Kant gives us the deepest version of the reason. For Kant a judgement of taste is subjective: its determining ground is a feeling of pleasure, not any concept or rule under which the object could be subsumed. Because there is no concept involved, there is nothing for a report to transmit to me. Nora's testimony tells me that she was pleased in a certain way, which is a fact about her, not a reason for me. So Kant is effectively a non-cognitivist about aesthetic judgements, and on non-cognitivism testimony is obviously useless, since there is no truth there for it to convey. This is why the principle is not really about reliability at all.

But in Nora's case the principle proves too much. Nora is reliable by stipulation, so my belief is formed by a reliable process and is therefore justified, and if it is justified and true then I know it. Any other answer makes criticism, art history, and the teaching of literature unintelligible, since almost everything a student walks into a first seminar believing about a work they have not read came to them from someone else. So (a) and (b) are both yes, and the principle has to be watered down or restricted to something weaker.

On (c), what is missing is acquaintance itself. Appreciation requires having the experience, so I can know that the pacing is beautiful without being able to appreciate it until I have read the novel. Hopkins's account belongs here. On his view the trouble with aesthetic testimony is not that the resulting belief is unjustified, but that there is a norm of belief formation that forbids forming it that way. So the belief I get from Nora is unjustified after all, in the sense that actually matters, even though it is true: the violation of the norm is what reveals the underlying epistemic defect. That is why the principle is best read as a claim about (ii) knowledge rather than (i) justification. Testimony can get you a justified true belief about an aesthetic property, but not knowledge, because knowledge of such properties requires the right kind of causal contact with them.

Hume looks like a counterexample, because the standard of taste is set by the joint verdict of true judges, and that has the shape of a licence to defer. But this gets Hume backwards. His true judges are marked out by delicacy, practice, comparison and freedom from prejudice, and every one of those is acquired only through first-hand exposure to a lot of works. So the standard of taste does not undermine the acquaintance principle; it explains where the principle's authority comes from. That is also the answer to the last part of the question: trust in critics is a second-best substitute for perception, so the question was never about how much of it you should have. Finally, Goodman's split between autographic and allographic arts gives Nora no help. A novel is allographic, but the notation fixes the identity of the work, not its aesthetic properties, so the script does not carry the pacing across to anyone who has not read it.
