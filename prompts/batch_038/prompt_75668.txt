I run a volunteer mutual-aid fund in a mid-size city. About 40 requests a week come in — rent, utilities, a car repair, a pharmacy bill — grants from $150 to $1,200. Two volunteers look at each request over a shared spreadsheet and are supposed to land it in about ten minutes. Nobody has any training, I have final say, and I don't want to be the tiebreak on 40 things a week.

For years we've been running one rule that nobody ever wrote down: if the trouble came from something you chose, we pass; if it just landed on you, we help. I eventually found out this has a name — brute luck vs option luck or something like that — but knowing the name doesn't help me at the spreadsheet. And the rule is producing results I can't defend to my own volunteers. The woman who picked up night shifts driving for a delivery app and got T-boned on the way home: was driving "her choice"? The guy whose furnace died in January with a kid under two is an easy yes, except he also ignored three years of repair quotes. Half our donors want every dollar to prevent as much misery as possible, which pushes us toward ranking people; the other half think that's a betrayal of the whole point, which as I understand it is that nobody should end up worse off because of luck they didn't choose. One volunteer quit last month saying we've become judges of other people's lives.

So: write me a decision procedure my volunteers can actually run. Ten minutes per request, no philosophy background, no documents (we don't ask for proof, we don't want to be cops), self-reported info only, and scarcity built in — we can't fund everyone, so at some point the question stops being yes/no and becomes who-goes-first, and the procedure should say that out loud instead of hiding it.

Give me the procedure itself as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "procedure_name": string
- "steps": array of objects, each with
  - "step": int, in the order the volunteer hits them
  - "ask": the literal question the volunteer reads out or types into the sheet
  - "why_this_step_exists": one sentence, what distinction it's protecting
  - "sufficient_answer": what counts as having answered it well enough to move on
  - "outputs": which of "approve" / "decline" / "move_to_ranking" / "escalate" this step can produce, and what triggers each
  - "who_decides": volunteer alone / pair / me
  - "time_budget": seconds or minutes
  - "failure_mode": how this step goes wrong in practice and who it goes wrong against
- "escalations": array of {"trigger": …, "goes_to": …, "why_no_procedure_can_settle_it": …}
- "distinctions_kept": array of {"distinction": …, "plain_words_version": …, "what_the_simplification_loses": …}
- "distinctions_dropped": array of strings — the ones ten minutes can't carry, and why it's safe-ish to drop them
- "what_this_cannot_do": array of strings

Then, in prose under the JSON:

1. Run it on three requests. (a) a man asking for $900 toward a debt he says came from slot machines, and the debt is why he's behind on rent; (b) a woman who left a stable job two years ago to try starting a business, it folded, now she's asking $1,100 for her son's breathing equipment; (c) someone asking $600 to get out of an apartment with mold that keeps sending their kid to the ER. At least one of these has to come out a no — say it plainly, don't soften it into a "maybe with more information," even if I'm going to hate it, and tell me which step of the procedure did the killing.
2. Two requests the procedure will get wrong on purpose, and what it costs us.
3. What I actually say to the volunteer who quit. Is she right that the whole framing punishes people for how their lives started? If the procedure has an answer to that, show me which step carries the weight; if it doesn't, say so.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and please don't invent any either — I'm not going to read them and I'd rather you just think.
