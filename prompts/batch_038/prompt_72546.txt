I am in the middle of revising my third novel, and I find that I need a sharper vocabulary than the one my trade supplies. The chapter in question is written in the second person throughout: a narrator addresses his younger brother as "you," recounting an afternoon the two of them spent at a reservoir. On the final page the reader learns that the brother died two years before the afternoon being recounted — so the narration, which has kept the two of them side by side at the water from the first line, has been addressed to someone who could not have been there and could not have heard it. My editor's letter disposes of the chapter in a single sentence, saying that it demands too great a suspension of disbelief and should be recast in the third person, while two pages earlier in the same letter she reports that she read it twice and wept, and calls it the emotional heart of the book. I want to answer her precisely rather than defensively, and to do that I first need to know what her phrase is actually asserting.

I have heard that phrase in craft talk for twenty years, and it plainly does not mean the same thing in every mouth. A friend who writes commercial thrillers deploys it as a rebuke to fussy readers, on the principle that credulity is the reader's obligation and not the writer's. My editor deploys it as the diagnosis of a technical fault in a chapter she otherwise admires. And it is generally credited to Coleridge, whose formulation — "that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith" — sounds like something else again: a temporary and voluntary withholding that terminates in a kind of faith rather than in tolerance.

Could you set out, as a numbered menu I can work from, the distinct precise claims this one phrase might be making? For each, I would like to know what is supposed to be suspended — assent to some proposition about the actual world, a judgment of improbability within the story's own terms, an objection to a convention of the medium, a resistance to imagining something one finds repellent or unthinkable — whose act the suspending is, whether it is voluntary or automatic, what it is suspended in favour of, and how long it lasts. Then give me a verdict on each reading: trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false. For those that survive, say whether the reading would actually license my editor's proposed fix, or whether it would instead locate the fault somewhere other than where she has put it.

Finally, I would like your judgment on the apparent contradiction in her letter. Is it coherent to weep over a chapter while simultaneously disbelieving it in whatever sense the phrase requires, and does her grief count as evidence for or against her objection? If the two remarks are compatible, tell me which reading of the slogan makes them compatible; if some readings would force her to retract one of them, say which and why.
