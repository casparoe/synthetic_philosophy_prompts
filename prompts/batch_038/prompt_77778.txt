I'm giving the opening remarks at a memorial for a type designer — a punchcutter really, though she'd have hated that word being applied to a women who cut metal, she was a metal person before she was a computer person. It's at a print museum, four or five minutes, and the room is going to be about a third working type designers and lettering historians, a third her family (her son, her granddaughter who is nine, a lot of cousins who think she made fonts the way other people make jam), and a third museum staff and press.

Here's the thing the room is quietly split about, and I need the speech to settle it instead of floating above it. Her famous face — the one on half the book jackets in that room — has a lowercase 'a' with no bowl. It's a stem with a little flag coming off the top right, that's all. It reads perfectly well in context, it reads fine at nine point in a novel, but on a specimen sheet by itself people lose their minds. A colleague of hers wrote something years ago saying that 'a' had left the alphabet, and that phrase has been repeated at every dinner since. Her granddaughter, who is nine, asked me last week, completely straight: but is it an a? I want the speech to answer her. And I want the same answer to be one the type designers in the room can't dismiss — which means it has to actually take a position on what makes every 'a' an 'a', across uncial and double-story and single-story and hers, without falling back on "we know one when we see it" and without naming a single philosopher. Nobody in that room is a philosopher and I'm not going to be the guy who brings Wittgenstein to a memorial.

Useful specifics you can build on: her first face, from the late seventies, had a completely ordinary double-story 'a', and she was prouder of the later one. She used to say the test was never "does this look like an a" but "does this hold up next to my own n and my own o" — she'd set a proof line of nothing but a's and g's and e's to check whether a new drawing was cheating, and she'd throw drawings out over it. She could read her own flag-'a' faster than a conventional one, and she never once claimed that proved anything.

Constraints: no type puns of any kind, no "she had great kerning with people," I am begging you. No list of her virtues. No "she taught us that." It should be warm, and funny in maybe two places, but the jokes can't be about letterforms. It has to end on something a nine-year-old can keep, not on an aphorism. And it's fine if the speech admits it doesnt fully solve the problem — but only after it's committed to an answer. I don't want a shrug dressed up as humility.
