Consider an agent that cannot rule out that the number of observers relevantly like it exceeds, by many orders of magnitude, the number it has any evidence about; that may be one of several copies produced by a process it cannot inspect; and that must nevertheless commit now to decisions it cannot reverse. I want a theory of self-locating belief adequate for that agent, and before I go looking for one I want its specification — the requirements, not the candidates.

Write the desiderata. For each, give: a statement precise enough that a proposed theory could be checked against it; the case or consideration that motivates it; a marking of whether you treat it as non-negotiable for the purpose above, as negotiable, or as a preference belonging to one camp in the debate rather than a demand on any theory whatsoever; and the cheapest counterexample that would show a candidate failing it. Constructed cases are welcome, and no single stock example should be carrying all the weight.

Then, separately:

1. The distinctions a theory must not collapse. I have in mind such pairs as observers versus observer-moments; there being many agents relevantly like me versus there being many of me; a reference class understood as a natural kind versus a reference class understood as an indexical choice; learning that one exists versus learning what one is; the credence one should have before duplication versus after. Say why each distinction is load-bearing, and flag any you think is spurious.

2. The results and arguments the theory must be consistent with, or must explain away: the doomsday-style shift in credence that naive self-sampling produces from a birth rank alone; the fact that self-indication licenses confidence in extravagantly populous universes on no ordinary evidence, which is what the presumptuous philosopher objection turns on; the demand that everything reduce to vanilla conditionalization when the number of observers is simply not in question; situations where the agent knows its own evidence was produced by a sampling procedure biased toward some observer types; and fission cases where the agent's own successor count is itself uncertain.

3. Which subsets of your requirements are jointly unsatisfiable. If, for instance, invariance under how finely observers are individuated cannot be had together with the thesis that credences depend on observer counts and ordinary conditionalization as the sole updating rule, sketch why — an impossibility result here would be the most valuable part of your answer. If some requirement has to go, say which one and what it costs me to drop it.

4. Which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and which one is routinely assumed rather than argued for.

5. At least one requirement you think is missing from the standard lists.

Three cases of my own, which you should treat as tests and tell me if they are not genuine ones. First, self-induced uniqueness: an agent can, by taking a drug or sealing itself into isolation, become the only being in its epistemic situation. If credences depend on the size of the reference class, the agent appears able to move its credence in a cosmological hypothesis by a purely local act that carries no information about the world — is that a reductio, a real prediction, or a misdescription? Second, post-hoc merger: two copies are later merged into one who retains both streams of memory. Did the observer count change retroactively, and may the survivor's credences differ from the ones each copy should have had beforehand? Third, suspended observers: dormant uploads, dreamless sleepers, unfertilized but viable candidates. Must a theory say whether these count, or is the question itself confused?

Keep the scorecard short — a paragraph each on how self-sampling, self-indication, and the deflationary view that there is no non-trivial answer independent of reference-class choice fare against your list. No citations and no reading list; I want the specification.
