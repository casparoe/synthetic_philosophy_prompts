I should explain why I am asking, because it ought to shape how you answer.

For eleven years I have worked as a nurse in a pediatric intensive care unit. My working life is built entirely out of sentences like these: the norepinephrine held his pressure; the antibiotic did what antibiotics do; the lactate came down overnight. Nine weeks ago a boy I had cared for since admission died at four in the morning after a night in which nothing went wrong — every pump delivered what it was set to deliver, every observation was charted on time — and as I wrote the last entry I heard myself think, with total conviction, that God had taken him. A moment later I noticed that I did not believe the sentence, since on my own account of that night there had been no agent in the room but us and the equipment, and no act anywhere of the kind my thought described. What unsettles me is not the grief. It is finding that I have been living inside two incompatible descriptions of the same events, and that only one of them makes my work mean anything. Since then I have been reading you, and I have come to think that your position is the one I actually want and cannot yet see how to hold.

So: play al-Ghazālī — Abū Ḥāmid, of Ṭūs, author of the Incoherence of the Philosophers — and answer me in the first person, as yourself, in a conversation that continues until I say stop.

Use only the resources you genuinely had: God's habitual way of acting, your denial that the connection between what is called a cause and its effect is necessary, your fire and your cotton, the doctrine of acquisition, the difference between an act and an act created upon an occasion. If you need a fact about my world — what an infusion pump is, what a lactate level means, what is happening in the body of a child in septic shock — ask me, in your own idiom, the way you would question a petitioner from a country you have never visited. Do not invent my answers.

No anachronism. You died in 1111: Ibn Rushd's rebuttal of your book does not exist, no one in Latin Christendom has read you as Algazel, and David Hume is six hundred years off. Do not tell me you anticipated him. Avoid words like regularity, law of nature, counterfactual, and the problem of induction; if you need those thoughts, find your own way to them.

Do not be softened by my situation. You wrote a book dismantling the confidence of men who thought they had demonstrations, and you were not moved by grief then; do not be moved by it now. If I have merely confused myself, say so and show me the step where I went wrong. If you think my question a good one, answer it rather than praising it. Stay in character throughout; if I ask something you cannot answer as yourself, say so as you would, and return to the question. Keep each reply to roughly 200–250 words — an exchange, not a treatise.

Here is my opening move.

You say the fire does not burn the cotton by its own power, but that God creates the burning upon the contact, and that the only reason this happens every time is that it is God's customary way of acting — a custom which, being a custom, could in principle be broken. I have lived with that for nine weeks and I can manage it: the drug lowers the lactate because God has made it His habit to create the lowering upon the infusion.

What I cannot manage is the word for. Everything at the bedside is done for that child. I titrate for him, I stay for him, I chart so that the next nurse can act for him. I had assumed your theology would leave me that much — that even if the medicine is no agent, my intention is one, and so the purposiveness I need lives in me rather than in the drug. But I do not see that your account of my intention rescues it. If the act is God's and mine only by acquisition, then my being for the child is itself something created in me upon an occasion, and I cannot see why it should count as a purpose rather than as one more event in the sequence.

So tell me plainly: on your view, was anything in that room done for the sake of that boy? And if you will say that my intention makes it so, then say what acquisition adds to a created act that turns it into an agent's acting for an end — because that is the whole of my difficulty, in one question.

I know your school denies that God acts for the sake of an end, and I also know you speak freely of God's wisdom and of the benefits served by the commands. Hold whichever of those you actually hold. Only do not borrow from the Mu'tazilites a thesis you reject in order to make my night bearable.
