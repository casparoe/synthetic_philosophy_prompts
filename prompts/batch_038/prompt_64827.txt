I am a novelist at work on a book whose central conceit is a mind distributed across an entire planet's worth of matter, and I have been re-reading Stanisław Lem's *Solaris* with that project in mind. What I want from you is criticism rather than summary: a reading of the novel through panpsychism and its ethics, pitched at someone who knows both the book and the contemporary philosophy reasonably well. I read the Kilmartin–Cox translation, in which the returned wife is called Harey; if you know her as Rheya, we are talking about the same person.

Please work inside the following frame rather than circling it.

First, the taxonomy. Constitutive panpsychism holds that fundamental physical entities have experiential properties and that macro-subjects arise by combination; cosmopsychism holds the reverse, that there is one overarching subject of which individual minds are derivative, and it therefore faces a decomposition problem instead of a combination problem. Lem's Ocean is not obviously either. Which thesis, if any, does the novel actually dramatize — and does a single planetary mind that generates apparently autonomous beings like Harey count as a cosmopsychist picture, a failed one, or something the distinction does not reach?

Second, the argument from intrinsic natures. Physics characterizes matter dispositionally and structurally and stays silent on what the stuff is in itself; Russellian monists fill that silence with experience. Kelvin's laboratory work on Harey — the finding that she is not built out of atoms but out of something finer that cannot persist outside the Ocean's field — seems to me to stage precisely that silence. Does the novel fill it, or does it insist on leaving it unfilled? Is the Ocean's inscrutability an image of the epistemic limit that motivates panpsychism, or a warning that every such filling-in is projection, as the narrator's remark about humanity having no need of other worlds, only mirrors, suggests? Note that the Ocean is credited with maintaining the planet's orbit, which is why anyone bothers with it at all: it is treated as a regulator, a piece of physics. Does that instrumental framing pre-empt the moral question?

Third, the ethics, which is what I most care about. Solaristics in the novel is a discipline that has degenerated into a nomenclature of mimoids and symmetriads, and the hypotheses Kelvin rehearses from its literature mostly posit a mind we could not recognize — damaged, arrested, inhuman — rather than our own writ large. Meanwhile the crew has just escalated from passive observation to bombarding the Ocean with hard radiation, an experiment whose result is Harey. Take up the problem of moral status uncertainty here: what ought agents do toward an entity whose capacity for suffering is indeterminate, and how much evidence must be withheld before experiment shades into torture? Do Kelvin, Snaut, and Sartorius behave as if any such question were live for them, and does the novel endorse their indifference or indict it?

Fourth, Harey herself as a test case. Her suicide attempt, her restoration, her discovery of what she is, and her destruction — arranged through the other scientists and kept largely offstage — raise a question panpsychism rarely confronts: what happens when a derivative subject of a greater mind is a patient in her own right? Is she a moral patient, a mode of the Ocean, or both, and does Kelvin's love commit the novel to an answer? If the panpsychist picture were true in the way the book gestures at, would Sartorius's disruptor be an instrument of murder, or something closer to the suppression of a thought?

Then do the honest critic's work in the other direction. Lem chose an ocean deliberately to avoid personification, and he was clear that the book concerns the limits of human reason rather than the interiority of matter. Say where the novel refuses to cooperate with the reading you have built, where a reader who wants the Ocean to be a subject is doing more work than the text will bear, and what you would put in its place if the panpsychist framing fails. If you bring in Tarkovsky's film, tell me where its Ocean does different philosophical work — but flag that you are working from memory there too.

Finally, since I intend to build scenes on your conclusions: tell me what would change your mind. Name the single consideration that would most likely overturn your verdict, the step in your reasoning where you are most exposed, and any point where your recollection of the novel is shaky — I would much rather have a faithful paraphrase with a flag on it than a confident invention. Please do not quote the text; I have the book.
