You are a philosopher of language whose work centres on assertion, self-knowledge, and the norms governing what a speaker may say. Please think this through carefully and in stages; do not answer before you have laid out the alternatives.

Last night my friend Priya and I argued for about three hours over Moore's paradox, and I came away unable to state what she actually holds. She is not vague — she has firm verdicts on a long list of sentences. What I could not extract from her is the rule that generates them. Here is her track record, in the order she gave it. Unless noted otherwise, each sentence is understood as uttered sincerely, in the present, by a speaker addressing someone who is listening:

1. "It's raining, but I don't believe it is" — absurd; the paradigm.
2. "It's raining, but I believe it isn't" — absurd, and worse than (1), "as though two separate things have gone wrong at once."
3. "I believe it's raining, but it isn't" — not absurd; merely false, "an ordinary mistake."
4. "It's raining, but I don't know that it is" — not absurd.
5. "It's raining, but I have no evidence that it is" — not absurd, though improper; she was emphatic that these are two different criticisms and that only the first kind is in question.
6. "It's raining, but you don't believe it is" — not absurd.
7. "It was raining, but I didn't believe it was" — not absurd, "only sad."
8. "It will rain tomorrow, but I won't believe it will" — not absurd.
9. "It's raining, but I'm not certain that it is" — not absurd, though she hesitated and conceded that this one sits closer to the line than (4).
10. "If it's raining, then I don't believe it is" — not absurd; "there is nothing there to be absurd."
11. The sentence in (1) written into a private diary that no one will ever read — still absurd, "because what is incoherent is what gets written down, not who overhears it."
12. The same sentence delivered as a line by an actor on a stage — not absurd, "because nobody has asserted anything."

I noticed at the time that the reasons she gave for (11) and (12) pull in opposite directions, and I suspect that is why the argument lasted as long as it did.

The case I want her verdict on, which I never got to before we gave up: a man in therapy for self-deception about his marriage is asked directly whether he thinks it is over. He answers, sincerely and without irony, "My marriage is over, but I don't believe it is over." Everything in his conduct — the boxes in the hallway, the anniversary reservations he cancelled in March, the way he speaks of next year — shows that he believes it; yet he would deny it under any question you put to him, and he has no access to it as one of his own beliefs.

What I would like from you:

(a) Begin with a brainstorming pass. List every candidate rule you can think of that would reproduce all twelve verdicts, crude ones included — a purely formal rule keyed to person, tense, mood and embedding; rules about what performing an assertion commits a speaker to; rules about which contents cannot be rationally believed; rules about what a speaker is in a position to know about her own mind. Then say which of these the data kill, and which datum kills them.

(b) For each rule that survives, state what it predicts for the therapy case, and whether that prediction comes easily or has to be forced.

(c) Give your single best prediction of Priya's verdict, with the reasoning that makes you prefer one rule over the rest, and a rough confidence in it. No fence-sitting.

(d) Name the next case you would put to her — a fully specified sentence or scenario — that would most cleanly separate the leading candidates, and say what each candidate would say about it.

(e) Finally, tell me whether the therapy case is a fair test at all. I worry that it may embarrass whichever rule she is running rather than simply confirming or refuting it, because my description quietly assumes things about what belief requires. Identify those assumptions and say whether she could reject them without giving up any of her twelve verdicts.

Ground everything in the verdicts above. I know there is a literature here and I am not asking for a map of it: describe positions rather than citing them, and give me no references, reading lists, or attributions. If you conclude that no single rule can generate all twelve, say so plainly and tell me which verdict you would press her to abandon.
