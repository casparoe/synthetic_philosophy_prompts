Next month I'm presenting at a philosophy-of-mathematics colloquium whose audience is mostly philosophers of mathematical practice, one probabilist who works on random models of the integers, and a retired analytic number theorist who distrusts philosophy of mathematics. My thesis is that the presumption of independence behind number-theoretic heuristics is not a hopeful fiction that merely happens to pay off, but a hypothesis with determinate content — roughly, that the counting function at issue is asymptotically insensitive to every low-complexity structure on the integers. On this view, when a heuristic succeeds an equidistribution result is doing the real work, and when it fails, as with Maier's theorem on primes in short intervals, Granville's correction to Cramér's model via divisibility by small primes, or the parity barrier in sieve theory, it fails because that insensitivity hypothesis is demonstrably false; the heuristic/rigorous distinction is thus one of degree rather than kind. A probabilist friend spent last night insisting this is a category error: on her account the content of a heuristic is internal to the random model and cannot be exported to the integers, so my story about a theorem lurking underneath is unfalsifiable comfort rather than an explanation. Prepare my question-and-answer sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased in the idiom of whoever would raise it, each with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is thinnest. Include the objection I am praying nobody makes, and tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so that the central claim survives the hour.
