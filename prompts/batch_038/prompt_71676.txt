I run a graduate reading group on the philosophy of mind, and this term we are working through the dispute over whether any intentionality is intrinsic. To sharpen the discussion I wrote up the following argument as a handout. Half the group now thinks it is sound, and its conclusion is not one I am willing to accept, so I would like a diagnosis rather than a summary of the debate.

(1) A state has original intentionality only if what the state is about is fixed by that state's own physical constitution, independently of anyone's interpreting it.

(2) For any physical state s of any system, there are indefinitely many candidate contents that s could be assigned. A single stored bit, for instance, is in one running program part of a number, in another a pixel of an image, in a third an element of an instruction; nothing in the bit itself favors one of these roles over the others. Dennett's point with this example is that the indeterminacy is general, and it applies to neural states just as much as to silicon ones.

(3) Therefore, for any s, s's own physical constitution does not select a content; the selection is made by an interpretive scheme that someone brings to s.

(4) So no state has original intentionality. Human neural states are included in that verdict.

(5) Searle's position is that intentionality is a causal, higher-level biological feature of brains, not something borrowed from interpreters. But by (3), whatever the brain's biology contributes, it does not by itself settle which content its states have, so the only remaining candidate for the settling is an attributor's scheme.

(6) Hence Searle's view is not a rival to Dennett's: it is the same interpretive strategy pointed at a favored class of objects. There is no intrinsic aboutness anywhere in nature.

(7) An interpreter is itself a physical system, so (2) through (4) apply to the interpreter's states as well. The scheme then settles nothing either, and on this argument nothing has ever been about anything, including this handout.

Three things I want from you. First, tell me which step you reject and why it looked acceptable at the time — and be explicit about whether you are treating the argument as invalid but with all true premises, as valid with a false premise, or as valid with a premise that is true but harmless once its scope is fixed. Second, assess whether (3) presents an exhaustive set of options. If it does not, describe what the alternative selector of content would have to look like, and what its advocates must say to keep it from being a relabeled interpretive scheme; if it does, say so and explain why the alternatives people reach for in this debate do not survive (2). Third, price the exits. For each of rejecting (1), rejecting (2), restricting (3) to states considered in isolation, and simply accepting (7), say which position in the literature is left holding that bill and what it can no longer explain once it does.

I will tell you where my own sympathies lie, since you may want to know what I am trying to protect. I would most like to keep (2); it seems to me very hard to deny of any physical state, and I suspect that anyone who denies it owes an account I have never seen given. I would much rather give up (5) or (6). I do not think I am entitled to dismiss (7) as a rhetorical flourish, since if it really follows then I have proved something I consider obviously false, and I would like to know whether that fact locates the error or merely restates my reluctance.

One further request about the answer itself. When you have given the diagnosis, state how confident you are in it, and single out the point in your own reasoning where you are least sure you have got it right — the step whose rejection you are most tempted to reconsider. I would rather have that weak point marked explicitly than have a clean line of reasoning that hides where it is load-bearing.
