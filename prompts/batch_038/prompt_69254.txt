For roughly twelve years I have been a reader of, and occasional contributor to, the rationalist blogosphere — the sequences, the forums, the various successor newsletters — and the milieu has left me with habits I no longer try to break. I want the crux of a disagreement stated out loud rather than circled. I want to know whether a thinker is defending a position or dissolving the question. And I have very little patience for arguments that succeed only against an opponent who has already conceded. Next month I am guest-hosting a fifty-five-minute episode of a small podcast, and my guest is a philosopher-psychiatrist whose view I find productively unnerving.

His view, as I understand it: Hacking's looping effects — the observation that the kinds we apply to people change the people we apply them to — are almost always deployed defensively, as a reason for psychiatric taxonomy to be humble. He thinks that is a squandered observation. Once one grants that a diagnostic category is causally entangled with the self-understanding, help-seeking behavior, and strategic presentation of the people who receive it, the category stops looking like a botched attempt at carving nature at the joints and starts looking like an intervention with foreseeable consequences. He therefore proposes treating nosology as a mechanism-design problem: criteria should be written not to match a latent kind but to select a good equilibrium — good in terms of who gets treated, how the diagnosed come to understand themselves, and what happens when actors who want the label, or who desperately want to avoid it, optimize against the wording. He accepts a certain volume of false positives and strategic self-presentation as the price of a well-chosen loop, and he regards critics who insist on truth-tracking first as nostalgic for a species of objectivity psychiatry has never possessed.

Write me the question list for the episode. I want ten questions arranged in three blocks, with the arc made explicit: first what the view actually costs, then whether it can be evidentially constrained at all, then who is doing the designing and what happens when the design becomes public knowledge.

Specific things it must contain:

- No question asking him to explain looping effects, or Hacking, or what a diagnostic manual is. I will record a two-minute cold-open myself and he may assume it.
- In the evidentiary block, press the endogeneity problem: if categories shape the symptoms they are meant to measure, then the epidemiology that would justify moving a diagnostic boundary is partly produced by the boundary currently in force. I want a question about what, on his account, could count as evidence that a boundary is in the wrong place, and a follow-up confronting the suspicion that "whichever boundary yields the better equilibrium" is not an empirical claim at all but a polite way of declining to answer one.
- One question on the natural-kinds objection, constructed so that his obvious reply — that psychiatric kinds were never natural kinds — cannot close it. The follow-up should go after the asymmetry: some presentations travel across eras and cultures with recognizable structure, while others, like the fugue states Hacking studied in fin-de-siècle France, bloom and vanish within a couple of decades. Ask whether his framework can predict in advance which kinds will be stable and which will be transient, or only narrate the difference afterward.
- One question built on the history of the declassification of homosexuality and on the compromise categories that temporarily replaced it, which were openly negotiated settlements rather than discoveries. Press the ambiguity: is that episode a vindication of loop design, or is it the clearest possible demonstration that a profession cannot tell, at the time, whether its engineering is an improvement? Then ask him to classify a second case — the deletion of the bereavement exclusion for depressive disorders in DSM-5 — as a design error, a design choice whose costs were never priced, or a genuine revision, and to say which cost he thinks was accepted.
- One question on the identity of the designer. The we in "we should design the taxonomy" is in practice a workgroup with industry ties, an insurer that pays only for coded diagnoses, a disability or forensic system with its own adversaries. Ask whether his mechanism designer is any more immune to incentives than the strategic patient he is modeling, whether Goodhart's law simply recurses one level up, and what institutional feature he would point to that breaks the recursion.
- One question on the second-order loop created by his own candor. Telling people that their category was chosen for its effects rather than its truth is new information delivered into the loop. Ask whether transparency destroys the uptake that made the engineered loop work, and if so whether honesty is self-defeating on his own terms.
- At least one question that is genuinely fair to him, where a good answer would move me toward his position: ask him to name the concrete case in which truth-tracking nosology has, on his account, produced worse outcomes for the diagnosed than an avowedly designed alternative would have, and to state in advance what he would accept as evidence that he is wrong about that case.

For each question, add a short note naming the strongest answer I should be prepared for, the evasion I most expect, and a follow-up aimed at the evasion rather than at a restatement of the question. Flag any question where I should be ready to paraphrase a technical term for a lay listener.

Two house rules. Write only my side — no scripted dialogue, no fabricated replies put in his mouth. And no bibliography, no gesturing at particular studies: if a question turns on an empirical claim, state the claim in plain language inside the question, so that he can simply accept or deny it.
