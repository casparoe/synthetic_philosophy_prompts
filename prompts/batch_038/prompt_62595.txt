A reader's report on my chapter has come back with a single charge: that my account of aggregation is unstable, borrowing from the personal case exactly what it refuses in the social case. I do not want to argue with the report line by line. I want the package audited from the ground up, on the working assumption that the charge may be correct.

Here is what I am prepared to defend in print.

(i) I accept the separateness-of-persons objection to aggregative consequentialism. Such views go wrong, I think, by extending across persons a mode of practical reasoning that is legitimate only within a single life, treating the distribution of benefits and burdens among different people as though it were as normally inert as the distribution of costs and gains across the stages of one life. When the loss falls on one person and the gain on another, there is no subject for whom the two come together, so the gain does not offset the loss.

(ii) I state the underlying premise as a principle I call Compensation Locality: a burden borne by someone can be justified by a compensating benefit only if there is a single subject for whom burden and benefit are jointly available — either at once, or across time bound together by memory and anticipation in the ordinary way.

(iii) I am a reductionist about personal identity. There is nothing over and above psychological connectedness and continuity and their physical basis, and these hold to varying degrees. Identity is not a further fact, and it is not what does the normative work.

(iv) It follows, I think, that the strength of my present special concern for a future person should track the degree of that person's connectedness to me now. Radical discontinuity weakens my reason to care. It does not eliminate it.

(v) Nothing analogous holds between persons. If I stand to a stranger in a relation of connectedness exactly as strong as the one I bear to my own remote future self, that gives me no licence to burden the stranger for a benefit to myself or to a third party. Interpersonal connectedness does not soften the moral boundary; it does not stiffen it either; and it does not turn it into a matter of degree.

(vi) On the numbers. Where I can save either one person or five from death and the harms are otherwise identical, I hold that I ought to save the five. The view that I may settle it by a coin toss strikes me as evasive.

(vii) But I reject aggregation across unlike harms. Where I can either prevent one person's death or prevent each of five people from losing an arm, I hold that I ought to prevent the death. A sum of lesser claims may not outweigh a greater one.

(viii) I am broadly a prioritarian: the worse off someone is, the stronger the reason to benefit them. I see no conflict with (i), because weighting each person's situation taken separately is not adding situations together.

(ix) Two case verdicts I have already worked through and which I take to support (ii). First, after fission, each survivor retains about half my memories and half my standing projects, and neither retains the whole; if the choice now lies between giving one of them a severe headache and giving the other an afternoon of great enjoyment, I deny that I have any reason to prefer the pleasure. Second, in the rescue cases just described, my verdicts are as in (vi) and (vii) and I do not feel them pulling apart.

(x) A methodological constraint I would like to keep: a moral principle should not need a clause indexed to how many people are involved in order to generate the right verdict. If my principles require a headcount term somewhere, I count that as a mark against them, not a neutral bookkeeping device.

(xi) One case I cannot settle and have left open: I can either prevent one person's death or prevent ten thousand people from each sustaining a moderate and lasting injury. I feel strongly that the death should be prevented, but I am aware that (vii) seems to make that a lexical claim, and I do not know whether I can hold it at every scale.

What I want from you is the audit, not a defense of my view and not a lecture on the literature.

First, go through the pairs. Which are in genuine conflict, and which only appear to be? I would rather you tell me plainly that some of my claims are unobjectionable together than have you flag everything, since over-revision is the failure mode I am trying to avoid.

Second, rank the genuine conflicts by severity — which threaten to collapse the position into a view I have already rejected, which force an unacceptable consequence, and which are merely awkward and can be lived with.

Third, for each genuine conflict, say what has to give, and identify the least costly revision along with what that revision costs me elsewhere. Then tell me which single change to the package would clean up the most, and whether that change leaves anything recognizable of the view I started with.

Fourth, address (xi) explicitly: does any principle that survives the audit deliver a verdict there, and is that verdict one I can accept without abandoning (vii) or (x)?

Finally, I want to know where your own audit is most likely to go wrong. If any of my claims admits a reading on which an apparent conflict disappears — Compensation Locality read as a thesis about justification rather than about reasons, say, or (v) read as a claim about permissions rather than about the weight of reasons — say which reading I should adopt and what it would then leave unexplained. State which consideration would most likely move you to a different ranking than the one you give me, and whether there is a point at which you think the reader's charge is simply correct and no revision short of dropping (i) or (iii) will repair the package.
