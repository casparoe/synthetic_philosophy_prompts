A colleague and I have been arguing about whether Malthus can be saved from two centuries of evidence, and I suspect his case proves far more than he wants, but I cannot name the step where it goes. As he puts it:

(1) A theory of long-run tendency predicts what happens when nothing counteracts it, not when that will be.

(2) Malthus's theory is of that kind: unchecked population grows geometrically while the means of subsistence grow arithmetically, so any improvement in living standards is consumed by the extra population it induces, holding the mass of humanity at subsistence.

(3) Two centuries of rising output per capita therefore do not refute him. Agricultural innovation, the demographic transition, and global food trade are precisely the counteractions the theory leaves open, and their occurrence is perfectly compatible with the tendency's being real.

(4) Nor is a general return to subsistence needed for confirmation. Wherever the positive checks have operated — the Irish potato famine, the Bengal famine — the mechanism is on display, arriving late rather than not at all.

(5) The same defence is available for any tendency theory that has not yet come off, while any tendency theory that has come off can point to its instances; so no tendency theory can ever be told that it lost.

(6) A theory that cannot lose is not an empirical theory at all.

(7) Hence (1) is false, and Malthus is not merely premature but refuted — or rather, by the same route, every tendency theory in economics and ecology is either refuted or non-empirical.

I would rather keep (1) than (7), since ceteris paribus claims look indispensable across the special sciences, so what I want is a diagnosis: which step should I reject, why did that step look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost me? If instead you think the argument is sound as it stands, then explain what separates a legitimate claim that a theory was right but early from a rescue of this kind, and why that distinction does not collapse back into (5).
