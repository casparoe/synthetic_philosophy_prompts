I moderate a monthly podcast on the empirical study of political regimes, and next month's guest is a political scientist whose recent book argues that the concept of democratic backsliding should be replaced by a much narrower test. Her proposal, as I understand it: a regime is backsliding only when the likelihood that the governing party loses an election and then actually vacates office has declined relative to that country's own prior record. Everything else ordinarily counted under the heading — judicial reorganization, media capture, pressure on opposition figures, the erosion of minority protections — enters only instrumentally, insofar as it moves that likelihood. She is emphatic that the test is to be applied using observable events alone (was an election held, was the result certified, did the transfer take place), with no expert coding and no attribution of intentions, and she presents this as the source of its political neutrality: the criterion asks whether power changes hands, not whether we approve of who holds it. Among the four cases she examines, Turkey and Hungary qualify; the United States does not, since the 2020 result was certified and the transfer occurred; Poland is the awkward one, because the ruling party lost the October 2023 parliamentary election and the opposition coalition did take office, yet she still wants to characterize the preceding eight years there as backsliding.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for a forty-minute conversation, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Constraints:

- No question may be answerable by restating the thesis, and none may be a generic career-or-motivation question. Each should name the specific commitment it is targeting.
- Include at least one question on each of the following: (i) the lag problem — the criterion can register a decline only after transfers have begun to fail, so it picks out precisely those cases in which any response it might motivate is already too late; (ii) the tension between speaking of a decline in a likelihood and insisting on observable events only, since likelihoods of losing office cannot be read off events without modeling or counterfactual judgment; (iii) Poland, and what the account says about a regime in which power changes hands regularly but the incoming opposition finds that it cannot govern; (iv) the neutrality claim — why treating the transfer of office as the essence of democracy is any less a substantive normative choice than a coder's choice of indicators; (v) whether an attempted but unsuccessful non-transfer scores zero on her measure, and if it does not, how she reads intentions without reintroducing the judgment she means to exclude.
- Two of the questions should be ones she can answer well. Mark those two, and state in a sentence each what a strong answer would look like; I would rather expose the position than ambush it.
- Close with a single question asking what evidence would force her to abandon the criterion.

Finally, tell me which question is load-bearing — the one whose answer, if she holds it, would blunt most of the others — and flag any point at which you think she has a genuinely good reply, along with what that reply is.
