I'm a med student auditing a philosophy of medicine seminar and I've come out of it with an argument I can't shake. It's supposed to show that nobody who donates organs after circulatory death is actually dead at the time of procurement. I don't believe that, but I can't find the bad step.

(1) The model US law from 1981 that most states have adopted says an individual is dead if they've sustained either "irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions" or "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem."

(2) So both criteria have the same shape: some function F has ceased, and can't be brought back.

(3) Irreversible means can't be reversed by any means we actually have. Otherwise it was reversible and we were wrong to call it irreversible.

(4) In DCD, death is declared after a handful of minutes of asystole on the monitor. During those minutes circulation can absolutely be brought back — compressions, or for that matter the pump the same team hooks up a few minutes after the declaration to perfuse the abdominal organs, with the arch vessels blocked so none of it goes to the head. Same physiology, opposite verdict, and the only difference is what the people in the room have decided to do.

(5) So at the moment of declaration the cessation isn't irreversible; by (1) the donor isn't dead; and by the dead donor rule, procurement is what kills them.

(6) Same move on the brain side. Plenty of brain-dead patients still have working hypothalamic-neuroendocrine function — that's why your chasing diabetes insipidus with vasopressin for days. That's a function of the entire brain that hasn't ceased, so on the literal wording they don't meet the criterion either.

(7) Now push (3) the other direction. Unwitnessed arrest, valid DNR, nobody will ever start compressions: circulation stops and will never be restored, so by (1) and (3) that patient is dead at the moment of arrest. Take the identical patient, identical heart, in the next bay, where a resident happens to be holding a defib and does get them back — during their arrest the cessation was reversible, so they were never dead. One body dead, one alive, same physiology, and which is which depends on who's standing there and what they intend.

(8) Death can't be a fact about who's in the room. It's supposed to be a fact about the organism.

(9) So (3) is false. But every way I've tried to weaken it makes either (5) or (7) come back. If irreversible means won't in fact be restored, the DNR patient is dead on the table and DCD is fine, but then whether you're dead gets settled by other people's decisions; if it means can't be restored, then DCD is killing and a good code is resurrection.

Where does this break — once, or in several places? I'd like to keep the dead donor rule and I'd really like to keep (8). Tell me which step goes, why it looked obviously true going in, and what the survivors cost. And is (6) actually the same argument as (4)-(5), or have I run two arguments and called it one?
