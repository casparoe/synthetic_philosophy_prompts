For the past three years I have run a small housing nonprofit, and a friend of mine — not a colleague, simply someone whose life I have long admired — has become the person I call before any hard decision: whether to accept a large gift from a landlord whose practices I despise, whether to let go of a devoted staffer who is quietly wrecking the program, whether to tell one client's story publicly. I have never once regretted following her advice, and several of our best outcomes trace directly to it. But two things have begun to trouble me. First, when a junior staffer asked me last month why we had turned the money down, all I could manage was that it hadn't sat right with us; two years ago I could have laid out the reasons in detail. Second, I can no longer anticipate what she will say — I used to be able to.

She and I argued about this for hours last night. Her position is that there is nothing peculiar in any of it: I defer to our accountant about the books and to a lawyer about the lease without a shred of hand-wringing, and she is simply better at this than I am, so trusting her is the same sensible move in a different subject matter. I half believe her, and yet the two situations do not feel alike to me at all, and I cannot articulate why. I am now weighing whether to add her to the board with a formal veto over decisions of that sort, and I want to think it through before I act.

Please answer in four sections, in this order: "What is actually at issue" (about 150 words, identifying the question my situation raises that the accountant case does not); "The case that my friend is right" (about 200 words); "The case that my unease tracks something real" (about 250 words); and "What I should do about the board seat" (about 150 words, ending in a definite recommendation).
