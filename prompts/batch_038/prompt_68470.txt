In the African philosophical literature on ubuntu, the processual or "achieved" account of personhood — associated above all with Menkiti, and contested from a more individualist direction by Gyekye's moderate communitarianism — holds that one does not simply arrive in the world a person but becomes one over time, by being progressively woven into a network of reciprocal obligations, and that communal recognition is constitutive of that standing rather than merely honorific toward it. The stock objection is that such a view cannot secure moral equality: if personhood comes in degrees and is earned through participation, then the newborn, the recluse, the profoundly cognitively disabled, and the ostracized all slide down a scale, and the position stands one short step from licensing a hierarchical politics of standing.

I want you to argue against that objection, but not in the abstract. Use the following stipulated arrangement as your test bed.

A mid-sized municipality allocates its adult social-care budget by an explicitly processual rule. No resident holds a fixed entitlement by birth or by species membership. Each quarter, a panel of long-standing residents assigns every adult a standing score reflecting the density and reciprocity of that resident's actual relationships: who they feed and who feeds them, whose grief they attend, who would be measurably diminished by their absence. Care hours, housing priority, and emergency funds are distributed in proportion to the score. The rule's designers defend it in precisely the vocabulary of the achieved account. They insist they are not grading human worth but tracking how thickly a given life has been incorporated into a web of mutual obligation, and they note that scores rise, sometimes steeply, when a resident takes on dependents or repairs a rupture.

Argue the following as a connected case rather than a list:

1. That this scheme is not an abuse of the processual account but a natural institutional expression of it, and that the two moves a sympathetic philosopher is most likely to make in order to distance the view from the scheme — that the score tracks nothing morally relevant, so the policy is merely a bad policy carrying no metaphysical commitment, and that the processual view concerns moral status rather than distributive shares — both fail, for reasons you state explicitly.

2. That the distinction doing the real work is between personhood as a status one holds and personhood as a standing one occupies. Say precisely what makes the second notion different from the first, rather than relabelling it.

3. That the standing notion commands a resource the status notion lacks in exactly the case the scheme is designed for: the resident whom nobody would miss. Show what the status view is committed to saying about such a person, and why its answer is worse than the one the scheme gives.

You will also have to confront a serious internal difficulty rather than step around it: once relationships are measured and paid out in benefits, the measurement alters what it measures, and the panel's scoring may corrode the reciprocity it is supposed to register. Say whether the processual view has a reply here that the status view lacks, or whether this is where your argument breaks.

Do not conclude that the scheme is indefensible, and do not treat the moral-equality objection as trivial; meet it head-on. If some premise in your case is one a defender of Menkiti would want to disown, identify it and explain why she should not.
