Okay so I've been going around in circles on gambles where the expected-value math goes haywire, and I want to know what principle my actual gut verdicts add up to, if there is one. Let me lay out the cases and what I say about each.

Plain fair coin, heads pays $2: worth exactly a dollar, I pay a dollar, no problem. St. Petersburg — flip till heads, pay $2^n on flip n: the math says its worth infinity, and I'll happily agree it has a value and that the value is infinite, and yet I'd pay $20 for a ticket and I don't think I'm being irrational. $10 million on a one-in-2^1000 shot: perfectly well-defined value, worth nothing to me, and I feel zero tension about that.

Now the alternating one, which I think people call the Pasadena game. Flip till heads; on flip n you collect 2^n/n dollars if n is odd and you owe 2^n/n if n is even. Each outcome has probability 2^-n, so the contributions come out +1, −1/2, +1/3, −1/4, …, which adds up to about 69 cents. But it only lands there because of the order I summed in — reorder the same terms and you can make it come out to any number you like, or blow up. My verdict: this gamble has no value at all. Not 69 cents, not infinity, just no fact of the matter. And so there's no true ranking between it and a sure dollar either. Except — here's the part I can't clean up — if you actually put the choice in front of me I take the sure dollar, and if a friend took the gamble I'd think he'd done something wrong, not something merely permissible.

Same game but I never fine you on the even flips, so the contributions are +1, +1/2, +1/3, …: this one I'm confident has a value, an infinite one, and I'm confident it's better than the alternating one — it's at least as good on every flip and strictly better on the even ones. But notice I've just ranked a gamble that has a value against one I said has none.

Cap the alternating game so no payoff exceeds $1,000: totally well behaved, worth roughly 69 cents, I'd pay 60. And the chance the coin ever reaches a flip where the cap changes anything is about one in eight thousand. So I feel like the uncapped version has to be worth about 60 cents too. But I also just said the uncapped version has no value at all. Both feel right and I can't hold them together.

Nastier one. Hand me the mirror image of the alternating game — same magnitudes, signs flipped, contributions −1, +1/2, −1/3, … — and let me buy both tickets. Then on every flip the payoffs cancel and I net exactly zero, guaranteed. That combined position is clearly worth exactly zero. But I said each half was valueless. How do two things with no value add up to something worth precisely zero?

And the rearranged one. Someone builds me a different game where the payoff at flip n is chosen so the contributions run +1, +1/2, −1/3, −1/4, +1/5, … — exactly the same list of contributions as the Pasadena game, just re-attached to different flips, arranged so the sum is $100. Part of me says that's obviously the same kind of bet, same ingredients, so if Pasadena has no value neither does this. Another part says its value is $100 and I'd pay $99 for it. I keep flip-flopping.

Underneath all of it I keep wanting to say that the value of a gamble is just what your average payout settles down to if you could play it a zillion times. But with the alternating game I don't think the average settles down anywhere, and I feel like that's exactly what's gone wrong — and also that it can't be the whole story, since for St. Petersburg the average doesn't settle down either and I'm still perfectly confident that one has a value.

Here's the principle I've been operating with, and it's the one I want you to attack: a gamble has a value just in case adding up probability-times-payoff gives the same total no matter what order you add the terms in; when it doesn't, the gamble has no value and no comparison involving it is true. That's what earns my verdicts on the alternating game and on the all-positive one.

So: does it survive the rest? Give me the principle (or the two or three candidates) that best fits everything above, tie each clause to the specific case that motivates it, and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it flat-out contradicts — if the price is dropping one of them, tell me which one should go and why. Then tell me what your principle says about two cases I didn't list, one where it's comfortable and one where it's ugly. And if your"re going to say my sure-dollar preference is just prudence rather than a value judgment, say so explicitly, because that move does a lot of work here and I want to see whether it's principled or a patch.
