"Virtue ethics going back to Aristotle says that what makes an action right is that it comes from a stable character, a set of traits like honesty or courage that a person has across all kinds of situations and that explains why they act the way they do. The situationist challenge attacks the psychology underneath that picture. Situationists say we have a built in bias, basically we overestimate personality and underestimate the situation when we explain other peoples behavior, so we invent character traits that were never doing any work. The classic evidence is Milgram's obedience study, where around 65% of ordinary people kept shocking a stranger up to the highest level just becuse a guy in a lab coat told them to keep going, and the tiny changes to the setup moved that number around a lot. There's also the study where people who found a dime in a phone booth were way more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers than people who didn't find a dime, which is a pretty embarrassing thing to have your character depend on. So situationists say character in the traditional sense doesn't exist, and Gilbert Harman argued we should just stop using trait words to explain behavior at all.

John Doris offers a softer version. He says traits are real but they're local, not global, so someone might be honest-at-work but not honest-at-home, and there's no deep unity holding it together.

I think Doris is right that traits are local but I don't think that destroys virtue ethics like he wants it to. The big proof is the ego depletion stuff, where researchers claimed willpower is like a battery that runs down, and then those studies failed to replicate in the big registered replication a few years back. That shows the situational findings were overhyped too, so Aristotle wins by default. And anyway if all Doris is saying is that your traits are local, then you can still have local virtues, like being a courageous soldier or a loyal friend, which is everything Aristotle actually needs, so his whole project survives unscathed. The only thing we lose is the idea that being a good friend makes you good in general, and that was never a serious claim."
