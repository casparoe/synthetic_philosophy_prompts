I am three months into a mixed reading group on the units-of-selection debate: two philosophy graduate students, an ecology postdoc, and a friend of mine who works in condensed-matter theory and joined because he wanted to see whether the controversy was a real one. He has concluded that it is not, and the analogy he leans on is coarse-graining in statistical mechanics. His line is that the gene's-eye view is simply the microscopic description of the same process that group-selectionists describe macroscopically, in exactly the way that molecular dynamics and the ideal gas law are two descriptions of one gas; that the Price equation makes this explicit, since it can be partitioned at any level you like and the between-group and within-group terms are just a change of variables; and that a dispute about which level selection "really" acts at is therefore a dispute about which variables are convenient, not about the world. He then pushes the analogy further with renormalization-group language: you integrate out the fast degrees of freedom, the fixed point is indifferent to the details you have thrown away, and group-level traits are like irrelevant operators that wash out at long scales — which, he says, is precisely why only the replicator level persists as a locus of heritable variation over evolutionary time. He also insists that nothing objectionable is happening at the macro level, because in physics too the macrostate does not push the microstate around: temperature supervenes, is autonomous and indispensable for prediction, and yet causes nothing beyond what the molecules do. To illustrate the supposed harmlessness of the whole affair he offered the example of someone explaining superconductivity by appeal to an order parameter while someone else insists on doing it with individual electrons, and said both are right and are merely working at different scales.

Before I push back I want the analogy audited properly, because it is doing a great deal of work and I am not sure I can yet say which parts of the work it can bear. Act as a philosopher of biology who is equally at home with the technical apparatus of population genetics and with the physics being invoked here, and think carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary of the debate. Begin with a short reconstruction, in neutral terms, of what would have to be true of some entity for there to be selection acting at that level — heredity, variation, differential fitness, and whatever else you think the literature has added or refined — since I suspect much of the audit turns on that. Then give me the mapping explicitly, ideally as a table of correspondences: what plays the role of the microstate, the macrostate, the coarse-graining map, the thermodynamic limit, the observer, and the fixed point, and which of those slots the biology actually fills. Then set out the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the use my friend is making of the comparison and those that are merely picturesque. I have two half-formed suspicions I would like you to test rather than endorse: first, that the analogy slides without argument from a part–whole hierarchy to the replicator–vehicle distinction, which cuts across it rather than coinciding with it; second, that statistical mechanics has no counterpart to a group begetting a group, so that group-level heritability — colony fission, propagule founding, the ecological inheritance groups leave behind — has nowhere to go in the mapping. Address in particular whether the mathematical availability of a partition at any level licenses the claim that there is a *cause* or a *unit* at that level, how the distinction between selection of groups and selection for group-beneficial traits fares under the analogy, and whether the gene is really the micro-level description at all.

Deliver, in this order: (1) the reconstruction; (2) the correspondence table; (3) the disanalogies, with the load-bearing ones flagged; (4) a direct verdict on my friend's conclusion — that the debate is verbal bookkeeping and that the gene's-eye view and the group's-eye view are interchangeable descriptions of one process — stating plainly whether it survives, survives in weakened form, or collapses, and if part of the dispute genuinely is verbal, say which part and why; and (5) a better analogy, if one exists, together with a note on what work the improved analogy can and cannot do. No bibliography and no citations, please; I want the reasoning, not a reference list.
