I teach a first-year seminar on the philosophy of time, and each spring I hand out a piece of fiction instead of another textbook excerpt, because I've found that students grasp the stakes of an argument faster when somebody has to live inside it. This year I want something on the argument Rietdijk and Putnam pressed in the late 1960s: that because simultaneity is frame-dependent, two observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are already real, and if you grant that what is real for me includes events that are still future for you, then there is no objective present and the future is, in the relevant sense, already there. The trouble is that every treatment I know of leans on Roger Penrose's illustration of two pedestrians passing on a street while an Andromedan fleet either has or has not launched, and my students have all met that example already, usually in a pop-science video. I want a story that keeps the argument's structure but relocates it somewhere the arithmetic actually bites and where the disagreement has a price attached to it.

Here is the situation I've sketched with a friend who does trajectory work; please keep the story consistent with these numbers, since the whole point is that the effect scales as velocity times distance over c squared and therefore has a fixed, modest size in this setting. A single-crew courier ship runs between an Earth-side departure point and a colony 4.1 light-years away, both effectively at rest with each other, cruising at 0.21c. That puts the ship's simultaneity slice at the colony about ten months ahead of the colony's own clocks for the entire outbound leg — a constant desynchronization, not a growing one. Seven months after departure, back at the colony, the pilot's husband has said he will decide whether to take a berth on a departure to a farther system or to keep waiting for her; he was explicit before she left that he would not hold the choice open indefinitely. So from the moment she leaves, that decision sits inside her past on the argument's own terms: settled, real, and utterly unavailable to her, since the earliest light carrying news of it reaches her roughly four years into a nineteen-year crossing. The engine of the plot I have in mind is a superstition she forms about the mid-course correction burn. If she throttles the profile down to 0.05c, the desynchronization collapses to about two and a half months, and the decision day slides back across her present into her future — which, by the argument's logic, means it is open again. Nothing at the colony changes; the burn costs real delta-v and delays the news by the better part of a year, and her navigator makes both of those facts uncomfortably clear. Let her want it anyway, and let the wanting be the story.

On execution: aim for roughly 2,300 words, and feel free to structure it however you like, though I have had good results alternating terse navigation-log entries against voice messages she records and never transmits, since the un-sendability of everything she says is already the point. No character may deliver a lecture, no philosopher may be named in the dialogue, and the physics should surface only as the crew's working vocabulary. I do want one character — the navigator, or a passenger, or the husband in an old recording — to press the strongest available objection in their own idiom: something in the neighborhood of Howard Stein's line that in relativity the present is a local notion, that there is no principled way to stretch it into a global slice, and that the argument smuggles in a transitivity assumption about reality that the physics never licenses. Give that objection real force rather than making it a foil; if the story has to tilt, let it tilt by what happens to the pilot, not by who wins the argument. And please do not resolve the husband's decision in a way that turns on new information arriving from the colony — the epistemic emptiness of a future that is already real is what I most want the students to feel.

After the story, add a note of about 250 words for my own use, which I will not distribute. Tell me which premise of the argument the story leans on hardest and which ones it merely decorates; flag every place you took a liberty with the physics or with the metaphysics, including any place you smoothed over the difference between an event's being real and its being fixed or knowable. Then answer this directly: if you judged Stein's objection decisive, or otherwise came to think the argument fails, would this story have to change, and how far? Say which ending or which scene you would have written differently, and where you think your own reasoning about the case is most likely to be wrong — I would rather see you name the weak joint than defend the draft.
