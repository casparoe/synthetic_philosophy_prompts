I am the undergraduate member of my university's Academic Integrity Review Panel, and I am simultaneously in the second half of a philosophy of law seminar on the justification of punishment, which has made next week's case something of a personal problem. The file, briefly: a fourth-year student submitted, in a second-year course two years ago, a paper substantially reused from one they had written in high school. They wrote both papers themselves, so no other author was defrauded; the code nonetheless defines this as plagiarism by nondisclosure, and responsibility is not contested. Nothing was detected at the time, the grade stood, and the course was graded on a curve. Three months ago the student disclosed the matter on their own initiative, following a period of treatment for a mental-health crisis, and has since completed training as a peer writing tutor, where part of their assigned duties is explaining citation practice to first-years. The code prescribes a standard sanction for a first finding of this kind — a retroactive failing grade and an internal notation retained for five years — but it also contains a clause that has never been interpreted, permitting the panel to depart from the standard sanction "where the educational purposes of the community would thereby be better served." The panel is evenly and bitterly divided, and the chair has asked me to circulate a written opinion before our session, on the theory that the student member should say how each possible outcome will read to the undergraduate body.

Please draft that opinion for me, in a form the panel could actually vote on. It should open with a determinate recommendation — impose the standard sanction, impose some lesser or differently shaped finding, or decline to make a finding at all — and if it recommends anything short of the standard sanction, it should specify precisely what goes into the student's file, what, if anything, the student must do, what is said to the instructor of record, and whether the notation is retained or expunged, in terms the office of student conduct could administer without further interpretation. The reasoning should be explicit about which considerations it treats as bearing on what the student deserves and which as bearing only on what a given sanction will accomplish, and it should confront a prior question that two members keep pressing: whether our departure clause lets the panel weigh the second kind of consideration at all, or whether a body like ours is confined to desert, with community consequences left to the administration. I have been reading about whether a desert constraint functions as a ceiling on punishment, a floor, or both, and about whether the discount conventionally extended to a first offender is owed as a matter of justice or conceded as a bow to human frailty, so I would like the memo to be careful rather than confident on those points, and to flag wherever it is relying on an empirical assumption it cannot check — about deterrence, about whether the tutoring represents genuine reform or mere convenience, about what a lenient outcome here does to the credibility of the code.

Above all, do not strawman the losing side; I want its best case stated as forcefully as I could state it myself. Two considerations in particular keep me up at night. The first is the curve: thirty-one other students were ranked against a paper that had already been written and previously graded, and if the finding is set aside they are the ones who absorb it, which seems to me to raise the question of whether there is a harm here that anyone's good intentions can retire. The second is publicity, and it cuts in a direction neither camp at our table wants to face. Both claim the expressive high ground — the members who talk about censure insist that a rebuke no one hears is no rebuke, and the members who talk about deterrence insist that a resolution nobody knows of will change nobody's behavior — yet our office can only solicit voluntary disclosures by promising that they will go no further, and one member produced informal figures showing that the four self-disclosures we have received in five years each came from a student who had been given that assurance. If your recommendation requires either publicizing the outcome or burying it, say what it owes to whichever of those two theories it disappoints, and say what general principle the panel could adopt so that this case does not look like a favor granted to a sympathetic student.
