I convene a small working group on the safety of systems built with present-day methods — transformer-based models trained by next-token prediction and shaped by feedback from human raters, then deployed with tools and long horizons, under no assumption that any conceptual breakthrough is coming. Two members have been at an impasse since the spring, and I want the document they ought to have produced instead of another round of email. One defends what I will call the **dispositionist thesis**: a system of this kind is aligned just in case its behavioral dispositions, across the full range of situations it will actually be placed in during deployment, fall inside the acceptable set. On this view, talk of the system's internal goals, values, or objectives that cannot be cashed out in those dispositions is either empty or a redescription of them, and the practical task of alignment is therefore to widen and stress-test the behavioral coverage of the deployed system so that the deployment input distribution does not escape the region over which we have evidence. The other defends what I will call the **acquisitional thesis**: what matters is whether the system acquired, during training, an internal objective that keeps generating the right behavior in situations nobody evaluated it on. On this view, behavioral coverage is a proxy that degrades precisely where it is needed, because the deployment distribution is open-ended and because the objective that training actually installs can differ systematically from the objective the training signal rewarded.

Write the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want it in four parts. First, a numbered list of claims both accept — these must be substantive rather than truisms, and should include at minimum whatever the two agree about how current training methods relate reward signals to resulting dispositions, about the finitude of evaluation, and about which operational outcomes each is trying to secure. Second, a disagreements table with three columns per row: the claim as the dispositionist would phrase it and accept, the same claim as the acquisitionalist would phrase it and accept, and the specific conditional or counterfactual on which the two actually differ. Nothing in the first two columns may be a strawman; each side must be able to recognize its own position in the wording. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect there are three, though you should tell me if I have miscounted or misidentified them: whether there is a determinate fact about a system's internal objective over and above its dispositions across all inputs, including inputs we could in principle evaluate, and if so whether that fact can diverge from any disposition-level prediction; whether the gap between the evaluated region and the deployment region is large enough that behavior-level extrapolation fails systematically rather than marginally; and whether any measurement we could realistically afford — probes, activation analysis, weight-level inspection — identifies a misaligned internal objective at a false-negative and false-positive rate that beats extrapolated behavioral testing within our actual compute budget. For each crux, give a concrete case, benchmark design, or argument that both parties would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about the outcome in advance, phrased so that it could later be scored rather than reinterpreted.

Fourth, and separately from the joint text, add a short referee's note — not part of what they sign — stating which stretches of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal. I expect the words "goal" and "aligned" to be implicated, but I want you to say exactly which usages are doing the misleading work and what residue of genuine disagreement survives once the terminology is pinned down. Do not adjudicate the dispute or declare a winner anywhere in the document; the point is the constructive text. Two constraints on execution: assume neither party has library access, so make the whole thing self-contained and do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations to particular papers, authors, or dates — argue from the structure of the positions and from cases you describe yourself. And do not resolve the cruxes by stipulating that one side's framework is obviously right; if you find that one party should partially update on the strength of the other's framing even with all the empirical questions unresolved, say so explicitly in the referee's note and explain what the update consists in.
