I keep coming back to the Jallianwala Bagh business and I want to work through it with the balance-sheet-of-empire frame, but pushed harder than it usually gets pushed. Facts first so we're on the same page: April 1919, Dyer's troops fire on an unarmed crowd in an enclosed ground in Amritsar, no warning to disperse, the one exit blocked; the official British count is 379 dead and Indian counts run much higher. The Hunter inquiry censured him and he left the army, but sections of British opinion treated him as a hero, and the Commons voted against him 247 to 37 the following year. Britain has never formally apologized. On the centenary in 2019 Theresa May's government told the Commons that Britain deeply regretted the massacre but stopped short of an apology, and there was a fair amount of explicit worry in that debate that apologizing would set a precedent. On the other side you have Tharoor, whose 2015 Oxford Union speech on whether Britain owes reparations became Inglorious Empire, saying what he wants isn't money but atonement — I think he floated a token payment so trivial it's basically free, take that as the spirit rather than the letter. Ferguson's answer to all of this is the ledger: weigh what the Raj cost India against what it delivered, lean on counterfactuals, and reject the apology-politics package as bad accounting.

Here's what I actually want. Take the accounting metaphor seriously as an accounting framework rather than as a figure of speech and run the 1919-to-2019 episode through it. Corporate accounting draws a hard line between recognizing a provision on the face of the balance sheet — you do that when a past event created a present obligation, an outflow is probable, and you can estimate it reliably — and merely disclosing a contingent liability in the notes, which is what you do when the obligation is possible but not probable, or when you can't size it. My hunch, which I want you to test rather than flatter: regret-without-apology is the sovereign version of a note disclosure. Britain isn't denying the event, it's declining to recognize it, becuase the exposure is unquantifable and recognizing one item would open a class of claims it can't size. If that's right then the Ferguson-Tharoor argument was never really about whether the net balance came out positive — it's about the recognition threshold, and the two sides have been talking past each other for a decade.

So set the mapping up properly before you argue anything. Who is the reporting entity: is the UK of 2019 the same entity as the Crown in India of 1919, a successor, or something else, and does any of the analysis turn on that? What is the reporting period? What counts as an asset and what as a liability here, and where does the counterfactual baseline Ferguson needs actually sit in that structure — is "what India would have become without the Raj" an asset, a contra-asset, or a thing the framework has no line item for? Who is the auditor, and what happens to the whole apparatus when there isn't one? Related wrinkle: in some colonies Britain destroyed or shipped out the records on the way out, so parts of the audit trail simply don't exist any more.

Then tell me what the ledger frame explains about the episode, specifically. Why did Britain book something in 1920 — censure, a Commons vote — and nothing in 2019? Under the framework, was that a charge taken against the officer rather than the entity, and does that make the later refusal to apologize a case of double counting or a genuine gap in the books? Why does the precedent worry come out in exactly the language of unbounded off-balance-sheet exposure? And going the other way: does Tharoor's insistence that he wants atonement and not money actually reject the ledger while speaking its language? Is a token payment a recognized liability of roughly zero, or is it an attempt to change what kind of speech act an apology is?

Then where the frame breaks, honestly. The creditors are dead; the obligation can't be discharged by transfer; the entity can't be wound up; a state may not be a going concern in the relevant sense at all. Which of those is a real disanalogy that does damage and which is just the metaphor creaking? I'd rather you tell me the whole thing is decorative in places than force it to speak.

And finally run it back the other direction: does this episode confirm the balance-sheet frame, expose an anomaly in it, or is it a case the frame never anticipated? If Ferguson is right that the question is what the net balance was, what does he predict Britain should have done in 2019 — and is that prediction embarrassed by the fact that Britain expressed regret while refusing recognition? Does a framework that can't tell an apology from a non-apology have a hole in it? And if the provision-versus-disclosure distinction ends up carrying all the weight, say so plainly and say what the balance-sheet frame owes us that it hasn't delivered.

No bibliography needed, I'll add citations myself. Just be disciplined about the mapping and say where it's silent.
