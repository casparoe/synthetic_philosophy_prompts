So, hunch I want you to argue for as hard as you can — not neutrally assess, actually commit to it. I keep seeing karma presented as a theory of moral desert: the cosmic ledger that makes sure people get what they deserve. But a desert claim seems to need what I'll call a diachronic anchor — the subject who earned it and the subject who receives it have to be the same someone, in whatever sense the theory of desert requires. I dont want to just assume numerical identity is the only candidate; lay out the options (identity, psychological continuity, bare causal continuity, some conventional-person pragmatism) and what each costs.

The trouble is that the Buddhist version of karma is precisely the one that refuses to supply an anchor: no self persists even within a single life, let alone across lives, and the classic account of rebirth is that nothing crosses over at all, only a causal link does — the seal-pressed-into-wax picture, or a flame handed from lamp to lamp. The school that tried to smuggle an anchor back in (the personalists, with their inexpressible person) got branded heretical for it, which tells you the anchor isn't optional furniture in that tradition, it's contraband.

First half: argue that this doesn't merely make karmic desert hard to spell out, it makes it impossible — that what's left is a cosmology in which suffering reliably tracks past action but nobody gets what they deserve, becuase there is no one to get it. Give me the argument in explicit steps, and mark which step a defender should attack.

Second half, which is what I actually care about: argue that this is a win rather than a scandal. Karma's real work was to make unequal fortune intelligible and to keep moral effort rational, and a tracking-without-desert cosmology does both while dodging what the desert version has to swallow — an anchor that's either a soul or a fiction, plus a cosmic order that somehow owes creatures something.
