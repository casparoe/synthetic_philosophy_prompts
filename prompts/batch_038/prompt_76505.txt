So I got volunteered to give the opening remarks at the last of three public workshops before our county board votes next month on whether to keep using the pretrial risk score. Eight minutes, about ninety people in a library meeting room: two judges who have been using the thing for six years, three assistant public defenders, a rep from the state AG's office, the vendor's two engineers, some people from a victims rights group, a handful of grad students, and a lot of people whose kids have come through the jail. I built the current version of the score, so everybody in the room already knows I have skin in this.

Here's the knot I need the speech to untie. The renewal ordinance says the tool has to be "equally well calibrated across racial groups" and also "produce equal false positive rates across racial groups." Our data has different base rates by group and the score is nowhere near perfect, so those two can't both be true of any score at all — that's not an artifact of our model, it falls out of what calibration and equal error rates mean. The vendor's proposed fix is to set different cutoff thresholds by group, which buys you the false positive rate and spends the calibration. That's mathematically coherent and politically radioactive, and the AG's rep is in the room specifically to listen for anything that sounds like a quota.

Meanwhile the lead public defender's line, which she will repeat during Q&A, is that the base rates are themselves an artifact — they come from where patrols got sent, so feeding them into the math launders enforcement decisions into arithmetic. I think there's something real there. I don't think it makes the theorem go away, and I don't want to either dodge her or talk down to her.

Can you write the remarks? What I need: the math stays true but is sayable out loud by a non-mathematician in a library, with no equations and no jargon that needs a gloss; it can't land as "your law is dumb"; it names my conflict of interest in roughly one sentence without turning into a confession; and it moves the question from is the algorithm fair to which mistake are we choosing, who eats it, and who's answerable for choosing it — then actually proposes something concrete the board could vote on Thursday rather than a warm call for continued dialogue. No jokes about mathematicians. Assume maybe fifteen people have heard of the impossibility result and the rest never have, and that the ones who haven't still have to feel addressed rather than educated.

Then, separately after the speech, give me a short note: which parts of what you wrote are settled results, and which parts are genuinely contested among people who actually work in this literature. I want to know where you'd be smuggling your own opinion in as if it were the consensus — I suspect that risk is highest in the last third, where you'll be telling a room what the right target is.
