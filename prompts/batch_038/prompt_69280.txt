Last night a friend and I argued for roughly three hours about how one ought to forecast the lifespan of large political orders — empires, hegemons, whichever framing you prefer. He is committed to what he calls the outside view: assemble the class of comparable historical orders, look at the distribution of how long they lasted, take that as your starting estimate, and adjust downward or upward only for genuinely case-specific reasons. I resisted this and lost the thread of my own objection somewhere around midnight. Having now had a day with it, I think there were two distinct objections tangled together, and I cannot find a name for either.

First, the class he wants to sample from can only be assembled after the fact. You can list its members because their stories have finished. A currently existing order cannot be placed on the list without already knowing how it ends, so the base rate meant to discipline my prediction is drawn from a set that no live case can be assigned to in advance — and the instant you do assign your case, you have settled something about the outcome. When I pressed him, he said membership can be fixed by observable features, a certain kind of administrative reach, a certain tributary structure, so classification is prospective after all. I suspect this conceals the difficulty rather than resolving it, since the features he listed are precisely the ones historians use to pick out these units once the units' careers are visible; but I could not say why, which is what cost me the argument.

Second, and I believe separable: how many units are in the class depends on how the histories turned out. If a polity fractures and is later reunified, you count one long-lived order; if the fracture sticks, you count two short ones. Rome is the obvious illustration — depending on whether the eastern continuation is treated as the same empire or as a successor state, its position in the distribution shifts by a factor of three or so. Comparable discretionary rules, such as whether a dynasty constitutes one order or several, move the mean duration of the entire class without anyone having made an empirical discovery.

I am aware of survivorship bias and hindsight bias, and I know the expression reference class carries its own technical problem in the probability literature. None of these seems to be what I am pointing at. Survivorship concerns which cases reach the sample; hindsight concerns what the judge remembers afterward; I am describing something about when membership and unit-count become determinate in the first place.

So: is there an established concept — or an established pair — for these? I am not after a label alone, and a bare name would leave me dissatisfied. I want the phenomenon carved: what it is exactly, how it differs from each of the near-misses above, whether my two objections are genuinely two things or one thing seen from two angles, whether the problem is peculiar to historical cases or extends to startups, languages, species, and firms, and where it stops biting. I can imagine that a class defined by strictly prospective, outcome-independent criteria escapes it altogether, and I would like to know whether that escape actually holds or merely relocates the trouble. If nothing established covers the thing, say so plainly, then coin a term and define it, including at least one boundary case that looks like the phenomenon but is not. Please write in American English.
