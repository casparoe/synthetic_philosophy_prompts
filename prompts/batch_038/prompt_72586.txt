DRAFT — "Deference as Mixture"

The Principal Principle is usually read as a conditional schema. For a time t, a proposition A, a chance x, and evidence E: if the chance at t of A is x and E is admissable, then the credence at t in A given E is x. Almost all the philosophical work goes into "admissible," and there are two main strategies: characterize it directly (evidence that isn't about later chances, roughly), or define it away, as Lewis does in his later reformulation — conditionalize instead on the chance given your total evidence, whatever that evidence turns out to be, and no restriction is needed at all.

I think both strategies get the direction of explanation backwards. Deference to chance isn't fundamentally a conditional norm. It's a structural constraint on a credence function, and once you state it that way the admissibility question mostly evaporates.

Let H1, H2, … Hn be the candidate chance hypotheses at t: complete, mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive specifications of what the chances are. Write ch_i(A) for the chance of A according to H_i. The principle I want is:

(M) Cred(A) = Σ_i Cred(H_i) × ch_i(A)

Your credence in A should be your expectation of the chance of A. No evidence parameter, no admissibility clause.

Two things follow, or so I'll argue.

First, admissibility comes out as a derived notion rather than a premise. Evidence E is admissible just in case conditionalizing on E leaves your weights on the H_i unchanged — E doesn't move you between chance hypotheses. Given that, (M) applied to the conditionalized credence function yields the old schema as a special case. This also explains a pattern people have found hard to characterize: why information about the past and about the laws is admissible while information about later chances isn't. The latter shifts the weights; the former doesn't. (Strictly I also need ch_i(A | E) = ch_i(A) whenever E is weight-neutral. I think that follows from H_i being a complete specification, but I'm not going to argue it here.)

Second, the undermining problem looks like an artifact of the conditional formulation. If chances supervene on the whole history, then a specification of the future is itself evidence about which chance function obtains — which is why the reformulated principle seemed to license bizarre credences in futures that "undermine" the very chances they were supposed to have. (M) licenses nothing of the sort, because it never tells you to hold the weights fixed while the evidence moves them.

Here's the case I want to build on. A lab device produces heads with chance 0.2 on one setting and 0.8 on another. You're told it's on one of the two settings, credence 1/2 in each, but not which. (M) says your credence in heads on the next run is 0.5. Now suppose you learn the outcomes of the next thousand runs: heads exactly 500 times. That pattern is terrible evidence for either setting — it points at a chance near 0.5, which was supposed to be excluded. On the conditional reading there's a puzzle about what you're now required to defer to. On (M) there's no puzzle: you re-weight, and the fact that the mixture now wants an H you left out of the partition is itself the information. Undermining cases are just cases where your partition was wrong, noticed late.

The worry I can't shake: is (M) trivial? Lewis himself remarked that his principle implies your credence in A should equal your expectation of the chance of A, so (M) looks like a corollary rather than a rival. My answer is that as a corollary (M) is derived from a conditional norm plus an exhaustiveness assumption, whereas as a starting point its stronger, because it constrains the credence function even when no single chance hypothesis is known admissibly. If that's right, the reformulated principle isn't a competitor to Lewis's — it's what Lewis's principle was really about all along.
