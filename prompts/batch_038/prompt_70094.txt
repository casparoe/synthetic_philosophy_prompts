I set out to settle Molyneux's problem for a reading group and ended up with an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. Take the original case: a man blind from birth, able to tell a globe from a cube by touch, has his sight restored, and the two objects are set on the table in front of him.

(1) For him to pick out the globe by sight alone, the shape he now sees would have to be the very shape he has always called spherical: cross-modal identification presupposes one shape concept accessible to both senses.
(2) The reported cases say he cannot do it. From Cheselden's eighteenth-century patient onward, people who gain sight late can look at an object and describe what is before them, yet cannot say which of two visible things is the globe until they have handled them — after which, and remarkably quickly, they can.
(3) So the shape he knew by touch is not the shape he now sees. His vision carries shape information, but not in a form his existing concepts can take up.
(4) What holds of him holds of all of us; the rest of us merely lack the chance to notice. There is therefore no one concept of shape, only a family of modality-specific concepts that share a name, with nothing amodal in the neighborhood at all.
(5) But then the blind geometer is impossible in principle. She could have no concept of the sphere, only of the tactile-sphere, and a theorem stated without reference to any sense modality could not be so much as entertained by her.
(6) Blind people demonstrably do geometry, and their results agree with ours. So (4) is false, there is an amodal concept of shape, and Molyneux's man ought to be able to see the globe.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (2), the only empirical premise I have, and I suspect the trouble is in (3) or (4), but I cannot tell whether the slide is in the reasoning or in the word concept. I also suspect (6) is evasive: the blind geometer may be working with tactile diagrams, with algebraic and verbal surrogates, or with other people's descriptions, and none of these obviously gives her the concept of the sphere rather than a reliable way of getting correct answers about something she never entertains. Please mark off what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed reading of (2) or of (6) as if it were the consensus.
