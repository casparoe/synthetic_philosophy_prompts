I am an undergraduate in a seminar on decision theory and the formal foundations of risk, and my term paper is supposed to reconstruct a line of reasoning that most of the class seems to accept and that I have not been able to make peace with. I would like a diagnosis of it rather than a summary of the debate around it.

The running example in seminar is a repeated coin toss. Each round you stake a fixed fraction of your current wealth. Stake the whole bankroll and you gain fifty percent on heads and lose forty percent on tails. The expected wealth multiplier is 1.05 per round, so the expectation of your fortune grows without bound, but the geometric multiplier is the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, about 0.949, so the median path, and indeed almost every individual path, decays toward zero. The Kelly-optimal stake here is a quarter of the bankroll, and it buys a growth rate of barely six-tenths of one percent per round. Everyone in the class agrees that staking everything on a positive-expected-value gamble is a catastrophic mistake. The argument I want checked starts from that agreement and ends somewhere I did not expect.

(1) A rational agent facing a gamble that will be repeated should adopt the policy that maximizes the time-average growth rate of her wealth, because that is what she will actually live through; an expectation taken across an ensemble of outcomes is an average over copies of her that do not exist.

(2) Maximizing the time-average growth rate of wealth is identical to maximizing the expectation of the logarithm of wealth.

(3) So a rational agent should maximize expected log wealth, and this was established without consulting her preferences at any point.

(4) The prescription in (3) is therefore a piece of arithmetic about her situation rather than a representation of her tastes. An agent who prefers a different policy — who stakes everything on the gamble above, or who turns down a mildly favorable Kelly-sized bet — is not expressing an unusual attitude toward risk. She is wrong about a calculation.

(5) The same reasoning governs any situation in which outcomes compound multiplicatively and losses cannot be undone by borrowing, whether the sequence is long, short, or in principle unrepeatable: a one-shot gamble is just the first round of a process the agent is inside of, and careers, research programs, and reputations compound the way capital does.

(6) Hence there is exactly one rational risk attitude; disagreements over stakes are never disagreements in taste; and whether a given bet is rational is fixed by the entire multiplicative situation it sits in.

Stated that way, the conclusion seems to dissolve part of my subject. If (4) holds, work on how people actually weigh risks is a branch of applied mathematics with one correct answer and a great deal of error to catalog. If (5) holds, then whether I should commit to one paper topic rather than another turns out to depend on gambles I will never face. I also cannot shake the sense that the whole thing is hostage to how I describe my situation: when I redo the calculation treating my expected future earnings as part of my wealth, the prescription changes, and nothing in (1) through (6) tells me which description is the correct one. I do not know whether that is a real objection or just the observation that two different situations receive two different answers.

What I would like from you:

- Say whether the inference from (1) to (6) is valid. If it is, identify the premise that is least costly to surrender. If it is not, name the step at which it fails and state what the conclusion would have to be weakened to in order to follow from the premises.
- For each of (1), (2), and (5), give the conditions under which the premise is exactly true rather than approximately true, and say whether the coin-toss case meets them. I specifically want to know whether (2) is a theorem or a rule of thumb, and what would have to change about the example for it to stop being one.
- Explain why the premise you reject looked acceptable, and what has to be true of the example for it to look that way.
- Price the three retreats I can see. What is lost if I keep (1) and (2) and give up (4)? What is lost if I give up (2) and treat the logarithmic prescription as one utility function among many, thereby conceding the very thing the argument was supposed to avoid? What is lost if I give up (1) and accept that maximizing expected value is rational even when it reliably ruins the agent who does it?
- Tell me which retreat you would take, and whether the anti-ruin intuition that motivated the argument can be preserved without accepting anything in (4) through (6).

One thing you should know about my priors: (1) is what made the argument persuasive to me in the first place. The thought that a real agent lives through a single path rather than an average over many is what I found compelling, and I would rather pay somewhere else than pay there. Please tell me if that is precisely the wrong instinct.
