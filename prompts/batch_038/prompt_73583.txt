For five years I have sat as a panel member hearing appeals against sanctions imposed on claimants of a means-tested unemployment benefit with an activity test. I have kept a record of my votes, and I now want to know what principle they add up to, whether that principle is defensible, and which of my verdicts I should surrender. What follows is my own summary rather than a transcript; the sums and hours are illustrative, but the patterns are ones I recognise.

1. A claimant declines a warehouse job because the required shift pattern would cost more in childcare than the job pays. Sanction wrong.
2. A claimant with the same earnings profile declines a job identical in pay, hours and content to one they held for eight years and left voluntarily on good terms; the stated reason is that the work would be beneath them. Sanction right.
3. A claimant declines a job assembling components for a weapons system they regard as immoral; the objection is sincere, long-standing, and would apply to any employer. Sanction wrong. Yet I would sanction a claimant who declines work at a betting shop or a payday lender on the same sort of stated ground. I cannot say why these two feel different to me.
4. A claimant fails to declare cash earnings of about £80 a week from babysitting for a neighbour, over four months. Fraud; sanction right; the overpayment recoverable. But I hold this less blameworthy than case 5.
5. A claimant makes a false statement about savings on the initial claim form, declaring £9,000 where the figure was £23,000. I hold this worse than case 4, though the sums are comparable and case 4 involved repeated concealment while case 5 involved a single utterance. I concede the ordering looks arbitrary.
6. A claimant volunteers three days a week at a hospice for six months and turns down two interviews in that period. Sanction wrong: the activity requirement should be treated as met.
7. A claimant spends comparable hours over a comparable period writing a novel and turns down the same two interviews; nobody outside the household can attest to the hours. Sanction right. What troubles me is that I would give the same answer even if I were persuaded the novel was excellent and the hours exactly as described.
8. A claimant with a fluctuating and largely invisible condition fails a points-based capability assessment calibrated for stable impairments, and is sanctioned for non-attendance. Sanction wrong; and yet I also think a properly designed assessment would find them capable of some work and would then sanction them for refusing it. I am not sure both beliefs can stand.
9. I think it legitimate to require a specified number of hours of unpaid work placement, and not legitimate to require thirty-five documented job applications a week, even though the second requirement is cheaper to administer and far easier to verify.
10. A claimant with thirty years of contributions and one with none should, I think, receive different amounts for different durations; but I do not think that difference should affect the strictness of the activity test.
11. An in-work claimant on a zero-hours contract turns down offered additional shifts because accepting them would forfeit their childcare place. Sanction wrong. I also think it objectionable that public funds top up wages of that kind, since the top-up lets employers pay less than they otherwise would. In case 1 I excused a refusal precisely because the wage did not cover the childcare the job required, so something here has to give.

My working hypothesis is that what I am really tracking is legibility: whether a claimant's activity leaves a trace a stranger could verify — a placement supervisor's signature, a payslip, an employer's letter. Cases 6 and 7, and the second half of case 4, point that way. Cases 3 and 9 point against it, since in case 3 sincerity rather than documentation seems decisive, and in case 9 the more legible requirement is the one I reject.

What I would like from you:

(a) State one or two candidate principles precisely enough to be applied by someone who has never met me, and tie each clause to the case or cases that motivate it. If legibility appears as a clause, say whether it does independent work or acts as a proxy for something else — evidence of sincerity, say, or a way of assigning the risk of self-deception.

(b) Identify which of my verdicts no single principle can accommodate. Where the choice is between a complex principle that saves all of them and a simpler one that overturns one or two, say which you would take and what is lost by taking it. I would rather abandon a verdict I care about than end up with a rule that is really eleven rules wearing a trench coat.

(c) Apply your preferred principle to four cases I have not listed: a claimant who refuses any work requiring them to labour on their sabbath, where the employer would accommodate but only at reduced hours; a claimant in a former single-industry town who declines a job ninety minutes away by public transport in a different occupation for which they are qualified; a claimant who accepts work at sixty per cent of prior earnings in the same occupation and leaves it voluntarily within four months; and a claimant sanctioned for missing a mandatory appointment because they were attending an interview they had arranged themselves and told nobody about.

(d) Several of my verdicts appear to turn on the duration and severity of the sanction rather than on the requirement itself. State what your principle implies about how long a sanction should last and how much income it should remove, and whether duration can do normative work that the underlying requirement cannot.

(e) Finally, tell me whether my principle genuinely dispenses with the distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor, or merely re-encodes it under names such as reciprocity, contribution, and legibility. And tell me whether what I have reconstructed amounts to a version of the old less-eligibility idea — that the conditions of relief ought to be made less attractive than the worst independent wage labour available. If it does, point to the verdicts in which that idea is already doing the work.
