I am a novelist at work on a book whose pivot is a scheduled execution, and for research I have been reading Beccaria's mid-eighteenth-century essay on crimes and punishments, specifically the chapter arguing against capital punishment. What follows is my own close paraphrase of that chapter's argument rather than a quotation, since I do not have the translation's exact wording before me; the sequence of moves, though, I am confident I have kept.

Beccaria begins from the origin of the sovereign's right to punish: men in the state of nature surrendered, for the sake of tranquillity, the smallest possible portions of their liberty, and the aggregate of these minute portions constitutes the right to punish. Since what each deposited was only the least necessary slice of his freedom, no one can be taken to have deposited a right over his own existence; a man cannot grant what he has no power to dispose of. Capital punishment therefore is not a right at all, but the war of a nation with one of its members, justified only insofar as his death is genuinely necessary to the security of the whole — as when, even imprisoned, he retains connections and influence sufficient to foment a revolution, or when the nation is losing or recovering its liberty and the disorder of the moment makes ordinary procedure impossible. Otherwise, lifelong hard labour serves better, because what deters is not the intensity of a punishment but its extension in time: a long, repeated impression on spectators outweighs a single spectacular death, which is over in an instant and stirs pity rather than fear. Finally, he observes that the laws, which abhor homicide, themselves commit one in public view, and that a society which stages executions sets its citizens an example of atrocity.

I would like help understanding how these moves hold together, because they seem to belong to three different kinds of argument and I cannot tell which one Beccaria treats as load-bearing.

First, what is the logical force of the deposit claim? Is the objection that no one in fact consented to the sovereign's power to kill him, or that such consent would be void even if given — and if void, what voids it: the impossibility of alienating one's own life, or the disproportion between a small portion of liberty and the whole of one's existence?

Second, does the exception consume the rule? If the state may kill whenever its survival requires it, is the contractarian argument doing independent work, or has it collapsed into a self-defence claim a Hobbesian would sign? And what does describing execution as war rather than punishment imply about the offender's standing — has he ceased to be a member, and can that be squared with the story about deposited portions?

Third, are the deterrence claim and the atrocity claim allies or enemies? The second seems to assume spectacles communicate something forcefully, which is just what the first denies. If the empirical prediction about duration over intensity failed, would the atrocity argument still carry abolition — and is it an empirical claim about imitation, a claim about the state contradicting itself, or both?
