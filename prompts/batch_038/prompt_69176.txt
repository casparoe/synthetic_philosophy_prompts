I am in the third year of a doctoral program in moral philosophy, working on intention and the ethics of war, and my reading group is spending this term on Anscombe: *Intention*, her essay "War and Murder" from the late 1950s, and the 1975 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to oppose Oxford University's conferral of an honorary degree on Truman. I have been asked to present on the last of these, and rather than walk through the pamphlet's rhetoric I want to build the talk around a single episode, because I suspect the episode tests her argument in a way the secondary literature has not really pressed. The episode is the second bombing, on 9 August 1945, and specifically its administrative shape. As I understand the facts — please correct me wherever I am wrong, since I cannot check any of this against archives — the written directive issued at the end of July authorized not merely the first special bomb but additional bombs against the same short list of cities as soon as they were ready, so no fresh decision by Truman was needed for Nagasaki; the mission was moved forward by several days because of a forecast of bad weather; the primary target that morning was Kokura, obscured by cloud and by smoke from a conventional attack on nearby steel works the day before, and after several aborted runs the aircraft went on to the secondary target, Nagasaki; Nagasaki was clouded over as well, but the bombardier reported a break, the weapon was released, and it landed far from its aiming point, in the valley north-west of the centre, where the cathedral and the densest residential quarters were; and on 10 August, the day after, Truman ordered that no further atomic bombs be dropped without his express authorization, reportedly because the prospect of another hundred thousand casualties appalled him, with a third weapon being assembled at the time. Two further details bear on what I want to ask. The cities on the target list had been deliberately spared conventional firebombing so that the new weapon's effect could be measured against a largely intact urban fabric, and Truman publicly described Hiroshima as a military base.

Anscombe's charge, as I read it, is not the consequentialist's charge that the bombings were imprudent or disproportionate; it is the sharper one that the killing of the innocent was here chosen as a means, that the terror of the population belonged to what was being aimed at, and that the description "military target" under which Truman and his defenders present the act is not the description under which it was done — hence murder, and hence the real point of the pamphlet, which is not that Truman should have been prosecuted but that a university's public honour is itself a form of moral endorsement and that Oxford was proposing to extend it to a man who had done this. So: take the Nagasaki raid and run it through that framework, and tell me what happens. I want the mapping done carefully rather than impressionistically. Who is the agent of the second bombing on her account — Truman, who authorized a class of future drops a fortnight earlier and was not consulted again; the officers who drafted and executed the standing directive; or the aircrew, who had no authority to choose otherwise and whose only discretion that morning concerned which of two cities would burn? What does her notion of intention do to a choice made in the abstract about cities named only on a list, where which city actually burns is settled by cloud over the primary target and by a fault in one aircraft's fuel system? Is a deferred, pre-authorized intention to kill the innocent easier or harder to locate than one formed at the moment of acting, and does her framework have the resources to say — or does it silently presuppose an agent who deliberates and then acts, which nobody in this chain of command did? Then the two features that strike me as most awkward for her. If Kokura had been clear, some hundred thousand people in Kokura would be dead instead, and not one element of anyone's practical reasoning would have differed, so on her view the substitution ought to be morally invisible: is that a vindication of her insistence that intention carries the moral weight, or does it show that her account cannot register what looks like the most salient moral fact about the raid, namely that who died was a matter of weather? And the order of 10 August reads as an intention to refrain from a third drop formed after the intention that produced the second was already spent: does that hand her a revocation of the very intention she indicts, or does it confirm the indictment by showing that the intention was all along the kind of thing one could simply have declined to form at the end of July?

Give me a committed analysis rather than a survey. Take a definite position on whether the Nagasaki raid confirms Anscombe's charge, strains it, or falls into a gap she never considered, say plainly which of the three, and give your reasons. Where her framework genuinely has nothing to say — about the aircrew, about the weather, about the diffusion of authority along a chain of command — say that it has nothing to say instead of recruiting some neighbouring doctrine to speak on her behalf; and if you think the honest verdict is that she is right about Hiroshima and structurally unable to handle Nagasaki, argue for that rather than hedging between readings. Close with a short paragraph on the reflexive question the pamphlet itself raises: whether the material above strengthens or weakens her claim that the real wrong in 1975 lay in Oxford's proposed act of praise rather than in anything a court could have charged. Finally, since I will be fielding questions from people who know this history far better than I do, flag any of the facts I have supplied that you believe to be contested or mistaken, and tell me how much of your analysis would survive their correction.
