For two years I've sat as the lay member on a regional hospital system's ethics committee, and the committee keeps arriving at the same impasse. Someone proposes a payment or a market — a compensated donor program, a fee for expedited processing, a stipend tied to a patient's behavior — and the room splits into one half that says it is exploitative and another that says it is a voluntary exchange between adults who both gain. Neither half can say what the disagreement is actually about, and the tie-breaking votes end up tracking temperament rather than reasoning. So I have been doing something I find more useful than reading position papers: writing down my own verdicts on a long list of concrete cases, in the hope that the pattern will show me what I am committed to. I'd like you to examine the pattern and tell me what principle, if any, generates it.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A stranger pays a healthy thirty-year-old $60,000 for one kidney under a regulated scheme with independent advocacy, full risk disclosure, and a cooling-off period. Wrong. Not monstrous, and I wouldn't imprison anyone over it, but wrong — and I wouldn't want to be the one defending the scheme.
2. The same stranger pays $60,000 into the estate of a person who contracted in advance to donate a kidney after death, so that the money goes to the donor's children. Fine. This surprises me: the organ is the same, the recipient is the same, and the donor's body is if anything less invaded.
3. A charity that works with people in recovery pays a woman $5,000 to be sterilized. Clearly wrong.
4. The same charity pays a man $5,000 to use a long-acting reversible contraceptive for three years, the payments stopping if he stops. Fine. I can feel that the difference is reversibility, but then compare case 5.
5. A mining company pays a twenty-five-year-old a wage premium that everyone concedes compensates him for a modest additional lifetime risk of death underground. Fine — ordinary labor, and I'd be embarrassed to call it repugnant. Cases 1 and 5 look to me like the same irreversible risk priced the same way, and I don't have the same verdict on them.
6. A monthly stipend to low-income parents conditional on their children's school attendance and vaccinations. Fine, and I'd advocate for it.
7. Two dollars to a third-grader for every book she finishes. Fine, though I suspect it ruins reading for some of them; that suspicion feels like a parenting worry rather than a justice objection.
8. Paying a college student $50 to hold your place in line for a passport appointment. Wrong — and notably worse, in my reactions, than paying $50 for a stranger's place in a queue for concert tickets, which I find harmless.
9. Surge pricing on hotel rooms in a city during a hurricane: wrong. Surge pricing on rides during an ordinary rainstorm: fine. Surge pricing on bottled water at a gas station two days after the hurricane, when the mains are contaminated: very wrong — and my verdict here is more emphatic than the hotel verdict even though far less money is at stake.
10. Selling whole blood: fine, I've done it. Selling plasma twice a week to a firm that exports the fractions: fine, though distasteful. Selling bone marrow: wrong. I know this is not defensible on the biology. Most marrow donations today are peripheral stem-cell collections — a few days of injections, then blood drawn from one arm, spun, and returned through the other, closer to giving plasma than to surgery, and repeatable. I have been told this by people who know it, and I still cannot say that marrow sales are fine. I am holding the verdict anyway, and I want to be told whether I should.
11. A fund buys the rights to an off-patent drug and raises the price fiftyfold. Wrong, and I want to say wrong in the market way, not merely in the ordinary greedy way. A collector buys a Stradivarius at auction, never plays it, and locks it in a vault: fine. A developer buys a run-down building, clears out the tenants, and flips it: fine, or at least not condemned by anything about markets specifically.
12. Paying a witness $1,000 to testify truthfully. Wrong, and I can't explain why — the testimony is identical to the testimony he already owes.
13. Selling your vote: wrong. Paying someone to abstain: also wrong, which astonishes me, since an abstention cannot distort the tally the way a purchased vote does.
14. Selling a lock of your dead child's hair to a museum that will display it alongside the family's account: fine. Selling the same lock to a private collector who wants it because the child was famous: wrong. It is the same hair and the same seller.
15. Two kidneys, two motives. A man sells a kidney to pay for his daughter's surgery: permissible, and I'd call it admirable. A man sells a kidney to clear debts he ran up gambling: impermissible. The price, the buyer, the surgical risk, and the degree of financial pressure are stipulated identical; only the destination of the money differs. I also think that selling a kidney to buy a boat is permissible but tacky, which I recognize does not sit well with the gambling verdict.

My working hypothesis, which I have been trying and failing to make work, is that a market is repugnant when the good in question is one whose value depends on being allocated by something other than willingness to pay — need, desert, membership, or luck. Three problems. It verges on circularity: I identify the goods whose value depends on non-market allocation by consulting my own reactions, then explain those reactions by the fact. It gives identical treatment to the passport queue and the concert queue, which my verdicts separate. And it cannot see anything wrong in case 15, since the organ is allocated by willingness to pay in both variants. It also seems to condemn the attendance stipend, which I support without reservation.

So: what principle, or what small set of candidate principles, best fits this list? For each candidate I would like a statement precise enough that a colleague could apply it to a case I never described and we would reach the same verdict; an account of which of my cases motivates each of its clauses, so that I can see what work every clause is doing; its verdicts on four cases I have left out — an auction of immigration slots to the highest bidder, commercial surrogacy run through a licensed agency with independent counsel for the carrier, a market in positions on a transplant waiting list in which the recipient pays to move up and no organ is bought, and a firm that pays low-income people for continuous access to their medical and genetic data; and an honest reckoning of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, rather than a principle with enough free parameters to swallow all fifteen. Then the thing I actually care about: can both of my stubborn pairs be saved by one account — marrow against plasma in case 10, and the two kidneys in case 15 — or must a serious theorist tell me that one of them is simply a mistake? If I have to give one up, tell me which, and tell me what it costs. I suspect that surrendering the marrow case makes the rest of my list look as though it was never tracking anything at all, and I want to know whether that suspicion is justified or merely protective.
