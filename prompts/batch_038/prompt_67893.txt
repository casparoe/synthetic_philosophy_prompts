I'm a novelist and I'm on the third draft of a book with a subplot about a woman who runs a small private foundation and has to decide whether to put real money into insect welfare — specifically into changing how a certain row crop gets harvested, because the machinery grinds up an enormous number of field insects every season. Two of my readers reacted to the same monologue in opposite directions and I cant tell which of them is right.

My smartest reader, a guy who does political philosophy, wrote in the margin: "this is just Pascal's wager with legs. A tiny probability attached to a big enough payoff, multiplied until it swamps everything else. If that move worked, she'd also have to fund precautions against a teapot orbiting between here and Mars. It's a mugging, and she's the mark." My other reader, who reads a lot about animal minds, says that's a cheap shot — that the analogy is doing something sneaky and that the scale arguement (astronomical numbers of individuals × a low but not crazy probability that each one matters morally) survives it fine.

So: act as a professional philosopher who works across animal minds and decision theory, and audit the analogy for me. Think carefully, and before you commit to anything, brainstorm out loud about the candidate mappings — what in my scene is supposed to play the wager, what plays the infinite payoff, what plays the hidden deity, what plays the stranger who corners you with the bet — and then give me a correspondence table, one column for what lines up and one for what doesn't.

Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually matter for how the analogy is being used (namely, to tell me the monologue is empty) and the ones that are just decoration. Two I especially want pressed on: first, whether the probability in my character's situation is the kind of thing that can be *offered* to her by someone who profits if she pays up, or whether that feature of the mugging case is doing most of the work; second, whether multiplying by headcount is even legitimate when the probability that one insect is sentient presumably isn't independent of the probability that the next one is — if it's effectively all-or-nothing at the level of the whole taxon, does the multiplication collapse, and if so does that rescue the monologue or bury it?

My second reader also floated a rival image, that we're like someone about to pour a foundation slab over ground nobody has checked for people in, which is meant to make caution look obviously correct. Is that one any better, or is it smuggling in its own assumptions about how confident you're entitled to be that the ground is empty?

End with a verdict I can actually use: does the monologue stay as written, get rewritten, or get cut — and does the dismissive conclusion (that expected-value reasoning under these conditions can be made to license anything, so it licenses nothing) hold up once the disanalogies are on the table? If there's a better analogy out there, give it to me in a form a novelist could put in a character's mouth without it sounding like a lecture.

One last thing, and it matters to me: keep a clear line between what's settled and what's genuinely disputed among people who work on this. I know insect sentience isn't a solved question, and I don't want you writing as though the experts have converged when they haven't. Wherever you're taking a side in a live argument rather than reporting a shared view, say so.
