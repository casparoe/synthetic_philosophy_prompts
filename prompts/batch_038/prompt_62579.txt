A reading group I attend spent an evening on Kavka's 1983 toxin puzzle, and two members ended the session in a genuine stalemate. Here is the setup as we worked with it, then their exchange move by move; I would like you to referee it.

**Setup.** An eccentric billionaire shows you a vial of toxin that would make you painfully ill for a day, with no threat to your life and no lasting effects. He will pay you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. He emphasizes that you need not drink it in order to be paid: the money will be in your account hours before the drinking time arrives, and the presence or absence of the intention at midnight is settled by a brain scanner of his devising. You are perfectly free to change your mind once the money has landed.

**The exchange.**

1. *Nora:* This is not a puzzle about rationality but a piece of conceptual sleight of hand. You cannot intend to do what you believe you will not do; after midnight you will correctly believe there is no reason to drink; so the required intention is simply unavailable. Nothing irrational occurs, because there is nothing to be done.

2. *Dev:* That is a diagnosis, not a rebuttal, and it concedes what makes the case interesting. If the intention is impossible, some step in the argument that it is possible and rational must be faulty — the payoff for intending is enormous, the cost of drinking is trivial beside it, and reasons for acting appear to transmit to intentions to act. Naming the conclusion impossible without locating the bad step is precisely what the puzzle dares you to do.

3. *Nora:* The bad step is the transmission principle. A reason to intend is not thereby a reason to drink, and an intention is assessable only against what the agent expects to have reason to do when the time comes. Since the million is secured by the intention alone, at the drinking hour there is nothing to gain and something to lose, so failing to intend is no failure of rationality.

4. *Dev:* That principle is too strong to be believed, because it would make ordinary promising impossible. The point of a promise is to bind yourself against your later inclinations, and at the moment of performance keeping a costly promise frequently serves no independent interest of yours. If you may intend to do something only when you expect a reason to do it at the time of doing it, nobody could ever promise anything — a reductio of your principle, not a defense of it.

5. *Nora:* Promises are disanalogous in a principled way. Promising creates a new reason, one generated by the act of promising and standing at the time of performance. The toxin creates no such reason: at the drinking hour the money is already yours, and the bare fact that you once intended to be ill is not a reason to be ill. So transmission can be restricted to cases where the agent expects a reason at the time of action, and promising survives the restriction intact.

6. *Dev:* Then test your restriction. Suppose the same billionaire runs the deal a hundred times over a year — a fresh vial each month, payment conditional each time on the midnight scan, drinking scheduled for the following afternoon. Drinking in an early round now has forward-looking value: an agent who knows herself as someone who follows through on her toxin-intentions is better placed to intend again next month, and each such intention is worth a million. On your own account she has a reason to drink, so your disanalogy with promising is a matter of degree rather than of kind — and once it is a matter of degree, the puzzle you dismissed as merely conceptual returns as a real problem about how dispositions and their token instances relate.

7. *Nora:* That variant proves my point, not yours. Modify the case so that drinking serves the agent's later interests and the puzzle evaporates — which is only to say that the puzzle depended on there being no reason at the time of action. You have shown the toxin case is fragile under modification, not that the unmodified case admits of an intention. And your repeat version smuggles in an empirical premise: that one's future capacity to intend depends on one's past follow-through. That is a claim about psychology, not about the logic of intention.

**What I want from you.**

(a) Rate each of the seven moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating. Judge the moves as actually made, not as they might have been better made.

(b) Identify the single step on which the whole dispute turns, and say which side, if either, is entitled to it.

(c) Dev's move 6 introduces a variant that I do not think is standard in the discussions I have read. Assess it on its merits: does repeat play make the intention available, or does Nora's reply — that it changes the subject and that it assumes a psychological claim about follow-through — dispose of it?

(d) Say what both of them are missing. In particular, neither engages the response that treats the formed intention as a commitment constraining later deliberation rather than as a prediction about it. Tell me whether that line, developed properly, helps Dev or Nora, and why.

(e) Give an overall verdict: who is more right, and by roughly how much — is one of them close to winning outright, or are they each half right about different questions?

Begin your answer with the overall verdict in your first sentence — a side and a rough margin, no throat-clearing — and then work through (a) to (e). No preamble, no restating of the setup or of the question.
