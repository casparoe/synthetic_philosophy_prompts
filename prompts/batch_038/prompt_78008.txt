I am assembling the central lecture of a graduate seminar on the philosophy of privacy, and I want to build it around Hitchcock's Rear Window (1954), the film after Cornell Woolrich's short story, in which the wheelchair-bound photojournalist Jeffries spends a heat wave watching his neighbors across a shared Greenwich Village courtyard and becomes convinced that the jewelry salesman Thorwald has killed his wife. The plot is common knowledge, so I am not concerned about spoilers; what I need from you is criticism, not summary, and you should describe scenes rather than reproduce dialogue.

My own working position is roughly a contextual-norm account. On it, a privacy wrong is a wrong in the flow of information, and the norms that fix what counts as an appropriate flow are internal to the social context: what a neighbor may observe across a courtyard is governed by different norms than what a lover may demand of a partner. Two consequences I hold to, and which I want tested against the film, are these. First, there is no residual wrong once the contextual norms are satisfied; privacy is not a freestanding interest that survives the norms. Second, looking that generates no record, transmits nothing to any third party, and never causally touches the person looked at — call it observation without uptake — is either not a privacy wrong at all or a marginal one, since nothing about the subject's informational situation has actually changed.

Tell me where, and how severely, the film presses on that position. Jeffries' looking for most of the picture appears to be observation without uptake in precisely this sense: no shades are drawn, nobody objects, and nothing he learns leaves his apartment until Lisa and Stella cross the courtyard themselves. Does the film present that looking as a wrong, and does it do so by dramatizing a loss to the neighbors, or only by making us uneasy on our own account? I am especially interested in the point at which Thorwald looks back — first into Jeffries' darkened apartment across the courtyard, and then, as he crosses to it, outward toward us. Two-party accounts should say what changes when the gaze is returned, and I suspect the film is trading on something here that my account cannot capture: that the wrong tracks an asymmetry of position rather than any change in what information exists. Say plainly whether the reversal is doing real philosophical work or is only suspense.

Then consider what the film's structure of reward does to a position with any deontological color at all. The voyeurism solves a murder, retrieves the evidence Lisa displays from Thorwald's own apartment, and, by a piece of staging the film seems pleased with, saves Miss Lonelyhearts from the overdose she is contemplating while Jeffries and Stella are too busy watching Thorwald to intervene. Is that a consequentialist verdict the film actually endorses, or does the ending balance the ledger by breaking Jeffries' other leg and dropping him out of his own window? Relatedly, Thorwald's apartment is the film's clearest case of privacy shielding a killing: on my account the norms of the courtyard context are what they are regardless of what is concealed behind them, but I need to know whether the film treats Thorwald's seclusion as forfeited, as still protected, or as simply irrelevant to the question of whether Jeffries may look.

I also want the film's final gesture taken seriously as a claim about privacy and intimacy. Lisa, with Jeffries asleep beside her in his casts, picks up his adventure travel book to signal a shared life, and then, once she has confirmed he is not watching, puts it down for the fashion magazine she actually wants. Read that as an ending about the opacity persons retain from those closest to them: does the film endorse the view that intimacy requires such opacity, that a relationship between two people wholly transparent to one another would not be a relationship between two selves? Or is it merely a comic beat, with the philosophical reading laid on by me?

Throughout, be honest in both directions. Flag every place where I have described the film inaccurately, and every place where it simply is not attending to the distinctions my account needs — it never separates binoculars from telephoto lens from the naked eye, it never raises Jeffries' professional ethics as a photojournalist, and its treatment of Miss Torso may just share his gaze rather than indict it. Say whether the film is about privacy at all, or about looking, boredom, and marriage, with the privacy reading imposed from outside. And finish with a comparative judgment: among control or access accounts, the informational-norms account I hold, the reductionist account on which privacy dissolves into property and bodily rights, and the accounts that ground privacy in intimacy, which one does Rear Window best fit — and is there anything the film shows about the value of being unseen, or about the difference between being observed and being observed back, that argument alone does not deliver?
