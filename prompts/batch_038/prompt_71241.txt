I'm an infrastructure engineer, twelve years in, mostly build systems, deployment pipelines, and the incident-review machinery that surrounds them. I have no formal philosophy training. I read James on truth a few years ago, then went back to Peirce, and I've been arguing about it ever since with a friend who came out on the other side: he holds the Peircean line, on which truth is the opinion inquiry would converge on if it were carried far enough, and I have been defending the Jamesian line, on which a belief's truth is bound up with its proving itself good in the course of experience and with what it lets us actually do. We've agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I've been asked to produce the first draft of the joint statement. I don't trust myself to write that draft without tilting it, so I'd like you to write the document both of us could put our names to.

1. Agreed claims, numbered. Substantive claims that a committed Peircean and a committed Jamesian about truth would both sign exactly as written, with no footnotes and no silent reservations. I expect the list to include shared negative commitments (both reject some version of the picture on which truth is a static copying relation between a belief and a fact), a shared methodological commitment about how to settle disputes, and something about the tie between belief and conduct. If a candidate agreement is trivially true and empty, cut it — I'd rather have six claims that cost something than twenty comfortable ones. Anything one side could sign only with a qualification belongs in section 2 instead.

2. A disagreement table, with these columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; how the Peircean would state it, in wording he'd accept; how the Jamesian would state it, in wording I'd accept; and a classification of the difference as verdict-changing, real but only a matter of priority or emphasis, or verbal. Please cover at least: whether truth is a property a belief has at a time or a limit property of inquiry taken as a whole; whether a belief's having worked is what makes it true or merely how we come to see that it was true; whether one belief can be truer than another about the same subject matter; whether there are questions no possible inquiry could ever settle; and whether the two views ever deliver different verdicts about a particular belief.

3. Cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. For each, give the concrete case or argument both of us would agree bears on it, my prediction and his prediction written down in advance so neither of us can revise quietly later, and what each side would accept as counting against its own view. If both sides predict the same outcome on some crux, say so and mark the crux non-diagnostic. The real question I want answered is whether a year of argument has had any cash difference at all.

4. My proposed test cases, drawn from work. Use them, and tell me which are bad ones — cases that don't discriminate between the two views because both handle them identically. A written interface specification that no deployed version of the service has ever satisfied and none will, the shipped system violating the documented guarantee about three times a quarter. A postmortem that blamed an outage on a timeout in a downstream dependency, which better instrumentation two years later showed was a garbage-collection pause: was the original conclusion true when we acted on it? A test that passes 99.7 percent of runs, which we ship against anyway. A question about whether a given request was dropped by a machine that has since been decommissioned, with its logs deleted and no other artifact extant, so nothing obtainable could ever settle it. An algorithm with a published correctness proof that is unusable in production because of its cost. For each, tell me the verdict the Peircean gives, the verdict the Jamesian gives, and whether the two verdicts actually differ.

5. A short section stating how much of the dispute is merely verbal, with the test you used to reach that judgment, and then applying that test to the pragmatists' own quarrel. James opens his book with a seemingly silly dispute about a man chasing a squirrel around a tree and dissolves it by asking what practical difference each answer would make; I want that same move turned on us. If the result is that no case exists on which the two views differ in verdict, say so plainly and say what is left of the disagreement. I expect to lose this section, and I want to lose it explicitly rather than by omission.

6. I keep reaching for a translation into systems vocabulary: Peircean truth as the fixed point a consensus protocol converges to given enough rounds and no further faults, Jamesian truth as the value an anytime algorithm must return now, judged by the cost of being wrong. Assess whether that translation is neutral or quietly favors one of us, and state what each side would object to about the rendering of its own view.

Format: the numbered agreed claims first, then the disagreement table, then one headed subsection per crux, prose for the rest. No bibliography, no further reading, and nothing in quotation marks attributed to either philosopher — I have no way to check a quotation, and paraphrase does the job. Write the document itself rather than an essay about the document: something two people could read aloud in one sitting and then sign.
