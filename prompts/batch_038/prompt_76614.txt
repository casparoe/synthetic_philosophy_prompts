ok so I'm prepping a design problem for a grad seminar on temporal consciousness and I'd rather hand my students something to build than another summary of the debate. Can you construct the case for me?

The set-up I'm working from, correct me where I've got it sloppy: the colour phi thing. Two brief dots, different colours, different spots on the screen, a short gap between them. Subjects don't report dot-one then dot-two; they report a single thing gliding smoothly across and then abruptly swapping colour somewhere around the middle of the path — even though nothing coloured differently ever appeared at the midpoint. Two ways to tell that story: the Stalinesque one, where the visual system worked out the whole motion-plus-colour-swap package before any of it became available for report, so there was never an experience of a stationary first dot followed by an edit; and the Orwellian one, where there was such an experience and it got rewritten out of the record afterwards.

What I want you to build: a variant of that experiment in which the two accounts genuinely diverge in prediction, rather than offering different glosses on identical data. Constraints:

- one paragraph for the scenario itself, and it has to be runnable in a real lab this year — a couple of stimuli, masks if you want them, ordinary timing hardware. No fMRI, no neural decoding, no "measure the correlate" moves, because then the whole thing just becomes a neuroscience question.
- the discriminating response has to be a single forced choice, and it can't be a question about the order or the timing of the two dots themselves — both accounts already agree about what people say there, that's the whole problem. Make it about a third thing: another stimulus, a location, a feature, something that lets the two stories disagree about what the subject is in a position to answer.
- tell me the timing window the probe has to land in for the divergence to exist at all, and what happens to your case if you shrink the gap between the two dots toward simultaneity — does it survive, or does it collapse back into equivalence?
- say up front what would count as cheating. My worry is a design that smuggles the verdict in by assuming the second dot's onset is already the moment of content-fixation, which is exactly what's in dispute. If your case only works because you've assumed one side's answer about when experience gets settled, flag that yourself.

Then after the scenario: write out each account's explicit prediction for the forced choice, name which single feature of the design is doing the discriminating work, and remove it and tell me what's left. Most important for my seminar — give me the counterattack. A multiple-drafts person is going to say the probe is itself a fixation event so the divergence is fake, or that anything that differed got overwritten before it could be used. Steelman that as hard as you can, then say whether your design survives it, and if it doesn't, what modification would.

No bibliography, no citations, don't name any papers or people — I just want the design and the reasoning. And please don't short-circuit to "they're empirically equivalent, here's the general argument why" without actually trying to build something first; if you end up concluding equivalence anyway, I want to see the best candidate and exactly where it dies.
