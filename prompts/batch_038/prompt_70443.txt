The question as set:

> South Africa and Argentina both faced large-scale criminality by a prior regime, and both relied at some point on truth-seeking bodies rather than trials, yet the two amnesty arrangements are usually said to differ in kind and not merely in degree. In 500–700 words:
> (a) Distinguish a blanket amnesty from the conditional, individually granted amnesty administered by the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, identifying the statutory criteria an applicant had to satisfy and the scope of what amnesty extinguished.
> (b) Set out the strongest argument that a conditional truth-for-amnesty bargain can be defended on the ground of justice itself rather than conceded as a political compromise forced by a weak transition, and then state the most serious objection to that defence.
> (c) Say whether, on your account, a state may waive or extinguish the claims of victims of atrocity on their behalf, and justify your answer. Explain what your position implies about the constitutional challenge to the South African amnesty provisions.

The student response:

> (a) The South African commission was created by the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act of 1995 and chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, with its work divided among three committees: Human Rights Violations, Reparation and Rehabilitation, and Amnesty. The decisive structural feature is that amnesty was not declared in advance over a class of persons or offences. It had to be applied for, individual by individual, and the applicant had to make full disclosure of all relevant facts and show that the act was associated with a political objective, with a further check that the means used were proportionate to that objective. The commission's competence was limited to gross violations — killing, abduction, torture and severe ill-treatment — committed within the mandate period running from 1960 to 1994. Where amnesty was granted it covered criminal liability and civil liability alike, so that neither the state nor the victim's family could afterwards sue. A blanket amnesty, by contrast, is what an outgoing regime enacts for itself: it attaches to a described category of conduct without any inquiry, without disclosure, and without any assessment of the individual case, and it is therefore over before it begins. Argentina supplies the example. CONADEP, established by President Alfonsín at the end of 1983, was never an amnesty body at all; it was a commission of inquiry that could not compel testimony, and it reported through Nunca Más in 1984, referring what it had found to the courts, which produced the 1985 trial of the juntas. The Punto Final and Due Obedience laws that followed were blanket amnesties of the kind South Africa refused, and once they were struck down and annulled prosecutions resumed.
>
> (b) The defence does not rest on saying that reconciliation is nice. It rests on the observation that the goods a trial can produce were unobtainable in this case. Covert operations leave no paper trail and no surviving complainants, and the people who could describe them would say nothing under threat of imprisonment. Because amnesty could be bought only with disclosure, and because the disclosure had to be full and was tested against what the committee already knew, the commission acquired information that a prosecutorial strategy would never have surfaced — including information that identified victims whose remains would otherwise have gone unrecovered and whose families would have been left with nothing but rumour. That is a gain in justice, not merely in historical record, since the truth about the fate of the disappeared is itself something owed to them. The cost of the bargain was also real, and there is an asymmetry worth noting: amnesty was available only to those who had something to trade, so a perpetrator whose crimes were unverifiable could simply stay silent at no risk, which skewed the resulting account toward what could be independently corroborated anyway.
>
> Against this, the serious objection is that the bargain makes the victims pay for the transition. The state takes from them the criminal process that was their due and hands the benefit to the offender, and it does so precisely in those cases where the offence was gravest, since gravest offences are what the amnesty was designed to purchase. Even if more truth emerges, truth is not a substitute for accountability, and a scheme that lets the torturer walk free on condition that he describes the torture dignifies impunity with the language of repair.
>
> Because amnesty was granted only on full public disclosure, however, the applicant bore a genuine cost: he had to name himself and detail his conduct before victims and the press and forfeit the protection of secrecy. In that sense conditional amnesty preserved rather than abandoned retributive justice, since the confession functioned as the punishment and the proportionality test calibrated its severity to the offence. This also disposes of the objection from international law. The duty to prosecute bars blanket amnesties, which foreclose accountability wholesale; a conditional amnesty that exacts disclosure does not fall within that prohibition, so South Africa discharged its obligations without having to prosecute the amnestied.
>
> (c) A victim's claim against her tormentor is her own and cannot be surrendered by the state acting in her name, since the state is not the holder of that interest and has nothing to give away. The point is not merely theoretical. When the amnesty provisions were challenged as unconstitutional, the Constitutional Court upheld them, holding that the right to see the perpetrator punished belongs to the victim and cannot be waived on the victim's behalf — which is why the legislature was required to pair the amnesty power with the Reparation and Rehabilitation Committee. So the correct conclusion is that a state may not waive victims' claims, and the commission's design, with its restorative conception of justice and its pairing of amnesty with reparation, was the closest available approximation to respecting that constraint. It also explains why victim participation was built into the amnesty stage: no amnesty could issue unless the identified victims had been heard and had signalled a willingness to forgive.
