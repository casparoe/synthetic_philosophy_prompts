I'm putting together a session for our department's film-and-philosophy night and I got handed the consciousness-and-cinema slot. I want to screen Pixar's Inside Out and then talk about Dennett — the Cartesian theater, multiple drafts, all of it. Problem: half the room is going to say the movie is a perfect illustration of what Dennett was attacking, and the other half is going to say it's a homunculus cartoon with nice lighting, and both of those are boring. I want to hand them something with more teeth.

So — a reading of Inside Out through the multiple drafts model, and please get past the obvious move (Headquarters is a theater, the five emotions are little viewers at the levers, Pixar has drawn exactly the picture Dennett spent a book demolishing). I already have that one. What I want is the harder version:

- Keep the theater distinct from Cartesian materialism — the finish-line view, where order of arrival equals order of presentation — because I think the film actually raises a problem about the second one, not just the first.
- Take the recoloring gag seriously. Sadness touching an old happy memory and retroactively making it sad, Riley crying in front of the class on her first day, the hockey memory that flips from sad to happy when Joy watches it again down in the dump. Dennett's whole Orwellian-vs-Stalinesque point is that there's no fact of the matter about whether the revision happens before or after the experience, since both need a finish line to even be defined. But the film has a camera inside the head and shows us the orb changing color in storage. Does that settle a question Dennett thinks can't be settled? Is the movie committed to Orwellian revision, and if so what does that cost it?
- Then tell me what the ending looks like on this reading — the mixed happy-sad core memory, Joy handing the console over to Sadness, Anger's idea bulb stuck in there. If no draft ever gets a stamp of approval from a central experiencer, whose act is that handover, and does the emotional payoff need somebody to be home?

And be straight with me about where this framing breaks. I've got candidates — Bing Bong fading away in the Memory Dump, which never becomes a memory for Riley at all, and the fact that the emotions watch a screen but nobody watches them — but tell me if those arent the right pressure points, or if I'm just dressing the movie up in philosophy it isn't interested in. End by saying what the film actually adds: does it know something about consciousness that an argument wouldn't get across, or am I using it as a picture book?

No need to quote the film or the book and please no reading list — this is from memory and for a live discussion, so I need something I can argue with out loud.
