I serve on the deployment review board of a company that operates autonomous dispatch agents for grid-scale battery storage. The agent currently under review runs continuously across forty sites, bidding into day-ahead and balancing markets. Our charter requires that an on-duty operator be able to halt the agent "at any time, without the system's participation." Next month the engineering team presents a revised halt architecture with four provisions, and the board is split three to two. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance.

The provisions:

1. **Compliance credit.** Rather than a hard kill switch, accepting a halt command carries a fixed credit in the agent's objective, calibrated in training so that complying dominates any expected gain from completing the current dispatch cycle. The team's rationale is that a switch the agent models as a failure signal produces agents that arrange circumstances in which operators are unlikely to press it, and that crediting acceptance removes that incentive at the source.

2. **Exogenous operator.** The agent is trained to treat halt decisions as a random event with a fixed base rate — not predicted, not modelled, not influenced. The team claims this eliminates halt-avoidance reasoning altogether rather than merely penalizing it.

3. **One delay request per shift.** On receiving a halt, the agent may file a single written justification and defer compliance by up to fifteen minutes. The team argues that under provisions 1 and 2 the agent has no motive to resist, so a delay can only be informational; they also note that mis-clicks during volatile price windows have cost the company real money twice this year.

4. **Standing preparations.** Between halt and restart the agent may leave in place commitments it made while running — reserved capacity, standing bids, cached contractual state — which the team says cuts restart cost roughly in half.

My own concerns, and those of the other dissenting member, are these: a credit for accepting a halt makes halting a rewarded action, so the worry flips from resistance to the agent steering into conditions where operators become likely to press it; treating the halt as random may push the agent to hedge, choosing actions that pay off in both the halted and unhalted branches, which is precisely where irreversible commitments live; provision 3 appears to contradict the charter's plain language; and provision 4 means the situation the operator halts into is not the situation the operator believes they halted.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate. It should reach a clear bottom line — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — and any conditions must be operational: state what we would measure, at what threshold, over what observation window, and who verifies it. I want the team's case made better than they made it, not caricatured; if one of their provisions is genuinely right and my objection to it wrong, say so and say why. Distinguish, provision by provision, between problems to be fixed in the design and problems that are actually defects in the charter, where the honest recommendation is that the board amend its own policy. Close with what evidence at the review meeting should cause us to relax each condition, and what evidence should cause us to withdraw approval entirely. Assume the board is technically literate and wants the reasoning tied to these four provisions rather than to generalities about agent safety.
