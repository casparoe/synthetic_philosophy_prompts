I serve on the board of a community land trust in a river town of about four thousand people. We have eleven members and, this year, one consequential decision: whether to commit most of our reserves to raising the electrical room and the heating plant of a building we lease to three local nonprofits so that they sit above the level the water reached in the last bad year. Two of our members ended up arguing less about the project than about how one ought to reason when the odds are unknown, and the rest of us could not resolve it. I am asking you to resolve it.

A word about me first, so you can calibrate. I have no education past secondary school and no training in statistics or philosophy; I ran a machine shop for most of my working life. I have thought about this argument a great deal over the past two months, though, and I will report it turn by turn as nearly as I can, rather than summarize it. Please do not assume I have gotten the labels right, because I probably have not.

The two members are Del, who owns the hardware store, and Marguerite, who taught secondary-school mathematics for thirty years. The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Del said that when you genuinely do not know, you should not write down one number. Asked for the chance that water reaches the ground floor at least once in the next ten years, he declined to give a single figure and instead gave a pair: no lower than five in a hundred, no higher than forty in a hundred. He said that anyone who answers "fifteen percent" is manufacturing a precision that nobody possesses, and that a range is the honest form of the answer.

(2) Marguerite replied that this is indecision wearing a technical costume. We must vote, she said, and voting requires settling on something. If Del ends up behaving as though the answer were forty, then forty is what he believes, and the five is decoration that costs him nothing and does no work.

(3) Del answered that the pair does do work, but on the choice rather than on the number. His stated practice: when the consequence of being wrong can be undone later, plan around the encouraging end; when it cannot be undone, plan around the discouraging end. He said the range is what tells you which end applies, so it is doing precisely the work Marguerite denied it.

(4) Marguerite said that rule is unstable in both directions. Undoing-ness comes in degrees — a spent reserve fund is partly recoverable over a decade — so the rule smuggles back in the very judgment it claimed to eliminate. And a person who takes the discouraging end on everything irreversible will eventually refuse nearly everything, since almost no decision is wholly reversible.

(5) Del then turned to the new evidence. The county had released a survey showing that of eleven buildings it judged comparable to ours along this watershed, two had flooded since 2005. He said that if you hold a single number, this nudges it, and the nudge looks like knowledge. But if you push the same survey through both ends of his range, the low end moves to about eight in a hundred and the high end to about thirty-eight — barely narrowed at all. Marguerite said that was a demonstration that the method is worthless: the whole purpose of evidence is to shrink uncertainty, and a procedure that absorbs a survey and returns almost the same ignorance it started with has not learned anything.

(6) Del said it demonstrates the opposite. The county later acknowledged that its list of eleven had excluded three comparable buildings that had since been demolished, and that two of those three had flooded at least once. He said the high end therefore jumps to somewhere above fifty, while the low end stays roughly where it was, because if those three were left off for reasons having nothing to do with flooding, the survey tells us what it told us before. So his range widened after he learned more. He said that is the honest result: the new disclosure revealed a genuine ambiguity about what the original survey had been counting, and widening is the faithful record of that ambiguity. Marguerite said that any method under which a person becomes less certain the more they are told is self-refuting.

(7) Finally Marguerite said: name a price and take the other side. If Del will not accept any wager on the outcome, he cannot be checked, and an estimate that no fact could ever embarrass is not an estimate. Del said he would decline any wager priced inside his range as unfair to him and would gladly take either side of one priced outside it, and that a refusal to bet at bad odds is not the same as a refusal to have a view.

Here is what I would like from you. Take the seven turns one at a time and say, for each, whether it genuinely advances the position of the person making it, merely restates something already said, or fails — and where it fails, identify the failure precisely rather than by name alone. Where a move is partly sound and partly not, please separate the two. Then tell me which of them has the better of the argument overall and why, and identify what neither of them raised that a careful person should have. If there are established terms of art for the moves they were making without knowing it, you may supply them, but explain each in plain language rather than presuming I recognize it. Please write your answer in American English.
