I'm working on a piece about how PTSD got made and then kept remaking itself, and I want to run the whole arc past Hacking's looping effects instead of the usual "real discovery or political concession" fight, which I've read a hundred times.

Bare bones as I have them: the label came out of the seventies work with Vietnam veterans, their advocates pushed it into the DSM in 1980, and that first definition insisted the triggering event had to sit outside the range of what people normally go through. Every revision since has chewed on that opening criterion — partly because the epidemiology kept showing how common such events turn out to be, and partly because one edition added a clause making the survivor's own fear or helplessness at the time part of what counted, and a later one cut it again. Along the way the diagnosis became the gateway to VA benefits, courtroom testimony and insurance payouts.

So: who's classifying and who's classified here, and where does the feedback actually run — testimony, benefits, or survivors learning to narrate their own experience in the manual's vocabulary? What does looping explain about the drift in criterion A that a plain accumulating-evidence story can't? I keep wanting to seperate a loop from below, where the classified change the category, from a loop from above, where institutions change it by attaching money and legal standing to it, and I'm not sure its a distinction the theory can hold.

Then tell me where it goes quiet: do soldier's heart, railway spine and shell shock mean this was an indifferent kind all along, or just an earlier lap of the same loop? Does looping say anything about whether the category is true, and what would make you think a loop had stalled or run backwards?
