I'm an SRE — I do failover and disaster recovery for a living — and I've gotten unhealthily obsessed with the gradual-replacement uploading thought experiment, mostly because the failure modes map onto stuff I actually deal with all day (checkpoints, restores, split brain, recovery point objectives). I'm trying to work out what principle my intuitions add up to, and I've noticed that some of them can't all be right. Here's the list.

1. A clinic replaces 1% of my neurons a night with synthetic ones that do exactly the same job, over a hundred nights. I sleep through it, wake up each morning with my memories and my temper intact. Verdict: that's me at the end, no problem, I'd sign up.

2. Same clinic, same final wiring, same functional profile — but the swap happens in one pass while I'm under, in about ninety seconds. Verdict: not me. What wakes up is a copy with my memories. I know thats embarrassing given (1).

3. Same as (1), except the clinic doesn't discard the removed neurons. They keep them alive in a dish, wired back up in the same connectivity, and at the end the dish is running too. Verdict: the one walking around is me, the dish is a leftover. I feel firm about this one.

4. Now suppose that at the end of (3) they take the dish, grow it back into a body, put it under, wake it up, and shut the synthetic one down. Verdict: the biological one is me now?? I said yes out loud the first time I thought about it and I'm not sure I want to keep saying it.

5. Nightly full checkpoint, standard practice. On night 50 something corrupts and we restore from the night-20 checkpoint; the person who wakes up thinks it's day 20. Verdict: the night-50 person is dead. Not amnesiac — dead. I wouldn't say I lost a month, I'd say somebody was killed and I got rolled back.

6. Identical mechanism, but the corruption happens mid-procedure and we restore from a checkpoint taken ten minutes earlier, same night, and nobody ever notices. Verdict: completely fine, no different from not remembering the last ten minutes of a party. I'm aware (5) and (6) differ only in how much elapsed time and whether anyone can tell.

7. The synthetic modules in (1) are individually dumb — no interesting internal states, just the right input-output behavior, implemented however. Verdict: doesn't matter, still me. I don't think what-it's-like-to-be-a-module is doing any work here.

8. I already lose continuity every night in ways I don't care about (anesthesia, dreamless sleep), and that's fine. But I can't use that to dissolve (2), because from the outside (2) is continuous too.

The ones I keep flip-flopping on are (4) and the (5)/(6) pair. And there's a case I have no verdict on at all: the replacement run backwards — someone who is already an em gets gradually swapped over a year into biological neurons, ending up a functioning human brain. I want the principle to hand me a verdict there rather than me guessing.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that generates these? State it precisely enough that I could tell whether some new case falls under it, and say which clause is doing the work for which numbered case. Then tell me which of my verdicts have to go — I assume at least one does — and if the cheapest coherent principle kills (3) or (8), say so and say how bad the surviving view is to actually live with. Please don't hand me names of papers or a reading list, I'm not after a lit review, just the reasoning.

Format I'd like:

- **The pattern** — about 150 words, what my verdicts jointly commit me to, in plain language.
- **Candidate principles** — at most three, roughly 150 words each, every clause tied to the case number that motivates it.
- **What each one costs** — about 250 words total: which verdicts each overturns, and how unpalatable the survivor is.
- **The cases you have to rule on** — about 250 words: (4), the (5)/(6) pair, the em-going-biological case, plus one case of your own devising that you think is the real test of the principle you favor.
- **Confidence** — about 100 words: how confident you are in the principle you'd recommend, and the single claim in your whole answer you're least sure of and why.
