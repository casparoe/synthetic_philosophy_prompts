I am giving a twenty-five-minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on the moral status of non-human animals, hosted by an animal welfare science center, and I would like a preparation sheet for the question period.

The room, as best I can reconstruct it: five comparative cognition and animal welfare scientists who assess welfare behaviorally and physiologically — preference and avoidance testing, judgment-bias paradigms, stress endocrinology — and who are temperamentally hostile to any metric they cannot validate against an observable indicator; one invertebrate cognition researcher who works on cephalopods and insects and regards vertebrate-centrism as the field's original sin; two philosophers, one a subjectivist about welfare who thinks nothing matters but the valence of experience and one who holds that full moral status requires something like agency or being the subject of a life rather than mere sentience; and the workshop organizer, who advises a regulator and will judge every claim by whether it could be operationalized in a code of practice. Nobody in the room specializes in philosophy of mind.

My thesis, in the form I will actually present it:

1. What grounds moral weight is the capacity for affective experience that is integrated across modalities and extended in time — not nociceptive reactivity as such.
2. Total neuron count, and secondarily neuron count in the structures that plausibly subserve that integration, is the best proxy we currently have for that capacity: better than behavioral flexibility, better than phylogenetic distance from us, better than brain-to-body ratios of the encephalization-quotient variety.
3. My justification for (2) is intraspecies, not cross-species. It comes from within-species situations where we have independent evidence that the capacity is diminished — depth of anesthesia, lesion and ablation data, early developmental stage, neurodegeneration — and where neuron number and degree of integration covary with that diminution. The cross-species extension is an inference to the best explanation, not a measured correlation, and I say so on the slide.
4. I do not use the proxy to assign scalar moral weights. I use it to allocate the burden of proof: a high reading raises the prior that capacity should be presumed absent contrary evidence; a low reading lowers that prior without ever eliminating the precaution. I concede explicitly that the proxy is coarse, ordinal, and probably supports threshold claims rather than a linear scale.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

- The eight or nine objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument as structured above. Not the standard inventory of speciesism and marginal-cases boilerplate: objections that presuppose my four points and attack their particular joints, above all the intraspecies-to-cross-species move in (3) and the burden-of-proof reframing in (4).
- Each objection attributed to the audience member most likely to raise it and phrased in their vocabulary, the way they would put it across a table, including the measurement-theoretic language the welfare scientists would reach for.
- For each: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; a candid note on where that reply is softest; and a flag wherever the honest response is to concede rather than answer.
- A short list of concessions I should make in my opening three minutes, before anyone has to extract them from me.
- Which pairs of objections are in tension with each other, such that satisfying one audience member hands another their weapon. I expect this to be true of the welfare scientists and the invertebrate person in particular, and I want to know how to avoid being pulled apart by both.
- The single reply that has to land for the talk to be judged a success: the objection that, if I fumble it, the rest of the room stops listening.
- And the question I am hoping nobody asks. I have a guess about what it is, connected to the fact that my view is formally species-neutral while my presentation stays entirely within the non-human case. Tell me what you think the question is, whether my guess is the right one, and what the least damaging answer I could give in public would be.
