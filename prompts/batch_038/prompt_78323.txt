Three months ago I was seconded from clinical informatics onto the medical policy unit of a statutory health insurer, and my first assignment has turned out to be a philosophy problem in disguise. I have to draft the definitional clause that sits at the head of our coverage rules: the sentence that fixes what counts as a "disease" or "medical condition" for purposes of entitlement. The drafting is deadlocked. The actuarial and coding colleagues want a criterion that maps cleanly onto diagnosis codes and can be presented as value-free, because they fear appeals; the patient-advocacy and legal colleagues want a criterion keyed to harm and unmet need, because they observe that our own code list is already a sediment of political compromises and shifting thresholds. To break the deadlock I went back to the literature — Boorse-style biostatistical accounts, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, Nordenfelt's vital-goals account, and the normativist and social-constructionist critiques — and I have come away convinced that the literature will not settle our clause. Not because it is shallow, but because it has been stuck for roughly half a century with every argument on the table and nobody moving.

I want a diagnosis of that stuckness: not a survey of the positions, and not a verdict on which one is right.

First, what kind of disagreement is this? Is it verbal — are naturalists and normativists simply using "disease" for two different things, mistaking a stipulation for a discovery? If it is substantive, what is the substance: a factual claim about how biological functions are fixed and against what background environment, or a normative claim about which considerations may enter a definition at all? Do the two sides differ about which cases count as data — ordinary speakers' usage, clinical practice, patient testimony, the theorist's own reflective equilibrium — in such a way that they would fail to converge even given perfect information? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, for instance whether the extension of "disease" in competent use is actually tracked by biological facts rather than by institutional practice? Or is the question ill-posed, because "disease" is a cluster term for which necessary and sufficient conditions were never available?

Second, my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and entirely fair game for rejection. I suspect that "disease" is being asked to do at least two jobs at once: a classificatory job, sorting biological and psychological states, and an entitlement job, authorizing the mobilization of resources, exemption from ordinary obligation, and professional attention. On that picture each theory is optimized for one job and penalized on the other, and the disputes over extension are proxy fights about which job the word should be made to serve. A related suspicion: the signature refutations on each side are mirror images. The naturalist says that value-relative criteria let social deviance in — drapetomania, the psychiatric diagnosis of dissidents in the Soviet Union. The normativist says that value-free criteria let mere statistical abnormality in and screen out socially salient harm. If the arguments are structurally symmetric, does that symmetry undercut both sides equally, or does it indicate that they are answering different questions under one heading?

Third, test the diagnosis against the cases that actually generate heat in our committee, rather than the textbook ones. For each of the following, say what a naturalist criterion, a normativist criterion, and a hybrid criterion deliver, and whether the theories genuinely disagree about the case or merely describe the same outcome differently: (a) screening-detected, asymptomatic stage-one hypertension in a person who feels nothing and whose threshold was revised downward by a guideline committee; (b) a pathogenic BRCA1 variant in a thirty-year-old with no present functional deviation; (c) phenylketonuria in a person maintained from birth on a phenylalanine-restricted diet; (d) infertility in a twenty-eight-year-old woman as against infertility in a sixty-two-year-old woman; (e) prolonged grief disorder, where the duration threshold is itself contested; (f) male-pattern baldness, given reference classes relativized to age and sex; (g) drapetomania and its modern analogues, where I want to know whether the naturalist account really escapes the problem, since naturalists too need a social gate deciding what gets treated.

Fourth, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a study of usage or of clinician judgment, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Say which of these would actually move the two factions in my committee, and which would move only one of them.

Finally, give me your best defensible advice about the clause itself — what a definition drafted in full awareness of this impasse should say, and what it should refuse to say.

You do not need references; I have read the literature and citations are not what is missing. Please argue rather than summarize.
