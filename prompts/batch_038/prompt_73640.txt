I ended up going down a rabbit hole this week and I want to see if someone can help me pull it apart, because I think there's something real here but I can't name it.

Bit of context: I do the comms for workplace pension enrolment at a mid-sized firm in the UK, so I spend a lot of time reading the auto-enrolment rules. The way it works, the employer just puts you into a scheme and starts taking money out of your pay, and you have roughly a month to opt out — and if you opt out inside that window, your contributions get refunded and it's treated as if you were never in the scheme at all. Miss the window and the money is yours but locked up until retirement. Then separately, I tried to cancel a gym contract last month and got told the 14-day right to cancel only applied because I'd signed up online; if I'd signed the paper form at the front desk they'd have had no obligation to let me out. And then there's the organ donor register — England switched to an opt-out system a few years back, so if you've never registered any decision either way, you're deemed to have consented (with carve-outs for kids, people who haven't been resident for a year, and people who lacked capacity for a significant period before death), though in practice the family still gets asked.

Here's what's bugging me. In all three cases the thing actually doing the work seems to be the same human fact: people don't get round to things, forms sit in drawers, and whatever the starting position is tends to stick. But the law reads that same inertia as meaning opposite things depending on which scheme you're in. In the cooling-off case, if you don't send the cancellation in, your silence after the deadline apparently counts as a real endorsement of a purchase you made under time pressure or sitting at your kitchen table with a salesman — which is exactly the sort of context the cooling-off right exists because we don't trust. In pensions and organs, silence counts as no evidence of anything, and the state supplies the preference for you. And the refund rule in auto-enrolment is weirder still, because it seems to say the first month is when you're most likely to be able to see your own true preference clearly — which is precisely the month you're newest, busiest, and getting the least information about the scheme. So what theory of preference is all this presuposing? I mean: what does a choice have to look like for these rules to count it as genuinely yours, and what makes something a bias rather than a preference?

Could you reconstruct the implicit view as carefully as you can, and then tell me which parts of it the practices actually *need* versus which parts they just happen to fit comfortably? I'd especially like to know which rival accounts they rule out — a flat revealed-preference line where choosing just is preferring, so a default is meaningless; or a strict consent line where nothing counts unless you actively said yes. Are the three schemes even consistent with each other, or is one of them doing something different in kind (does the cooling-off right protect autonomy, or just draw a line on when transactions become too messy to unwind)? Where is the design underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same paperwork? And finally: if you swapped the implicit view for a hard-line revealed-preference one, what would concretely have to change — would the cooling-off right go, would we all have to opt in to pensions, would the register become opt-in again? Also, does the fact that families still get consulted in the organ case sit awkwardly with the whole idea of deemed consent, or is it a clue about what the practice really thinks it's tracking?
