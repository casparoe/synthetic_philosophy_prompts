Was up till about two last night arguing with a friend about the colour phi case and we got no where, and honestly I've stopped caring who's right and started caring about why nobody seems able to be. We'd meant to be watching a film. Quick set-up so you can actually engage with it instead of giving me the textbook tour: two dots of different colours flashed in sequence at different places on a screen, and at certain spacings and timings people report one spot travelling across the gap and changing colour roughly halfway along, i.e. before the second dot has appeared at all. Ever since the original demonstrations in the mid-seventies and the philosophical fallout in the early nineties, the standard framing has been that either experience was delayed — the Stalinesque option, where nothing like a mid-path colour change was ever consciously present and the whole episode gets assembled once the second dot lands and is then reported as though it had unfolded in that order — or experience went one way and memory got rewritten afterwards, the Orwellian option. Then there's the line that the choice itself is bogus, because asking what the subject was conscious of at the earlier moment presupposes there is somewhere and somewhen experience gets finalised, and there isn't. Plus two escape hatches people reach for: the deflationary one, where lateral inhibition or something similar makes the two stimuli produce effectively the same internal state so the report is accurate and there's no illusion needing a story; and the temporally-extended-content one, where experience just isn't the sort of thing that comes in instants, so "what did you see at t" is a category mistake.

My friend thinks this is a live empirical dispute that better methods will settle: stop collecting verbal reports, use forced-choice discrimination or no-report measures, and the timing question gets an answer. I think that move is doing all the work and smuggling in precisely what's contested, because a no-report measure of when the colour change was represented isn't obviously a measure of experience rather than of some subpersonal process, and the delaying side needs that identification to be free. But I might be wrong about that, and I might be wrong in the other direction too. So: diagnose the stall. Is it (a) a verbal dispute about what "conscious of" means, (b) a substantive dispute that is genuinely empirical but blocked because every scrap of evidence is a post-hoc report that could itself have been revised, making the rewriting hypothesis unfalsifiable in principle, (c) two camps answering different questions under one heading — one asking where experience sits in time, one asking what it's like from inside — (d) a question that's ill-posed rather than merely hard, or (e) something else you'd name instead? I have my own suspicion, which is that the whole thing rests on a shared premise nobody has ever written down explicitly, roughly: for any subject and any instant there is a determinate, fully specific answer to "what was she conscious of at that instant". Call it the snapshot assumption. Drop it and the two rival descriptions start to look like different ways of carving one temporally smeared process — empirically indistinguishable, but not merely verbal either, because they'd still disagree about whether the smear is fixed in advance or settled afterwards. Tell me whether that's right, half right, or just confused.
