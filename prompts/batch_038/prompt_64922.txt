I teach a 200-level course on practical rationality — 35 students, two 75-minute meetings a week for seven weeks — and we reach weakness of will in week five. I want the students to arrive at that week having already produced their own behavioral record, not a survey they filled out in ten minutes and not a thought experiment they discussed abstractly. The specific thing I'm after is preference reversal under delay: a student choosing one option when it is distant and the other when it is near, with no relevant feature of the options having changed in between. My constraint is that the choice architecture should be embedded in something the students would plausibly treat as ordinary course administration — participation points, an optional make-up slot, a deadline they can move — rather than announced as an experiment. I know this sounds like a trick, and I want your help making it honest rather than merely effective: tell me exactly what I should disclose at the start of the semester, what I can withhold until week five, and where the line is between avoiding demand characteristics and deceiving people who are depending on me for a grade. A colleague who tried something similar with candy reports that the whole thing collapsed in the first session because everyone took the immediate reward and there was no variation left to discuss, so please design for the case where the temptation is obvious from the first day.

On mechanics: give me the reward structure (no cash, no food, since I have allergies and dietary restrictions to manage and I don't want the ethics committee to have opinions about glucose), the schedule across seven weeks and fourteen meetings, and at least one revision window in which a student can change a choice already made, with a real but small cost attached to revising. I'd also like a costly commitment device on offer — something a student can lock themselves into early, which is unattractive precisely because it forecloses later revision — and I want the design to let me compare students who took it against students who didn't. Specify whether choices are made privately or in front of the class, how they get recorded without anyone feeling surveilled, how I aggregate them so that the week-five reveal shows a pattern in the room without exposing any individual's humiliations, and what the opt-out looks like for a student who declines to be a data point at all. Please also say what happens to students who never engage with the mechanism, which in my experience is a quarter of the room.

The part I most need thought about is the debrief, and here I want the philosophy rather than the logistics. Someone sharp will say that they didn't act akratically at all: they learned something between week two and week six, their workload shifted, they re-weighted how much the reward mattered given what else was going on, and so what looks like a reversal is just ordinary rational revision in response to new evidence. That objection is not obviously wrong, and a good activity should be built so that the objection can be tested rather than waved away — so build into the design at least one choice pair where the options are held fixed in every respect except temporal proximity, and tell me what the aggregate result would have to look like for me to be entitled to say the room displayed time-inconsistent preference rather than updated belief. Then tell me honestly what the activity cannot deliver: I suspect that trading points over six weeks reproduces intertemporal choice but not acting against one's own considered judgment about something that actually constitutes a life — a thesis, a health habit, a relationship — and that the gap between those two things is where the interesting philosophy lives. Say which feature of akrasia the game structurally cannot supply, what I would have to establish by argument in the second half of week five, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, including how I should handle the student who figures out the design in week one and announces it to everyone.
