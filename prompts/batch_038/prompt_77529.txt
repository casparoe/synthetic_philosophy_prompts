Leibniz died three centuries before anyone could build a machine that enumerates possible futures, scores them, and executes the highest-scoring one. Suppose a government now proposing exactly that — an artificial optimizer given a welfare objective, computing the policy that maximizes expected aggregate well-being, with the state legally bound to implement its verdict and human deliberation surviving only as a tie-breaker among equal scores — were put to him for judgment. Argue his response from the internal machinery of his moral philosophy rather than from a generic Enlightenment reaction to machines, and be as willing to have him reject the scheme as to have him embrace the parts of it that flatter him. At a minimum: does his account of God's choice of the best among compossible worlds license a finite creature doing something structurally analogous, when the divine intellect surveys infinitely many complete series at once while every created mind perceives only confusedly and from a single point of view; is expected aggregate welfare even the same kind of maximand as what he took perfection to be — the greatest variety of phenomena at the least cost in the means — or does the proposal quietly trade perfection for arithmetic; what do his separation of perception from apperception and his test of grasping necessary truths imply about the standing of a system that handles such truths fluently while perhaps lacking any reflective awareness that it does; and does surrendering deliberation to the machine's verdict fall into the fatalist inference, from the determinateness of the future to the uselessness of acting, that he took himself to have blocked? Close with a definite verdict — endorse, endorse under specified reforms, or reject — and state how confident you are in that verdict and in each of the four claims, naming the one you are least sure of and saying what would move you on it.
