I keep hitting the same wall in the situationism-vs-virtue-ethics argument and I want a diagnosis, not another run-through of the two sides. Pattern I've noticed: whenever a defender of character points to longitudinal data showing that honesty or aggressiveness is stable in people over years, the situationist replies that stability across similar situations tells you nothing about the breadth a virtue is supposed to have; and whenever a situationist points at a cross-situational study — the hurry one, the dime in the phone booth, obedience — the defender replies that those weren't virtue-relevant situations. Each move makes the other side's evidence inadmissable, and neither side seems aware it's doing it.

My hypothesis is that the fight isn't really about the experiments but about how situations get sorted into types, and that sorting can only be settled by normative judgment about what a virtue is supposed to govern — which would mean no quantity of social psychology can decide it, and part of the impasse is just two notions of 'trait' (one predictive, one evaluative) being run together under one word. Is that right? Or is there something substantive and potentially decidable underneath — say, how much cross-situational consistency a real disposition actually requires — that a cleverer experiment or a formal model of trait robustness could pin down?

Then tell me what would count as progress, including the possibility that nothing can: both sides have by now conceded so much (local traits on one hand, rare-but-achievable global ones on the other) that maybe there's no real disagreement left to have.
