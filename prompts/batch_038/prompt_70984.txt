I should say at the outset that I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so please define terms of art on first use and do not assume I have read the primary literature. What I want from you is structure, not simplification.

I have been working through two-dimensional semantics for the past few months, largely because a friend of mine who runs a reading group keeps telling me that the framework dissolves the problems it is usually brought in to solve. Here is how he explains it, in language he chose for my benefit. A sentence's meaning, on this picture, is a function of two arguments rather than one: the first is a world, namely the world you are standing in when you utter the sentence, and the second is a world at which you evaluate what was said. In his words it is exactly currying — partially apply the first argument and you have the primary intension, partially apply the second and you have the secondary intension, and the full two-dimensional intension is the function before either argument has been supplied. He adds that the matrix diagrams in the literature, worlds along one axis and worlds along the other, confirm the reading: a two-dimensional semantics is just a lookup table keyed by a pair of world-coordinates, with the interesting proposition picked out along the diagonal.

From this he draws a deflationary conclusion about Chalmers's use of the machinery. His view is that the zombie scenario is what you get when the two arguments are supplied in the wrong order: you pass a world into the slot that expects a context, notice the output has changed, and announce that you have found a gap between the physical and the phenomenal. Keep the argument order straight, he says, and there is no gap, no two-dimensional argument against materialism, and nothing left for the philosophy of mind to explain.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than adjudicate the metaphysics. Concretely:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly; a table is fine. What plays the role of the outer function, the inner function, the first argument, the second argument, the intermediate value, and the final result? Where do Kaplan's character and content sit in that table, and where do Chalmers's primary and secondary intensions sit — and are those two pairs the same pair of things under different names, or different things occupying structurally similar positions?

2. Check my friend's extraction rule. I am not convinced that partially applying one argument yields the primary intension and partially applying the other yields the secondary, since the diagonal appears to involve the two arguments being constrained to be equal rather than either one being held fixed. Tell me what the correct rule is, and then tell me whether his misstatement is a local slip or a symptom of the analogy failing at exactly the point where it is load-bearing.

3. Identify what the currying and lookup-table pictures quietly import that the target lacks. I have one suspicion I cannot articulate: in currying the intermediate value is itself an ordinary function of the remaining argument, and either argument can in principle be fixed first, whereas worlds taken as actual and worlds taken as counterfactual are supposed to stand in some asymmetric relation. Develop that, add the disanalogies I have missed, and then sort the resulting list into the ones that bear on my friend's deflationary conclusion and the ones that are merely cosmetic.

4. Say whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole weight of it. I want to know specifically what work the argument-order gloss is doing that the technical apparatus does not license.

5. If there is a better analogy, offer one. Candidates I have reached for on my own: a generic function before and after instantiation at a type; a container image versus a container run from it; a configuration file versus the process that reads it; the same build executed under two different environments.
