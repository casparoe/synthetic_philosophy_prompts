For some years I have read the rationalist corpus closely, and one argument in it has recently rearranged my thinking about bet sizing: the ergodicity economics line of work associated with Ole Peters. As I now understand it, expected utility theory evaluates a gamble by averaging over an imagined ensemble of parallel agents, each of whom faces the bet once, whereas what actually befalls me is a single trajectory of wealth through time. Under multiplicative dynamics those two averages come apart, and only the time-average growth rate describes anything that happens to anybody. The corollary that struck me is that the growth-optimal stake is then fixed by the structure of the gamble rather than by anything about the bettor. Risk preference, on this reading, is not a parameter to be elicited but a confusion to be dissolved: the Kelly fraction is not the right size for agents who happen to have logarithmic utility, it is the right size, and a bettor who stakes less is not expressing a taste so much as accepting a slower growth rate for no compensating reason.

I would like to test that reading against a concrete situation of my own, and to learn where it breaks, if it breaks.

An acquaintance offers me a recurring even-money wager which I estimate, from a few hundred observed rounds, to land in my favour roughly sixty percent of the time. I have been staking a fifth of my bankroll each round, which I take to be the Kelly figure. He expects to keep the arrangement going for a few dozen more rounds and then retire it permanently; there will be no successor opportunities. That bankroll is most of my savings, though I also draw a salary.

First: nearly every practitioner account I have read recommends staking some fraction of the Kelly figure — half, commonly — and none of them present this as an allowance for the squeamish. If the growth rate is the whole of the normative content, and full Kelly maximises it, what could make half-Kelly anything other than a mistake?

Second: does the shortness of my horizon matter? Asymptotically the growth-optimal stake wins, but a few dozen rounds is not asymptotic, and I have no obvious interest in a limit I will not live to see. If the answer is that I should stake less because the number of rounds is small, say precisely what is being traded against what — and say whether making that trade reintroduces exactly the subjective risk attitude the argument was supposed to have eliminated.

Third: my acquaintance's sister is weighing the same arrangement, except that her bankroll is her entire net worth and she has no salary. Scale invariance appears to say she should stake the same fifth that I do. That strikes me as reckless rather than merely uncomfortable, and I cannot tell whether my reaction is an objection to the framework or an intuition I have not earned.

Finally, and here I suspect I have gone wrong somewhere without being able to locate where: the growth-rate objective rewards reducing variance at a given arithmetic mean. I do not think this is a concession on my part, since the framework is still delivering a single number rather than a menu indexed to temperament — but I would like to be told, plainly, in what sense it dispenses with a utility function rather than installing one and declining to call it that.
