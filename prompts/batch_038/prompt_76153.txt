I do comms at a tiny nonprofit that campaigns on AI governance — five staff, almost entirely grant-funded. Last month a big industry funder offered us a multi-year grant with a memo attached that basically asked us to drop a line of criticism we're known for. Our ED took the meeting, came back saying "if you can't beat em, join em," and we took the money along with some softened language in our public positions. Two people on our advisory board resigned over it, saying wed drifted. Then at the same all-hands, someone else used the exact same phrase approvingly about a former colleague who went to work at one of the labs we critique — like it was a smart move that would give her leverage.

It feels to me like that one sentence is doing at least three different jobs and nobody in the room noticed which one they meant. Can you lay out the distinct claims "if you can't beat them, join them" could be making? Off the top of my head there's a descriptive one about the option set (beating them just isn't available), an instrumental one (joining is a means to your original end, which survives intact), a convergent one (you end up actually agreeing with them and that's not a failure at all), maybe a subversive/entryist one, and a resigned one where joining is just defeat with better benefits. But I want the full menu rather than my half-list — each reading stated precisely, what would have to be true for it to hold, whether it's plausible in a case like ours, and whether it's compatible with the board members' drift worry or quietly assumes it away.

Two extra things I care about. First, does the meaning shift depending on what "em" is — an institution you get hired by vs. a coalition you ally with vs. a funder you take money from without joining anything? Those felt like different moves to me but everyone talked as if they were one. Second, there's something I can't articulate about who's saying it and when: the person who joins stops being the "you" in the sentence, so the next round of it gets said by somebody else about them. If that's a real feature of the proverb and not just me being weird, say what it implies.

For each reading also flag which ones are platitudes nobody would dispute, which are substantive gambles, and which are just false in our situation — and then tell me which reading, if any, would actually justify what our ED did. Bullet list I can bring to a strategy meeting, please. No sources or further reading, I just need the distinctions sharp enough to use out loud.
