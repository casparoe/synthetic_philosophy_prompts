ok, background first: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and there's an arguement that's been running in our team slack for about six weeks that I'd like someone to actually adjudicate rather than just shout about.

We build the rota software for a home care provider — the thing that decides which carer visits which client and when. In the spring we shipped a new optimiser and ever since the carers have been moaning about split days: an 7am visit, then nothing until six, then two more calls. Turns out that's genuinely optimal if what you're minimising is total idle minutes across the day, which is what the metric says. Product's fix was to add a config flag, split_days_allowed, defaulting to true, and let each branch manager switch it off locally if they want.

Our tech lead defends the whole thing with a line I've now heard maybe forty times: the code is a pipe. He says the optimiser is just plumbing, it doesn't care what flows through it, and any politics in there came from the business picking the metric — so if you don't like the output, go and have the row with the people who wrote the requirements, not with engineering. Someone on the care side pushed back last week with a different one: no, it's a road. Where you put a road determines who can get where for the next fifty years whether or not the engineer meant anything by it, and nobody ever thinks about the roads they're driving on.

Can you audit both of these for me properly? I want the correspondences spelt out — for the pipe, the walls, the pressure, the water, whoever owns the pipe; for the road, the destination, right of way, who maintains it, how long it lasts — and then where each mapping falls over when you actually apply it to software. Especially: what does each one quietly assume that isn't true of a system like ours? Then tell me whether his conclusion survives. The specific bit I want tested is that 'we only implement the metric' gets the engineering team off the hook, given that we chose the data model, chose what a shift even is in the schema, chose that travel time between clients counts as idle, and chose the defaults.

Third thing, and this is the one I'm least sure about. In-house we distinguish 'concrete' from 'configuration' — hardcoded into the data model versus left as a flag someone can toggle. Tech lead says that distinction is purely about flexibility and has nothing to do with values. My hunch is that it's doing loads of work here, because a flag can be flipped by whoever happens to hold the branch admin role, whereas changing the schema needs a migration and a release, so which bucket a parameter lands in decides who gets a say and how expensive it is to change your mind later. Is that a real point or am I inventing a thing?

I've also half-remembered an essay where someone argued a bunch of parkway bridges were deliberately built too low for buses to fit under, so the beach at the end stayed overwhelmingly white and middle class. No idea if that story holds up, so please treat it as a hypothetical case rather than as history and don't build anything on the details — I just want to know which of my two analogies, pipe or road, explains that case better, and what that says about which one explains ours.

And if you think they're both bad for this, say so and give me a better one: something that fits a system where most of the important decisions were taken as defaults nobody has re-examined since, and by which fields we made mandatory back in 2019.
