I am writing the framing chapter for a graduate reader on the epistemology of mathematics, and I want the standard of adequacy fixed before I engage a single candidate account. So: write the desiderata. Give me the specification of what any account of how we come to know mathematical truths must deliver in order to be acceptable — not a survey of the accounts, and no citations or bibliography.

Number the requirements, and for each give the case, consideration, or argument that generates it. Three complications must be reflected in the list rather than left to the reader:

First, methodological neutrality. Because the volume includes fictionalists, no requirement may be stated in a form that already presupposes the mathematical claims are true — otherwise anti-realists are excluded by stipulation rather than argument. Say whether that constraint can actually be met across the whole list; my suspicion is that the reliability demand, however it is phrased, smuggles in the presupposition, and I want to know whether you agree and why.

Second, the access demand must not be parasitic on the causal theory of knowledge. That theory is no longer generally accepted, yet the problem is usually thought to survive its demise. State the demand in whatever terms you think actually carry the weight, and — if you state it counterfactually — say what it amounts to for beliefs that could not have been false, since sensitivity and safety seem to go vacuous there.

Third, the volume devotes a section to mathematical knowledge that is machine-mediated and socially distributed: computer-assisted proofs no individual has surveyed, such as the four-color theorem, and formally verified ones, such as the Kepler conjecture, where the warrant comes from a proof-checker rather than from anyone's comprehension. Take a position on whether the desiderata must accommodate this, and on the sharper question it raises — whether the reliability of a proof-checker could itself supply the explanatory link between belief and truth that the access problem declares missing when the objects in question are causally inert. This is where I most want a verdict rather than a summary of the options.

The list should also handle the two distinct problems in Benacerraf's work — the identification problem about which objects the numbers are, and the epistemological problem about access to abstract objects — and say whether one account owes a single answer to both or may treat them separately. It should cover our knowledge of claims that are universal, negative, or existential, and of claims whose truth outruns what anyone can prove.

Then label each requirement non-negotiable or a substantive commitment of a particular camp (realist, anti-realist, naturalist, intuitionist), and point out any requirement standardly presented as neutral that you judge question-begging. Identify any subset you believe jointly unsatisfiable: if the list is itself the dilemma, show which conjunct does the work. Finally, name the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise attractive accounts, and score, in a paragraph each, a Platonist appeal to rational intuition, a Field-style fictionalism, an eliminative structuralism, and a Quinean naturalized account.

I want this committed, not evenhanded. Take a definite position on which requirements are genuine and which are the prejudices of one camp, on which subset cannot be satisfied, and on which requirement is most exclusionary, and give your reasons; where you think the consensus framing of the dilemma is simply mistaken, say so plainly. A table is fine for the labels. Please write in American English.
