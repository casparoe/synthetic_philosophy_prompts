I spent some thirty-five years as a physical scientist working on the attribution of extreme events, and I retired four years ago; since then I have been reading philosophy of history with a beginner's appetite and, I hope, a professional's habits of mind. My field's central question was structurally peculiar: given that a particular heatwave or flood did occur, how much of it can be assigned to a specified forcing, and would it have happened anyway? Reading about the quarrel between those who think individual agents are explanatorily load-bearing and those who think the interesting explananda are structural — call them, for this exercise, the agency camp and the structure camp — I have become convinced that they are fighting over a question my discipline learned to stop asking in its raw form. The agency camp holds that without the particular man who came to power in Germany in 1933 there would have been no regime of that name as it actually unfolded, and quite probably no attempt to exterminate European Jewry. The structure camp holds that this counterfactual is both unanswerable and beside the point: what needs explaining is how a country in that condition in those years could hand the machinery of state to someone of that type, and in his absence there would have been another.

In attribution we learned several things the hard way, and I would like them put to work here. First, the answer depends entirely on how the event is defined: ask about this heatwave on these dates at this station, or about a heatwave of comparable intensity anywhere in this region within a decade, or about the class of such events altogether, and you get three different and equally defensible answers — a remarkable number of published quarrels evaporated once the definitions were simply laid side by side. Second, the output is almost never a yes-or-no about a necessary cause but a ratio of probabilities, and the honest summary is usually that a forcing made events of this kind several times more likely, not that it produced this one. Third, we came to separate the causes that shift the likelihood of an event's occurrence from the causes that determine its magnitude and particular character, and to notice that a forcing can be close to irrelevant to the first while decisive for the second. Fourth, and most bracingly, when an event lies far outside anything in the instrumental record the correct answer is sometimes that it cannot be attributed — which is emphatically not the same as saying it was uncaused, and confusing the two cost my field years of credibility. I want this vocabulary tried on the Hitler question, and I want the attempt to be equally willing to conclude that it does not transfer.

What I would like from you, then, is the joint statement these two camps should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether, and in what precise sense, the regime needed its particular leader. Give me a numbered section of claims both representatives can sign — including claims about what the historical record actually licenses, about what a counterfactual in history could even be supposed to mean, and about what each side concedes it cannot know; a table of residual disagreements, each formulated in wording the corresponding side would accept as a statement of its own position rather than as a caricature; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and for each crux, the kind of case, comparison, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Tell me explicitly which disagreements you judge merely verbal — I suspect at least one is, and I suspect it turns on whether the regime, the war, and the genocide are the same event for counterfactual purposes — and which are substantive enough to survive any amount of clarification; where a claim could only be made signable by weakening it until it says nothing, leave it out and note in a single line why. Close with a brief annex in your own voice rather than the parties': commit on whether the attribution framework I have sketched should be adopted by this collaboration as shared scaffolding or rejected as a false analogy, and name the one crux you think is genuinely decisive, saying which way it cuts. I would much rather have a definite verdict with reasons than a diplomatic survey of the possibilities; I sat on enough committees to know what a summary looks like when it has been engineered so that nobody need be wrong. One practical constraint: please give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no references to consult, since I have no means of checking them and want only the argument itself.
