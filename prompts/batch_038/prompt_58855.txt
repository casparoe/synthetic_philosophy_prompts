Got dragged into a four-hour pub argument on Sunday about Bell's theorem and I'm still buzzing from it, so I'd like a referee who wasn't in the room. I mostly just took notes and drank. It was between two friends: Kit, who's doing a philosophy of physics PhD, and Anouk, who builds photon-pair sources for a living and is way better at the actual experiments than Kit is. Neither of them gave an inch and I couldnt tell who was winning. Here's the argument in order — compressed, but each numbered point is a move somebody actually made.

(1) Kit opens by saying Bell's theorem is a no-go for locally causal *explanations*, not a proof that nature is non-local. Its premises are a factorisation condition — once you've conditioned on everything physically relevant in the overlap of the two past light cones, what happens at one detector shouldn't depend on anything in the other lab — plus the assumption that the knob settings are statistically independent of whatever the source is doing. So the honest conclusion is just that no account satisfying both works. Non-locality is one way to lose, but it isn't the only way.

Anouk says that's relabelling defeat. If nothing in the world satisfies the condition, then the world doesn't satisfy the condition, and that's all "the world isn't locally causal" ever meant. You haven't saved locality, you've saved a sentence about it.

(2) Anouk: and the experiments tell us precisely what we have — strong correlations plus no way to use them to send a message. Non-local correlations with no channel is a perfectly coherent package, it's the package we observe, so we should say so and stop agonising over it.

Kit: you're sliding between two different conditions. There's the one Bell cared about, and there's the weaker one saying Alice's marginal statistics don't depend on Bob's knob. Standard quantum theory satisfies the weaker and violates the stronger, which is exactly the point of Jarrett's decomposition and of Shimony renaming the two halves parameter independence and outcome independence. No-signalling is fine for relativity's bookkeeping, but it is not Bell's locality, and you keep using the first to defend the second.

(3) Anouk then gives her own argument, which I liked: the correlation isn't an event anywhere. Each lab just has a list of outcomes on a hard drive. The joint statistics only come into existence when somebody hauls the two lists together and compares them, and that's a slow, classical, subluminal process. Nothing at Alice's end changes when Bob measures; all that changes is whether a comparison has been made yet. So Bell shows you can't assemble the joint statistics out of local common causes — that's a failure about how the numbers have to be put together, not an influence crossing the gap.

Kit: you've described how we find out about the correlation and then called it an account of how the correlation arises. The records already exist in both labs before anybody compares them, and the inequality is violated by records that already exist. Moving the comparison to next Tuesday changes nothing about whether any assignment of local pre-existing values could have generated them. And your picture makes exactly the same predictions as mine, which makes me suspect it isn't a picture — it's my description with the word "influence" deleted.

(4) Anouk: right, then the older line. The theorem proves you can't have locality and realism together, and physics dropped realism. Properties aren't there until you measure them. That's been settled since the twenties and it's local in every sense anyone could ever test.

Kit: realism is not a premise of the theorem. Nowhere in the derivation is there a line saying properties have values before measurement. What's assumed is the factorisation condition and the independence of the settings, and quantum theory itself — instrumentalism, shut up and calculate, the whole lot — violates the factorisation condition. Dropping realism doesn't discharge the theorem, it just means you've stopped asking what would.

(5) Kit goes positive: the theories that actually reproduce the data are all non-local. Bohm's version has each particle's motion depending on where the other measurement is being made, and collapse theories have a real physical change propagating. Everettians claim to be local, but only by putting the wavefunction on configuration space, demoting spacetime, and then still owing an account of why everything looks like one definite outcome.

Anouk: demoting spacetime isn't a dodge, it's probably just true. If spacetime is emergent, a condition defined over spacetime regions is being imposed at the wrong level, and the theorem doesn't get a grip on the fundamental story. Your beables aren't beables at all.

Kit: careful, that relocates the theorem rather than escaping it. Whatever the fundamental level is, it has to reproduce the observed joint statistics, and any fundamental account that does will either violate a Bell-type condition or give up the independence of the settings. You can't outrun a no-go result by moving to a nicer stage; you can only re-derive it there.

Anouk: so non-local has become a word you can stick on anything, which means it isn't telling me anything.

(6) Anouk takes the other branch: the settings aren't independent. The knobs and the source have a common past, and everything in the observable universe has one, out of a very small and very correlated region. The runs that use light from distant stars to pick the settings only push the common cause further back — a matter of degree, never of principle. The guilty premise is the free-settings one, not locality.

Kit: true, and that's the honest part of your position — those experiments shrink the loophole, they never close it logically. But look at the price. On your view, whatever I build the settings out of — a quantum random number generator whose output nobody has seen, my own decision, a photon from a quasar — has to be correlated with the source in exactly the way needed to fake the violation, and it has to be so for every variant of the experiment including ones nobody has designed yet. That's not a hypothesis, it's a refusal to let anything count against it. It would also wreck the logic of randomised trials, where we assume the coin flip is independent of the outcome mechanism.

Anouk: not the same. In a trial the randomiser is physically disconnected from the mechanism producing the outcome, and you can check that it is. Here the experiment has already shown they aren't disconnected — that's what the violation *is*.

Kit: that's the question begged. "The experiment shows they aren't disconnected" is Bell's theorem with its conclusion smuggled back in as a premise about settings.

(7) Endgame. Anouk: so the menu is (a) the world is non-local or (b) the settings aren't independent. You say (b) is unscientific; I say (a) is empty, because you cannot name a single experiment that comes out differently under (a) and (b). If you can't, this is a fight about vocabulary, and I'd rather keep the vocabulary that lets me build the machine.

Kit: they differ in cost, not in prediction. (b) buys locality with a conspiracy that has to be re-tuned for every new experiment; (a) buys a straight account of what's going on at the price of Lorentz invariance being a mess at the fundamental level. I'll pay that. But I'll concede the narrower point: no experiment settles it, and anyone who says Bell's theorem was proved by the data rather than being a theorem with premises is talking rubbish.
