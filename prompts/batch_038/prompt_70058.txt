I'm a science-and-ideas columnist putting together a piece on how groups decide things, and I want to commission an argument I can push back against.

The set-up: a standing five-member panel has to rule on whether to approve something, and it's rulebook says approval requires both that the evidence supports it and that it's worth the cost. Vote premise by premise and a majority says yes on each, so the panel approves; vote straight on the conclusion and a majority says no. Every person I've spoken to says the premise-first route is obviously the right one, because a group ought to be moved by reasons rather than by free-floating bottom-line opinions.

I want the opposite case made properly. Two wrinkles to build the argument around: the rulebook's list of premises was drawn up decades ago by a small subcommittee nobody can now name, so the "reasons" the panel is held to were picked by someone with their own agenda; and everyone on the panel knows the procedure in advance, so a member who only cares about the verdict has an obvious incentive to vote insincerely on the premises.

So: argue that premise-based aggregation is the worse choice for a group that actually cares about getting things right. Commit to it — no even-handed tour of both procedures, just your strongest line of attack and an account of why you think it's the strongest. British English, please.
