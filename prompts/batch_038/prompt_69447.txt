I'm deep into a novel and stuck on a chapter that turns on a piece of medieval Jewish philosophy I only half-understand, so: help. Two of my leads are a retired Talmud professor in Jerusalem and his daughter, a large-animal vet who became religious as an adult and can't stand the idea of blood on an altar. In the scene they're argueing over dinner and he reads Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed, part III chapter 32 — the old Friedländer translation, which I pulled off a public-domain site — aloud at her, and she fires back that the same guy's own law code says the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and that the sacrifices will be offered again in his days. The passage he reads is this (ellipses are mine; I cut the scriptural proof-texts he splices in between):

"Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. … It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner … By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established; this result was thus obtained without deterring or confusing the minds of the people by the abolition of the service to which they were accustomed and which alone was familiar to them."

Three things I can't get a handle on. First: the chapter opens with a bunch of anatomy — nerves gradually hardening into sinews, milk provided for a newborn that can't take dry food — before it gets anywhere near the law. Is that just decorative framing, or is it doing real argumentative work? Does he mean the commandments themselves have a developmental arc, i.e. that a rite can be outgrown the way a calf outgrows milk? Second: he says God "allowed" these services to continue and "transferred" them to Himself, but elsewhere in the Guide he insists every commandment has a reason, and a bit earlier in part III he says we can give general reasons for the laws even where the details (why this many lambs, not one more) are unexplained. So is a permitted-and-redirected practice still a commandment with value in its own right, or is it pure scaffolding that's supposed to come down once the building can stand? Relatedly, does swapping the object of the rite leave the form of the rite intact and meaningful, or does he treat the form as disposable? Third: that counterfactual — a prophet today telling us to serve God in thought alone, no prayer, no fasting — reads to me like he's naming an ideal we weren't ready for. If that's right, doesn't the chapter's own logic predict sacrifices lapsing in messianic times, which is exactly what his code seems to deny? Which of my two characters is overreading?

Can you put your bottom line first, in a sentence or two — is the professor's reading defensible, or is he doing a nineteenth-century reformist number on the text? — and then work through the three points. Hard cap of 450 words. No reading list, no citations, no "scholars have debated"; I just want the argument.
