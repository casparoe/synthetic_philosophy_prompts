I'm a novelist and I've spent about four years now inside the head of a scribe at Pylos — well, an accountant really, one of the people doing the tablet-writing in the palace storerooms around 1200 BCE — and the book is finally close to done. My partner's brother is an archaeologist and he read the draft, and his big note was that I shouldn't be thinking of what happens next as a collapse, that societies don't collapse, they reorganize: the people didn't disappear, the language didn't disappear, the gods didn't disappear. His examples were Rapa Nui, where the deforestation story turned out to be mostly rats and the population crash was Peruvian slavers and epidemics in the 1860s rather than islanders eating themselves into extinction, and the Maya, where the north came up while the southern lowlands emptied, so in both cases what looks like an ending is really a relocation. I sort of caved at the time because he's the expert. But the more I sit with it the more I think he's sliding between two different things, and I want the argument spelled out properly so I can either fix my book or push back on him at Thanksgiving.

So: make the case that the Late Bronze Age Aegean really is a collapse and not merely a transformation — and that this isn't just me being gloomy for the sake of the plot. The things I keep coming back to are that Linear B stops and doesn't come back to the Aegean for centuries (Cyprus keeps writing, which I think cuts in my favor rather than against it), that the wanax and the whole redistributive palace apparatus have no successor institution for a long time, that the diplomatic world those tablets sit inside — the brother-king correspondence, the copper and tin moving by ship — goes with it, and that Homer, when he wants to describe something magnificent out of that world, ends up describing a boar's-tusk helmet as an heirloom that's been handed down through generations, meaning the epic tradition preserves the object but not the society that made objects like that routine. i want you to say why that pattern is qualitatively different from the Maya and Rapa Nui cases and not just more severe, and I want an actual criterion of collapse with teeth in it — tell me what would have to have happened in the Argolid in 1150 BCE for you to say no, that wasn't a collapse, that was a reorganization. If nothing could have counted, the word is empty and he wins by default.

Then deal with the two objections I'd make if I were him. First, that everything I'm calling lost is elite stuff: writing, palaces, and kings were the apparatus of a small minority, so collapse here just means the top came off and everyone else got on with it, which is what happens in most centuries anyway. Second, the political one — that collapse talk has a bad track record, it's how you get a bestseller lecturing islanders about their own ancestors and how you get a discipline that used to sort peoples into civilized and not, so maybe the word should be retired whatever the facts say. Tell me whether that's a reason to give up the concept or only a reason to be careful with it, and where the line falls between a description and a verdict. Don't split the difference, and dont give me a bibliography or a reading list — I can't chase any of that down and honestly I want the reasoning, not the citations. Argue it as strongly as you honestly can, and if there's a piece of his position you think survives my case, say which piece and why.
