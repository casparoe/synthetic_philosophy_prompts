I'm about 200 pages into a novel where a woman with an agressive neurodegenerative condition signs up for a module-by-module brain replacement program: synthetic equivalents swapped in for dying tissue, one small cluster at a time, each swap function-preserving at the moment it happens. The book's big scene is her sitting in a clinic with a consent form and a schedule to pick, and that scene only works if speed is actually the load-bearing variable rather than set dressing. Right now my draft has her choosing the slow option because slow feels safer and more humane, and my partner, who reads this stuff much better than I do, says that's backwards — or at least that it can be made backwards — and I don't want to rewrite the chapter on a hunch.

So: build me a thought experiment in which the slow, careful, stepwise replacement turns out to be the worse one. Worse meaning it involves a death, or a duplication, or the quiet coming-into-being of a second somebody, where a much faster version of the *identical* procedure wouldn't. Constraints. Same patient, same disease, same endpoint — by the end all the same modules are replaced, nothing gained, nothing lost, functional architecture preserved at every instant, and the technology never misfires, because the badness has to fall out of the procedure being done correctly, not of a botch or a bad surgeon. Isolate the rate: what varies between the two versions is only how long the biological and synthetic parts are running side by side while the biological ones get dialed down. Keep it clinical and boring — outpatient program, a nurse who brings her coffee, a parking garage — no beam-me-up pods, no cryonics, no soul detectors, no omniscient neuroscientist who can just tell us what happened to her. And please don't hand me the standard branching setup where a copy is switched on somewhere else while the original lives out a few more days; in my case the two systems are wired into one skull and one life, sharing a single stream of experience while it lasts, and the fade-out is part of the same operation. The scenario itself should be three paragraphs maximum, tight enough that I could lift sentences out of it and put them in the book.

Then show me the case actually does what I asked, because a case that just stipulates the verdict is useless to me. Walk through what each view says about the slow version and the fast version separately: a psychological-continuity theorist who needs a no-branching clause, the functionalist gradualist who thinks step-by-step isomorphism carries consciousness and identity along with it, the animalist who thinks she's an organism and the whole program is a slow suicide either way, and a closest-continuer person if they have anything interesting to add. Tell me where the rate variable bites and where it's simply invisible to the theory, and which single feature of the scenario is doing all the work — then tell me what happens if I pull that feature out, i.e. squeeze the overlap down to nothing, or lock the two systems so tightly that they can't drift apart at all. If it turns out my constraints aren't jointly satisfiable — if holding function fixed at every instant already rules out the divergence the slow version is supposed to produce — say so plainly and tell me which constraint to relax and how far. Finish with how a defender of whichever theory comes out worst would attack the case, since I'd rather hear that from you than from a reader with a blog, and give me one sentence on whether her choice is a real choice or whether the two schedules are only described differently by people using "she" in different ways. Write the whole thing in American English, please — the book is set outside Cleveland and I can't have "programme" or "realise" wandering in.
