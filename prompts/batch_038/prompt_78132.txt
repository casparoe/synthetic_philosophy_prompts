Second-year political theory PhD student here, and I've talked myself into a position for a dissertation chapter that I want properly stress-tested before I commit to it.

The position: moral compromise between agents with different values — people, movements, parties, whatever — is fundamentally a gains-from-trade phenomenon, not a tragic concession or a failure of principle. The version I'm defending has four moving parts:

1. Each agent holds a bundle of value-commitments that differ in *intensity*, not just content. You care enormously about X and comparatively little about Y, and that ordering is a fact about you.
2. That intensity ordering licenses trades across issues in exactly the way comparative advantage licenses trade across goods: you back my X, I back your Y, and each of us advances the things we care most about more than either of us would have under non-cooperation. Neither of us has to be right about the other's values for this to work.
3. Permissibility criterion: a trade is morally fine if each party, judging by their own values and all things considered, does better than in the no-trade baseline. I want that to be *sufficient*, not just necessary.
4. The repugnance people feel about "selling out" a sacred value is what I'm calling moral money illusion — an artifact of bookkeeping our commitments in separate non-fungible categories when they're really intensities along a common scale of value-realization. The disgust is a heuristic against geting intensity-measurement wrong, not an independent constraint.

So: what would count against this? I don't want a defense of compromise in general, I want the exposure map.

- Rank the defeaters, fatal down to merely awkward, and say which is which.
- For each one, tell me what a defender of my view would concede as genuinely counting against them versus wave off, and whether the wave-off is legitimate or a dodge.
- Include the defeaters that aren't arguments but cases the view can't describe at all. I already suspect one: a trade made by someone whose values then shift — conversion, drift, the person you become — so that it was Pareto-superior at signing and worse by the signer's own later lights. Does that break (3), and how badly, given that intensity orderings are supposed to be the input?
- The third-party problem: in markets the object traded is usually owned by the traders, but in moral politics the object of the trade is usually somebody else's treatment. Is my analogy structurally broken, or does it just need an externality clause?
- What does my view predict that the integrity-or-sacredness rival doesn't predict — something someone with institutional history or voting data could actually go check?
- And be honest about whether (1)–(4) have been qualified into unfalsifiability, since any trade that goes badly can be redescribed as a mis-measured intensity or an unmodeled third party. If it is insulated, is that fatal here or just the normal condition for a view of this kind?

What would move me most personally: a semi-formal argument that criterion (3) isn't even well defined once values are incomparable or the evaluating standard moves over time, and a concrete case of a stable logrolling arrangement that left both traders' core values worse off then the no-trade baseline. If you can build either one, build it — keep cases self-contained and invented, no citations or bibliography.

Verdict: [well-insulated | genuinely exposed | mixed] | most decisive defeater: [five words or fewer]
