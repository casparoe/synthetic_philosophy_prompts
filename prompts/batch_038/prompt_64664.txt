I am drafting a chapter on the explanatory structure of the July Crisis of 1914, and I want to run it against Braudel's scheme of historical time rather than against the usual diplomatic and military history of the outbreak of the war.

Braudel's picture, as I understand it: history moves at several speeds at once. At the bottom is a nearly motionless layer of human relations to environment, climate, and geography. Above it a middle, rhythmic layer — economies, states, societies, civilizations — moving in cycles and conjunctures over decades. At the surface, event-history and individuals, which he famously likened to foam carried along by currents it does not create. Explanation, on this view, has to be sought in the slower layers, and the event layer is precisely where history is least intelligible.

First, sort the standard explanations of 1914 into those three layers as faithfully as you can, and say which of them actually does explanatory work rather than merely sitting there: the alliance system and the preceding decades of arms competition; the railway networks and mobilization timetables, which required the German plan to march through Belgium and made Russian mobilization, once ordered, very hard to call back; the strategic assumption that France could be knocked out before Russia finished mobilizing; and then the personal and contingent material — the assassination at Sarajevo, the assurance Berlin gave Vienna in early July, the misreadings and telegrams of the last week of the month, the decisions for and against general mobilization.

Second, tell me where the sorting breaks down. My own suspicion, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that the items which mattered most in 1914 are slow without being Braudelian. A railway network or a mobilization plan is a human artifact, built within living memory and revisable in principle; yet for anyone trying to change it inside a fortnight it is about as immovable as a mountain range. Braudel's axis is speed of change. What the July Crisis seems to call for is an axis of cost-of-reversal: how expensive, for the actors in the moment, is it to undo a given fact? Develop this objection as strongly as you can, then say whether it is fatal to the framework or whether the middle layer can absorb it without losing its content.

Third, run the test in the other direction. Is 1914 a case the framework was built to handle, a case it can accommodate with revision, or a case revealing that it was never a theory of explanation at all but a research priority about where to look first? Distinguish those verdicts explicitly rather than blurring them, and state what evidence would tell them apart.

Finally, be candid about where the framework falls silent — in particular, whether it can say anything about why these structural conditions produced a general war in August 1914 rather than at some other moment, or whether it must concede that timing belongs to the layer it declines to explain. I would rather have a clean account of the limits than a forced reading that makes Braudel speak on every question.
