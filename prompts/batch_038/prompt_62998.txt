You're a philosopher of science who's actually spent time inside the structural realism debates, so please think this through carefully before you commit — work move by move rather than summarizing, and write in American English.

My philosophy-of-science reading group ended in a standoff last week between two members, D and M, over the Fresnel case. I was sitting there taking notes and honestly couldn't tell who was wining. Roughly how it went:

1. D opened with the pessimistic meta-induction — every past theory turned out false, so ours probably will too. But look at Fresnel: the luminiferous ether is gone, and yet his equations for how light reflects and refracts at an interface survived, re-derived inside Maxwell's electrodynamics. Revolutions throw out the stuff and keep the structure. That's exactly what structural realism claims, so the case that's supposed to kill realism is the best evidence for it.

2. M: you can only say that in hindsight. Nobody in 1850 thought "the structure is the content." What counts as the surviving structure got picked out afterwards, by later physicists re-deriving the same formal relations from a totally different ontology. If structure just means whatever happens to survive, the position can't lose — it's true by construction.

3. D: it can lose. If the pessimistic induction were right across the board, the formal apparatus would have gone down with the ether, the way the ether did. The repeated survival of the mathematics across revolutions is the surprising fact that needs explaining, and SR explains it.

4. M: so now you're running the no-miracles argument again, with the same hole in it. Two theories with different domains can always be made to share some formal structure if you look hard enough — that's Newman's point against Russell. Postulate enough relations and any big enough domain can be arranged to fit your structure, so the structural claim collapses into a claim about how many things there are.

5. D: Newman's objection hits Russell's version, not the current one. A structural realist today restricts the second-order quantifiers to nomically loaded relations, the ones the theory's own equations pick out, not to arbitrary re-labellings. Ramsey-sentence it properly and the claim is not satisfiable by every model.

6. M: and now you've paid for the fix with exactly what you were trying to avoid. To say which second-order properties are the nomically legitimate ones, you need a criterion that isn't itself structural — and any such criterion is a claim about the nature of what stands in the relations. Either you admit that, and SR is just realism with extra steps, or you leave the criterion vague, and you're back at move 2 picking out the survivors after the fact.

Both of them walked away thinking they'd won.
