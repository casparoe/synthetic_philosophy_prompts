I'm on the board of a mid-sized community foundation here in Ohio — not an academic, I've run an HVAC supply company for thirty years — and this year we got a bequest that's earmarked for one thing: a program that actually changes a harmful tradition among young people in our state. I sit on the seven-person panel that has to pick it, and we've got two finalists, both about hazing in Greek life and in a couple of the trade apprenticeship programs up north. The first is from a coalition of student-affairs people at a state university plus a nonprofit of hazing survivors: assemblies, survivor testimony, an anonymous reporting hotline, a push to get state criminal liability widened, and a signed pledge plus mandatory training for every new member. Their whole theory is that people haze because they haven't really registered what it does to the kid on the other end of it — fix the understanding and the behavior follows. The second is from a guy who used to work for a national fraternity headquarters with an ad agency attached: no moral argument at all, or as little as possible. Instead they want to go after the prestige structure — a certification awarded by prominent alumni and a handful of employers who agree to prefer it, a public ranking of chapters, redesigning the initiation ritual so the brag-worthy thing is something else, and getting well-known old members to publicly disown their own hazing stories. Their pitch line is that nobody who hazes thinks he's doing wrong, he thinks he's doing something honorable, so you argue with his honor and not his conscience. We can only fund one; the gift letter says it has to be a single coherent program, so we can't just split it and hope, though we can attach conditions to whichever we pick. Decision meeting is in about two weeks and I've been asked to circulate something beforehand.

Here's the part that's making my head spin. The foundation has a one-page thing left behind by the program officer who retired last spring — everybody calls it the status-first rule — which says we should fund work on what makes a practice a mark of honor rather than work on why it's wrong, and that conscience-type campaigns are only worth funding once the practice is already considered shameful. So proposal two basically is our house doctrine walking through the door, which makes me suspicious that I'm being told the answer before the question. But there are complications both ways. One of my fellow board members, who's a criminal defense lawyer, says both proposals ignore the fact that the universities quietly want the fraternities because they absorb housing and take the parties off campus, and the alumni networks feed hiring and donations, so there's real money protecting the tradition and no amount of honor or conscience work touches that. Then the survivor group sent us a survey they say shows most members privately think the worst initiations are wrong but go along anyway, and the fraternity guy cites what looks like the same survey to say the opposite, that most members think it's fine and builds character. I don't know which reading is right and I suspect the two sides are each hearing what their theory predicts. And the status-first camp has an answer to the hotline idea — they say reporting mechanisms just push the practice underground and make it more dangerous — but I notice their certification depends on chapters self-reporting, which seems to me like it pays chapters for looking honorable rather than for being honorable, and if honor is about appearances I'm not sure that's a bug in their plan so much as the plan. I've also been poking around on my own and I keep landing on two things I half understand: that Appiah book about honor codes and moral revolutions that half my book club read, where the practices that ended were things like dueling and footbinding and the slave trade; and some British philosopher named Williams — I might be mangling this — who made a point that the argument against slavery was sitting around for centuries and didn't abolish anything, partly because people believed the arrangement was just inevitable. If I'm getting either of those wrong, tell me, because I don't want to wave them around at a meeting where one board member's daughter is a sociology grad student. Also relevant, maybe: I've read a bit about how norms persist because everybody expects everybody else to keep doing them, which is not the same thing as everybody approving of them, and I can't tell whether hazing is more the first or more the second.

So: act as someone who's spent a career on the philosophy of social norms and on how moral change actually happens, and who has also sat on real grant panels and knows what a memo that a board can vote on looks like. Before you write anything formal, think out loud for me — list the considerations on each side, and work out separately what would have to be true for each proposal's theory to actually work, including whether the two theories need the same facts about what members believe. Then give me the document I'll circulate: a decision memo of roughly two or three pages with a clear bottom line — fund one, fund one with specific conditions spelled out, or send both back — the reasons, and a criteria section the panel could reuse next round since the status-first rule is up for review anyway. I lean toward the honor proposal and I feel queasy about it, because it looks like it deliberately works on young men's vanity and status anxiety and drags employers in as leverage, where the survivor coalition at least talks to people like reasoning adults; take that objection seriously as its own section rather than batting it away, and also give the strongest honest case for the side you don't pick. And please separate out for me what's actually settled among people who study this versus what's a live argument — if some part of this rests on a contested theory I want it labeled as contested, not sold to me as consensus, because I'll be repeating it to six other people who trust me.
