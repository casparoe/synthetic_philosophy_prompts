I TA a section of an intro political philosophy course — 22 students, mostly sophomores, Thursday 3pm, 75 minutes, a room with movable chairs and two whiteboards but no projector and no budget for printing anything fancier than a couple of handout pages. Next week is our church-and-state week. They'll have read the usual pair of excerpts on establishment vs. disestablishment going in, and in past years the section just turns into 20 people repeating "well, separation of church and state, obviously" at each other until the hour runs out. I want to break that by making them build a regime instead of opining about one, and I want the argument to happen to them rather than being delivered by me.

Here's what I want them to come away seeing: that "separation" isn't a neutral baseline that the other options depart from. Every arrangement — a state church funded by a tax the government collects on its behalf, plural recognition of several bodies, strict separation with no money and no official acknowledgment of anything, and the messy middle where one region keeps an old holdover arrangement — distributes both burdens and dignities, and every one of them is a bet on who the majority will be in thirty years. So: design me a constitutional-drafting game. Small groups each get a card describing a community's religious demography and some of its history, and they have to write actual short articles on funding, recognition, religious instruction in schools, public symbols, and exemptions from generally applicable rules. Then I want a time-skip you hand me — demographics shift, groups merge or split or secularize — and the articles they wrote get invoked against them by another group under the new numbers. I also want an exit mechanic, because the "nobody's forced to belong, just leave" reply is the one my students always reach for: to shed the tax or the obligation you have to file a declaration with a registrar in front of the room, with some small cost attached, and they should run into the fact that a right you have to claim publicly is not the same as no obligation at all. Seed one of the cards with an invented region inside an otherwise secular state that still pays some clergy and still does religious instruction in public schools because a century-old arrangement survived a border change — model it on whatever real case you like, but give it fictional geography so nobody spends the activity fact-checking me.

For the deliverable I want: the rules, the community cards and any registrar/token apparatus, a payoff or scoring scheme that works without real money, a minute-by-minute timeline that actually fits 75 minutes, a materials list, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the concepts it's supposed to surface (including naming the ideas only after they've hit). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover — especially the two or three pre-law students who will spend the whole session lawyering the drafting language instead of playing, and the risk that the time-skip just reads as me rigging the deck. Tell me whether the time-skip should be announced up front as a known possibility or sprung mid-game, and say why that choice matters for what they learn. And be frank about which feature of the real problem the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I've got the readings covered, I want the machine, not the scholarship.
