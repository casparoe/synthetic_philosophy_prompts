STUDENT ANSWER. Both Rawls and Nozick accept (A) and disagree about (B), and I think the disagreement is really about where desert comes from.

On (A), Rawls's point is that the distribution of native talent is a lottery that nobody entered and nobody can take credit for. He pushes this further than is usually noticed: it is not only raw ability that is undeserved but the disposition to cultivate it. Whether someone has the character to work hard at their talent depends heavily on the family and circumstances they were lucky enough to be born into, so the meritocrat cannot fall back on effort once ability has been conceded. Since neither ability nor the will to develop it is deserved, a distribution of income that tracks either cannot be defended by saying that people get what their contributions earn.

That is why Rawls needs (B). A society can be scrupulous about opening every position to talent and about making the competition fair, and still hand out shares that reflect nothing but who won the lottery. What Rawls does not conclude is that the talented should gain nothing: the inequalities are permitted, and permitted because they work to the advantage of the least well off. The better-off are paid more than they deserve, in effect, because paying them more leaves everyone else better off than an equal division would.

The concept doing the work here is legitimate expectation. Under a just scheme the public rules generate expectations: if the rules say that holding this office carries this income, and I hold the office, I can legitimately expect that income. Rawls's claim is that these expectations are what desert amounts to in a just society. Nobody has a pre-institutional moral claim on their share, but once institutions are just, the shares they assign are what people deserve. The two ideas differ in their origin, not in their function.

Nozick agrees about the lottery and about pre-institutional desert, but thinks Rawls draws the wrong conclusion. For Nozick the shape of a distribution is the wrong thing to examine; what matters is how the holdings came about. His Wilt Chamberlain case is meant to show this: start from any patterned distribution you like, including an equal one, let people voluntarily pay to watch Chamberlain play, and the pattern is upset by nothing but free choices. Entitlement, then, is desert produced by voluntary exchange — Chamberlain ends up with more not because he is more virtuous but because a great many people chose to give it to him, and once they have, he deserves it. This is also the basis of his objection to redistributing earnings, which he treats as a claim on someone's time and labour.

On Young: his meritocracy is Nozick's target, not Rawls's. Rawls is himself committed to careers open to talents and to fair equality of opportunity, so a system that sorts people by measured ability and effort is close to what Rawls endorses; what Young depicts is that ideal corrupted by hereditary advantage built into the measuring apparatus. Nozick, by contrast, rejects any principle that assigns shares according to some dimension of persons, and merit is exactly such a dimension. Young's book is a satire, a warning about a society run on intelligence plus effort, and its lesson is Nozick's: distributions should not be constructed to match a formula.
