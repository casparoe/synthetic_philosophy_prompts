ok so I spent about three hours last night arguing with a friend about this and I'm not sleeping until someone tells me which of us is right, so you're the someone. She's a hard individualist — "there are people, that's it, everything else is a manner of speaking" — and her charge against me was that my package is incoherent, that I'm a reductionist about groups on weekdays and a holist about them on weekends. I told her that's cheap and she couldn't point at the actual contradiction. But now I'm not so sure she couldn't. Here's what I hold, written down because our social ontology reading group is doing a round of these next week:

1. There's no such thing as a group mind. When people do something together, that's nothing over and above a bunch of individual attitudes meshing — each of us intending that we J, each planning on the other playing her part in a shared plan, plus the mutual knowledge that we're at it. No irreducible we-intention floating around in a super-head.

2. Even so, some groups are literal agents. A research institute, a company's board. When one of them screws up I think its proper — not a figure of speech, not shorthand — to blame the group, and in some cases to hold the group responsible in a way that isn't just a distributive way of blaming the members.

3. I'm fine with, actually I like, the result that a group can end up committed to a position that not one single member holds, because the members' votes come apart across issues and the group's own procedure stitches the results into something consistent. To me that's a feature and not a bug.

4. What makes a group an agent, on my view, is that it has a standing procedure for settling what it's committed to when the members disagree. That procedure, not anybody's psychology, is where the group's intentions live.

5. Separately: if you and I are doing something together — walking to the shop, shifting a sofa up the stairs — we each acquire an obligation to the other and an entitlement to rebuke if one of us just wanders off. Joint activity isn't only coordinated psychology, it's normative. A pair with no such entitlements isn't really acting jointly, they're just happening to move in parellel.

6. And the one-off case is the derivative one for me. The basic unit isn't the pickup game, it's the standing arrangement — the band, the co-parenting setup, the lab with an understood rule for what happens when someone flakes. That's what joint action fundamentally is; ad hoc cases are the arrangement stretched thin.

7. I also think a parent and a two-year-old can genuinely build a block tower together. Actually together, not pretend-together. But I don't think a dog and a human can, whatever people say about that.
