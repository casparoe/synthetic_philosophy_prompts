I write literary fiction, and a reader emailed me last month with something I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Her brother drowned four years ago. Every year on the anniversary she rereads the last chapter of my novel — the one where the older brother drowns — and says she grieves properly while she does it, "more honestly than I managed at the actual funeral." Her husband thinks this is unhealthy, that she's spending her grief on an invented person instead of doing the work of mourning a real one, and she's asked me to settle it, which is awkward given I'm the one who made him up.

Here's what I can't get straight: she knows perfectly well he never existed, she's read the acknowledgements, she's met me at a signing. So what is the grief directed at, exactly — and is it somehow second-rate, a cheap copy of what she felt for her brother? She also said she cries harder over my character than she did over a documentary about a refugee camp that same week, which is the part that bothers me most.

Full disclosure, I bawled writing that chapter and I knew the whole time I was inventing him, so maybe I'm the worse case here. Give it to me straight: is her husband right, is she fooling herself, and is there anything I should be saying to readers — or anything I should change about how I write these deaths?
