I work as a paralegal at a small immigration legal aid shop, so I sit in on a lot of arguements between attorneys, and there's one line that keeps coming up that I've never been able to pin down: "an unjust law is no law at all." Last week two different people used it to make almost opposite points and I couldn't tell whether they were disagreeing or just talking past each other.

First case. A statute that's procedurally spotless — passed the right way, published, clear, applies going forward, enforced the same against everyone, no secret guidance memos — but what it requires is monstrous, we're talking about sending people back to a place where they'll very likely be killed. My supervising attorney says the slogan covers this: it isn't really law, so a judge shouldn't apply it.

Second case, the reverse. A policy that's substantively fine — nobody in the office thinks the outcome it produces is unjust — but it was never published anywhere, it gets applied retroactively to things people did before it existed, and what the officials actually do doesn't match what's on paper. A different attorney says the slogan covers this one too, and that it isn't law either.

Those two can't both be what the sentence means, or so it seems to me. I think the background here is some famous mid-century fight between a positivist and a natural lawyer, Hart and Fuller maybe, but I don't know it and I don't want to go read it, I want the analysis in front of me.

So: break that sentence down into the distinct claims it could be making. I want the full menu, from the ones that are just word games up to the ones that would actually blow up a legal system if you took them seriously. For each reading tell me (a) what precisely it claims, (b) whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false, and (c) whether it's a claim about the word "law" or about the thing the word picks out, because I suspect those are getting mixed up constantly. Some of these readings should be claims about validity, some about what judges are authorized to apply, some about what citizens owe, and some maybe aren't claims at all — flag which is which.

There's also an axis I keep tripping over: some people clearly mean it only for extreme, spectacular injustice and others seem to mean it for any injustice whatsoever. Tell me whether that's a separate reading or a dial on several of the readings, and whether turning the dial changes what kind of thing the claim is.

Then answer the questions I actually care about. Is there any single reading that covers both of my cases, or does the slogan only look like one idea when it's really two? Which reading was my supervisor invoking, and would that reading, if true, get him what he wants — a judge refusing to apply a valid statute? Which reading was the other attorney invoking? And when people trot the line out to shut down an argument, which reading are they usually leaning on, and would it survive being stated out loud?

Approach this as a legal philosopher who's taught this material for decades and has opinions. Do some rough brainstorming out loud first — including uses that aren't truth-apt at all, honorific uses, expressive uses — then give me the cleaned-up list, because I care a lot more about the final list being exhaustive and non-overlapping than about the draft.

One more thing: commit. I don't want a neutral survey where every reading gets an "it depends." Give me your verdict on each one, say which you think is the most defensible version of the slogan overall, and if you think one of my attorneys is simply wrong, say so and say why.
