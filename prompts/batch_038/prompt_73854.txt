I am one of two TAs for a fourth-year seminar on collective action and moral responsibility, and I have been given the take-home final to mark. The instructor's question was this, worth 25 marks with a suggested length of about 600 words: "State the inefficacy objection to individual abstention duties, using factory-farmed meat as your example, and identify the premises it depends on. Explain how Kagan's expected-harm reply is meant to answer it, and assess whether that reply survives the objection that real supply chains contain buffers or margins of slack. Finally, explain whether voting in a large election is relevantly like the meat case or relevantly unlike it, and what your answer implies for the inefficacy objection." The rubric weights accuracy in stating the arguments at half the marks, the quality of the student's own assessment at thirty per cent, and clarity and organisation at twenty per cent. I have read the answer twice and formed a provisional impression, but I would rather not tell you what it is, since I suspect it would anchor you.

Here is the answer in full.

The inefficacy objection has two premises. The first is causal: if I stop buying factory-farmed meat, exactly the same number of animals will be raised and killed, because a single purchase is absorbed into a system that orders in batches of tens of thousands and would have restocked anyway. The second is moral: an act can be wrong only if it makes some difference to how things go for someone. Putting the two together, buying the chicken is not wrong, and nothing I do at the checkout counter is either. The same structure generates Sinnott-Armstrong's conclusion about the climate: because one person's emissions are negligible against the background of the atmosphere, driving a gas-guzzler for the pleasure of it on a Sunday afternoon is morally permissible.

Kagan's reply attacks the causal premise and leaves the moral premise alone, which is the right way round, since the moral premise is much harder to deny. His point is that the question is not whether my purchase causes harm but whether it raises the expected harm. Because retailers restock in fixed batches, purchases accumulate until some threshold is reached and a fresh order for thousands of birds is placed. My purchase has some small probability p of being the one that triggers that order, and its expected harm is p multiplied by the suffering caused by the whole batch, which is not zero. So each purchase does some expected harm, and the objection collapses. This is Parfit's harmless torturers applied to poultry: in Parfit's case each of the thousand torturers turns the dial by one degree, which is too small for any victim to notice, so each torturer is below the threshold at which harm begins, but Parfit holds that each of them is obliged not to be the one who pushes past it.

I do not think the reply succeeds. Budolfson's buffer objection is that actual supply chains hold safety margins wide enough that the probability that one purchase triggers additional production is not small-but-nonzero but effectively zero, in which case the expected harm is zero and Kagan's arithmetic has no input to work on. Nefsky then makes the deeper point that expected benefit is simply the wrong frame for these cases, which shows that the inefficacy objection is right after all: if an individual act has no expected effect, it is not the kind of thing that morality can require of anyone.

Voting is relevantly unlike meat-buying. In a close election my vote can literally decide the result, and because elections do not have buffers in the way that supply chains do, voting is a case where individuals make a difference while meat-eating is a case where they do not. If voting were like meat-buying, we would have no reason to vote at all, and since we plainly do have reason to vote, the two cases come apart.

The whole exchange assumes a consequentialist framework, incidentally. A deontologist could hold that taking part in a harmful practice is wrong whatever its expected effects, which would make the inefficacy objection beside the point rather than answered.
