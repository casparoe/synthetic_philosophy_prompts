I am Head of Collections Care at a municipal museum of applied art and historical science, and I need a written decision procedure for a question that reaches us several times a month but that we currently settle by instinct. Our accession register, our insurance schedule, our loan agreements and our gallery labels all rest on claims of the form "this is the same object we acquired in 1931", and after decades of repair, consolidation, replacement, dismantling and reassembly such claims are frequently questionable. Our informal practice is a rule I inherited and now distrust: an object survives so long as more than half of its original material, measured by mass, remains present. I can see at least three defects in it. Mass is the wrong kind of measure. It says nothing whatever about an object whose every part was replaced one at a time over a century. And it silently assumes that "same object" means one thing, when in fact we ask it in three distinct registers: for insurance and indemnity, for attribution and authorship, and for what may truthfully be printed on a label or asserted in a loan agreement.

Please convert what the philosophy of material constitution genuinely offers — the sortal-relativity of identity claims, the dominant-kind view, the closest-continuer approach, the four-dimensionalist option of treating an object as extended across time rather than wholly present at it, and the difficulty that arises when two candidate objects coincide in one place at one moment — into a procedure that conservators and registrars with no philosophical training can run. It should be a numbered sequence of questions, each with a stated sufficient answer; it should identify the point at which it terminates in "the object recorded under this accession number no longer exists: close the record and open a new one"; it should identify the point at which it terminates in "this is a question of institutional policy or of value, not of fact: refer it to the Director and the trustees"; and it should say candidly which steps are judgement calls dressed as procedure. We allow a fortnight per case and the reasoning must fit on a single sheet, so the instrument has to be short enough to run inside those constraints.

We are also introducing an Object Continuity Ledger, recording for each intervention the component affected, the proportion of that component's material replaced, whether the intervention was reversible, and whether the removed matter was retained in store. Specify what further fields the ledger must carry if your procedure is to be runnable from it at all.

Then run the procedure on three live cases.

Case A. An eighteenth-century longcase regulator by a provincial maker, accessioned complete in 1931. Every component of the movement except the frame plates and one wheel has since been replaced, at intervals, by successive curators; each removed part was retained, and together the stored parts suffice to reassemble the movement as it stood in 1931. The case, dial and finial are original. The clock is on open display and runs. A donor-restricted bequest attaches to the regulator as acquired, and a planned redisplay would exhibit the running clock and the reassembled 1931 movement side by side.

Case B. A kinetic light installation of the 1960s, acquired in 1979 under a certificate from the artist's estate authorising substitution of components as they become obsolete or unserviceable, provided each substitution is recorded. Eleven substitutions have been made. The last component dating from original manufacture — a drive motor — failed this year and a functionally equivalent motor has been fitted. We must now tell an indemnifying lender what proportion of the work is original and whether the work itself survives.

Case C. A fragmentary ceramic vessel excavated in the 1920s and acquired by us in 1954. Roughly two-fifths of the wall is ancient; the remainder is modern fill, painted to match. It has been dismantled and reassembled twice, and each reassembly introduced further new fill. An external institution proposes to borrow it on condition that the accompanying label describes it as an original vessel of its period, and my registrar wants to know whether that sentence would be true.

In at least one of these three I expect an answer I shall not like; I would far rather the procedure say so plainly than find a route around it, so please do not soften the verdict where the verdict is unwelcome. Where the three registers come apart — where the answer for insurance is not the answer for the label — say so explicitly and state which of them governs the accession number itself.

Finally, set out what the procedure cannot do: the classes of object and intervention it will misclassify, the disputes it leaves untouched, and the points at which a well-run museum must simply make a choice and record that it has done so.
