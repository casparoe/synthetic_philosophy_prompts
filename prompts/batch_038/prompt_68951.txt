Please take on the role of a philosopher of mathematics and mind who has spent years teaching formal systems and Gödel's incompleteness theorems to audiences with no technical background, and who knows Douglas Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* well enough to separate what it actually argues from what readers commonly take it to argue. Before drafting anything, think the problem through carefully: work out the mathematics of the puzzle yourself, rule by rule, and open your reply with a short paragraph brainstorming where the real crux of the question lies and where you expect students to go wrong. Only then produce the materials.

I should explain my situation, because it determines what I need. I have no academic training of any kind; I left school at sixteen and spent my working life as a draughtsman. For seven years I have run a monthly reading group of about nine adults in the back room of our public library. We are not a course and nobody receives credit, but we like to be held to a standard, so each year I set the group one take-home question and mark the essays against a model answer prepared by someone more knowledgeable than I am. This year we have spent four meetings on Hofstadter's MU puzzle and on the idea, which I take from him, of jumping out of the system: the difference between grinding away inside a set of rules and stepping back to reason about what those rules can and cannot produce.

The question should turn on an idea of my own, which may well be confused and which I want the assessment to test rather than assume. Over the years I have noticed two distinct things happening at our table. Some members arrive unaided at the observation that the number of I's can never be a multiple of three. Others reach it only after somebody says, count the I's. I have been calling the first jumping proper and the second jumping by proxy, and I have assumed the distinction matters only if the two differ in kind rather than merely in speed. Last winter I put the same puzzle to a conversational AI. It floundered, and produced several strings that break the rules. When I added one line to my message — consider how many I's there are, modulo three — it wrote a correct impossibility proof within seconds. On another occasion, when I asked it only for code, it wrote a program enumerating every string derivable within twenty steps and reported that MU had not been found within that bound.

Please write the following:

1. One take-home question in three parts, answerable in ninety minutes and roughly nine hundred words. Part (a) should require the student to prove that MU cannot be derived, and should be phrased so that skipping the delicate step in that proof costs marks. Part (b) should require a definition of jumping out of the system precise enough to be applied to a case, and to fail to apply to one — not a slogan. Part (c) should set out my three cases (the unaided group member, the AI given the one-line hint, the AI that produced the bounded search) and ask whether each involves jumping out of the system, and whether my jumping-proper / jumping-by-proxy distinction survives. The question must be entirely self-contained: state the axiom and all four transformation rules in full inside it, define every term, assume no knowledge of logical notation or set theory, and do not ask for citations, references, or a reading list — nobody in my group could chase those. Likewise, do not put any words in Hofstadter's mouth inside quotation marks; paraphrase instead.

2. A model answer of about 750 words.

3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, built around five must-hit points, giving the marks each is worth and, for each, one line naming the way it is most often botched.

4. A short list of four or five wrong turns that should cost marks even in an otherwise fluent essay.

Where the difficulty should come from: I want this to be unanswerable by anyone who has merely memorised the labels. I have a suspicion, which you should correct if it is wrong, that the discriminating point about my third case has nothing to do with whether the reasoning was done by a machine. A bounded enumeration does not establish the impossibility result at all, whereas the invariant argument establishes an unbounded claim by a finite one; so the three cases may differ in what is established rather than in who or what established it. If you agree, design part (c) so that a student who reaches immediately for mechanical versus insightful loses marks. If you disagree, say so in your brainstorming paragraph and build the question around the real crux instead.
