Our national federation comprises 34 independent home-care cooperatives, each owned by its own aides and clinicians. I sit on its governance committee, and I have been asked to prepare the paper the committee will work from at Thursday's meeting. Two petitions are before us, and they pull in opposite directions.

The federation's charter grants the national board a short list of enumerated powers and provides that any matter not among them shall be settled at the level closest to the worker-members that is capable of settling it effectively. It also provides that the board itself determines which level that is.

The metro cooperative, our largest, has voted by a wide margin to pay its aides 18 percent above the federation's common wage floor and to substitute continuous remote monitoring for the charter's requirement of a weekly in-person nursing visit for stable clients. It asks the board to recognize both measures as within its own competence. Two adjoining cooperatives object that aides living near the boundary have transferred or begun commuting to the metro co-op, that clients are requesting transfer to it, and that neither of them can match the wage. The metro co-op replies that it owes its neighbors nothing for their own choices, that its members voted, and that the point of a federation is to let chapters try things.

The rural cooperative, our smallest and weakest, asks permission to go the other way: to pay below the wage floor and to relax the nursing requirement further, warning that it will dissolve within a year otherwise. A group of its aides has written to the board separately, arguing that a vote taken under threat of dissolution is not a free one and that the board should protect them from their own cooperative's decision. The rural co-op calls this paternalism and points out that its aides voted too.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a disposition for each petition, with any conditions and a review date; give reasons the two cooperatives could actually respond to, rather than resting on the charter's bare text; propose a standing rule the board can apply to future petitions without relitigating first principles each time; and set out the strongest version of the case against your recommended dispositions — whichever cooperative your ruling disappoints more — together with why you think it nonetheless fails.

One thing I would like the memo to confront rather than sidestep: both petitions turn on what makes a level capable of settling a matter, and the more I try to specify that, the more it looks as though a chapter counts as capable only when its decisions stop at its own borders, which in a federation this interconnected would leave almost nothing to the chapters. I also cannot see my way around the fact that the board is being asked to use a power to fix the boundaries of decentralization that the decentralizing clause itself seems to distrust. If you think that worry is mistaken, say so and explain why; if you think it is not, the memo should still end somewhere the board can act. Two pages is plenty.
