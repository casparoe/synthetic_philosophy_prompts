I am a retired physical chemist. For thirty-five years my professional life consisted of analytical measurement: calibration hierarchies and traceability chains, uncertainty budgets, limits of detection and quantitation, resolution as against repeatability, scale types and the transformations each admits, and the unglamorous daily task of deciding when two numbers genuinely differ and when they differ only within the noise. In retirement I have turned to philosophy and become absorbed by the literature on incommensurable values and hard choices — the claim that some pairs of options are neither better than, worse than, nor exactly equal in value to one another; the small-improvement argument offered in its support, according to which a slight improvement to one option fails to make it the better of the two, which is said to show that the two were never precisely equal to begin with; the proposal that a fourth relation, parity, does the work; and the voluntarist conclusion that in such choices the agent must commit and thereby settle the matter rather than discover it.

I can follow these arguments, but I cannot audit them, because none of them is stated in a vocabulary in which I know how to check a claim. I should like you to restate the position as a whole in the vocabulary of measurement that I actually use: measurands and units, concatenation and the other operations that underwrite a scale, resolution and detection limits, the propagation of uncertainty, calibration and traceability, the admissible transformations of ordinal, interval, and ratio scales, and the distinction between a quantity that is undefined and one that is merely unmeasurable by me. Be as literal as you can manage, and let me see how far literalness will carry you.

Then give me a residue report. Sort every component of the philosophical position into three classes: what translates cleanly and without loss; what translates only with a distortion, which you must describe precisely and say what it costs; and what cannot be expressed in measurement vocabulary at all. For the third class I want a judgment rather than a shrug — is the failure a limitation of my discipline, which has simply never needed to say such a thing, or is it evidence that the philosophical claim is less determinate than its confident presentation suggests? Say which, and why, item by item.

Two further requirements. First, do not settle the dispute by importing the presuppositions of my framework. The inference that where there is no unit there is no quantity is readily available to me, and it is precisely what begs the question here, since the philosophical claim concerns whether the comparative relation holds, not whether anyone is able to measure it. Flag each point at which you feel that inference pulling at you, and say how you resisted it. Second, I anticipate that the most seductive mapping will be the one between an undetectable difference and an absent difference: my instruments report all day long that a difference lies below my uncertainty, which is not the same as reporting that no difference exists. Treat that mapping as a hazard to be handled explicitly rather than as a bridge on which to build the translation. Relatedly, where the literature offers competing diagnoses of the phenomenon — vagueness in the comparative predicates, several dimensions with no rate of exchange among them, non-Archimedean structure, or a genuine fourth relation — tell me which of these has a real counterpart in measurement theory and which only sounds like one.

Please write in American English, and give me the argument rather than its provenance: no bibliography, no citations, and no attribution of positions to named authors.
