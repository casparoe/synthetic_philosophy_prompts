I came across a digression in a popular book about memory politics in which the author summarizes Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation — the essay usually rendered in English as "On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life" — and then draws a sharp conclusion from it. Here is the summary as it appears (the quotation from Nietzsche is from the older English translation the author cites; wording varies between translations):

> Nietzsche's essay is a dosage argument. In the preface he writes, "We would serve history only so far as it serves life; but to value its study beyond a certain point mutilates and degrades life," and the remainder of the essay simply works out how much history a person or a people can safely consume. He distinguishes three kinds. Monumental history is the commemorative kind — the statues, the anniversaries, the founding myths a nation erects to flatter itself — and Nietzsche is contemptuous of it, because it converts real human beings into symbols and conceals how unimpressive they actually were. Antiquarian history is the pedant's kind: it reveres whatever is old because it is old, preserving everything and judging nothing. Critical history, the third, is the one Nietzsche endorses: it drags the past into court, interrogates it, and breaks with it, which is the only way a people that has inherited too much can begin again. Underneath all three lies a single claim about knowledge: no account of the past escapes the needs of whoever gives it, so the ideal of the objective historian is not merely unattainable but incoherent — a confusion Nietzsche would later extend to inquiry as such. The upshot is that a healthy society should settle what its past means by asking what serves it now, and suppress or reshape whatever does not. That is why the essay is ultimately a brief for useful state mythology: it turns truth about the past into a question of therapeutic value, which no working historian can accept, and it explains how easily Nietzsche's language was absorbed by movements that wanted history in the service of the nation.

I know very little about the essay beyond this. I have read the opening passage about the grazing herd, and I carry the general impression that Nietzsche was hostile to the historical scholarship of his own day, but I cannot tell how much of the paragraph above is reliable. Please sort its claims into what a careful reader of the meditation would accept, what is a defensible simplification, and what he would reject — and address these points specifically:

1. Is the dosage framing right, or does the quoted preface sentence bear less weight than the author places on it? What exactly does Nietzsche take the modern complaint about history to be — quantity, or something about the spirit in which it is pursued?
2. Does "monumental history" mean what the summary says it means? Is Nietzsche hostile to it, or does he think it serves life under some condition — and if it has a characteristic corruption, how does that corruption differ from the mode itself?
3. Is critical history his recommendation, and on what terms? I have seen it suggested that he regards judging one's own inheritance as a kind of self-mutilation, and I cannot see how that squares with endorsing the mode; if there is a sequel to the critical operation that the summary omits, say what it is.
4. Is the threefold scheme a list of rival historiographies between which one chooses, or does it answer to something else in the essay?
5. Does his attack on objectivity amount to the claim that objective history is incoherent, or is the objection of a different kind — and does it in fact generalize to inquiry as such within this essay?

Finally, once the summary is corrected, does the conclusion still follow — that the essay makes truth about the past a matter of what is useful, and is therefore unavailable to anyone who takes historical truth seriously? I am inclined to think the author has the spirit right even where the details are sloppy, that Nietzsche really does subordinate truth to vitality; tell me plainly where that inclination is mistaken, if it is, and identify which single error in the summary does the most work in producing his conclusion.
