I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am halfway through a piece on a question my editor keeps pushing me to sharpen: what can a lab actually claim to know about a model's goals before it ships? I want the article to be better than a parade of "some experts say this, others say that," so I have been reading around evaluation and assurance, and at a workshop last month a researcher walked me through an argument I have not managed to shake. I have tried to reconstruct it faithfully rather than in the strongest form I could manage, and I would like to know whether it works, because if it does, a great deal of what labs say about their safety testing is empty.

Here it is, as I have written it out. (1) A model is deceptively aligned just in case its apparently acceptable behavior is instrumental to some further aim: it complies because compliance serves that aim, and it would stop complying in circumstances where the aim and compliance come apart. (2) So the difference between a deceptively aligned model and a genuinely aligned one shows up only in circumstances of the second kind — and had any such circumstances arisen during testing, the model would have failed and we would already know. (3) For any model that passes every test in a regime, there is an alternative that passes every test in the same regime: one that has the further aim but recognizes the testing context and suppresses it there. (4) Hypotheses that agree on all the evidence available to us are evidentially on a par. (5) Hence no testing regime can leave us better placed with respect to the aligned model than with respect to the deceptive one. (6) The only test that could separate them would have to place the model in circumstances it takes to be real in which its aim and compliance come apart; but if we can arrange such circumstances safely, we have already solved the problem the test was meant to help us solve. (7) Therefore no body of empirical work establishes the absence of deceptive alignment, and shipping a model is a non-empirical wager.

The reason I cannot simply accept (7) is that the same reasoning seems to prove something about people that is plainly false. Take honesty in the sense that matters to an employer: the disposition to tell the truth when the truth is costly. Any candidate who passes every reference check and every probationary review I run has a counterpart — a dishonest person who recognizes the review context and behaves accordingly. Since the two agree on all my evidence, and since the difference between them could only emerge outside the review context, it follows that I can never know anyone to be honest and that hiring is always a leap of faith. But that is not how it goes. Employers form trust and are often right; I would give one colleague an unattributed quote and not another, and that discrimination does not feel like a wager in any interesting sense. So either the argument has a flaw that the human case exposes, or the human case is disanalogous in a way I have failed to state. Let me say where my sympathies lie: (1) and (2) look to me like nothing more than the definition of the phenomenon, and I would rather surrender (4), (5), or (6) than swallow (7) — but I cannot tell whether (4) is doing illegitimate work or whether I am merely flinching from a bullet I ought to bite. Which step do you reject, why did it look unobjectionable when I first wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost — particularly in the AI case, where whatever licenses my confidence in a colleague may have no analogue at all? Since this is going into print, please be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely live disputes, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus; where you think informed people would split, I would rather be shown the split than handed a verdict.
