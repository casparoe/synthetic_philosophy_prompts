Next month I present a workshop paper to a mixed room: mostly cross-cultural psychologists who work inside the tightness–looseness program, several of them involved in building the multinational survey instruments; two political economists; and one quantitative methodologist who works on causal inference with observational data. My thesis is deliberately unfriendly. Tightness, I argue, is not a cultural primitive but an artifact of sanctioning density: what the scale actually tracks is a respondent's expectation of being observed and punished by third parties. Condition on the reach of enforcement institutions — state policing, religious sanctioning, dense kin and village monitoring — and on the respondent's own exposure to sanction risk (urban versus rural, majority versus minority status, public-sector employment), and the residual national-level variance that does the explanatory work in the literature largely evaporates. The ecological-threat story then runs backwards: threat builds enforcement capacity, and enforcement capacity produces both the behavior and the self-reports we code as tight.
