We're showing Memento at our department's film night and I'm writing the handout that goes with it. I don't want the usual memory-and-personal-identity gloss, I want to read it against a decision-theory puzzle instead, and I'd like you to do the actual reading so I have something to argue with.

The puzzle is the absent-minded driver, the Piccione and Rubinstein one. A driver has to leave a highway at one of two exits, but once he's moving he can't tell which exit he's at — no landmarks, no memory of the last junction. So all he can do is fix a plan before he sets out. The odd part is that the plan which is best from the start looks wrong once he's sitting at an intersection, and that happens even though nothing has changed: not his preferences, not his information. He's just lost track of where in the problem he is. Dynamic inconsistency with no change of mind at all.

Leonard is obviously that driver — he's literally driving around not knowing why. But here's the bit I keep coming back to and I want you to take it seriously instead of waving at it. The driver only has to cope with a hole in his memory. Leonard fills the hole in advance, with notes, polaroids and tattoos he wrote earlier and that his later self will trust without being able to check. And at the end he doesn't just leave a record, he corrupts one: he deliberately installs a lead he knows is false. So I've started calling this the poisoned signpost case — one where the only way to make sure your ex ante plan actually gets executed is to wreck the evidence your future self would use to decide whether to execute it. Odysseus and the mast is self-binding. A conversion is a preference change. Is the poisoned signpost a third thing, or is it just self-binding where the rope is made out of lies? And does the decision-theoretic setup even have room for it, given that the driver case quietly assumes the future self's inputs are honest?

Then the structure. Because the film runs backwards, the audience only ever gets the in-the-moment perspective, and the planning perspective — why any of this was arranged — arrives last. Does that make the film take the acting self's side against the planner, or does it show the acting self as a puppet of a planner who no longer exists? I want a view here, not both-sides.

And Sammy Jankis. He's the story Leonard tells about a man with the same condition who couldn't learn from consequences, and it turns out to be at least partly Leonard's own. Read that as the film's little experiment in which memory substitutes actually work — conditioning versus notes versus writing on your own body, i.e. precommitment devices of different rigidity. Is Sammy the case where the device fails, and Leonard's the case where it succeeds at something horrible?

Bring in Parfit's point that it can be rational to make yourself irrational, and say whether Leonard's final act is irrational from the planning seat and rational from the intersection, or whether one of those two verdicts is bogus.

Two honesty requirements. First, tell me where the analogy breaks. Leonard has no repeatable payoff structure, his plan was made by a person who's gone, and its genuinely unclear whether he still wants the stated goal at all — maybe that makes the ending a preference change after all and deflates the whole reading. If that's your view, say so. Second, don't flatten the film into an illustration of the puzzle; end by saying what Memento shows about imperfect recall that the puzzle can't state, if it does show anything.

About a thousand words. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — is Memento a counterexample to the driver result, an illustration of it, or a case the framework can't represent — and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating what I asked. After that, a short list of three or four scenes I should cue in the handout, one sentence describing each, no quotes since I can't check them and neither can you. Then a closing paragraph on what the film adds. Don't worry about spoilers, half the room has commited the ending to memory already.
