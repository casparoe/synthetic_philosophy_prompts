so I'm in year 12 doing an EPQ and my topic is basically the technological determinism debate, i.e. whether artefacts have politics or whether that's all a bit of a stretch. Along the way I've ended up inventing a test and I want you to try and break it. Could you write me an essay on it? Not for handing in obviously, it's for me to argue with.

My test — I'm calling it the swap test, because I couldnt think of anything better — goes like this. Take some artefact. Ask whether a designer working with the same budget, the same materials and the same physics could have built something that does the same job for the same users but distributes power differently. If they could, then the politics isn't in the artefact, it's in whoever picked that version, and the artefact is just a neutral tool with someone's decision frozen into it. If they couldn't — if doing that job at all requires handing power to some particular group — then the artefact genuinely has politics, in the strong sense people mean when they say technology is never neutral.

The point of it is to stop people calling everything political. My teacher's line is that basically every artefact embeds values, and I find that abit cheap, because if everything is political then the word isn't doing any work and the debate is over before it starts.

Cases I've already got, feel free to add your own:

1. The one from our reading, with the parkway bridges built too low for buses to get under, so the beach at the end of them was realistically only for people with cars. Someone in my class reckons the history of that story doesn't actually check out, but assume it does for the sake of the argument. My test says neutral, because you could have built the same bridges a foot higher and nothing else about them changes. Except everyone treats that as the textbook case of a political artefact, so either my test is wrong or everyone else is definately wrong, and I can't tell which.

2. Our school put swipe-card turnstiles in at the gates, for safeguarding. Swap test says neutral, a paper register does the same job. Except the log exists now and it's already being used for detentions and for moaning at parents, and doing all that by hand would've been so tedious nobody would've bothered. So maybe the right question isn't "could you swap it" but "does it make some bit of behaviour cheap enough that it happens".

3. A lock on a door. For it to do its job it has to exclude everyone without a key, so power sits with whoever holds the key and there's no swap available. Which means my test says a lock is political in the strong sense. That feels like a reductio tbh.

So: write an essay weighing up whether the swap test actually picks out the thing people are arguing about, and come down on one side — keep it, patch it (and say exactly how the patch works), or bin it. Don't end on a both-sides shrug, the verdict is the whole point. Then tell me what would change your mind: which of those cases, or which consideration I haven't thought of, would actually flip your verdict rather than just making you hedge, and where in your own reasoning is the weakest link, the step you'd most expect to fall apart if someone pushed it. Also don't stick a reading list on the end, I can't chase any of it up, I just want the argument.
