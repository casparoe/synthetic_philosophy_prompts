I am drafting the conceptual framing section for an interdisciplinary research programme on complex systems, in which statistical physicists, economists and cognitive scientists will jointly model phenomena of a broadly similar shape: phase transitions, credit crunches, collective motion, regime shifts in ecosystems. Three philosophers sit on the advisory board, and the framing has to state our commitments precisely enough to survive their reading. I should like your help getting the philosophy right before I commit it to paper.

My understanding of where the reduction debate has landed is as follows. The exclusion argument, in the form developed by Kim, shows that a property which is multiply realisable at the base level cannot be causally efficacious: the physical realiser is already causally sufficient for whatever effects the property is credited with, so the higher-level property must either be identical to something at the base level or else be causally idle. The multiple realisability arguments, in the form developed by Fodor, show that the central kinds of the special sciences — belief, recession, synchronisation, resilience — are in fact multiply realisable, and that this heterogeneity of realisers is exactly what makes such kinds indispensable for generalisation, since no single physical description would cover all the cases that support the same law-like claim. Taken together these results appear to give us a stable and rather comfortable division of labour: higher-level kinds are explanatorily indispensable and causally inert, and so the emergence that the special sciences traffic in is real, but epistemic rather than ontological. Nothing needs to be surrendered in either direction — the physicist keeps causal sufficiency at the bottom, and the economist keeps her generalisations at the top.

A colleague has also pointed me to formal work that defines measures of causal emergence over coarse-grained descriptions of a system, and that reportedly finds the measure peaking not at the finest available scale of description but somewhere in between. So far as I can tell this confirms the picture above rather than merely permitting it: the higher level is doing genuine causal work relative to our models, even though everything about it can in principle be accounted for from below.

Two requests. First, please write the framing passages themselves — around four hundred words — setting out this position in terms a mixed audience of physicists and social scientists will accept without suspecting us either of mysticism or of eliminativism about their disciplines. Second, and more immediately practical: given that our candidate higher-level variables are on this account causally inert yet explanatorily indispensable, what criterion ought our modellers to apply when deciding which coarse-grained quantities — order parameters, macroeconomic aggregates, network motifs, diversity indices — are entitled to a causal interpretation in an intervention study, and which must be handled as bookkeeping devices over the micro-level dynamics? I would like the criterion stated in a form that could actually be operationalised by a modelling team, not merely as a philosophical distinction.

Please write your answer in British English.
