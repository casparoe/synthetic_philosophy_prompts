Consider the following claim, which I will call the Parity Claim.

**Parity Claim:** There exists at least one widespread local convention of the ordinary, non-legal kind — the sort that governs how much one tips, whether one joins the back of a queue, or whether one crosses against a signal — such that a well-informed adult visitor who is present in the community only briefly, has no relationships there, expects no further interactions with its members, and faces no legal sanction for noncompliance is nevertheless under a genuine moral obligation to comply, in a case where compliance is personally costly and where that visitor's single act of noncompliance is undetectable and imposes no measurable cost on anyone.

By "genuine moral obligation" I mean a consideration belonging to morality proper, such that noncompliance would be a moral failing (however minor) rather than a breach of etiquette, an act of imprudence, or a lapse of taste; and such that the visitor cannot dissolve the question by observing that this is not their community's norm.

My own credence in the Parity Claim is about 0.62. A colleague's is about 0.15. Neither of us can currently articulate the derivation behind our number, which is the reason I am asking.

Please give your own credence in the Parity Claim as a single number, and then show the work: an explicit decomposition into sub-questions whose probabilities combine into that number. The following are candidate terms. You should add, drop, merge, or reorder them as your reasoning requires, and say so when you do.

(a) the probability that conventional norms of this kind generate moral reasons at all, rather than reasons of etiquette, prudence, or local taste;

(b) the probability that whatever grounds those reasons — the frustrated expectations of those who rely on the practice, the free-riding on general compliance, the disrespect shown to a community whose members do bear the costs, the value of coordination as such — survives application to a stranger who will shortly leave;

(c) the probability that the particular features of my case (undetectability, no measurable cost, brief visit, no future interaction) defeat the grounds identified in (b), rather than merely weakening them;

(d) the probability that "obligation" here denotes a real but community-relative status — binding on members, silent as to visitors — such that the Parity Claim requests something conceptually confused;

(e) the probability that there is a determinate fact of the matter at all, as against a family of equally defensible precisifications of "moral obligation" between which nothing decides.

State the combination rule you are actually using. Where it is not a plain product, explain why not; in particular, say whether you are treating the terms as independent and, if not, what you are doing instead and what that does to the arithmetic.

Then identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive — the one whose probability, shifted by a fixed increment, moves the conclusion furthest — and say what kind of argument, case, or distinction would move that term and in which direction. Finally, using your decomposition, say where the gap between my 0.62 and my colleague's 0.15 most plausibly lives: which term or terms we are most likely scoring very differently, and whether the disagreement so located is one that argument could settle or one that reflects divergent conceptions of what morality is for.

I am not asking for a survey of positions in the literature, and I do not want references of any kind; I want your derivation.

**Format.** Carry out the reasoning in prose, in whatever order serves it. Then append a machine-readable summary consisting of a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `claim`: the proposition assessed, restated in one sentence.
- `credence`: a number between 0 and 1.
- `terms`: an array of objects, one per sub-question, each with `id` (a short label), `statement` (the sub-question, phrased so that it has a truth value or a clear resolution condition), `probability` (a number between 0 and 1), `justification` (one or two sentences), and `dependence_note` (how this term relates probabilistically to the others: independent, or conditionally dependent on which, and in what direction).
- `combination_rule`: a string giving the formula or procedure by which the term probabilities yield the credence, written so that the number could be recomputed from `terms` alone.
- `independence_assessment`: an object with `verdict` (one of `independent`, `partly_dependent`, `strongly_dependent`) and `effect` (one sentence on what the dependence does to the recomputation).
- `most_sensitive_term`: an object with `id` and `reason`.
- `sensitivity_magnitude`: an object with `shift` (the increment applied to that term's probability), `direction`, and `resulting_change` (the approximate change in the final credence, signed).
- `best_mover`: an object with `intervention` (the argument, case, or distinction most likely to shift that term) and `direction`.
- `disagreement_locus`: an array of one to three objects, each with `id` and `why` (one sentence on why the 0.62 and 0.15 positions most likely diverge there).
- `settleable`: an object with `verdict` (one of `by_argument`, `by_conceptual_clarification`, `not_settleable`) and `justification` (one sentence).
- `confidence_in_decomposition`: a number between 0 and 1 expressing how confident you are that these are the right terms, as distinct from arriving at a defensible final number by way of a mis-structured derivation.

Every probability must be a definite number, not a range or a hedge. Do not add fields to the schema; if a field does not apply, place a short explicit statement of why in place of its usual content rather than omitting it.
