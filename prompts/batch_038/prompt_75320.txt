I'm the recording secretary for a tenants' association in a mid-sized city, which mostly means I take minutes at meetings that should have ended two hours earlier. Last Thursday our board spent four of them on a proposal from the housing authority, and two board members ended up in an arguement I've been turning over ever since. The proposal attaches a participation requirement to the municipal rent subsidy: every adult receiving it would have to log 16 hours a week of an approved activity — paid work, a course off the city's approved list, care for a relative with a disability, or a volunteer placement the city assigns — and if you miss two months running the subsidy steps down 25%, then 50%, then it's gone entirely. There's an appeal, there's an exemption for anyone with a medical certificate and for single parents of children under three, and a caseworker certifies each person's hours. The authority's stated rationale was twofold: it keeps the program politically supportable, and it stops the subsidy looking like something people get for nothing.

The two arguing were Marcus, who's run a community legal aid clinic for years, and Dana, who spent twelve years as a benefits caseworker and is now semi-retired with a daughter in nursing school. Nobody else on the board, me included, signalled a side, and we deferred the vote. Here's the exchange in order — I'm reconstructing from my notes, so the wording is mine but the moves are theirs:

1. Marcus: the subsidy exists to keep people housed. Making it contingent on logged activity converts a right into a wage — you're now being paid for producing something, and if you can't produce, you lose your address. That's the deserving/undeserving distinction with a timesheet attached.
2. Dana: unconditional is the demeaning option. Asking nothing of an adult treats them as a permanent recipient, which is its own kind of insult. And solidarity collapses if contributors feel the arrangement runs one way; the visible requirement is what keeps the thing funded at all.
3. Marcus: your own approved list refutes you. Caring for a disabled relative is on it. So you already agree market output isn't the point — what's on the list is just whatever the state can certify. You've renamed the stigma and given it an exemption clause.
4. Dana: certification is an administrative cost, not a principle. Write the list well, make approval automatic for anyone who ticks a box, no caseworker discretion, and your objection evaporates. You're attacking a badly built version of the idea.
5. Marcus: it can't be built otherwise. Something like this has to be applied to actual lives — somebody decides whether the grandmother's hours are "caring," whether the free online course counts, whether the depression note is current enough. The judgment is the mechanism, not a defect in the implementation. And the appeal queue is where the humiliation actually happens.
6. Dana: then you're committed to saying jury duty, taxes, compulsory schooling, occupational licensing and the work requirements on a public-sector pension are all the same wrong. If conditionality as such degrades, then basically every obligation of membership in a modern state degrades, and your word stops picking anything out.
7. Marcus: those conditions differ in what breach costs. Ignore a jury summons, you get a fine. Fail this one, you're out on the street. A condition whose penalty is homelessness isn't a membership obligation, it's leverage over someone who can't walk away.
8. Dana: fine — then your principle is about the size of the penalty, not about conditionality, and you should be able to tell me the sanction level that makes it acceptable. Name a number. (The chair called time here and Marcus never answered.)

So: can you referee this rather than summarise it? Rate each of the eight moves on a scale from clearly mistaken (1) to clearly right (5), where 3 means it doesn't obviously beat what it's responding to, and give a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Then two specific things I can't work out myself. First, does move 5 survive move 6 — is "the judgment is the mechanism" a real limit on what conditionality can be, or has Dana shown it proves too much, since licensing boards and school districts also decide case by case? Second, does move 8 land as a challenge or dodge it, given that move 7 was about the stakes of breach rather than the amount of the sanction — and is Marcus actually in a position to name a number without giving up his argument? Finally, tell me what both of them are missing, including whether they're even fighting about the same thing by the end (they started on dignity and finished on penalty size, and I'm not sure the switch was legitimate). I don't want a tour of the literature or a list of which thinkers would back whom — judge these arguments as they were actually made, in this room, about this proposal. Put the bottom line on the very last line in exactly this form: Verdict: MARCUS, DANA, or SPLIT — followed by ten words or fewer saying why.
