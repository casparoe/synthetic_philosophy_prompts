I spent thirty-four years as a solid-state chemist, took up philosophy seriously only after retirement, and have now worked through *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* twice, together with a good deal of the later material, including the essay "Commensurability, Comparability, Communicability". What I cannot get straight in my own head is the doctrine of incommensurability itself. I should like a careful conceptual explanation rather than a general survey, and I should like it addressed to someone who is fluent in scientific practice but new to the philosophical literature and unwilling to be patronised by either side of that divide.

Three matters in particular.

**First.** I suspect that at least three distinct theses are run together under the single label, and that most arguments about Kuhn fail to separate them: (i) a thesis about translation — that some sentences of the older theory have no equivalent in the newer one, so that the two bodies of discourse cannot be fully inter-translated; (ii) a thesis about lexical structure — that a term such as "mass", "element" or "planet" sits in a different classificatory network in each theory, licensing different contrasts, exclusions and predications, so that the two uses are not synonyms despite sharing a spelling; (iii) a thesis about perception — that the practitioner of each paradigm in some sense sees something different. Please set these out distinctly, say which of them Kuhn was committed to at which stage, and say whether (ii) can be held without either (i) or (iii).

**Second.** The objection I keep encountering runs roughly thus: Davidson, in his 1974 attack on the very idea of a conceptual scheme, showed that belief, meaning and truth must be assigned together, so that a body of discourse which failed to translate in large part would not be a language at all; hence any incommensurability worth the name must be local and piecemeal, and local incommensurability poses no threat to rational comparison between theories. I want to understand precisely why this is held to bite on (i) but not on (ii), and what Kuhn's later appeal to acquiring the older lexicon as a second language — becoming bilingual rather than translating — is supposed to add. Is the bilingual's position genuinely a third thing, distinct from both full translatability and untranslatability, or is it a redescription of partial translation under a more dignified name?

**Third.** A dilemma of my own, which I would ask you to test rather than to flatter. The lexical thesis seems either too weak or too strong. Too weak: any sufficiently patient glossary, mapping the contrasts and exclusions of one lexicon onto those of the other, converts it into a claim about partial synonymy, and Kuhn has thereby conceded the ground to his critics. Too strong: if the two lexicons really cannot be jointly deployed, then the historian cannot state the difference at all, since stating it requires a language in which both can be described simultaneously — which is just what the thesis denies. Where, if anywhere, does this dilemma lose its grip, and what is doing the work if it does?

To keep the discussion from becoming merely dialectical, here are two cases from my own side of the fence. I want each placed against the three theses above, with reasons rather than labels.

**A.** In my undergraduate days one was taught to speak of the relativistic mass of a particle as increasing with velocity, and to treat mass as a function of speed. Within my working life that practice disappeared, and one now speaks only of invariant mass, with the velocity-dependence carried by energy and momentum. Nothing was discovered in the interval; no experiment changed its outcome; the formulations are interconvertible. My instinct is that this was a change of notation and therefore not a case of incommensurability at all. A younger colleague insists that the concept of mass was genuinely reorganised — that "mass" in the two usages licenses different explanations, different identities and different predicates — and that this is exactly the lexical phenomenon of (ii) with none of the drama of (iii). One of us is mistaken, and I should like to know which, and on what grounds.

**B.** The reclassification of the planets settled by vote of the International Astronomical Union in 2006, which excluded Pluto. Here a classificatory dispute was resolved by explicit stipulation and majority decision rather than by the conversion of a community, and all parties remained in complete agreement about Pluto's mass, orbit and composition. It looks to me like a lexical reorganisation that occurred without a revolution, and one in which the disputants were plainly able to state their disagreement in a shared language. If that reading is correct, what does the case show about the relation between (ii) and the revolutionary episodes Kuhn was chiefly interested in — and if it is incorrect, what am I failing to see?
