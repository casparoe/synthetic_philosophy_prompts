I work for a small private foundation in Bristol and I've been asked to design the rules for a new prize for studio ceramics — about £40k, awarded annually, first round next autumn. Our charter has one unusual feature the donor insisted on: we don't publish any criteria. Instead we appoint five jurors, and their consensus verdict just *is* the decision; no appeal to an external standard, and no written reasons required. His theory is that published criteria get gamed by applicants, and that nobody can say in advance what makes a good pot anyway.

My colleague Ruth, who did a philosophy MA years ago, pointed out that this is basically Hume's picture of the standard of taste — the standard is the joint verdict of qualified judges — and she reckons it's fatally circular: you identify good judges by their good taste, and good taste by what good judges approve, so our prize is just whatever five comfortable people happen to like, with a bit of ceremony round it. I'm not sure thats the biggest problem, or even a problem in the way she means, and frankly I don't have the time to work out whether it is. Six months, me part-time plus Ruth one day a week. So here's what I want: take the live problems in Hume's account and rank them *for our purposes* — i.e. by whether resolving them would change something concrete about how we recruit, brief, seat or constrain the jury. The candidates I've got, in no order: whether the true-judge account really is circular, or whether the marks of a good judge (delicacy, practice, breadth of comparison, freedom from prejudice, good sense) can be checked independently of the verdicts they produce — noting that Hume's own illustration of delicacy is the story of the two wine-tasters who separately pick out iron and leather in a hogshead, which is a lovely picture but not obviously a selection method; whether those marks are trainable in a person or only detectable after the fact, which decides whether we run an actual assessment or just recruit and hope; the fact that Hume leans heavily on longevity of reputation — the same ancient poets still being admired centuries later, through all the changes of climate, religion and government — which is useless to us since everything eligible was made last year, and what if anything can stand in for it; how much to worry that Hume's sense of who counts as qualified looks suspiciously like Hume, so that the model quietly excludes outsiders and work from traditions he'd have waved away; whether equally good judges can genuinely disagree, and if so what our tiebreak should be given the charter demands consensus; and whether we should be chasing convergence at all, given Hume also holds that beauty isn't a quality in the objects themselves but lives in the mind that contemplates them.

For each one, tell me how far upstream it is of the others, how tractable it is for two non-specialists with half a year, and — the bit I care about most — what we would concretely do differently if it came out one way rather than the other. If the honest answer is "nothing, it's a scholarly knot that never touches our operations", say that and put it at the bottom; I'd like at least one or two candidates knocked off the list outright, plus some sense of what evidence or argument over the next few months would make you re-order the rest. Please be decisive: commit to an ordering and give your reasons, rather than laying out the considerations on both sides and leaving me to pick. No bibliography, no citations, no suggestions of things to go and read — I can't chase them and don't need to. And write it in British English, as I'll be forwarding the whole thing to the trustees.
