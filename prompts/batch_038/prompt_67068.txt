So a bunch of us have a little reading group — six or seven people, only one of whom has any actual academic training, a friend who did a theology degree years ago and has never quite let go of it. We just finished The Master and Margarita, and she turned up to the last meeting with a printout about the Jesus Seminar (the colored beads thing, the group Funk and Crossan ran where scholars voted on which sayings were authentic) and made what I thought was a genuinely provocative claim: that the Pilate chapters are basically a novelised version of the criteria of authenticity. Her argument was that Bulgakov strips out the miracles, the resurrection appearances, the whole theology of the church, and what's left is a bruised itinerant preacher with a headache-curing knack who insists everyone is basically good and that all earthly authority is violence — which is roughly the shape of the figure that a historical-Jesus reconstruction hands back to you. Another member disagreed hard, saying Bulgakov's Yeshua would fail every criterion worth having and that the novel is really a dig at the whole enterprise. Nobody could settle it, and I volunteered to bring something to the next meeting, which is why I'm asking you.

What I want is a reading of those chapters that goes criterion by criterion and actually does the work rather than hand-waving: dissimilarity (including the double-dissimilar version), multiple attestation, embarrassment, the linguistic/Aramaic-traces stuff, coherence, and the one about the brute fact of the execution being historically secure. And I want you to take the novel's source situation seriously as a source situation, because that's the part I keep tripping over. The only eyewitness account of the trial in the book comes from Woland, who claims he was standing there; the human record — Levi the tax-collector's notebook, if I'm remembering that bit right — is presented as getting Yeshua's words wrong, so wrong that Yeshua wants the parchments burned. What is a historian supposed to do with a corpus like that, and does the novel know what it's doing by giving us exactly one interested supernatural witness and one distorted human scribe? Related question: the Pilate chapters are about as embarrassing as a Passion narrative could get — a cowardly, migraine-ridden procurator as the real protagonist, a Judas who's basically a paid informer and gets liquidated by the state, a Yeshua who never claims divinity and never rises. Does that make the account authentic under the criterion of embarrassment, or does it expose the problem with that criterion, since embarrassment for whom and in front of which community is exactly what the criterion is supposed to settle and here can't?

Then be the honest critic and tell me where both of us are wrong. I can see a reading on which the novel isn't about source criticism at all: Bulgakov wrote it under a regime that shredded documents and made witnesses recant, so maybe the point is what testimony is worth when the archives lie and the safe thing to say is not the true thing — in which case his target isn't the criteria but our confidence in any record whatsoever, and the Jesus-Seminar comparison my friend made is flattering to her but beside the point. There's also the ending, which as far as I remember has Yeshua himself, via Levi, asking that the Master be given peace rather than light, after the devil has restored the manuscript the Master burned — which would mean the historical figure certifies a fictional reconstruction of his own trial. If that's right, what does it do to the idea that you reach the man by filtering the witnesses? So: something I can print out and circulate, maybe fifteen hundred to two thousand words, that treats the criteria as a real method with real arguments behind it and not a strawman, says for each one whether Bulgakov's Yeshua passes, fails, or makes the criterion look silly, doesn't reduce the novel to an illustration, and finishes by telling me what the novel contributes to the debate about the criteria that an article laying out arguments just couldn't. No need to quote the novel or worry about translation wording — we all read different editions anyway and I'm not looking for line-by-line.
