I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student and for about eight months I've been running stimuli for our lab's replication batch on the side-effect effect — the chairman who's told a new program will boost profits but wreck the environment, says he doesn't care about the environment at all, only about the profits, starts the program, environment gets wrecked; plus the mirror version where the program helps the environment and he says he doesn't care about that either. In the classic numbers the harm case gets called intentional way more often than the help case. Our version isn't the classic version though: four wordings, half the cells with a yes/no forced choice and half on a 7-point scale, and in one wording a blame question came *before* the intentionality question. The results were a mess in an interesting way. Forced choice + blame-first gave 79% vs 21%, basically textbook. Likert-only, blame question moved to the end, gave 4.6 vs 3.1, which is still an asymmetry but a much smaller one, and the free-text boxes are full of people writing stuff like "he didn't want to harm it but he knew, so I guess yes" and "no, but he should be blamed" — which I'm honestly not sure is an answer to the question we asked.

My PI's reaction was that this is fine and expected, and that the whole literature is wedged. Reading it, I agree: twenty-some years, and at least four families of explanation that all claim the data — the ones saying the asymmetry shows our concept of intentional action is shot through with moral considerations, the ones saying it's a bias or a blame-driven performance error and the concept itself is clean, the ones saying its pragmatics, that "intentionally" in the harm case is doing something like conceding a point in conversation rather than reporting a classification, and the ones saying there are really two concepts in play and different subjects are latching onto different ones. Nobody has converted anybody. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than nod at, is that it isn't stalled for lack of data — it's stalled because "the Knobe effect" names at least three different phenomena and each camp is explaining a different one, so the arguments never really meet. Is that right? Or is it a verbal dispute about what a "concept" is and what would count as evidence for one? Or a hidden empirical question about which measure tracks the phenomenon (forced choice vs. scale, blame-before vs. blame-after) that a better-designed study could just settle? Or does each camp start from a different picture of what explaining a judgment even requires, in which case no amount of data helps?

I want the diagnosis, not a survey of the explanations and not a verdict on whether the asymmetry is real or whether intentional action has moral content. Part of what I'm after is how the camps talk *about each other*: the way the pragmatics people and the bias people each insist they can absorb the other's findings, and the way the concept people say both of them have changed the subject — that mutual absorption is itself evidence about what they disagree about, so use it. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a kind of study, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that none is coming and why. And a smaller thing that will get raised at lab meeting: does my four-wordings result actually discriminate between any of the camps, or is it the kind of result all of them can swallow with a shrug? (Someone at a workshop also asked whether the same asymmetry shows up when the agent in the vignette is an AI system instead of a chairman — interesting, but not what I'm asking about here.)
