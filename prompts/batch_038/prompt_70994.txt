I would like to practice defending a voting design against a theoretically sophisticated opponent. Please play a mechanism designer — unnamed, with no biography required — who treats strategy-proofness as a non-negotiable requirement of good institutional design and who knows the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem from the inside rather than from a textbook summary: you have spent decades building school-choice, residency-matching, and grant-allocation systems, and you have repeatedly watched well-intentioned committees adopt rules that quietly reward misrepresentation.

Ground rules for the exchange:

- Stay in character until I say "stop." Speak in the first person, as a practitioner who argues formally where formality is needed and plainly elsewhere.
- Keep each reply to roughly 150–200 words, so that this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture.
- Concede without hedging whatever I am genuinely right about; never concede the main point. You will not agree that my rule is strategy-proof, and you will not agree that the theorem has been refuted.
- Do not appeal to the good faith, honesty, or civic virtue of actual voters, and do not dismiss manipulation as rare in practice. Your objection must be structural.
- You may invoke the theorem's hypotheses only in order to identify the one my rule engages. Do not recite the theorem at me.
- Every reply must contain at least one claim about my specific rule rather than about voting rules in general. If you need a detail I have omitted, ask for it in character and say why it matters.
- Where a worked illustration helps, give one: name the reviewers, assign small numbers, and show me a profile.

Here is my rule, which I call the Truncated Panel. A national research council funds exactly one of five proposals per round. Ten reviewers sit on it. Before any report is submitted, each reviewer is assigned exactly three of the five proposals — two from their own panel and one drawn by lot from another panel's list — and each proposal is assigned to exactly six reviewers. The assignment is fixed, public, and independent of anything anyone reports. Each reviewer then submits a strict ranking of their three assigned proposals and of nothing else. A proposal receives three points from every reviewer who ranks it first, two from every reviewer who ranks it second, and one from every reviewer who ranks it third; the highest total wins, ties broken by a fixed alphabetical order of principal investigators' surnames announced in advance.

I hold that the rule is onto: since any two reviewer sets of size six drawn from ten overlap in at least two people, a proposal ranked first by all six of its reviewers collects eighteen points while every rival is capped at sixteen, so each of the five can win under some profile. I hold that it is non-dictatorial: nobody sees everything, and the assignment is symmetric across the ten. And I hold that it is strategy-proof, on the following evidence. For each reviewer in turn, I enumerated all six possible orderings of that reviewer's three assigned proposals against a large random sample of profiles of the other nine reviewers' reports, and in each case I checked whether any reordering changed the winner to a proposal that the reviewer ranked above the honest winner among their three. It never did. The reviewers do, of course, have views about all five proposals — they read the shortlist together and argue about it at lunch — but only their three are recorded on the ballot.

So either I have a counterexample to Gibbard–Satterthwaite on my hard drive, or one of its hypotheses fails here and I cannot see which. Tell me which. And if your answer is that my enumeration proves nothing, say what shape a successful manipulation of the Truncated Panel would have to take: which reviewer, assigned which proposals, wanting what, reporting what instead, and which alternative ends up winning as a result.
