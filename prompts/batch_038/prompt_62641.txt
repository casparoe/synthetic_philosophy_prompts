**Question 4 (25 points).** A fair coin is flipped until it lands heads for the first time. In the **St. Petersburg game**, if heads first appears on toss *n* (n = 1, 2, 3, ...), you receive $2^n. In the **Pasadena game**, if heads first appears on toss *n*, you receive $(-1)^(n-1) · 2^n/n — so you gain $2 on the first toss, lose $2 on the second, gain $8/3 on the third, and so on. (a) Compute the expected value of each game, showing your work. (b) Explain why the Pasadena game is claimed to be a *worse* problem for expected utility theory than the St. Petersburg game, and explain what the phrase "the series converges only conditionally" has to do with it. (c) Say what you think a decision theorist should do about the Pasadena game, and defend it.

And here's the student's answer, transcribed as written:

> (a) The probability that heads comes up first on toss n is (1/2)^n. For St. Petersburg the payoff is 2^n, so the contribution of each outcome is (1/2)^n · 2^n. Since the payoff doubles while the probability halves, each successive outcome contributes more than the one before it, and the total grows without bound, so the expected value is infinite. That's the paradox: EU theory says you should be willing to pay any finite amount to play.
>
> For Pasadena the probability is still (1/2)^n but the payoff is (-1)^(n-1)·2^n/n, so the contribution of outcome n is (-1)^(n-1)/n. Adding these up gives 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, about 0.693. So the Pasadena game is worth roughly 69 cents.
>
> (b) Unlike St. Petersburg, Pasadena involves losses as well as gains, so you cant get out of it by saying that no casino could ever cover an infinite payout, or that the game is unrealistic because nobody has infinitely much money. The real problem is that the series converges conditionally rather than absolutely: the absolute values give 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + ..., which is the harmonic series and diverges. Riemann's rearrangement theorem says that any conditionally convergent series can be reordered to sum to any real number you want, or made to diverge. So there's a way of ordering the Pasadena outcomes on which the game is worth a million dollars, and another on which it's worth zero. That's what the week 6 reading means when it says the expected value doesn't exist: the game has perfectly well-defined probabilities and perfectly well-defined payoffs for every outcome, but no well-defined expectation, and the expectation is exactly what EU theory is supposed to hand you. It's worse than St. Petersburg because there you at least get a determinate answer — infinity — and you can argue about whether infinity is a sensible thing for a decision theory to output. Here there's no output at all, so the theory goes silent on a case it's supposed to cover.
>
> That said, I think the natural way to sum the series is the obvious one, in the order the outcomes actually occur, n = 1, 2, 3, and so on. Nothing about the game tells you to add the terms up in some other order, and the rearranged series don't correspond to any different game — they're just artificial reshufflings of the same one. So the Pasadena game does have an expected value, ln 2, and EU theory should say you'd pay up to about 69 cents to play.
>
> (c) The cleanest fix is Bernoulli's old one: replace linear utility in money with logarithmic utility. Diminishing marginal utility makes the contributions shrink fast enough that everything converges, and it handles St. Petersburg at the same time, so one move fixes both games. The lesson isn't that EU theory is broken, just that it needs to be applied to utilities rather than to dollars.
