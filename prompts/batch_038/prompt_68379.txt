For about eleven years I worked in the customer accounts and metering group at a small municipal electric utility in the Midwest — not as an engineer, but on the side that handled billing, service orders, and disconnects. I have no academic training in any of this, and I am not looking for encouragement. What follows is a view I arrived at by watching my own workplace change, and I would like it treated as something that could turn out to be wrong.

The change was this. When I started, cutting off a household for nonpayment was a sequence of human events: a work order, a mailed notice with a date on it, a two-person crew and a truck, a supervisor's initials, and usually at least one call to a city council member. Every January there was an informal understanding that we slowed down on disconnects during a cold snap, and that understanding had to be renewed each year by someone who could be asked to justify it. Over four or five years the utility moved to meters that could be read and switched remotely, and later to a prepaid option in which a balance runs down and service stops by itself when it reaches zero. I could not point to any moment at which a decision was made about the underlying policy. What I noticed afterward was that a question we used to have to ask in public — whether it is acceptable to take electricity away from a household that cannot pay during the worst week of winter — had stopped being asked, because there was no longer any point in the process at which it could be asked. It had become a property of the equipment.

That experience sent me back to an essay I had read years earlier, Langdon Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and its example of the Long Island parkway overpasses built too low for buses to pass under. I have since read that at least one historian argues that particular story does not hold up — that the clearances were not chosen for that reason. I mention this because it unsettles me, and I want to know how much of a thesis of this kind depends on its most famous illustration: if the flagship example turns out to be a legend, does the general claim lose support, or was the example only ever decorative?

The position I want examined is one I built myself. I have tried to state when a design does what I believe those meters did, and I settled on three conditions. First, the fork: at the moment of decision there was a genuine alternative — technically feasible, affordable, and actually available to the people choosing — that would have distributed power over the matter differently. Second, silence: after installation, the political question the arrangement answers stops being asked anywhere, in no meeting, renewal cycle, vote, or standing routine that requires anyone to reaffirm it. Third, the cost of reversal: undoing it requires dismantling more than the device, because the job descriptions, contracts, budgets, and habits built to fit it would have to go too. I say an artifact has politics in the strong sense only when all three hold. When one or two hold, I say we are looking at a tool with political side effects, which seems to me a weaker and less interesting claim.

So: what would count against this position? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want the ranking defended rather than listed. Please include findings that a careful researcher could realistically turn up — for instance, evidence that the old practice was already dying for unrelated reasons, or that a council vote on rates and budgets each year counts as the question still being asked — and tell me which of these someone sympathetic to my view would themselves concede as damaging, as opposed to which ones they would wave off.

I also want an honest verdict on whether I have insulated the position. It seems to me that I could always rescue the three conditions: I could loosen "affordable," or insist that the silence is only apparent because I had not looked in the right forum, or treat a failed prediction as a case where the fork was narrower than I thought. Is the test exposed to anything at all? What would I have to accept as evidence that it is simply a vocabulary for describing arrangements I already disapprove of, and what would count as evidence in its favor that could not be reinterpreted that way?

Finally, there is a rival account I cannot shake: that the politics is always in the people and institutions, and the artifact is only a medium that makes some existing arrangement cheaper to maintain. What observable difference is there between my view and that one — anything that could come out differently in the world, or anything the two accounts would describe differently given the same facts? If there is no difference, tell me plainly which of the two is the empty one, and whether my own case survives being redescribed in the rival's terms.

I am not after sources, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the argument itself, made from whatever you already know.
