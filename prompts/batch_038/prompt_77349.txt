I am the president of the Marrow Bend Historical Society, a river town of about four thousand people, and in three weeks I have to give the opening remarks at our annual meeting. That night we unveil a new three-panel exhibit on the flood of November 1961, and immediately afterward the members vote on the wording of the middle panel. The remarks are mine to write, and I would like you to write them for me.

Here is the situation that has made the middle panel impossible for two years. The panel is titled *Why It Happened*, and the title is the whole problem.

Ray Petroski, a hydrologist who does the county's floodplain work, wants the panel to explain the flood the way he explains floods for a living: six and a fraction inches of rain in thirty hours onto an already saturated upper watershed, a frozen crust above the Route 9 bridge, the channelization the state finished in 1948 that shortened the time of concentration, and a resulting discharge that puts the crest at something like a two-hundred-year recurrence interval. He wants the panel to include a small reproduction of the rating curve and to teach a visitor what a recurrence interval does and does not promise. His objection to the alternative wording is blunt. He says that an account of who knocked on whose door explains nothing at all: people go door to door on many nights, and if the panel cannot say why the water came when it came and how high it came, it has not explained the flood but merely illustrated it. He also says, and I think he is right, that the panel should not double as a memorial, because the society already has a memorial wall in the vestibule with all nineteen names on it.

Verna Lutz has been the society's recording secretary for thirty-one years and taught eighth-grade civics before that. She wants the panel to give the sequence of the night of the eleventh into the twelfth: which families on Lower Street were warned, which were not, who went out to move the trucks and therefore was not there to wake his brother, what the volunteer fire department knew at eleven o'clock that the west end did not know until four. Her objection is equally blunt. She says a recurrence interval is a claim about rivers in general, and this panel is about this river on this night, and that the general claim can be perfectly true while the thing the town is actually asking about is entirely absent. She adds — and this is the part Ray cannot answer easily — that in 2011 the Talley came to twenty-nine feet, and that nobody in town had been told what a two-hundred-year interval meant, and that the phrase on the old signage did real harm; she is not willing to put it on a panel again unqualified.

There is one object in the exhibit that neither of them disputes, and it will be mounted in the center of the second panel whatever the words around it end up being. The gauge at Route 9 read thirty-one feet four inches at 4:50 in the morning on 12 November 1961. Verna's uncle was the last person out of the Rausch house on Lower Street that morning; before he waded to the church he marked the waterline on the kitchen doorframe in grease pencil, and that mark has been measured twice since, by the state and by a graduate student, and both times it comes to thirty-one feet four inches above the same datum, to the inch. The doorframe is coming to the meeting on a flatbed.

Write my remarks under these conditions.

- Four minutes when read aloud; call it six hundred to seven hundred words, and write it to be spoken, not to be read on a page.
- The room will hold about ninety people: descendants of the families named on the memorial wall; two high-school classes on a weekday trip who do not care; two engineers from the state floodplain office who care intensely; the editor of the weekly paper; and Ray and Verna both in the second row, who have heard each other's position stated at every meeting since 2023 and can recite it.
- You must not tell anyone how to vote, and the remarks must not be fairly readable as an endorsement of either wording. The vote happens after the unveiling.
- You must not resolve the two years of argument with any version of the claim that we need both the science and the human story. That sentence has been said at every meeting by people who wanted it to end. Both camps now hear it as a refusal to think, and if it appears in my mouth I lose the room.
- You must include one sentence that Ray would be willing to have cut onto the panel exactly as it stands, and, immediately after it, one sentence that Verna would be willing to have cut onto the panel exactly as it stands. Those two sentences should be adjacent, and the speech should not comment on the fact that they are.
- At some point the figure thirty-one feet four inches must be brought into contact with both the gauge and the doorframe, in a way that does more than note the coincidence.
- The remarks must be followable by the high-school students without being condescending to Ray, and must not use the words narrative, covering law, historiography, or explanation as a term of art.
- Do not name any philosopher, book, article, or date of publication anywhere in the remarks.

After the remarks, add a separate note of about a hundred and fifty words addressed to me rather than to the room. In it, say in your own terms what the disagreement between Ray and Verna is really a disagreement about — you may name the philosophical positions or the people associated with them there, though not works, quotations, or citations — and say which of the two positions the speech is betting on, or whether it declines to bet and on what grounds. Finish the note by identifying which of my conditions above was hardest to satisfy and why.
