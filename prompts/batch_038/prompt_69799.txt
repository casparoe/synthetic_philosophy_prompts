I am drafting a section of a paper on the value of originals, to be presented to a mixed audience of philosophers and paintings conservators, and I need a thought experiment built to an exacting specification rather than drawn from the usual examples.

First, invent everything. Choose a fifteenth-century Netherlandish panel painter of your own devising and give me the artist, the work, and the two makers of the copies. Do not set the case inside a real attribution controversy and do not use a real forger: I want the technical stipulations to be mine, not borrowed from a historical case whose facts are contested.

The scenario itself should run to a single paragraph of no more than 250 words. A damaged or lost portion of the panel is completed twice over, by two different hands, producing two alternative versions of the same work.

The two versions must satisfy the following:

1. Indiscernibility earned, not asserted. Both versions survive every examination a conservation laboratory would in fact run. Say which examinations those are and why each fails to separate either version from the genuine thing: the panel support and its dating, the ground and paint-layer structure, the imaging of underdrawing, the identification of pigments and binding media. No fiat, no anachronistic technology, no projection-and-transfer shortcut that a cross-section would expose.

2. Exactly one variable differs: the character of the making. The first maker solved problems the master never faced — reconstructing an iconographic program from fragmentary archival records, recovering an obsolete material by period means, inferring the master's working method from what imaging reveals — and then signed and dated the result to pass it off. The second maker came into possession of the first's archive and worked from it, so that his task was the transcription of a solution already found. He is highly skilled; nothing in his work required him to think. Both intended the same deception, and both succeeded equally.

The purpose, so that you know what counts as cheating: I want to test whether two familiar anti-forgery arguments pull apart here. The first is Goodman's argument in Languages of Art that an original and a forgery which cannot be told apart by looking nonetheless differ aesthetically, because the difference is one that could in principle be found, and because knowing that a difference exists changes what looking amounts to. The second is Dutton's view that a work of art is essentially a performance, so that forgery is the misrepresentation of an artistic achievement — a view which, as I understand it, allows that the forger's own feat may be admirable even while the work offered under the master's name is aesthetically deficient. Reconstruct both in your own words, quote neither author, and flag any place where you think my summary of either is off; I would rather correct the framing than have the case built on a misdescription.

The target verdicts: the achievement-based account must distinguish the two versions, while Goodman's must not. Difficulty of making should be visible to the one and invisible to the other. Deliver, in order:

(a) the scenario;

(b) a step-by-step demonstration that each argument yields the specified verdict, with the premises of each argument stated explicitly and each premise located in a particular feature of the case;

(c) which single feature does the work, and what happens to the verdicts if it is removed — specifically, if the second maker had not had the archive and had been forced to reconstruct the solution independently, does the case collapse into two achievements, and does Goodman's argument then gain a grip it previously lacked?

(d) how a defender of Goodman would attack the case, and whether the attack succeeds;

(e) an honest assessment of whether my specification is self-undermining. If the work just is the performance, then two different performances are two different works, and I may be asking you to hold fixed an object the theory does not permit to be held fixed. Tell me whether that objection is fatal or is merely a restatement of the distinction I am trying to isolate.

If you judge the specification unsatisfiable as written, say so plainly and give me the nearest case that is, marking exactly which constraint you relaxed and what the relaxation costs. No bibliography, no citations, no page numbers.
