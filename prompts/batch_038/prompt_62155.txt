I am in my final year of high school, taking our school's senior European history sequence alongside an introductory philosophy elective, and I would be grateful for help untangling an exchange from class last week. We had just finished a unit on the outbreak of the First World War, and during the review discussion a classmate argued that by the summer of 1914 a continental war was overdetermined: the alliance system, the arms race and the general staffs' mobilization timetables, imperial rivalry, and domestic political pressures in several capitals were each independently sufficient to produce a general war, so that if the assassination at Sarajevo had never happened, some other trigger would have served within a year or two. Our teacher was unpersuaded. He answered by reciting the old proverb — "For want of a nail the shoe was lost. For want of a shoe the horse was lost. For want of a horse the rider was lost. For want of a rider the message was lost. For want of a message the battle was lost. For want of a battle the kingdom was lost. And all for the want of a horseshoe nail." — and concluded that the structural conditions my classmate listed are mere background while the trigger is the real cause, on the grounds that the trigger is the thing whose absence would have changed the outcome. He also circulated a photocopied handout that referred to something called an INUS condition, and I could not work out whether that notion supported him or my classmate. I should add that I know the proverb exists in many versions and I am not interested in its literary history; I am interested in what it is being made to claim.

What I want is a careful disambiguation of the proverb, because I suspect it is doing several distinct jobs at once and that my teacher may be sliding between them. Please set out, as a numbered list I could bring to class, the separate precise theses the proverb could be taken to assert, and for each one be explicit about which feature of causation is doing the work: whether the claim concerns the smallness of a cause relative to the size of its effect; whether the first link in a chain was necessary for the last; whether long conjunctive chains are fragile in a way that outcomes reached by many routes are not; whether the outcome counterfactually depends on the trigger; whether consequences are simply unpredictable in advance; whether there is no principled place at which to stop tracing a causal chain backwards; or whether the point is a prudential lesson about attending to small details. Then give each reading a verdict — an uncontroversial platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or something that is false or at least unsupported — and say whether it survives my classmate's assumption that the final event was multiply caused and would have occurred anyway by another route. It would also help if you said which of these readings the people who repeat the proverb most likely intend, and which one they need when the proverb is used to end an argument rather than to continue one.

Finally, I would like the payoff stated directly: which reading, if any, would license the step from the proverb to the conclusion that structural conditions are background and the trigger is the cause? Please identify the further premises that step requires — for instance, that causes are ranked solely by counterfactual dependence, that genuine overdetermination is rare in history, or that necessity and explanatory importance come apart — and say whether any of them are things a historian could actually test, as opposed to assume. I am especially interested in whether there is evidence a historian could look for that would distinguish a fragile chain of the proverb's kind from a robust, multiply produced outcome, and in whether the proverb and structural explanation might after all be answering different questions, in which case I would like those two questions formulated separately. Please do not include a bibliography, a list of references, or attributions for the proverb; I will track down sources on my own.
