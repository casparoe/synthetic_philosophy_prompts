I am a third-year student here, and two things I am doing this term have started to collide in an unhelpful way. The first is a seminar on moral motivation, in which we spent a fortnight on Kant's shopkeeper and his sympathetic philanthropist, then moved through Williams's objection about the thought too many, and then through the dispositional accounts of moral worth we read in Arpaly and Markovits. The second is a paid research assistantship on an experimental-philosophy project that will eventually ask lay participants to rate how praiseworthy the agents in several hundred short vignettes are. My job is to pre-sort those vignettes, and the principal investigator has asked me for a single coding rule she could hand to a second coder and get the same results — not a position I re-argue case by case. Trying to write that rule has shown me that my own intuitions, which I had assumed were tracking something coherent, do not obviously cohere, and I would like a real adjudication rather than reassurance.

Here are my verdicts, roughly in the order I arrived at them. The nurse who stays past the end of her shift to sit with a dying patient because she cannot bear the thought of anyone dying alone, and who forms no thought about duty or rightness at all: morally worthy, and near the top of my list. The philanthropist who has compared interventions and gives away a large share of her income because she wants to do what morality requires: also worthy, but worth less, and I cannot articulate why without sounding as though I am merely reciting Williams. The man who gives lavishly at a gala chiefly because his colleagues are watching, but who would, I think, have given anyway out of concern for the recipients had nobody been looking: I keep flip-flopping, but at present I count him worthy at a discount. The soldier who throws himself onto a grenade with no moral reasoning whatsoever, moved only by the thought that it must not be his friends: worthy, though I suspect praiseworthiness may not even be the right category for supererogation, which is itself a worry about my coding scheme. The man who does the right thing reliably and across a very wide range of situations purely in order to avoid the unpleasant feeling of guilt: not worthy, even though his disposition is as robust as anyone's. And here is the pair that has me genuinely stuck. Huck, who helps Jim escape because he cannot stand the sight of the man's misery while sincerely believing that what he is doing is a sin and a theft of another man's property: worthy. The sectarian, who has a standing and highly reliable desire to do whatever morality requires, but whose moral code tells him that only members of his own community are owed aid, and who helps a stranded motorist only because he has taken him for a co-religionist, so that the act is right by luck: not worthy, or at any rate worth less than Huck's. Structurally these look like the same case with the slots swapped — a false moral belief on one side, a desire doing the work on the other — and I cannot get one verdict for each without a rule that feels engineered. My current working principle, pieced together out of the readings and then mangled in my own direction, is roughly this: an act has moral worth to the degree that it issues from a desire whose content is among the features that actually make the act right, where the degree is set by how many nearby situations that same desire would get right. It handles the nurse, the guilt-avoider, and Huck. It seems unable to condemn the sectarian, since the desire that moves him is a desire to do the right thing and it is dispositionally robust, and it gives me no way to separate the gala donor from the nurse, since both look equally robust once the vignette supplies the private counterfactual.

What I want is a commitment, not a map. Give me one principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a hundred fresh vignettes without further interpretation, and defend it against the cases above clause by clause, telling me which clause each case is there to motivate. Then tell me what it delivers on three cases I have not listed: an agent of flat affect with no relevant desires at all who does the right thing from a cold judgment that it is required; an agent whose concern is aimed only at one particular person he loves, who would not lift a finger for anyone else, and who here helps that person in a way that also happens to be what morality requires; and an agent who acts from a desire to do what is right under a systematically false code, so that the act is wrong but his reason for it is the one he takes to justify it. I would also like the counterfactual run the other way: what does your principle say about praising the sectarian if we learn that his code was accurate and the act was in fact required, and about Huck if we learn that his compassion would have led him to abandon Jim the moment the escape became costly for him? Finally, be explicit about the cost. Name the verdicts of mine that your principle cannot save, say which of them you are asking me to give up, and argue that I should give them up rather than buy a more complicated principle that preserves everything; if you think the preservation is worth the complexity, argue that too. And if your view is that my coding project is conceptually confused — that praiseworthiness is being made to do a job that splits into two or three distinct notions that my vignettes run together — say so plainly, tell me how you would cut the categories, and tell me which of my verdicts are really verdicts about something other than moral worth. Please take a definite position on all of this and give your reasons, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
