I am a novelist, and for the better part of two years I have been reading in the cognitive science of consciousness for a book whose central character is not a scientist but a stage designer — a woman whose entire professional vocabulary is theatrical, and who slowly comes to suspect that her habit of imagining a single privileged vantage point from which a scene is "given" to an audience is a mistake about minds as well as about theatres. Getting that suspicion right matters to me because the form of the novel follows from it: I have spent my career dismantling the omniscient narrator, and I was initially glad to treat Daniel Dennett's attack on what he calls the Cartesian theater as a philosophical ally. Then I did the reading properly rather than by osmosis, and I found myself unable to say what the multiple drafts model denies that Bernard Baars's global workspace theory affirms. Baars describes his own model in unmistakably theatrical terms — a stage, a spotlight of attention, a darkened house full of unconscious processors receiving whatever is broadcast to them — and yet he too insists that there is no little self sitting in that house watching the show. Likewise the neural descendants of the workspace idea, with their talk of sudden ignition, late amplification, and long-distance broadcasting across frontal and parietal cortex, look to a lay reader less like a theatre with a finish line than like an enormous amount of parallel revision, which is precisely what Dennett says is going on. So I have arrived at a thesis I would like argued for me, and I want it argued as strongly as an honest person can argue it, not merely gestured at.

The thesis is this: the Cartesian theater argument, in the form Dennett deploys it, cannot do the discriminating work it is usually asked to do. What it actually targets is an inner spectator — a place where the show is watched by someone — and neither Baars nor the global neuronal workspace theorists are committed to a spectator; Baars explicitly disowns one. Once the spectator is disowned, what is left is a substantive and quite different question, namely whether there is a determinate fact of the matter as to when a given content becomes conscious, which is the question the colour phi case and the Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque distinction were meant to settle. On that question the multiple drafts model faces a dilemma: either it agrees with the workspace family about the underlying dynamics and differs only in refusing to dignify any particular stage of those dynamics as "experiential," in which case it is a notational variant of them rather than a rival; or it disagrees about the dynamics, in which case the anti-theater polemic was never the argument that was doing the work, and the real disagreement lies somewhere I have not yet located. Please write that argument out for me as continuous prose of roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, aimed at an intelligent reader with no technical training who will not accept vagueness in place of a premise. Build it as a visible chain: state your premises in order, mark the point at which the burden of proof shifts, and then press the three strongest replies I could make from the other side — first, that broadcasting is a graded, ongoing process rather than an event at a boundary, so the theater charge misdescribes the workspace view and cannot be turned against it; second, that the multiple drafts model is more than notational, because its claim that there is no fact of the matter about which revisions are Orwellian carries empirical content about report, forced-choice discrimination, and memory that the workspace view does not share; and third, that I have mislocated the dispute entirely, which is really about phenomenology rather than architecture. Do not finish with a balancing act. I am asking you to argue a side, and I would rather have a vigorous argument with one exposed weak joint than a fair-minded survey of both. Do not pad it with citations, page numbers, or a reading list; I am checking your reasoning, not your sources, and in any case I have no way of looking anything up while I am drafting.

One further request, which I will hold you to. Throughout the piece, keep separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this literature from what is contested among them, and do not let a disputed claim slide in as though it were common ground. The temptations here are obvious: whether the subject in the colour phi case has any determinate experience of the intermediate colour, whether the Orwellian and Stalinesque descriptions really are empirically indistinguishable, whether there is a respectable sense in which neural broadcasting is an event rather than a process, and whether anyone's first-person reports settle such questions at all. A novel built on a smuggled consensus is a novel I would have to rewrite, so where a premise of my thesis is something respectable people dispute, say so plainly in the argument itself and say what the disagreement turns on. Then, after the prose, give me a summary I can drop into the research notebook I keep for the book, as a single JSON object with exactly these fields and no others: "thesis", a one-sentence restatement; "premises", an array in which each element carries "id" (P1, P2, and so on), "claim" (the premise in one sentence), "epistemic_status" (one of settled, contested, or speculative, where settled means that people who know this literature do not seriously dispute it), "disputed_by_what" (the shape of the disagreement, or null if the status is settled), "strongest_counter" (the best objection to that premise specifically, not to the thesis as a whole), and "survives_counter" (one of yes, no, or partly, followed by a clause explaining which); "objections_addressed", an array of objects with "objection", "response_given", and "response_adequate" (again yes, no, or partly, and be honest rather than loyal to the thesis); "weakest_joint", naming the single place in the chain where you think it is most likely to break; and "what_would_change_my_mind", an array of one to three concrete findings or arguments that would undermine the thesis. No prose should appear after that JSON object.
