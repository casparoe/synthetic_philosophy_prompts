I am trying to read a primary source closely, and I want help pulling apart an argument that is written in a deliberately measured, unargumentative register.

**Context.** The passage below is from *The Irish Crisis*, by Sir Charles Edward Trevelyan, first printed as an article in the *Edinburgh Review* (No. CLXXV, January 1848) — articles in that review appeared unsigned — and reissued the same year as a pamphlet under his name. Trevelyan was at the time the senior Treasury official with day-to-day responsibility for famine relief in Ireland, which makes this text something more unusual than a tract: it is the administrator's own account of what the catastrophe he was administering amounted to. The famine was still killing people when it appeared. Footnote markers have been silently dropped, and square brackets mark omissions.

> **[A]** The time has not yet arrived at which any man can with confidence say, that he fully appreciates the nature and the bearings of that great event which will long be inseparably associated with the year just departed. Yet we think that we may render some service to the public by attempting thus early to review, with the calm temper of a future generation, the history of the great Irish famine of 1847. Unless we are much deceived, posterity will trace up to that famine the commencement of a salutary revolution in the habits of a nation long singularly unfortunate, and will acknowledge that on this, as on many other occasions, Supreme Wisdom has educed permanent good out of transient evil.
>
> **[B]** If, a few months ago, an enlightened man had been asked what he thought the most discouraging circumstance in the state of Ireland, we do not imagine that he would have pitched upon Absenteeism, or Protestant bigotry, or Roman Catholic bigotry, or Orangeism, or Ribbandism, or the Repeal cry, or even the system of threatening notices and midday assassinations. These things, he would have said, are evils; but some of them are curable; and others are merely symptomatic. They do not make the case desperate. But what hope is there for a nation which lives on potatoes?
>
> **[C]** [Trevelyan, on the productivity of the crop:] The same land, which when laid down to corn, will maintain a given number of persons, will support three times that number when used for raising potatoes.
>
> **[D]** [Trevelyan quoting with approval an 1822 paper in the *Transactions of the Horticultural Society of London*:] … since the potato, when in cultivation, is very liable to injury from casualties of season, and that it is not at present known how to keep it in store for use beyond a few months, a general failure of the year's crop, whenever it shall have become the chief or sole support of a country, must inevitably lead to all the misery of famine, more dreadful in proportion to the numbers exposed to its ravages.

Please work through the following, in roughly this order:

1. **The taxonomy in [B].** Trevelyan sorts Irish grievances into three buckets: evils that are curable, evils that are merely symptomatic, and the one condition that makes the case desperate. What model of a society does that sorting presuppose — what counts here as a cause, as a symptom, and as a background condition? Note where the specifically *political* items (absenteeism, the Repeal cry, threatening notices and midday assassinations) are placed, and what that placement accomplishes. Where an item's bucket is unclear, say so and give both readings.

2. **The closing question of [B].** Convert "But what hope is there for a nation which lives on potatoes?" into a declarative claim, and identify its real subject: a crop, a diet, an agrarian system, or a character? Is the sentence about what Irish people ate or about what they were?

3. **The theodicy in [A].** What work does "Supreme Wisdom has educed permanent good out of transient evil" actually do? Treat it as a hypothesis to be tested rather than as pious decoration: would the rest of the argument stand without it? Consider whether it converts a distributive question (who bore the cost, who could have acted otherwise, who did act) into a providential one, and whether the same sentence can simultaneously deny that anyone authored the catastrophe and claim the resulting "revolution in the habits of a nation" as a gain.

4. **The standpoint claimed in [A].** The author proposes to write "with the calm temper of a future generation" about an event of the year just past, in the editorial "we" of an unsigned review. What epistemic posture is this, and what does occupying it license that a present-tense account by a serving official would not?

5. **The mechanism in [C] and [D].** Reconstruct, in premise-and-conclusion form, the implied argument running from a staple that supports three times the population per acre, through a crop that cannot be stored and is vulnerable to the season, to famine as an inevitability rather than an accident. Then say what kind of cause this makes famine — a natural limit, a technological gap, an institutional arrangement, or a property of a people's habits — and whether [A]'s "salutary revolution" is offered as a prediction, a justification, or a hope.

6. **The classification question.** Later analysis of famines pressed the point that mass starvation can occur with no decline at all in aggregate food supply, because what determines whether people eat is their command over food rather than its availability; critics of Trevelyan have likewise pressed the fact that Ireland went on growing food and shipping it out of the country while people died. Which of his claims here are damaged by that point, which are untouched by it, and does [B] already contain a version of it, or is [B] precisely what it rules out?
