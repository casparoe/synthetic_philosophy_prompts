Question (20 points): Describe Kripke's fixed-point construction for a language containing its own truth predicate, and use it to explain the status of the simple liar sentence. What does the strengthened liar show about the limits of the construction? Does the resulting semantics retain classical logic?

Student's answer:

Kripke begins with a ground language that contains no truth predicate and adds a predicate T whose extension and anti-extension are both initially empty. A jump operation takes any such pair and returns the sentences that would come out true, and those that would come out false, if T applied exactly to the members of the extension. Because the operation is monotone — enlarging the extension can only enlarge the result, never contract it — iterating it from the empty pair generates an increasing sequence of stages, and at limit ordinals one takes the union of all earlier stages. The least fixed point is the first stage at which nothing new is added, and its existence follows from the monotonicity of the jump, as in the Knaster–Tarski theorem. At that fixed point the extension of T is exactly the set of true sentences and the anti-extension exactly the false ones.

The simple liar λ, which says of itself that it is not true, never enters either extension. At any stage λ goes into the extension only if it was already in the anti-extension at the preceding stage, and vice versa, so the two chase each other indefinitely. λ therefore receives no truth value at the fixed point; it is ungrounded. Whether a given sentence is grounded is not settled by the semantics alone but partly by the empirical facts that fix the interpretation of the ground vocabulary, and the choice of three-valued scheme — strong Kleene, weak Kleene, or supervaluation — changes the details without changing the outcome for λ.

The price paid is compositionality. Once gaps are admitted, the value of a complex sentence is no longer a function of the values of its parts, and this is why Kripke cannot retain every instance of the T-schema. The strengthened liar is worse still. That sentence also says of itself that it is not true, and Kripke's theory pronounces it neither true nor false — which is exactly what the sentence says of itself, so the sentence comes out true after all. The theory thus affirms and denies the same thing, and what we get is a flat contradiction generated inside the object language. The construction does not eliminate the paradox; it relocates it, and the relocated paradox is formally inconsistent.

Classical logic, by contrast, is untouched. The strong Kleene tables are just the ordinary classical tables with an extra row for undefinedness, so conjunction, disjunction, and negation behave as they always did; only bivalence is surrendered, and excluded middle continues to hold for every sentence of the language.
