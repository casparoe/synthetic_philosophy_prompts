I have spent a long time in the rationalist-blog and forum world and have absorbed its habits, including a bad one I want examined here: the reflex to reach for the equilibrium structure of a situation before reaching for the moral vocabulary, and to distrust any doctrine that turns out to be doing no explanatory work. I would like a careful case analysis that tests that reflex against an episode that seems designed to embarrass it, not a survey of the literature.

The theory is the moral equality of combatants: the claim, in Walzer's version, that soldiers on both sides hold an equal right to kill and are equally liable to be killed, so that fighting for an unjust cause is not murder and fighting for a just one confers no additional permission. Set against it is the revisionist rejection associated with McMahan, on which permissions track liability, and liability tracks both the justice of one's cause and one's own contribution to the threat. The episode is the Christmas Truce of December 1914 on the Western Front. Take the well-attested core and nothing more: after the stalemate of the Race to the Sea and the First Battle of Ypres, with armies short of men and shells, firing stopped across a large number of sectors, mainly between British and German units; men came out into no man's land, talked, traded food and small souvenirs, sang; there were joint burials of the dead and some exchanges of prisoners; and the following year such truces were far rarer, in part because commanders issued strongly worded orders prohibiting them and made clear there would be discipline.

Two further features matter and I want them used rather than mentioned. First, the fraternization was distinctly weaker between French and German units, and where British and German men did talk, a shared language plainly helped, many German soldiers having lived in England. Second, in early December 1914 Pope Benedict XV publicly asked the belligerent governments to let the guns fall silent at least on Christmas night, the governments declined, and the men then arranged substantially that outcome locally and without authorization. If you think either feature is misremembered, say so in a clause and proceed with the rest.

What I want from you runs in both directions. Going from theory to case: under the equality thesis, what exactly did the men who walked into no man's land do — something morality permitted, something it recommended, something it was silent about? Note that the thesis makes killing permitted but does not on its face make abstaining forbidden, which seems to predict that a truce is unremarkable; so tell me whether the shock the event caused, and the ferocity of the orders against it, is an anomaly for the view, or whether Walzer has resources in the idea of a war convention, and in the state's monopoly on deciding when the killing stops, to explain why high command treated fraternization as a grave matter. Then run the same exercise on revisionism. To a modern reader the 1914 asymmetry looks unusually clean — an invasion through Belgium against a defensive response — though I know the war-guilt literature is less tidy than that, and I want you to say how much the analysis depends on the clean version. If the asymmetry holds, revisionism implies that the German soldiers were required to stop and wrong to resume, while the British were permitted to resume and arguably wrong to stop. Is that a verdict the revisionist can accept? And what does the view make of an army threatening its own men with punishment for complying with what revisionism says was the correct moral conclusion — a reductio, a tragic cost, or a sign that revisionism quietly needs an institutional supplement it usually disclaims?

Going from case back to theory, I want the explanatory question answered honestly. Sort the moral explanation away from the non-moral candidates — exhaustion and shortage, the physical proximity of the trenches, rotation through quiet sectors, inexperience, the expectation of a short war, the availability of a festival date as a focal point — and say how much of the episode each accounts for. I have read the treatment of the live-and-let-live pattern Tony Ashworth documented and Robert Axelrod modeled as an iterated prisoner's dilemma settled by reciprocal restraint, and I also know that modeling has been attacked on the ground that the payoffs are not those of a prisoner's dilemma at all. Given that, does the moral equality of combatants earn any explanatory credit for the truce, or is it a redescription of behavior that incentives fully account for? My sharpest instrument is the Franco-German asymmetry: the moral status of the men was the same on both sectors, so if behavior differed, something other than moral status was driving it. Tell me whether that argument survives, or how an equality theorist absorbs it.

Then construct and assess a rival I will call the etiquette view, and treat it as a proposal rather than as something you have read about: the moral equality of combatants is not a claim about liability but an institutional rule that armies maintain because it makes wars fightable and terminable — a rule about who may decide when the killing stops, not about who may be killed. On that reading the truce was not a moral discovery by the men but a usurpation of authority, the crackdown is exactly what the view predicts, and the refused papal proposal is not a puzzle but the whole point, since what the governments objected to was the form of the ceasefire rather than its content. Say whether this is genuinely distinct from Walzer's own conventionalism or only a rebranding; whether it can still do the work the equality thesis is supposed to do, in particular explaining the prohibitions on killing prisoners and on torture; and whether it has ceased to be a position in just war theory at all if it turns out to be a claim about institutional design.

Finally, be disciplined about silence. Tell me where neither theory has anything to say about this episode: the joint burials, which look like a phenomenon of recognition and honor rather than of permission to kill; the fact that it happened at Christmas and not on an arbitrary Tuesday; and the fact that the men who fraternized returned to killing and mostly kept at it for four more years. End with your verdict on whether the truce confirms the equality thesis, is an anomaly for it, or is a case it never anticipated, and with what would have to be different about the historical record for that verdict to reverse.

No bibliography, no reading list, and no suggestions for further study; I know where these arguments come from. I want the whole thing as continuous prose of the kind one writes in a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no numbered questions echoed back at me.
