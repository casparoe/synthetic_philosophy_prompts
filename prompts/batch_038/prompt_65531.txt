So — I need a second pair of eyes on something before Thursday's seminar and my own eyes are fried.

Background, short version I promise. One of my first-years, Marcus, sits on the board of a community land trust. Its 1978 bylaws have what he's started calling a ladder clause, Article XII, which says roughly: amendable by two-thirds of the members, provided that no amendment may lower the threshold in this Article unless the amendment is itself carried by four-fifths. In 2004 the members voted four-fifths to a handful to drop the general threshold to a simple majority and to strike the proviso in the same measure. Now a faction wants to use the simple majority to sell a parcel, and one of the members who voted in 2004 is arguing that the 2004 amendment was void and the two-thirds rule still governs. Marcus was going to help her draft the challenge. Instead he wrote the following, handed it to me, and said he thinks it's about all law, not just the land trust.

(1) A rule is a rule only in virtue of some further rule that makes it one — its authorizing rule. Nothing is valid except by descent from something already valid.
(2) Descent isn't a one-off event. A rule has force at a time only if its authorizing rule has force at that time. You loose the enabling act, you loose the regulations made under it.
(3) So a rule can do exactly what its authorizing rule's terms permit and no more. What it does outside those terms isn't a defective rule, it isn't a rule at all.
(4) Now take an amendment enacted under amendment rule A, and suppose it repeals or rewrites A. From the moment it takes effect, A has no force.
(5) But by (2), the amendment's force at that moment depends on A's force at that moment. So the amendment extinguishes its authorizing rule and thereby itself. It is a nullity from the instant it operates.
(6) Therefore no amendment rule can ever be validly applied to itself. Not "difficult." Impossible.
(7) And it spreads. Any rule that binds or empowers a later rule-making act needs an authorizing rule not itself so bound, so the chain either bottoms out in a rule that authorizes itself — dead by (5) — or in a bare social fact that isn't a rule. Hence no legal system has ever contained a rule capable of binding its own future. What we call amendment is revolution in a nice jacket.
(8) Also, he says, the same kills private powers: wills letting trustees vary the trust, contracts with variation clauses, corporate charters, and — he was pleased about this one — the standing rule under which our department adopts its own bylaws.

His slogan: an amendment rule is a key that breaks off in the lock it opens.

Here's my problem. I don't want to give up (1) or (2). (1) is most of what I mean when I say legal validity is a matter of sources rather than merits, and (2) just seems to be how subordinate legislation works. And I certainly don't want (8), because departmental bylaws and wills are fine, I'm sure of that even if I'm not so sure about the land trust. But I've stared at (3) through (5) for an hour and each one looks like a tautology to me on its own.

So: which step do you break, and — this matters more — why did it look innocent? I have to say something to him Thursday other than "you're wrong," because he isn't exactly wrong, and he'll rightly want to know what my alternative commits me to. I want the repairs priced, not just listed. I've already noticed one thing that worries me: if I fix this at the top by saying the ultimate rule isn't the kind of thing that gets authorized by another rule, I seem to be conceding most of (7), and Marcus will take the win. Is that right, or am I misreading what (7) claims?

Two more things, and I don't know if they're real questions or noise. Would he get the same result from a plain, unentrenched amendment rule, or does the ladder clause — the fact that Article XII makes it harder to make itself easier — do essential work somewhere in (3) to (5)? And is (8) actually licensed by (1) through (7)? He presents it as a corollary and I have the feeling it isn't one, but I couldn't tell you why, so don't take my word for it.
