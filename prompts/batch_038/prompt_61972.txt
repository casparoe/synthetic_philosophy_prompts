I am the instructor for an upper-division undergraduate seminar on free will and punishment, and I need to grade a short-answer exam response. The course uses a ten-point scale, on which seven is a competent pass, eight a strong answer, and nine or ten are reserved for work that surfaces something the question did not anticipate.

The question I set was:

Dennett and Caruso both locate the difference between responsible agents and those we excuse in a set of competences — self-control and responsiveness to reasons — and both want a penal system considerably less punitive than the one the United States currently has. As they describe their disagreement, it concerns basic desert. Explain what each of them takes desert to be, why each thinks the other is mistaken, and whether anything turns on the disagreement for the justification of punishment.

The student wrote:

Dennett and Caruso agree about more than the popular framing of their debate suggests. Neither accepts the retributivist claim that an offender's suffering is good in itself, or that wrongdoers are owed hardship simply in virtue of what they did. Both hold that the dangerous may be restrained for the protection of others, and both would favor a system oriented toward rehabilitation rather than payback. Their dispute is therefore not about how much punishment is justified but about what could justify any of it.

Dennett is a compatibilist, and so is committed to the truth of determinism: human conduct, like everything else in nature, is the product of prior causes, a picture that decades of neuroscience have made difficult to resist. His strategy is to show that determinism is no threat to responsibility, because responsibility requires not authorship from nowhere but only the capacities that evolved creatures like us possess — the ability to anticipate how others will respond to our conduct, to adjust that conduct in light of those expectations, and to give and ask for reasons. Holding people responsible, on this view, is a social technology: it makes agents better at the very competences it presupposes. Desert survives in a practical register. A person who has had a normal upbringing, who is able to control himself, and who ignores the reasons the rest of us give him is a proper object of blame, and blaming him is not a confusion about the metaphysics of causation but an intervention that works.

Caruso is an incompatibilist who denies that we possess the free will required for basic desert. His argument is empirical in structure: determinism excludes being the ultimate source of one's actions, and since our best science indicates that behavior is caused, no one can deserve to suffer on account of it. What he proposes in its place is a quarantine model. Offenders are analogous to carriers of a contagious disease; society may isolate them to the extent the threat requires and no further, and it owes them treatment rather than suffering.

Because both positions converge on restraint justified by danger rather than by what the offender merits, the disagreement appears to be largely about vocabulary — whether we may go on saying that offenders deserve their sentences. Dennett wishes to retain that language because it sustains the practice of holding one another responsible; Caruso wishes to abandon it because it has licensed extraordinary cruelty in the American system. In practical terms the two views coincide, which suggests that the metaphysical argument between them does less work than either author is willing to admit.

Please grade this response and give me the comments that justify the grade. Work through it claim by claim rather than impressionistically: for each substantive claim, say whether it is accurate, inaccurate, or underdeveloped; where it is inaccurate, state the correct account of that author's position and indicate how consequential the mistake is to the answer as a whole; where it is merely underdeveloped, say what would have been needed to develop it. Call out separately any place where the student's framing quietly absorbs a disagreement the two authors treat as substantive, and any place where a claim is defensible but is reached by a route that does not support it. Then tell me which of these issues a grader reading quickly would most likely let pass, and finish with the grade and a short summary comment addressed to the student in the second person. Please write in American English.
