I'm writing a seminar paper for a course on the logic and probability of conditionals, and I want an essay that takes a side rather than surveys the terrain.

The background dispute is familiar enough: Lewis's triviality results are usually said to show that indicative conditionals cannot be propositions whose probability obeys Adams's thesis, P(A→C) = P(C|A). One response keeps a truth-conditional semantics and gives up the thesis, explaining its apparent force by appeal to conditions on warranted assertion. A second denies that indicatives have truth conditions at all. A third — the one I want interrogated hardest — grants that the derivation is sound but diagnoses an equivocation in it: the conditional sentence expresses a proposition fixed by the speaker's evidential state at the time of use, the thesis binds only that proposition relative to that state, and the derivation therefore slides illicitly from a thesis stated about one proposition to a thesis about a different one.

Here is my worry, and the essay should treat it as the central test. Suppose my P(C|A) = 0.6. I then learn E, where P(C|A∧E) = 0.9, and I am confident that my update on everything else was ordinary conditioning. The third response seems committed to saying that my degree of belief in the conditional I asserted before learning E should remain at 0.6, since that conditional now expresses a proposition the thesis no longer governs. Is that sustainable? Does it square with the intuition that learning E gives me new reason to believe C would obtain if A did, and with the frequently reported empirical finding that elicited degrees of belief in conditionals track conditional probabilities rather than the probabilities of the corresponding conjunctions? If the answer is that the theory simply stops constraining the stale proposition, say whether the thesis it preserves is still the thesis the debate was about.

The essay must also be exact about which premises the derivation genuinely needs — including why the existence of a suitable proposition relative to each individual credence function is not by itself an escape — and must close with a defended verdict on which of the three responses is best, together with what the third would have to add in order to survive.
