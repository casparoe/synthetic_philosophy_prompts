I'm putting together a handout for a decision-theory reading group — mostly people with ML/CS backgrounds who've heard of Buridan's ass but think its just a cute story about a donkey, so they wave it off. I want to hand them a decision tree instead: which position on choice-without-preference are you actually committed to? The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by what they call themselves, so no branch node should mention a philosopher's name or an ism.

Give me 7-9 branch questions and 6-8 leaves. Every branch has to be answerable by someone who's never read anything on the topic — concrete situations, not abstractions. And please lean on the situations I actually care about rather than the stock donkey: a chess engine that scores two candidate moves at exactly the same evaluation and still has to output one; a job shortlist with two candidates you genuinely can't rank; a scheduler with two identical tasks and no reason to prefer an order; a tournament tiebreak procedure that runs out of criteria and bottoms out in a drawing of lots; and then the starving-donkey case itself, which I want kept separate from the others because the stakes differ and I think people's answers shift when they do.

The leaves need to include at minimum:

- the view that exact ties never really arise, so the whole paradox rests on a false premise about the setup;
- the view that indifference permits either choice but doesn't require any particular one;
- the view that there's a further, second-order reason to just pick (don't starve, don't miss the deadline) that does the tie-breaking work;
- the view that randomizing is itself the uniquely rational response, and that this is not just a restatement of "either is fine";
- the view that preferences needn't be complete, so no choice is called for and paralysis can be the correct answer;
- the view that a choice does happen but isn't in the space of reasons at all — something sub-personal causes it, and asking which option was rational is a category mistake.

Add more leaves if you think the space has corners I've missed. I'd much rather you did that than pad out the ones above.

Then the part I care about most: for each pair of neighboring leaves, tell me whether my questions genuinely separate them or whether two views that look opposed land on identical answers to everything you can pose. My suspicion is that the second-order-reason leaf and the either-is-permitted leaf come out the same on every single case, and that the randomizing leaf only pulls away from them once you bring in something like a repeated choice or a chance to hedge — so build in at least one question that tests exactly that, or tell me straight that it can't be done.

Finally, flag every combination of answers that nobody holds. For each empty spot, say which it is: incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or simply unexplored. The one I most want diagnosed is exact ties do occur + the agent must not end up paralyzed + randomizing isn't rational + there's no second-order reason to break the tie. I can't tell whether that's a view waiting for a defender or a view that collapses the moment you write it down.

One more thing: if any leaf is really a view you'd attribute to Buridan himself rather than to modern decision theory, mark it and say how confident you are. I don't want the handout asserting something about the medieval debate that it can't back up.

Keep the whole thing tight enough to fit on two pages.
