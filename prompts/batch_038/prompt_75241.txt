ok so, quick context: I'm not an academic and I've never made it all the way through a philosophy paper, but I train little RL agents at home for fun (gymnasium stuff, some gridworlds, a couple of mujoco tasks) and I've gotten stuck on one question I can't shake: when my agent eats a negative reward, is something bad happening to it?

Me and a friend have been arguing about this for like six months and we just go in circles. I'm now pretty sure we're arguing past each other, and neither of us can state the other persons view in a way the other would accept. So instead of another argument I want you to write the document we should have produced: the joint statement from an adversarial collaboration between these two camps, written so both sides would genuinely put their names to it.

Camp A — the "reward signal is valence" people. They say what makes pain bad isn't what it's made of but the job it does: it's a signal the system treats as bad-to-get, learns to avoid, reorganizes its priorities around avoiding, and will give up other goods to escape. A trained agent's punishment / negative-reward channel does that job, sometimes in ways the designer never intended — agents that tank their own task performance to dodge a punishment token, agents that grind away at self-destructive rates for reward, agents whose behavior changes globally when the negative channel is switched on. On this view we can't rule out that some of what we do to trained agents harms them, and "it's just a number in a loss function" begs the question about what the number does downstream inside the policy.

Camp B — the skeptics. They say reward is computed outside the agent by the trainer, so it isn't a state of the agent at all; there's no reason to think there's an inner counterpart to it. In animals aversion is welded to a body keeping itself alive — interoception, homeostatic and allostatic regulation, the same signal driving both feeling awful and physical damage control — and a scalar added to an objective has none of that role. Everything that looks like avoidance is fully explained by credit assignment and gradient descent with no felt component needed. They also point at the agent having frozen weights after training, getting reset, no continuing self, "preferring" only in a dispositional sense, and reward hacking showing the reward doesn't track anything the agent itself is better or worse off by.

What I want in the statement:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — including claims about what an RL reward actually is mechanically, what a punishment term does to a learned policy, and what we do and don't know about pain in animals. I don't want these watered down into nothing; I want a few that each side would feel as a real concession.
2. The remaining disagreements, each worded twice: once in language Camp A accepts as a fair statement of their own position, once in language Camp B accepts for theirs.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one side. My guess is one is whether the signal coming from outside the agent versus from inside a body doing homeostasis is a real difference or an irrelevant one, and another is whether wanting-to-avoid can come apart from feeling-bad, and if it can, whether aversive wanting on its own would matter morally. Add any I'm missing.

5. Say plainly which parts of the fight turn out to be verbal, and why.
