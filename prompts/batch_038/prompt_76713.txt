**The question (20 points):**

"A Szilard engine consists of a single molecule confined in a box of volume V that is held in thermal contact with a heat bath at temperature T. A partition is inserted at the midpoint of the box, a demon measures which half contains the molecule, and a piston is then attached to the partition so that the molecule's isothermal expansion back to volume V can be harnessed to do work. In approximately 500 words: (a) state how much work can be extracted per cycle, and explain where that energy comes from; (b) explain precisely what the apparent violation of the second law consists in; and (c) explain how the demon is finally accounted for, identifying which step of the cycle bears the thermodynamic cost and why that step, rather than some other, is the costly one."

**The rubric:**

2. (5 points) Correct statement of the apparent violation and of what would have to be true for it to be a genuine one.
3. (5 points) Correct attribution of the resolution — which result is due to whom, and what each actually established.

**The student's response:**

(a) Treat the molecule as a one-particle ideal gas. Once the partition is in place and the demon has determined which half the molecule occupies, the molecule is confined to V/2, so the expansion back to V is an isothermal doubling. The work done is W = ∫ p dV with p = kT/V, giving W = kT ln(V ÷ V/2) = kT ln 2, which is about 2.9 × 10⁻²¹ joules at room temperature. The energy comes out of the heat bath: over the cycle the single molecule's kinetic energy is unchanged, so the heat absorbed from the bath is what appears as work. One bit of measurement buys kT ln 2 of work, which is the sense in which information is convertible into work at that exchange rate.

(b) The violation is of the Kelvin–Planck statement. Over a complete cycle the engine returns to its initial macrostate, there is only one reservoir, and yet a quantity of heat has been lifted from a bath at uniform temperature and converted entirely into work, with nothing else apparently altered. No second-law-respecting device can do that. So if the description in (a) is a complete description, the second law fails for systems of this size.

(c) It is not a complete description, because the demon is a physical system and must be entered in the bookkeeping. Landauer's principle holds that erasing one bit from a memory at temperature T dissipates at least kT ln 2 into the surroundings. The reason is that erasure is logically irreversible: two distinct memory states are mapped onto a single one, so the memory's Shannon entropy falls by one bit, and since Shannon entropy and thermodynamic entropy are the same physical quantity, that entropy must go somewhere — it goes into the bath as heat. Szilard had essentially this point in 1929: the demon can extract work only by keeping a record, and before starting another cycle it must return its memory to the blank standard state, and it is that reset which costs. Landauer's principle does not stop at erasure, however. Acquiring the bit in the first place is also a registration of one of two alternatives into the memory, which is likewise logically irreversible, so the demon pays kT ln 2 to measure and a further kT ln 2 to erase, dissipating 2kT ln 2 per cycle in order to extract kT ln 2. The second law is therefore not merely rescued but over-satisfied, and demons of this kind turn out simply to be inefficient engines. The only escape would be a demon that writes each record into a fresh, never-before-used register and never erases anything, which no finite physical memory can sustain; on this account, then, an immortal demon with an unbounded memory would defeat the second law outright. Modern treatments state the same result as an inequality bounding the mean extractable work by kT ln 2 times the mutual information the demon acquires about the molecule, which reduces to the case above when the measurement is perfect and hence worth exactly one bit.
