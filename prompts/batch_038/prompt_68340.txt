I'm a second-year economics PhD student and next week I'm giving a talk at our welfare-econ reading group where I just read out where I've actually landed on interpersonal utility comparisons, mostly so people can attack it in one go instead of over the next two years. My advisor read the list and wrote "not obviously coherent" in the margin and nothing else, which is unhelpfull, and then three friends each pointed at a different item, so now I genuinely can't tell which one is the problem. Here's the set, roughly in the order I came to hold them:

(1) Utility, for me, is nothing over and above an ordinal ranking. I don't think people have inner hedonimeters. The von Neumann-Morgenstern machinery is fine as a way of getting numbers out of preferences over lotteries, but those numbers don't mean anything extra. (2) So there's no fact of the matter about whether my headache is worse than your headache. I want to say cross-person comparisons aren't unreliable, they're empty. (3) Same-person comparisons across time are still fine though — I'm comfortable saying you're better off now than you were last year, and comfortable with within-person magnitudes, like how much of your own future you'll trade for your present. (4) Harsanyi's aggregation result is one of the very few things in this literature I find compelling: Pareto plus separability plus expected-utility axioms gets you a weighted sum, and with anonymity you get the plain utilitarian sum. I want to keep it. (5) The weights have to be equal. Any rule that weights some people's utilities more heavily than others is objectionable — that's exactly where I part company with people who want to license utility-monster moves. (6) But a linear sum is too crude normatively: I care about how well off people are relative to each other, not just about the total, so I want the planner's objective concave in individual well-being. In my applied work I literally use a social welfare function concave in income and I report distributional weights, and I also rank health interventions by cost per QALY, which I know quietly assumes a QALY is worth the same to everyone. (7) Social evaluation should be welfarist in the thin sense: it uses only information about individuals' own preferences, no independent moral facts smuggled in from outside. (8) And there's a protected personal sphere — for at least some choices that concern only the person making them, society should go with that person's preference no matter what anyone else thinks about it. (9) One case verdict I'm not giving up: a doctor, one dose of a scarce treatment, patient A gains enormously, patient B gains a little, no other morally relevant difference. A gets it, and that's not just a tie-break, that's the right answer.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how hard? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than dumping them all out as equally bad — I specifically want to know which pair, if any, only looks like a tension and dissolves once you examine it, because I've been losing sleep over something that might be nothing. For each real one, say what has to be dropped or reinterpreted and what the reinterpretation costs me, including whether it costs me my day job as an applied economist or just my seminar talking points. And then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage at once.
