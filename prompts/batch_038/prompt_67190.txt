You're a historian of medieval Jewish philosophy who's taught the ta'amei ha-mitzvot material for years — think carefully, and before you draft anything, jot a few bullets on each camp's strongest self-description, then set those aside and write the real deliverable.

Two people in my department, one a Maimonidean and one a Nahmanidean, have been going in circles for a year about animal sacrifice in the Torah. The Maimonidean holds that the cult is an accomodation: the ancient world knew no form of worship but slaughter-and-offer, so God took the existing form and redirected it — one deity, one place, no images — as a stage Israel was meant to grow past, per the argument in Part III of the Guide. The Nahmanidean holds, following his objection in the commentary on the opening of Leviticus, that the rites have their own point — the worshipper stands in for himself, and something real happens in both the visible and the hidden orders — and that explaining a commandment by what it displaces in pagan practice evacuates it, reducing Torah law to a footnote about other people's errors.

Write the joint statement the two of them should have produced. I want: the claims both can sign (which is more than they think — both accept the cult was commanded, that it lapsed with the Temple, that the rabbis went on with prayer and study, and that the Torah itself is nearly silent about why); the exact disagreements, each worded so that its own side would accept the formulation; and the cruxes, with for each one something both would grant actually bears on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Make at least three of the cruxes genuinely substantive — e.g. what a messianic restoration of the offerings costs the Maimonidean if the whole point was a scaffold meant to come down; how each side absorbs Ezekiel's Temple diverging from the Levitical rules; and whether the near-identity of Israelite and pagan sacrificial grammar reads better as evidence of accommodation or as evidence of a shared intrinsic grammar of worship. Finish by saying which stretches of the quarrel you think are merely verbal, and what turns on seeing that.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then cruxes with predictions. Cite biblical books and chapters freely if it helps, but don't put anything in quotation marks from the medieval sources — neither of us can check them, so paraphrase and don't put words in either authority's mouth.
