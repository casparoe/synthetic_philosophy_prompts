I never studied philosophy, I just read about this stuff online and think about it way to much. So the Mary case — the scientist who knows every physical fact there is about colour vision but has spent her whole life in a black and white room, and then walks out and sees a red tomato — everybody seems to agree the fight is over whether she learns a new *fact*. Both sides argue about that. I've come to think the framing itself is the problem, and I want you to make the case for my version as strongly as you can, cause I can't really do it myself.

My version: what Mary gains outside isn't a fact about red, and it isn't just a practical skill either. It's a new set of ways to be *wrong*. In the room she can't mistake a red thing for a green one, can't be fooled by a slightly off shade, can't be surprised by how much a ripe strawberry jumps out against the leaves, can't find her memory of a colour disagreeing with the colour in front of her. None of those errors were available to her before. So on my view, knowing isn't a question of how many true propositions you've got filed away — it's a question of how large your space of possible mistakes is, and how finely you can sort things within it. Add new possible errors and you've added knowledge, even if you haven't added a single new fact. Physicalism comes out untouched and Jacksons argument still goes through, it just proves something about knowledge rather than something spooky about consciousness.

Can you build that argument for me? I want the actual case, not a rundown of what other people think. Exactly five numbered steps, one or two sentences each, that together carry it from a premise that's hard to deny to the conclusion that Mary's new knowledge is a new batch of ways to be mistaken. No opening paragraph and no summary at the end, just the five numbered items. One of the five has to deal with the obvious objection, which is that a new way to be wrong seems to have to be a way of being wrong *about something*, and that something looks suspiciously like the new fact I'm trying to deny. When you need examples, use ordinary ones I'd recognise — a wine taster, somebody finally learning their own city, a mechanic who can now hear an engine fault — rather than more thought experiments about philosophers in rooms.
