The question I set was:

THE QUESTION (800 words, closed book, no sources expected): In The Expanding Circle, Peter Singer offers an account of why the range of beings whose interests count morally has widened over human history. Explain the metaphor, identify what Singer thinks actually drives the expansion, and then press one objection to his account. Say whether you think the objection succeeds.

The rubric the dept makes us use is: 40 points for accurate exposition of the argument, 30 for getting the driver-of-expansion claim right, 30 for the objection and how it's handled. Grades go out as a number out of 100 plus a letter.

Here's the essay, typed out as submitted:

THE ANSWER:

Singer's picture starts from a simple image. At any moment you can draw a circle around everything whose interests you take seriously: what happens inside it matters to you morally, what happens outside it doesn't, or doesn't much. For most of our species' history that circle was tiny — yourself, your family, your band. Singer points out that over time it has been pushed outward, first to the tribe, then the nation, then to all human beings regardless of race, and he argues it should not stop at the edge of our species but should take in nonhuman animals too. The historical evidence he leans on includes the abolition of the slave trade, the extension of legal rights to groups that had been excluded, and the growth of the animal welfare movement, all of which look like cases where the same interests were once simply invisible and later became impossible to ignore.

The background is Darwinian. Singer accepts that our moral sentiments have a biological history: kin selection explains why we care about relatives, reciprocal altruism explains why we help those who can help back, and group loyalty explains why we defend the tribe against outsiders. Where he parts company with sociobiologists is the conclusion they want to draw. He thinks you cannot read ethics off biology, because a genealogy of a belief tells you where it came from and nothing at all about whether it's true.

What does the pushing, then, on Singer's account, is sympathy. He is basically a Humean about this. We have a natural capacity for fellow-feeling, and what moral progress consists in is that capacity being stretched over a wider area. As people began to trade with strangers, travel, read novels and newspapers about lives unlike their own, the imaginative contact made distant suffering vivid, and vivid suffering is what moves us. Reason comes in afterwards, in a subsidiary role: it calculates costs, works out who is affected, notices that the reasons we give for caring about our neighbors apply equally to people further off. Singer's image of reason as an escalator is a way of describing this — you step on out of curiosity about your own case and get carried outward whether you meant to or not. The destination is what he calls the point of view of the universe, a standpoint from which your own interests have no special weight just because they're yours, and once you occupy it you cannot consistently keep the circle small.

That last step is where I think the argument is vulnerable, and where I want to press. If reason really does carry us to impartiality, why is the escalator so empty? Plenty of people reason carefully about their own lives and remain completely unmoved by the interests of strangers, and they don't seem to be making an error anyone can show them. Singer has to admit that reason by itself doesn't produce motivation, that you need a desire in place for the reasoning to grip — but then the expansion of the circle isn't driven by reason at all, it's driven by whatever happens to seed the desire, and the whole thing looks like a contingent historical accident rather than a rational achievement. I think this objection lands.

There is also a problem with the foundations. Singer spends a lot of the book on evolutionary explanations of altruism and then turns around and claims that impartial concern for all sentient beings follows from the nature of reasoning. But you can't get an ought out of an is, and that is exactly what he's doing: the facts about how our sympathies evolved are being used to establish how they ought to be distributed. Once you see that, the expansion stops looking like progress and starts looking like one more preference our species happened to acquire.

My conclusion is that Singer's account is attractive but too optimistic. The escalator, on his telling, only goes up: there is no mechanism in his story by which the circle gets smaller, so moral progress is written into the structure of practical reason itself and we can expect the circle to keep widening. That seems like a lot of confidence to place in a trend, and history suggests the boundaries of moral concern can be redrawn inward as easily as outward.

END OF ANSWER.
