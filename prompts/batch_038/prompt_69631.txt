EXAM QUESTION (30 points; 800-word limit)

Callisthenia is a newly independent state carved out of a larger federation. Its constitutional assembly was elected on a franchise consisting of all adult permanent residents of the territory, irrespective of nationality, and the draft constitution has now been ratified by a vote of that same assembly. The readings for weeks 5–7 (Dahl, Whelan, Goodin, Abizadeh, López-Guerra, Näsström, Song, Miller) bear directly on the case. In no more than 800 words: (a) Explain why the franchise rule used to elect the assembly raises what the literature calls the boundary problem, and why that problem is distinct from objections to majority rule as a decision procedure. (b) Explain how the all-affected principle and the all-subjected (coercion-based) principle each propose to fix the composition of the demos, and say which of the two yields the wider electorate in the Callisthenian case, giving reasons. (c) Assess whether either principle escapes the charge of circularity.

STUDENT ANSWER (approximately 760 words)

The boundary problem arises because every democratic procedure must already assume a body of people entitled to run it. Callisthenia's assembly was elected by all adult permanent residents of the territory, but nothing in the democratic ideal tells us that this was the right electorate. If the legitimacy of the assembly's decisions comes from their having been authorized by the people, then the prior question — which people? — has to be settled before any authorization can occur, and it cannot be settled by a vote, since a vote requires an electorate whose composition is exactly what is in doubt. Dahl noticed this difficulty, but it was Whelan who gave it its name and who drew the sharper conclusion that democratic theory has no internal resources for solving it: the boundary of the demos is a presupposition of democratic legitimacy rather than a possible output of it.

This distinguishes the boundary problem from the familiar objections to majority rule. Whether a bare majority is a defensible decision rule, or whether cycling among alternatives makes majority outcomes arbitrary, concerns how a given demos decides. The boundary problem concerns who constitutes the demos at all, and so is a problem about authorization rather than aggregation: even a decision rule that every Callisthenian agreed was perfectly fair would leave the composition of the electorate unexplained. The problem is also distinct from the complaint, familiar from the literature on birthright citizenship, that membership is distributed by the accident of birth. That is an objection to how the demos is filled rather than to how it is bounded, and in any case Callisthenia's franchise rule was nationality-blind.

Two principles have been proposed for settling the composition question. The all-affected principle says that everyone whose interests are affected by a collective decision ought to have a say in it. Its advantage is that it presupposes no institutional arrangement: affectedness is a matter of causal fact about who bears the consequences of a decision, so the principle can be applied even to the decision about the electorate itself. Its disadvantage is that affectedness comes in degrees with no natural stopping point. A decision about Callisthenia's currency will affect cross-border traders, then distant investors, then people who have never heard of the country; applied literally, the principle makes the electorate for any decision approach the whole of humanity, present and future. The all-subjected principle instead holds that the demos consists of those subject to the coercive power of the polity whose decisions are at issue. Because subjection is a legal status rather than a matter of causal degree, this principle yields a determinate electorate, and it is therefore the more inclusive of the two in the Callisthenian case: it enfranchises not only the resident foreigners whom the assembly's franchise rule already admitted but also everyone abroad whose conduct is governed by Callisthenia's laws and border regime, whereas the all-affected principle is confined to those within the territory who are actually coerced by its institutions. On this reading the assembly's franchise rule was too narrow, since it excluded non-residents who will nonetheless be subject to the new state's coercive power.

It is fair to ask whether either principle escapes circularity. The all-subjected principle looks like a genuine escape, because it identifies the demos by reference to a fact — subjection to coercion — that obtains independently of any democratic decision. But this is only half an answer. Which persons count as subject to Callisthenian coercion depends on where Callisthenia's jurisdiction is taken to run, and that is something the assembly itself decided. The principle therefore still needs a prior determination of the territory, and the regress returns one level up.

The conclusion, though, is not that Callisthenian democracy is a sham. If the boundary cannot be fixed by a vote of the demos, it can be fixed by a vote about the demos. What Callisthenia should do is hold a referendum in which the current electorate — the adult permanent residents who chose the assembly — decides whether to admit all affected outsiders to the franchise. Such a referendum would be authorized by the people who are actually there, and its outcome would be legitimate in a way the assembly's franchise rule was not, because it would be the affected themselves deciding to be included. Whelan is right that democratic theory cannot generate the boundary from within; but a democratic decision about the boundary is precisely what democratic theory can generate, and Callisthenia is well placed to make one.
