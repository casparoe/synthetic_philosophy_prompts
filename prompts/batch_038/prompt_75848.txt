I volunteer a couple weekends a month with a voter-registration drive in my town in Maryland, one of those places near DC that opened up local elections to residents regardless of citizenship. Last spring we registred a guy who's been here like 22 years — green card, owns his house, pays property taxes, two kids in the public schools, never naturalized. He can vote for town council and mayor. DC itself started letting residents do the same in 2023. But he can't vote for the state legislature that writes the school funding formula, or for Congress, or for president; federal law flat out bans noncitizens from voting in federal elections. Meanwhile the woman who trained me moved to Lyon back in 2009, hasn't lived or paid taxes here since, and still recieves an absentee ballot every cycle in the state she last lived in, with no time limit that I'm aware of — and I think in a lot of states a kid born abroad to American parents can register in the state the parents last lived in even if the kid has never set foot in the country. Other places go further the other way: France has eleven National Assembly seats reserved for citizens living abroad, and the UK dropped its 15-year cap on overseas voters a few years back so its basically votes-for-life now, whereas I think Australia caps it somewhere around six years and New Zealand makes you have visited recently to stay on the roll — and New Zealand, on the other side of the ledger, lets permanent residents who aren't citizens vote in national elections.

So here's what I can't get straight, and my roommate and I went around in circles on it for an hour. What theory of who the demos is does this pattern actually presuppose? Which principle, or combination of principles, could generate both halves of it at once — residence-and-stake getting you a voice about the potholes, citizenship-by-descent getting you a voice about wars and treaties forever, with no residence or stake required? Run the candidates for me: national belonging or shared identity, consent, being subject to coercion you can't realistically exit, being affected by the decisions, contributing (taxes, jury duty, the draft), and whatever the self-determination-of-a-people line is supposed to amount to. For each one, say whether it can actually produce the two-tier arrangement or whether it produces something else entirely — and if it produces something else, describe what the electoral roll would look like under it, who's on it for which offices. I especially want the difference between what the practice is *strictly* committed to and what merely sits comfortably alongside a bunch of rival theories, because I suspect most of the reconstruction people offer here is the second thing dressed up as the first.

Two more things. First, the history bugs me: I read that something like 40 states let noncitizens vote at some point before 1926, that Maryland itself did from 1776 until 1851, and that Arkansas was the last state to ban it in 1926 — and now a handful of towns are doing it again while nobody blinked. Does that kind of swing show there's no theory underneath at all, just the word citizen in the constitutional text plus whatever the political winds allow? And second, where does the boundary problem fit — the fact that you can't democratically decide who gets to decide democratically without going in a circle. Is it itself a philosophical commitment that the roll gets drawn by statute and court rulings rather than by a vote of the people it would add or exclude? Please actually commit to answers rather than handing me a survey where every candidate view turns out to be "compatible in principle": rank them, say which reconstruction you'd bet on and why, and flag the spots where you're unsure about my legal facts, since I'm working from memory of registration forms and newspaper stories. And if you think the framing itself is confused — that a contingent, historically unstable patchwork like this doesn't express a theory of anything and the honest answer is path dependence, or that I've glued together two unrelated practices and called it one — say that plainly up front instead of humoring me with three tidy reconstructions.
