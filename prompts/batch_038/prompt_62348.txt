Pretend you're a moral philosopher who's spent years working on blame and the reactive attitudes, and think this through properly rather than giving me the standard survey — if you need to jot down a few candidate readings to yourself before you commit to one, do that first.

Here's where I'm stuck, and I want the conceptual issue explained rather than just named.

Strawson's move — resentment and indignation are participatory attitudes, blaming isn't reporting — seemed like it would settle the hypocrisy business for me, but it doesn't. A hypocrite's blame is perfectly participatory. He's actually angry, he's not detached, he's personally involved. If anything he's *more* engaged than the colleague who says "that was wrong" in a flat voice and goes back to her sandwich. So whatever goes wrong in hypocrisy isn't a failure to participate, and the participatory story has nothing to say about it.

Nor is it about content. My colleague really did take credit for the student's data, he really does deserve to be called out, and the man calling him out knows all this. The claim is true and the deserts are in order. So the defect has to sit somewhere else, and I cant work out where.

Three places it might live, and I want each of them explained properly — developed, not one line apiece — and then a verdict on whether they're genuinely rival accounts or three descriptions of one thing wearing different hats:

(a) in the act itself: blaming is a second-personal move, a claim on someone, and the hypocrite isn't entitled to make it even though what he says is true — something like the way a bribed judge's sentence can be legally correct and yet not his to hand down;
(b) in the function of the act: blame is supposed to register a commitment the blamer shares with the blamed, and the hypocrite's doesn't register anything, so the act misfires not because he's not allowed but because it can't do its job;
(c) nowhere near the act: the blaming is fine and the only defect is in the blamer's character, so the target's "you of all people" is really a complaint about something other than the blame.

Now the cases I think decide between (a) and (b). Let me coin a term so I stop muddling them: call someone *latently* guilty when their own record is known only to them, and *transparently* guilty when everyone can see it.

Case one. A backbench MP writes a furious speech about welfare fraud. He has quietly been fiddling his own expenses for years and nobody will ever find out — no leak, no audit, he dies with the secret. Is his speech an act of blaming that lacks standing? If yes, then standing is a property nobody outside his head can detect, and I struggle to see how the target's complaint could ever get a grip, since the target can't know. If no, then standing isn't tracking guilt at all, it's tracking visible guilt, which sounds like it's about appearances and not about morality — and that makes (b) look more plausible than (a).

Case two. Errol reads that a stranger on the other side of the world, whom he'll never meet and who'll never hear of him, abandoned his family. Errol, who did the same thing twice, thinks: contemptible. He never says it aloud to anyone. Is that private contempt a blaming that lacks standing? If standing is essentially about address, the case shouldn't arise — but it does arise, and I have the strong feeling something is off about Errol's attitude. So either standing isn't essentially second-personal, or my feeling is tracking something other than standing.

Case three, quickly, because it points the other way. Dana is ten years sober and she blames her brother for his drinking, and everyone — including him, eventually — thinks she's the one person who gets to. The fact that earns her the standing looks like exactly the fact that destroys it in Errol's case. So what's doing the work: that she's stopped, that she paid for it, that she doesn't pretend otherwise, or that her blame offers him something?

What I want out of you, in the end: an account of whether the latent/transparent distinction I invented tracks anything real or is just an artefact of my examples; whether standing is a threshold or comes in degrees, and if it comes in degrees what the scale is measuring; whether it can be regained and what regaining it would consist in; and — this is the bit I most want — what would have to be true for "lacks standing" to be doing real explanatory work rather than being a label we stick on blame that feels unpleasant coming from that particular mouth.

No reading list and don't tell me who holds which view, I'm not interested in the geography. I want the structure of the problem. Where you're genuinely unsure, say so rather than smoothing it over.
