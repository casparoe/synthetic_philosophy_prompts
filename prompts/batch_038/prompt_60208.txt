I work as a data analyst for a regional education authority in England, and last Thursday our team sat in on an internal seminar about a new report that had estimated the heritability of a standardised reading score at roughly 0.72 in our population, using twin data. Afterwards two of my colleagues, Nadia and Elliot, got into a long argument in the office kitchen that I failed to settle, and they have since agreed to let a neutral party referee it, which is how it lands with you. Neither of them is a geneticist, though both are numerate, and I should stress that neither side was posturing: they both made points I could not answer on the spot, and I have tried to reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can rather than tidying it up in anyone's favour.

Elliot opened (1) by saying that a figure of 0.72 means genes account for about 72 per cent of any given child's reading ability and the environment for the remaining 28 per cent, so that literacy programmes are "pushing on the small share of the outcome that is actually up for grabs". Nadia replied (2) that he had misread the statistic: 0.72 is a ratio of variances computed within the sample, not a decomposition of any individual's score, and it says nothing whatever about how much of one child's reading level is genetic. Elliot conceded the correction but dismissed it as bookkeeping, arguing (3) that the practical implication survives untouched, because if most of the variation in the population tracks genetic variation, then a uniform intervention that lifts every child's score does nothing to close the gap between children and leaves the ranking exactly where it was. Nadia answered (4) with a thought experiment: take genetically diverse maize seed, sow half in uniformly poor soil and half in uniformly rich soil, and within each field the variation is entirely genetic, so heritability is 1.0 in both, yet the difference between the two fields is entirely environmental and could be abolished by simply changing the soil. Elliot said (5) that this proved nothing beyond relabelling, since a within-field heritability of 1.0 is precisely the situation in which the environment is irrelevant to differences, so the example shows the statistic behaving exactly as advertised rather than failing. Nadia then pressed (6) that the same report had noted an estimate of 0.41 for reading in a neighbouring country with considerably more unequal schooling, and that a quantity which swings that far when you alter the environment cannot bear any weight in an argument about how responsive a trait is to intervention. Elliot's closing move (7) was that this actually vindicated him: the fall from 0.72 to 0.41 shows that the genetic share rises as environments are made more similar, so our own figure of 0.72 implies our schooling is already fairly uniform, which is exactly why he doubted that further environmental change would accomplish much. At that point they both turned to me, and I had nothing useful to offer.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the seven numbered moves, give a rating — clearly right, partly right, clearly wrong, or unsupported rather than false — and justify it, being explicit about which errors are mistakes about what the statistic means, which are inferences that simply do not follow from premises both parties accept, and which are empirically plausible but unwarranted given the evidence they cite. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify what both of them are missing: if there are assumptions built into the twin-design estimate itself, or distinctions between the claims a heritability figure can and cannot license, that neither of them raised but which would have changed the course of the argument, I want them named. I would much rather have a committed ruling than a balanced survey — take a definite side at each step, give your reasons for it, and do not declare a draw unless you can show that a draw is in fact the correct answer. Please finish with a final line of the exact form Verdict: <a short phrase naming who is more right overall and on what ground>.
