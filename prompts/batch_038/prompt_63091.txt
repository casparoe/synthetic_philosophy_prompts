Three weeks ago I helped my mother complete the paperwork for a move from Dumfries to a town outside Toronto, and in the course of it learned the facts set out in section 3 of the draft below. That is the only reason I am writing about secession at all. Until then the subject had been something I followed in the newspapers with the mild partisanship of a person who votes in the territory but works for an employer registered south of the border; watching a pension entitlement get quietly denominated by an administrative rule made forty years before the move was planned turned an abstraction into arithmetic. A committee of the Scottish Parliament has since issued an open call for written evidence on the constitutional arrangements that ought to precede any further referendum, and I have drafted the short paper below as my submission. It is the argument in compressed form, with the passages that are giving me trouble flagged.

I would be grateful for comments of a specific kind, listed after the draft. Two ground rules: I am not looking for sources or further reading, and I would rather be given three objections I cannot answer than a summary of a debate I have been reading for a year.

---

**Compensated exit: secession as a liability rule**

**1.** Two positions dominate the literature. On one side, the view that a territorially concentrated group may secede only where it has suffered a grave and persistent injustice: a remedial right, triggered by systematic discrimination, occupation, or the withdrawal of the protections that made the union worth maintaining. On the other, the view that a fair vote by the population of the territory is itself sufficient, and that self-determination requires no prior grievance. I want to suggest that this dispute, though it consumes most of the argument, is about the wrong question. Both sides ask whether exit is permitted. Neither says much about what exit costs or who bears the cost. Once the question shifts from permission to price, a good deal of the disagreement loses its grip and a workable institutional design falls out of it.

**2.** A referendum on independence is ordinarily described as a decision about territory. It is more accurately a decision about the legal status of particular people, taken by a subset of them. Suppose the vote carries. For those who voted against, the result is not merely a change of government, which is what happens to every losing side in every election, but a change in the terms on which they hold membership at all. Their citizenship; their entitlement to pensions and health coverage accrued over a working life under the former sovereign; their professional qualifications; their right to live and work across what was an internal boundary; the language in which the state addresses them: all are renegotiated at once, by a procedure in which they had one vote and lost it. The ordinary remedies for political dissatisfaction are voice and exit. Exit is precisely what has been foreclosed, since leaving the new state requires a further secession, which confronts the identical problem one scale down. Note also that the franchise in the last referendum was drawn on residence rather than descent, so the status of people who grew up in the territory and now live elsewhere was likewise determined without them.

**3.** This is not hypothetical in the way constitutional abstractions usually are. My mother is 78. Her state pension will continue to be paid after she moves to Canada, but it will not be increased: the United Kingdom uprates pensions abroad only where it is obliged to, and no such commitment has been extended to a country outside Europe since the early 1980s. Her income is therefore fixed in nominal terms for the remainder of her life by a decision made decades before she planned the move. Now apply the same machinery not across an ocean but a hundred miles up the road. During the last referendum campaign, the apportionment of accrued state pension rights between the two prospective governments was among the most technically contested items, and much of the reassurance offered to pensioners in Scotland rested on the assumption that membership of the European Union would settle uprating as a matter of law. That assumption is no longer available to anyone. A new state would have to negotiate the point, and negotiable protections are contingent protections.

**4.** My proposal is that a referendum on independence should not be held on a bare question. It should be held on a question bundled with a published schedule of compensation to dissenting residents, fixed before the vote and binding on the successor state. Three elements:

(a) *Status guarantees.* Any resident at the date of the vote may elect to retain the citizenship of the predecessor state indefinitely alongside the new one; entitlements accrued before the vote are honoured in full and uprated on the same basis as for residents of the predecessor state; professional qualifications are reciprocal by default; and the right to relocate to the predecessor state is unconditional.

(b) *A consent premium.* Households that register their dissent receive a transfer, payable whether or not they relocate, financed by reducing the successor state's assumed share of the predecessor's public debt by the same amount.

(c) *A nested clause.* Any sub-territory contiguous with the predecessor state whose residents vote by a specified supermajority to remain may itself opt out of the succession, on the same terms, recursively. Representatives of the northern islands raised exactly this possibility during the last campaign: that they would prefer to remain within the United Kingdom whatever the Scottish result. That the suggestion was received as eccentric rather than as a straightforward application of the principle then being invoked against London is itself evidence that the principle is being applied selectively. (The islands, it is worth remembering, were pledged to the Scottish crown in the fifteenth century as security for a marriage portion that was never completed; territorial title is an inheritance of transactions, not a moral given.)

**5.** The obvious reading of (a) through (c) is that they compensate losers, and that compensation is a second-best substitute for consent. That reading undersells them. The schedule does most of its work before the vote, not after. A majority that must specify in advance what it will pay the minority is making a different decision from a majority that votes on an aspiration. It cannot treat the terms of separation as a detail to be settled later, in a negotiation where it will hold most of the cards, because the terms are already published and priced; and it cannot externalise the cost onto the minority or onto the predecessor state, because the cost is on the ballot paper. The function of the schedule is constitutive rather than compensatory: it converts self-determination from a claim to leave into a claim to leave at a stated price.

**6.** The theoretical shape of this is worth stating plainly. Calabresi and Melamed divide entitlements into those protected by property rules, transferable only with the holder's consent; those protected by liability rules, takeable on payment of an objectively determined value; and those rendered inalienable. Territorial self-determination is usually argued about as though the entitlement were held by the group under a property rule, with the referendum serving as the consent procedure. The difficulty is that the group is not a unanimity, and the dissenting minority inside it is being transferred without consent and without price. What I am proposing is that the entitlement be treated as protected by a liability rule with respect to insiders: the majority may take the minority's membership along with its own, provided it pays a determined sum. The minority's entitlement is not to veto, which would be a property rule and would hand a blocking position to every loser, but to a price.

**7.** There is an objection here I do not think I can presently answer, and I would rather flag it than paper over it. If membership is the kind of thing that can be taken for a price, then it is not the kind of thing that constitutes a political community; and the third of Calabresi and Melamed's categories exists precisely for entitlements whose sale would degrade what they are. My reply is that the inalienable element is being located in the wrong place. What should not be tradable is not the right to leave, but the right to make others leave with you for free. The premium does not purchase the dissenter's citizenship; it purchases the majority's exemption from the obligation to secure it. I am aware that this currently reads as a slogan rather than an argument.

**What I would like comments on**

1. Section 6 is load-bearing, and I suspect it may not be bearing. Does the property-rule/liability-rule framing do genuine analytical work here, or am I redescribing the familiar point that the terms of any separation have to be negotiated anyway, and dressing up ordinary practice as a normative claim? If it is the latter, is there a version of section 6 that earns its place, or should the paper drop the apparatus and rest on section 5 alone?

2. The consent premium in 4(b) strikes me as the weakest element, for two specific reasons: it is payable without relocation, so it compensates a declaration rather than a loss; and it creates a motive to register dissent that is independent of whether the dissent is sincere, which may corrupt the expressive function of the referendum itself. Please tell me whether either worry is fatal. I have considered restricting the premium to households that actually move, and restricting it to demonstrated loss of an accrued entitlement, but each version seems to relocate the problem rather than solve it: the first turns a status guarantee into a subsidy for exit and prices out the immobile, and the second requires an administrator to adjudicate losses that are, on my own account, partly constitutive and therefore not measurable.

3. Is the financing mechanism in 4(b) coherent? Reducing the successor state's assumed share of the predecessor's debt by the amount of the premiums appears to mean that the predecessor state ultimately funds the transfers. If so, the arrangement charges a party that did not make the decision for a decision it opposed, which is the exact vice the paper claims to be correcting. Walk me through who ends up paying and whether there is a version that does not have this property.

4. The nested clause in 4(c) invites a regress and an unmappable border. My defences are contiguity, a supermajority threshold, and cost. Are those sufficient, or should sub-territorial opt-out be severed from the paper and treated as a separate question? Relatedly: does the clause collapse into the position that any group of any size may leave, which would make the argument a reductio of the plebiscitary view rather than a reform of it?

5. Does section 3 overreach? I use a frozen pension for an emigrant to Canada to make a change in status imposed by a moving border vivid, and a colleague tells me the two are not the same kind of thing: one is the ordinary consequence of a person's own decision to leave a jurisdiction, the other is imposed. I want to know whether the example should be kept, hedged, or cut.

6. On section 7: is my reply available at all, or does the inalienability objection defeat the whole approach rather than merely the premium? If it defeats only part, please say which part.

7. Finally, tell me the three most serious problems with the argument, ranked, and if a section should be cut outright, say so and why. Please do not give me general observations on structure or style beyond what is needed to fix the items above, and do not suggest reading.
