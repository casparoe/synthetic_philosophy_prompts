For four years I have been the volunteer records coordinator at a small county historical society. I am a retired school-bus dispatcher, not an archivist; I taught myself accession numbering and descriptive cataloguing from free library guides, and I have spent more hours than I can count transcribing the registers of the county poor farm and the sheriff's jail blotter. Last month a member of our board put an argument to me that I have not been able to answer, and it has stopped me from doing work I think still needs doing. Here it is, as close to her version as I can render it.

(1) Almost everything that survives about ordinary people in a county like ours was written down by someone with authority over them: the tax assessor, the sheriff, the poor farm superintendent, the probate judge, the school board, the state inspectors.
(2) Those records were made in order to count, tax, judge, confine, or exclude the people named in them. Nobody made them so that those people could later be understood.
(3) So when I pull a name out of the poor farm register and build a life around it, I am still working inside the institution's frame: I can only say what the institution thought it worth recording, in the categories the institution supplied.
(4) Care does not escape that frame. Noting the race column, quoting the charge verbatim, adding a note explaining what the poor farm was — none of this steps outside the record; it only makes the frame clearer and therefore harder to dislodge.
(5) Therefore, the more thoroughly we document the people this county controlled, the further we extend that control.
(6) And the people the county did not bother to control left no trace at all, so there is nothing to recover about them either.
(7) It follows that the honest options are to leave these records unindexed and unsearchable, or to describe them strictly as records of the institutions that made them and never as records of the people in them.

Her conclusion is that our current project — publishing the poor farm registers and the jail blotter online, fully transcribed and name-searchable — should be abandoned. I cannot accept (7). Yet when I read (1) through (6) one at a time, each of them strikes me as simply true; that is what the boxes in our basement say. I have no training that would let me find the seam, and I keep feeling there is something she is not counting without being able to say what it is.

So I am asking for a diagnosis rather than encouragement. Which step or steps should be rejected, and what does rejecting each one cost — what do I lose, and what becomes harder to defend, if I give that step up? If I am forced to rank them, I would rather surrender (7) than (1) or (2), because those two look to me like plain descriptions of what an archive is. Tell me whether that ranking is a mistake.

Beyond the argument itself, I would like two practical things. First, is there a defensible course between publishing everything name-searchable and leaving the registers closed — and if so, what makes one version of that middle course principled rather than merely a compromise? Second, what should I do about the people who appear in no surviving document whatsoever? Is their absence a fact I can state responsibly, and if it is, how do I state it without filling the gap by invention?

Please write your answer in American English.
