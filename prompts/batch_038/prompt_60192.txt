I run a two-person workshop outside Providence making reproduction architectural metalwork: brass and iron hinges, escutcheons, cast finials, door hardware, worked to eighteenth-century patterns. About a third of our jobs are for houses where the originals are long gone, so we work from surviving examples, pattern books, and old photographs, and we sell everything stamped as reproduction. Nobody here has any training in philosophy; I read slowly, mostly in the evenings, and over the past few years I have accumulated from customers, dealers, and the trade a list of cases that I find genuinely confusing about what an original is worth. I have also read a handful of older essays on forgery — I remember one arguing that a forgery and an original you cannot tell apart must nevertheless differ aesthetically, another arguing that what the forger lacks is achievement rather than beauty, and a third about restoring paintings — but I have deliberately not gone looking for how these cases get sorted professionally, because what I want to know is whether my own scheme holds up or collapses.

My scheme goes like this. When I look at a made object, the features I seem to value fall into two groups: the ones somebody chose and the ones somebody inherited. The particular rake of a hinge's leaves, a proportion that could easily have gone a millimeter either way, the decision to leave a surface rough where a smooth one would have been faster — those are choices. A copy arrives with its choices already made; the copyist faces plenty of hard problems, but they are problems of execution, not of decision. So I have been trying to score objects by what I will call decision-cost: roughly, how much of what you are looking at was settled by someone standing in front of alternatives rather than in front of a model. The trouble is that the notion seems to break exactly where I would want it to work, and I cannot tell whether that is my fault or the notion's. Here are the eight cases, all of which are either real things I have handled or slight variations on them:

1. A brass hinge we cast last year from a mould taken directly off an original, sold and stamped as a reproduction. Nothing about it was decided by us except the decision to make it.
2. A hinge a competitor cast from that same original, who — unaware of the source, thinking he was designing from scratch — adjusted the proportions until they looked more right to him, and sold it as his own design. His adjustments land within a hair of the original.
3. A genuine 1790s hinge by no known maker, stamped in the 1960s by a dealer with a famous London workshop's mark and sold at ten times the price. The stamp is visible now to anyone who knows to look, and nobody alive profited from the lie who is still in the business.
4. A door left unfinished by a well-known cabinetmaker who died, completed by his apprentice exactly as the master's surviving notes specified, sold as by the master with the apprentice's role disclosed in small print at the bottom of the description.
5. A whole suite of hardware made in 1910 by a firm still using hand methods, in an eighteenth-century style, honestly marketed at the time as revival work, now routinely mistaken by buyers for antique and never corrected by anyone.
6. A cast finial that is about 70 percent original metal and 30 percent recast and repatinized sections that no conservator could pick out without radiography.
7. A modern piece made by a very good forger, correctly identified as a forgery within a year, and then resold for decades as a period pastiche, with everyone in the later chain telling the truth about what they believe.
8. An exact reproduction our shop made of one specific famous original — not of the type, of that object, including a repair somebody did to it in 1880 — sold as a reproduction, and now displayed beside the original in a museum case, where visitors consistently say they prefer ours.

For each of the eight, give me a number from 0 to 10 for how much of the object's value survives once the full truth about how it came to be made is public and believed by everyone looking at it, and a second number from 0 to 10 for how wrong the deception involved is, where 0 means no deception occurred. Then rank all eight by surviving value and tell me where that ordering and the wrongness ordering come apart and why. After that, go case by case and say whether the drop you gave is explained by decision-cost or by something else entirely, and give me a single number between 0 and 1 for how well you think decision-cost works as a general account of what an original is worth, with one sentence of justification. Flag the two cases you found hardest to place and say what makes them hard. Pick two cases and tell me whether your number would change if the truth were permanently unknowable, because that is the test I actually care about: I want to know whether I am valuing a history or a surface. Be blunt if you think my distinction is only a repackaging of one people already draw, and if it is, name which one. No bibliography and no reading list, please — names are fine, and I will chase down any references myself.
