Consequentialism is said to face a 'cluelessness' problem (Lenman): our actions have effects that run far into the future, and we have no reliable way of estimating what they will be. Set out this objection as clearly as you can. Then say whether you think it shows consequentialism is false, or only that it cannot be used as a decision procedure. And here's the student's answer, transcribed from their handwriting (I've kept their phrasing exactly, including the weird bits):

"The cluelessness objection starts from the fact that what we do ripples out much further than we can see. If I pull a girl out of a river, she goes on to have children, and those children have children, and there is no way for me to know whether the resulting chain of people and events makes the world better or worse over the next two centuries. It could easily be worse. Consequentialism says an act is right if and only if it produces the best consequences, and it is normally read as telling us to work out the consequences of each option and choose the best one. But if the relevant consequences cannot be known even in principle, then there is nothing to work out, because we have no non-arbitrary way of estimating them. So this is an epistemic objection rather than a metaphysical one: it doesn't say the act with the best consequences isn't the right one, it says we can never tell which act that is. That distinction is the whole point, and it's what separates this from an objection that says consequentialism gets the wrong results.

I think the strongest thing about the objection is that it isn't the paralysis worry dressed up. Paralysis says that if you had to gather all the information you'd never get round to acting; cluelessness says that even with all the information a human being could ever collect, the facts that matter are still missing. But otherwise it is basically the self-defeat problem: a theory which, if everyone followed it, would make its own aims worse achieved. And once you see that, the reply is the same reply that gets given to self-defeat — don't follow the theory act by act, adopt rules or dispositions whose general tendency is good, and then you are not required to predict the remote future of each individual act.

Does it show consequentialism is false? No. Whoever presses the objection is not claiming the criterion of rightness is contradictory, only that it cannot guide anyone. That is a serious practical problem but not a refutation. Also, the objection is not a specifically consequentialist one. Every moral theory has to care about consequences somewhere — a Kantian still needs to know whether his lie will start a war — so if cluelessness refutes consequentialism it refutes everything, which means it refutes nothing. That is a reason not to take it too seriously.

Two responses, then: (1) the rule or disposition response described above, and (2) simply maximise expected value using subjective probabilities, since decision theory is built for exactly this kind of uncertainty and we act on the best estimate we have. A third response would be to discount the far future so it matters less, but that looks ad hoc."
