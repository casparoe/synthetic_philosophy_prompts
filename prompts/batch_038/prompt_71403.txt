I am working through the centrepiece chapter of a trade-press book on the limits of self-knowledge, which is built around Williamson's anti-luminosity argument from *Knowledge and Its Limits*. The author is no hack — the chapter is careful with names and gets the broad shape of the argument right — but I do not know this literature well enough to tell where careful shading shades into something a specialist would reject. Here is the key passage, transcribed with the book's italics removed:

'Williamson's target is an old and seductive picture: that the mind is transparent to itself, so that if you are in pain you cannot fail to know that you are in pain. He calls a condition luminous when, necessarily, if it obtains then one is in a position to know that it obtains. Feeling cold, being in pain, being frightened, believing something — precisely the states that seem to announce themselves — are the candidates. His argument is a sorites. Take someone who feels cold at midnight and warms gradually, in steps too small to be noticed, until by noon they are comfortably warm. Nobody can know exactly when they stopped feeling cold; and in any case, if you knew at some moment that you felt cold, your feeling cold would have to carry over to the next moment, or else your knowledge would be sheer luck. So there must be some moment at which you feel cold and are not in a position to know that you do. Feeling cold is not luminous, and nothing else interesting is either. Luminosity — Williamson's way of restating the old Cartesian claim — is really just the KK principle, that if you know you know that you know, pointed at your own mind, and this argument is what finally kills it. The upshot is no local curiosity about thermoregulation. At every moment there are truths about your own state that stay beyond your reach however patiently you attend to them, and more of them the more finely you try to draw distinctions. Introspection turns out to be no better than perception: fallible, indirect, dark at the edges — which is what the psychological literature on introspective unreliability has been telling us all along.'

Three things bother me, though I cannot say whether they should. First, the passage defines luminosity in terms of being *in a position to know*, and then two sentences later talks freely about what you can and cannot know and about attending more patiently to your own states; I cannot tell whether those are being treated as interchangeable, or whether the switch is doing quiet work. Second, the identification of luminosity with KK: that is not how I had understood KK, which I took to be purely about iterating knowledge whatever its object, but the author sounds confident enough that I suspect a technical reason I have missed. Third, the final clause, which recruits empirical psychology as independent confirmation.

So: work through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a Williamson-sympathetic epistemologist would sign without qualification, what is a harmless simplification, and what is wrong or seriously misleading. Where a claim is wrong, give the correct version, and be explicit about the modal structure of the conclusion — whether the argument licenses one possible case per condition, or the standing ignorance at every moment the book announces, or something between. Then take up two further questions. Is the identification of luminosity with KK defensible in either direction: does KK entail the luminosity of the condition of knowing, does anti-luminosity entail the failure of KK, and is the refutation of KK what the argument is *for*, or at most a corollary? And which link would a defender of the transparency of phenomenal states attack — the impossibility of locating the transition, the requirement that knowledge carry over to the neighbouring case, the availability of the series for this condition at all, or something about the phrase 'in a position to know' itself?

Please do not turn this into a survey of possible readings. I want your own verdict: which sentences of that passage you would keep exactly as they stand, which you would rewrite and how, whether the author's overall conclusion — that self-knowledge is no more secure than perception of the external world — survives once the errors are corrected, and, if I read nothing further on the subject, how much of the passage I could safely rely on.
