I'm a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy writing a dissertation on the interpretation of probability in interwar Cambridge, and this term I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar module on uncertainty in policy advice. For the second week I'd like to hand out something other than the usual excerpts: a short dramatic dialogue that forces two rival accounts of partial belief into direct confrontation in a situation where the disagreement has consequences. The positions I want dramatized are roughly Keynes's and Ramsey's. On the Keynesian side: probability is an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, something apprehended rather than chosen; such relations are frequently not measurable by numbers, and sometimes not even ordinally comparable; and, crucially, the probability of a conclusion on given evidence is a different question from the weight of that argument — the quantity and comprehensiveness of the relevant evidence — so that two conclusions can stand at the same probability to their respective evidence bases while one of those bases is far better evidenced than the other. On the Ramseyan side: a degree of belief is not a relation anyone perceives but a dispositional property of an agent, displayed in choices and bet-like conduct; its normativity comes from coherence, since a set of betting dispositions that can be jointly exploited is defective, together with responsiveness to evidence; and there is no further fact about rational credence over and above that.

The scene I have in mind is this. Two members of an advisory panel — call them Nwosu, an economist seconded to a regional emergency-management authority, and Hertz, a decision theorist who sits on the same panel — are finishing a report late at night in a rented office above a hardware shop in the valley they've been studying. The authority must decide whether to deploy an experimental debris-flow warning system along that valley before the wet season, and it has demanded a single figure: the probability that the system fails to issue an adequate warning when a flow actually occurs. The panel has two evidence bases. The first is nine years and several hundred events of operational data from a closely comparable system in another mountain range — abundant, but different geology, different vendor, different warning protocol. The second is eleven deployments of the actual system in the actual valley, one of which produced a missed warning — scant, but direct. Both routes put the failure probability in roughly the same neighborhood, so the number the authority wants comes out the same either way. Nwosu wants the report to state that figure but to add an explicit sentence recording that the weight of argument on the direct evidence is very low, and to recommend deferring full deployment for a year so that further deployments can accumulate. Hertz objects that this is evasion dressed as sophistication: if a year of deferral is worth its cost, Nwosu's own dispositions already encode a number, indeed a whole distribution, and the weight clause either restates something about her higher-order uncertainty, in which case it belongs in the probability column rather than beside it, or it states nothing capable of changing what anyone should do. Have Hertz at some point offer her a small private wager on the outcome in order to prove she has a number; have her take it, while insisting that accepting it demonstrates only a convention she has adopted under pressure, and that reading a probability off her behavior confuses the causality of temperament with the logic of evidence.

Please write the dialogue at roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words, in prose with speaker labels and only light stage direction, and subject to the following. Both characters must be equally formidable: the Keynesian may not be allowed to retreat into mystery or come across as an obscurantist, and the Ramseyan may not be reduced to a technician who assumes all uncertainty is a spreadsheet. Each must take up the other's actual arguments instead of restating their own case, and I want genuine engagement with four things: whether weight can be absorbed into higher-order uncertainty, and in particular whether the two evidence bases described above can differ in weight while yielding the same spread of higher-order credences; whether the wager settles anything, given the Keynesian's distinction between what her conduct manifests and what the evidence warrants; whether coherence plus evidence-responsiveness can carry the normative load Keynes assigns to objective logical relations, or whether the Ramseyan ends up smuggling those relations back in through the demand that belief track evidence; and what, on each view, the report should actually say to the authority, since that is where the argument began. Don't let either of them convert or concede defeat, and don't send in a third character to adjudicate; end with the two trying to isolate the single claim on which everything else turns and failing to agree even about how to formulate it. Two housekeeping points: I have no way to check quotations, so please don't put verbatim words from either historical text into anyone's mouth — let them paraphrase and argue in their own idiom, with at most passing reference to the older debate — and don't append a bibliography, footnotes, or a list of sources.
