I am an undergraduate double-majoring in biology and philosophy, currently enrolled in a philosophy of biology seminar on function attribution, and I also work part-time as an editorial assistant at the company that publishes a widely adopted high school biology textbook. My supervisor has asked me to draft an internal procedure for a recurring problem. Our chapters contain dozens of sentences that assert or imply a function — "the function of X is Y," "X is for Y," "X serves to Y" — and we draw complaints from both directions. Some reviewers object that this phrasing smuggles in teleology or design. Others object that when we rewrite such sentences into strictly historical, selected-effect language ("structures like X were favored in ancestors that..., because..."), the result is unreadable at our grade level and reads to them as evolutionary content being pushed on students. Neither complaint disappears if we simply adopt one account of function and stick to it.

What I want is a decision procedure that a freelance copyeditor with no philosophy training can run on a single flagged sentence in ten to fifteen minutes: the questions to ask in order; what answer licenses keeping the function locution as written; what forces a rewrite into causal-role phrasing ("X does Y, which contributes to..."); what forces a rewrite into historical phrasing; what forces a referral to our subject-matter editor, who is a biologist and signs off on every referred sentence; and where the procedure simply stops and hands the matter to a human because the question is one of house policy or values rather than fact. Please mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and state what the procedure cannot settle.

Then run it on three sentences from a current draft: "The function of the penguin's flipper is propulsion underwater." "The human coccyx functions as an attachment site for muscles of the pelvic floor." "The appendix functions as a reservoir that repopulates the gut with beneficial bacteria after a bout of illness." At least one of these should come out in a way my supervisor will dislike; tell me which one and why.

Build the procedure on the philosophical distinction it is operationalizing rather than on citations. Where that distinction is itself disputed, separate what is settled among people who work on it from what remains a live disagreement, and do not present a contested position — including pluralism — as though it were the consensus.
