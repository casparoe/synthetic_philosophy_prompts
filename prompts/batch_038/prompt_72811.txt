Ok, I'm a third-year grad student running a replication-plus-variants project on the Knobe effect and I want a number from you, not a literature tour.

The target claim: the harm/help asymmetry — in the original CEO-and-the-environment vignette pair, something like 82% say he intentionally harmed the environment and 23% say he intentionally helped it — shows that moral evaluation is part of the *competence* people deploy in applying "intentionally." Built into the concept, in other words, rather than being a downstream urge to blame, or a conversational quirk of how the test question is phrased.

Here's the twist, which is my own data (n≈140 per arm, online sample):

1. SYSTEM arm. Same vignette, but the decision-maker isn't a CEO — it's an autonomous logistics system the engineering team deployed, and the lead engineer says on the record that she doesnt care at all about the environmental damage, she just wants throughput. Bad side effect happens. 71% said the *system* damaged the environment intentionally; help arm came in at 29%. Asymmetry basically intact.

2. DESERVING VICTIM arm. Standard human CEO, standard harm, but with an added paragraph establishing that the affected waterway belongs to a firm that has itself been dumping illegally for a decade and that everyone in the case agrees they had it coming. Intentionality attributions collapsed to 38%.

3. CONTROL arm reproduces the classic numbers (79%/26%), so my sample isn't weird.

My advisor thinks (2) settles it for the blame account: take away the appetite to punish and the intentional attributions go with it, so morality never touched the concept itself. I think (1) settles it the other way: a deployed software system is not a natural target of blame the way a human CEO is, and the asymmetry survived anyway, so whatever is driving it tracks agenthood generally rather than blame. Obviously we're both partly right, and I suspect (2) is contaminated becuase the deserving-victim paragraph probably changes how bad the side effect *seems*, not just how blameworthy the CEO seems — which is a confound I can't easily argue my way out of.

So: give me your credence in the target claim, decomposed. I want separate rough numbers for (a) the asymmetry is real and not an artifact of vignette wording, (b) given it's real, it reflects conceptual competence rather than motivated blame, (c) given competence, moral evaluation is constitutive rather than some correlated non-moral mechanism doing the work — something like sensitivity-to-reasons, or whether the act expresses the agent's deep attitudes — and (d) whether the question is even determinate, i.e. the possibility that "intentionally" is really two concepts and a chunk of this debate is verbal. Then tell me how you're combining them, and if you think they shouldn't just be multiplied, say why and combine them your way instead.
