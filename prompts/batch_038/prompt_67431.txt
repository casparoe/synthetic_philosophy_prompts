Our MA course in the philosophy of artificial intelligence closes with a three-hour written paper on which candidates answer four questions out of six; each carries 25 marks and is pitched at roughly fifty minutes of writing. I have been assigned the question on Turing's 1950 Mind article. My first draft came back from the co-examiner with the objection that a candidate who had merely skimmed the paper could collect most of the available marks by reconstructing the imitation game, listing the objections Turing canvasses, and attaching a stock reply to whichever one they remembered best.

Please write a replacement question whose centre of gravity is the closing subsection of that article, in which Turing takes up the objection from extra-sensory perception and, unusually for that stretch of the paper, concedes it some force: he judges the statistical evidence for telepathy to be unusually impressive, he entertains the possibility that a machine might itself be telepathic, and he suggests that the testing arrangements would have to be altered to screen such a channel out if it were admitted. The question must require candidates to decide whether that subsection is philosophically load-bearing or an historical curiosity, and to earn the verdict by working out what Turing's proposed alteration does and does not concede. Specifically, I want them to face the thought that if the interrogator's judgement has to be insulated from a non-behavioural route into the witness's states of mind, then indistinguishability under ordinary conditions cannot be sufficient for the inference the game is meant to license — together with the opposing thought that this is nothing more than routine experimental hygiene, attaching to every empirical test of every hypothesis. A first-class answer should also be able to say what the subsection implies for Turing's much brisker dismissal of the argument from consciousness earlier in the same catalogue, and whether the two treatments can be reconciled.

Deliver three things. First, the question itself, in two parts with a mark split you judge defensible, and with a stem that restates in a sentence or two what Turing says in the subsection so that the item is self-contained. Second, a model answer of about 700 words, written at the level of a strong first-class response rather than a survey. Third, a marking scheme distributing the 25 marks across five named components, each with a one-line statement of what earns full credit and a one-line note on how candidates characteristically forfeit it. The scheme should additionally state what caps an otherwise fluent answer at a mid-grade result — for example, treating the subsection as biography rather than as argument, converting the item into a dispute about whether telepathy exists, or reaching for Searle or Block where Turing's own text is doing the work.

Two constraints. The item must be answerable by a candidate who has read the article closely but encountered none of the secondary literature, so the model answer should cite no commentators. And because the paper will be printed and distributed, do not place any wording in quotation marks and attribute it to Turing: paraphrase the subsection, locate it within the article's argument, and omit any detail you are not confident of rather than reconstructing it.
