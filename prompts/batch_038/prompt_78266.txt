Last night I argued with a friend until two in the morning and came away with the distinct sense that I had lost without being able to say where. I would like a diagnosis rather than encouragement. I am a lawyer; she is a philosopher, and she defends a strong version of the view that hypocrisy destroys a person's standing to blame. When I pressed her for the actual argument, she produced the following, which she maintains is valid and whose conclusion I cannot accept. I have reconstructed it as charitably as I can, and I have probably made it tidier than she did:

(1) To blame someone for doing something is not merely to believe that it was wrong and to feel badly about it. It is to hold it against them: to register the act as a mark to their discredit and to be disposed to treat them in light of that registration.

(2) To register an act as a mark to someone's discredit is to assert that the standard the act violated genuinely applies, and that violating it genuinely counts against the violator.

(3) That assertion is made not only in words but in the blamer's own conduct as well. A blamer who has herself violated the same standard, and who has not repudiated her violation, has conduct that asserts the contrary: that the standard does not really bind, or that breaching it does not really count.

(4) No one can coherently assert a proposition and its negation. So the blame of such a person is internally inconsistent — defective in the way a self-contradictory assertion is defective, and not merely impolitic, uncharitable, or badly timed.

(5) An act of blame that is defective in that way fails to accomplish what blaming is for. It does not succeed in holding anything against anyone, in the way that a verdict issued without jurisdiction does not convict anyone.

(6) For any fault of ordinary seriousness, there is some version of that fault which nearly everyone has committed at some point, and no one has repudiated every fault they have ever committed.

(7) Therefore nearly no one has standing to blame nearly anyone for anything of ordinary seriousness. Blame of serious wrongdoing turns out to be the exclusive province of saints and small children, who are the people least likely to be moved to engage in it.

(8) But the practice of blaming one another for serious wrongs evidently works. Wrongdoers are sometimes moved by it, relationships and communities are regulated by it, and we treat the resentment of a victim as carrying a weight that the resentment of an uninvolved busybody does not. A theory on which this entire practice consists of failed performances is not a theory I can live with.

(9) So at least one of (1) through (6) is false.

Tell me which one you would reject, why it looked acceptable in the first place, and what you pay by rejecting it. I should say where my own sympathies lie, though I do not want that to constrain the answer: I would most like to keep (1) and (2), because the picture of blame as holding-against rather than as belief-plus-feeling strikes me as genuinely illuminating, and I would also like to keep (8). When I said as much, her reply was that I was simply refusing to follow the argument where it leads, and I had no answer to that, which is why I am asking.

There are three further things I could not get her to face, and I would like to know whether any successful repair has to accommodate them:

First, (3) appears to demolish self-blame along with everything else. My own past conduct asserted, on this picture, that the standard did not bind me; I have repudiated it only now, in the course of condemning myself. So it seems I cannot blame myself either, or at any rate not until the repudiation is complete — and once it is complete, I am not sure what is left for the blame to attach to.

Second, the condition looks neither necessary nor sufficient for what she wants. A man who has never been dishonest in his life, but who prosecutes the shoplifter with relish and says nothing about the embezzler on his own board, satisfies (3) perfectly; yet something is clearly wrong with his blame. And a man who did steal, was caught, repented, and has genuinely repudiated the theft also satisfies (3) — but that is close to the paradigm case of the person told that he of all people should stay quiet.

Third, the granularity of the standard in (3) is doing unacknowledged work. She insists the standard must be read broadly — dishonesty, rather than this particular lie to this particular person — because on a narrow reading almost everyone is clean almost everywhere and (6) loses its grip. But I could not extract a principled answer as to where the broadening stops. If the standard is typed so as to guarantee that (7) comes out true, then (6) is not an independent premise at all; it is the conclusion in disguise.

Two questions I could not resolve and that I suspect are load-bearing. Her presentation moved without comment among three quite different claims about the hypocrite's blame: that it is unfitting, that uttering it is impermissible, and that she lacks the authority to address it to the wrongdoer. In court those are different failures with different remedies, and I do not see why they should collapse into one another here. And she says the whole argument is downstream of the idea that blame is a reactive attitude, and that reactive attitudes are the sort of thing one can lose the entitlement to express; I cannot tell whether that is a further argument, an explanation of (1), or nothing at all.

I am not after a reading list, and I do not need the names of everyone who has held or attacked each position. What I want is the step, the reason it seduced me, and the price of giving it up — including, if you think (9) is false and I should simply accept (7), an account of what moral life looks like on the far side of that.
