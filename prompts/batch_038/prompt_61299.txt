I co-teach an environmental ethics seminar with a colleague — call her Rae — who works on wild animal suffering, and after four years of arguing with her I still can't pin down the rule she's actually running. I'd like you to try.

Her track record, all of it local land-use cases we've gone back and forth on. The question I put to her each time was the same: does this change make things better or worse for the animals, all-in?

- County stops mowing the highway verges three times a summer and lets them go to a tall flowery sward cut every third year. Worse, she said, and not close — shrews, nesting songbirds, and every beetle in the county move in.
- A complex of fishless vernal pools, where the surveys come back basically all dipteran, gets drained for a distribution warehouse. Better. She was almost cheerful about it.
- Wolves go back into a valley holding an overabundant elk herd that's been winter-starving for a decade. Better, though she added she wasn't counting the vegetation knock-ons.
- A dam comes out and sixty years of dewatered riverbed gets re-flooded, fish passage and all. Worse. Her line was that a river is a machine for turning water into animals.
- A farmland project plants beetle banks and hedgerows: a modest bump for birds, an enormous one for arthropods. Worse, but she conceded it was close, and said that if you weighted the birds heavily enough she might flip.
- An old-growth stand gets clearcut and replanted as even-aged conifer plantation with a mowed understory. Better — the plantation is, in her words, ecologically boring, and boring is good here.

One more thing I keep tripping over. She has this phrase she comes back to about whether an animal's life is long enough to amount to anything, whether there's enough there to be a subject with a welfare level at all versus just a hatch that turns over every nine days. But when she applies it she applies it mostly to fish and birds and mammals, and I cant tell wether the duration is doing the work or the taxonomy is.

New case. A beaver wetland is getting drained for a flood-control project nobody can stop, and the mitigation options are (a) a shallow managed stormwater basin, which by every account becomes a midge and mosquito factory with a fresh cohort every week or two and essentially no vertebrates, or (b) a small deep pond, stocked with fish, ringed by mown grass — far fewer invertebrates, but a real population of fish, turtles, frogs, and herons. Which does she pick, and does she pick it for the reason I'd guess?

So, in order: her verdict on (a) versus (b); the candidate rules that her six verdicts are jointly consistent with (I have headcount-totalism-across-all-sentient-beings and some kind of vertebrate-weighted view in mind, and I suspect the duration business is a third, and there may be a fourth hiding in the river and plantation remarks about how much biomass a system pumps out per year); which rules predict which option; your best guess with the reasons for preferring it; and the case I should put to her next that would tell the rules apart. I have a hunch it involves a long-lived invertebrate but I can't construct it cleanly. And if you think her precedents genuinely underdetermine the choice, tell me what she'd be committing herself to by answering either way.
