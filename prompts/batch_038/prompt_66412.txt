I spent thirty-eight years in experimental physics, most of them designing and calibrating instruments for measurements taken at the outer edge of what could be measured at all. I retired six years ago and have taken up philosophy with what I suppose is the disreputable enthusiasm of a man who no longer answers to a funding agency. My present preoccupation is Dennett's scheme of four kinds of creatures — the Darwinian, the Skinnerian, the Popperian, and the Gregorian — arranged as a tower in which each storey adds a new way of having candidate actions tested and eliminated before the organism itself is. I follow the general shape of it, and I follow why the topmost storey is named for Richard Gregory rather than for a theory of selection. What troubles me is a question of a different sort: how much of that tower marks a real joint in nature, and how much is convenient description. I should like your assessment in a form I can check rather than merely read.

First, a proposal of my own, which you are entirely free to reject. In my laboratory no one trusted an instrument until it had been calibrated against a standard external to it and until the magnitude of its likely error had been estimated. The capacity that made this possible was not the capacity to use the instrument; it was the capacity to treat one's own indicators as suspect, and to ask how one would know if they were lying. I would call the creatures that possess it Baconian creatures, and I suspect that this is a storey the tower is missing: an animal that not only inherits external mind-tools but audits the coupling between those tools and the world, and that can accordingly be deceived in a new way — by a miscalibrated standard rather than by a bad hypothesis.

I have three requests, and I should like numbers rather than hedged prose.

1. For each of the four canonical storeys, assign a diagnosticity score from 0 to 10. Let diagnosticity mean this: the likelihood that a competent team, given a newly discovered species and a year of carefully designed behavioral experiments, could establish that the species belongs at that storey and not merely at the one below it, in terms that an independent team would reproduce. Attach to each score a single sentence identifying the experiment that would do the discriminating, or explaining why no such experiment exists.

2. Rank the four storeys by those scores. Where two fall within a point of one another, say which of the two you would defend under pressure and why.

3. Score my proposed Baconian storey on the same scale, and then give me a single number between 0 and 1 for the probability that it constitutes a genuine further stage rather than a redescription of the Gregorian one. If you judge it a redescription, name the particular Gregorian capacity into which it collapses.

Finally, one more number. The tower is presented as cumulative, each storey presupposing those beneath it. Give me a probability between 0 and 1 that a species could be thoroughly Gregorian — richly scaffolded by inherited artifacts, notation, and convention — while remaining essentially Skinnerian in its own head, with the winnowing of its candidate behaviors done by its culture rather than by any internal rehearsal of consequences. If you regard that possibility as incoherent, say so, and tell me what in the argument turns on rejecting it. And if I have mis-stated any part of the scheme above, correct me before you answer; I came to this late and would rather be set right than flattered.
