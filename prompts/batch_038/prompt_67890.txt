I serve as registrar at a small university art museum and sit, without a vote, on the collections committee. For roughly thirty years we have displayed an ink-and-wash drawing on paper catalogued as the work of a well-known twentieth-century European modernist; I am leaving the name out because the matter is still live and I would rather the case not be traceable. It reached us in an alumnus's bequest and carries an insurance valuation in the high six figures. Last spring a visiting scholar working in our study room established, to the committee's satisfaction, that the sheet is a studio copy: it was almost certainly drawn by one of the artist's students from the finished original, which hangs in another collection, as a workshop exercise, and it bears the studio's blind stamp but no signature in the artist's hand. Earlier cataloguers evidently read the stamp as an attribution. The scholar also dated the paper stock to the studio's own supply. Nothing about the drawing's condition, its marks, or the way it looks on the wall has changed.

Two committee members, both of whom know the material well, have been arguing about what follows, and the argument has now outlasted three meetings. One is a professor of aesthetics (whom I will call M.), the other a painter who teaches in the studio department (D.). (1) M. opened by saying that a wall label is an assertion the museum makes under its own authority, that we now believe the assertion to be false, and that the drawing must therefore be relabelled as a studio copy; keeping it on view with the old attribution, or with a studiously empty one, is a way of getting visitors to believe something we know is not true. (2) D. answered that nobody can tell the difference. He has shown the drawing, unlabelled, to three studio classes and to several colleagues in the art history department, and not one of them suspected it was a copy. If the marks on the paper are the same marks, he said, then what there is to look at has not changed, and the label is not part of the drawing. (3) M. said the issue is not what can be seen but what the object is a record of: the original is a trace of the artist's own hand at a determinate moment in his development, whereas this sheet records a student's exercise. Two objects can look exactly alike and be different achievements, and in art value attaches to the achievement. (4) D. replied that this principle would demolish most of what museums hang. Anonymous medieval icons have no attributed author and are not valued less for it; studio pictures from a master's workshop that everybody agrees are first-rate are routinely shown as workshop productions; and in photography it has long been normal practice for an artist's master prints to be made by a printer working under the artist's supervision, which no one calls forgery. A criterion that turns on the artist's own hand, he said, proves far too much. (5) M. said those cases differ in a way she can state precisely: in none of them does the institution assert a false attribution, and in some the assistant's hand belongs to the work's sanctioned mode of production, authorized by the artist himself. Here we would be asserting, on our own authority, that this sheet is by someone we now believe did not make it. (6) D. said he could live with a corrected label, and that his real objection is to the rest of the proposal — that the drawing come off view pending review and that its valuation be cut to what amounts to nothing. That sequence, he said, shows the disagreement was never about aesthetics: the drawing has not become a worse drawing, only a cheaper one, and if a change invisible in the object produces a collapse in price, then what is being tracked is money. He added that the donor's descendants have made plain that a public downgrade would prompt them to withdraw the remainder of the bequest, some forty other works, so the price of M.'s truthfulness is a substantial net loss of public access to art whose quality nobody disputes. (7) M. answered in two parts. First, a false assertion is wrong to make whether or not it yields good consequences, and treating the family's threat as a reason to keep asserting something the committee believes false inverts the order of reasons entirely — it makes our word a hostage to whoever is most offended by it. Second, the collapse in valuation is not evidence that her objection is financial; if anything it shows the market registering, crudely, exactly the loss of achievement she described. She closed by turning D.'s own premise against him: if the marks are all that matter, then a museum facing a fragile original ought to be able to hang a flawless modern replica in its place indefinitely, since visitors would see the same thing — and no conservator alive would accept that, which suggests the premise is what is wrong.

The committee has asked me to put the exchange in writing for an outside adviser, and I would rather have a real judgment than my own hunch. Please adjudicate the argument as it was actually made, move by move: for each of the seven numbered items, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and justify the rating. I am especially interested in three things — whether D.'s point (4) actually refutes M.'s achievement-based account or merely shows that it needs qualification she has not supplied; whether "nobody can tell the difference" is evidence that nothing relevant has changed, or evidence of something else; and whether M.'s replica analogy at the end of (7) succeeds or depends on a disanalogy she has not acknowledged. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or a list of what to read next; bring in whatever distinctions you think bear on the case, but keep the deliverable fixed on these moves. I should add that I have reported both of them somewhat flatly, and that neither is a strawman — each has been right about things before in this committee.

Please write in American English and use the following sections, in this order. "Bottom line": two or three sentences saying who is more right overall. "The object of the dispute": about 150 words on what M. and D. are actually disagreeing about, including any places where they may be talking past each other. "Move-by-move": roughly 1,000 words, seven numbered entries, each opening with its rating and then giving your reasons. "What both of them are missing": about 300 words. "Verdict": about 250 words, saying who wins on which of the three sub-questions above and whether the committee has a way through that respects the legitimate point on each side.
