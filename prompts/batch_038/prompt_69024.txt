I am in the fourth year of a doctorate in the history of science, and I am currently drafting the methodological chapter of a dissertation on the consolidation of a new account of a class of gases between roughly 1770 and 1795. My committee has asked for that chapter to state my historiographical commitments explicitly rather than let them emerge from the practice, and having now written the list down I am no longer confident that the items on it are jointly tenable. I would like a careful audit of the set before I circulate it. The commitments, as they stand:

(a) Anachronism is the cardinal vice. I follow Butterfield's line in *The Whig Interpretation of History* (1931) that studying the past with reference to the present is not a stylistic failing but a category error, and that agents must be recovered through the problems they were actually solving. A narrative that treats them as precursors, scored by approximation to us, has stopped being history.

(b) My account is nonetheless realist. I hold that the theory that eventually consolidated was genuinely better than its rivals — it got more of the structure of the world right — and that this fact forms part of the explanation of why it won, rather than being an editorial comment added afterwards.

(c) The centerpiece of Chapter 3 is a provincial apothecary whose notebooks I have reconstructed. I argue that his 1774 compositional proposal was correct; that his contemporaries' rejection of it was not a reasonable response to the evidence available to them but a mistake; and that the existing literature has under-credited him precisely because it benchmarks the period against the metropolitan consensus of the period.

(d) My method in that chapter is rational reconstruction. I ask what an agent credibly informed of the experimental literature in print in 1774 ought to have concluded, and I deploy counterfactuals about experiments nobody performed in order to show that the alternatives were live options. I take this to be compatible with (a), since the reconstruction is indexed to the evidence of 1774 and not to what has been learned since.

(e) I insist that the outcome was contingent. Institutional accidents, two early deaths, and one translation decided which account consolidated; nothing in the state of the field in the 1760s made the later theory inevitable, and I want the chapter to make that felt rather than merely conceded.

(f) I also want to claim that the vice in (a) has an inverted form: writing the past as a gallery of roads not taken is just as present-centred as writing it as a march of progress, because it still takes our terminus as the thing that makes the past worth recovering. I intend this as a discipline on myself, not as a criticism of others.

(g) Finally, I hold that the central terms shifted sense across the transition — the word rendered in my sources as "air" does not pick out what our word "gas" picks out — and that comparisons between the rival accounts must therefore be conducted internally, from within each framework. I do not believe there is a neutral language in which both could be fully stated and weighed against each other.

What I am after is an audit, not a survey of the literature on presentism, which I have read and which the chapter already summarizes. Specifically:

1. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them flat. For each, say why the conflict is substantive rather than verbal, and identify precisely what would have to be surrendered or restricted to remove it.

2. Identify the pairs that I have probably mistaken for tensions when they are not. Where a dissolution depends on a condition I have not established, state the condition — I suspect that at least one of my own compatibility claims, and in particular the defense I offer of (d) against the charge in (a), is conditional in a way I have papered over.

3. Determine whether the verdict in (c) is one that (a) and (d) jointly license. If it is not, locate the step at which something unavailable to my own principles enters the argument, and say what a verdict about the apothecary would have to look like if it were derived only from what I have permitted myself.

4. Assess whether (f) is doing any independent work on this list, or whether it is in fact violated elsewhere in the set by an argument I have already committed to.

5. Tell me which single revision would clear the most, and what the dissertation would then no longer be able to claim. I want the repairs distinguished from one another, with their costs itemized, rather than collapsed into a general recommendation of moderation.

I would rather be told that three of these commitments are perfectly stable together than be told that the whole package is in crisis; what I need is the discrimination, since I cannot at present see where the fault lines run, and I do not want to signal a willingness to give up any particular item, because I do not know which one I would choose.
