You're a philosopher of decision theory who has also sat on a hospital ethics committee — take on that role, and sketch a few brainstorming notes to yourself before you settle on an answer.

Here's the practice I'm chewing on. Guideline bodies and low-value-care campaigns tell clinicians not to order a test unless the result would change management: no imaging for uncomplicated low back pain, no routine annual chest x-ray, no pre-op ECG on a healthy 30-year-old. The stated worry usually isn't the cost or the radiation, it's the cascade — a false positive leads to a biopsy, the biopsy to complications, an incidental finding to years of surveillance. But the same profession insists patients have a right to their whole chart, pushes open notes, and treats withholding results as a paradigm autonomy violation.

So what theory of the value of information does the first norm presuppose? There's a result I think is due to Good saying a free piece of evidence can't lower your expected utility as long as you act optimally on it — is the testing norm a rejection of that, or actually an application of it (value zero rather than negative), and which reading does the cascade reasoning really support? What does it quietly rule out — knowing as good in itself, a right not to know, distrust of laypeople's updating? Is the clash with open notes real or only apparent, and where is the practice underdetermined, i.e. which rival theories would license it just as well? End with what would have to change if we took seriously the idea that information has non-instrumental value.

Please separate what's settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — dont hand me a contested view dressed up as the consensus.
