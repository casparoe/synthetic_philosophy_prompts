I'm TAing a philosophy of probability course and I've got one script left to mark before tomorrows section. The question was:

**Q4 (12 marks).** State van Fraassen's Reflection principle. Explain the diachronic Dutch book argument for it. Then say whether you think that argument succeeds, and why. *(Marked 4 for the statment of the principle, 4 for the argument, 4 for the evaluation.)*

Here's the answer, exactly as written:

> Reflection says your present degree of belief in a claim should equal what you expect your own future degree of belief in it to be. So if I now expect that tomorrow, once the result is in, I'll be 0.8 confident that it rained, then I should already be 0.8 confident now. The principle generalises deferring to an expert: an expert is just someone whose opinions you expect to come round to sharing, so deferring to an expert and deferring to your future self are literally the same principle pointed at a different person.
>
> The argument for it is the diachronic Dutch book. Say my credence in p is now 0.3, but I'm certain that tomorrow it will be 0.8. Today the bookie offers me 30p for a ticket paying £1 if p; 30p is my fair price, so I sell. Tomorrow my credence is 0.8, so the bookie offers to sell me that same ticket back for 80p; 80p is my fair price now, so I buy. I'm down 50p whether or not p. He could equally well work with the negation: sell me a £1-if-not-p ticket today for 70p, which is my price today, and buy it back tomorrow for 20p, which is my price then. Same 50p loss. So the pair of prices was incoherent, and since the same construction goes through for any gap between present and expected future credence, breaching Reflection is just probabilistic incoherence stretched over time. Notice the arguement doesn't assume anything about how I get to tomorrow's credence.
>
> Does it work? Partly. It shows something real: opinion change you already expect should be built into your present opinion. But it also looks like it proves too much. If I know that tomorrow I'll be exhausted, or infatuated, or up against someone I find unnervingly persuasive, I expect my credence to move, and Reflection tells me to move it now — which rewards anticipated corruption instead of tracking evidence. Same if I've precommitted myself, mast and sirens style. So I'd reject Reflection as a general principle. What survives is that Reflection isn't doing independent work anyway: it's a special case of Lewis's Principal Principle, which is what really earns its keep. Where the Principal Principle says match your credence to the chance, Reflection says match your present credence to the chance of your future credence. Read that way the Dutch book is a symptom of the mismatch rather than a proof of anything.
