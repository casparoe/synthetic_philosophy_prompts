I've been doing tax controversy work for about eleven years and lately I've been reading jurisprudence on the side, and something about my own day job has started to bug me. Treat this seriously: play the part of someone who is both a practicing tax lawyer and a philosopher of law, think it through carefully, and if it helps, spend a paragraph brainstorming candidate readings before you settle on one. But do settle. I want a committed answer, not a survey.

Here's the practice, described as plainly as I can. In the US system a taxpayer can have a transaction disregarded — and get penalized — for doing something the published statutory text pretty clearly permits. Courts apply things like the business purpose test and the economic substance doctrine: an arrangement that fits the literal words of a deduction provision can be ignored if it didn't change the taxpayer's economic position in a meaningful way apart from the tax effect, or if there was no substantial purpose for entering into it other than the tax saving. So the operative law isn't the enacted text, it's the text plus a purpose the court reconstructs and supplies. And sitting on top of that is a whole ladder of confidence levels for how sure your position has to be — reasonable basis, substantial authority, more likely than not, and further rungs above that for formal opinions — where each rung buys you different penalty treatment, plus disclosure rules, plus a separate standard for what a return preparer has to believe before signing, roughly that there's a realistic possibility the position would be sustained on its merits. Which means the system openly lets you file a position you think is probably wrong, so long as you price the risk and disclose it.

Meanwhile the very same legal system, in criminal law, insists on fair warning and resolves ambiguity in a penal statute in favor of the defendant. Nobody there gets punished for doing what the words of the statute permitted. And officially we treat tax as an obligation you owe, not a fee you may opt out of by paying a surcharge.

So: what view of legal obligation does the tax practice actually presuppose? Does it presuppose something like the Holmes bad-man picture — law as a prediction of sanctions, with the duty to pay really being a price with a graduated penalty schedule attached? Or the near opposite — that the taxpayer is a participant in a purposive scheme who owes fidelity to its point rather than to its wording, in which case the pricing of uncertainty isn't a permission at all but a grudging administrative concession? Or something else again; I've wondered whether it quietly presupposes that legislatures sell ambiguity as a rent-distribution device and that nobody inside the practice really believes the code means what it says.

A few specific things I want out of you:

1. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. My suspicion is that most of the standard readings are underdetermined by the facts on the ground, and I want to know which ones the practice actually forces.

2. Tell me whether the tax side and the criminal side can be made coherent. Are they really two seperate pictures of what published rules oblige, or is there a single theory that licenses lenity in one and substance-over-form in the other? If it's a straight inconsistency, say so and say which half gives way.

3. The thing that started this for me. Does any of it help or hurt the taxation-is-theft slogan? The people who say that usually mean the state takes property by threat of force without your consent, and I've always found that empty as an argument. But I have to admit a regime where the amount owed isn't fixed by published rules, where you're expected to divine a purpose, and where getting it wrong costs you a surcharge rather than a finding that you violated anything — that doesnt look much like a rule-following property order either. Does the practice undercut the slogan, or does it end up confirming the version of it I thought was dumb?

4. If we swapped the presupposed view out for a strict enacted-text-is-the-law view, what would have to change institutionally — how the IRS litigates, how advice letters get written, how penalties are structured, what happens to all the transactions currently tolerated? Concrete fallout, not just theory.

Please don't hand me a menu of interpretations with the pros and cons of each and leave me to pick. Tell me which one you think the practice presupposes, argue for it, and flag the single place your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong.
