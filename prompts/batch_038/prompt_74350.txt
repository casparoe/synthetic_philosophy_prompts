Right, this is a weird one and I could do with some help. I'm the lay member — vice-chair, actually — of the conduct and recognition committee of a small professional institute. We regulate transport planners and we also run the annual awards, and because there's only seven of us the same committee does both, which everybody agrees is odd and nobody has ever got round to fixing. On Thursday we have two items arising out of the same road scheme, and over the last fortnight I've become convinced we're about to minute two findings that can't sit next to each other in the same document without somebody noticing.

Item one is a conduct complaint. A member of ours signed off a bus-priority junction last year. Her own team had put it in writing that the layout would remove the only step-free crossing serving a sheltered housing scheme on the corridor; when it was raised with her she said, in front of two colleagues and in the note itself, that she wasn't there to solve the crossing problem, she was there to hit the reliability target before the funding deadline. The crossing went. The complaint is under a clause saying members must not deliberately cause harm to users of the transport system, and the gap between "deliberately" and "carelessly" is the gap between a reprimand and a year's suspension from the register, so it isn't academic. Most of the committee, me included, are minded to find it deliberate. Item two is her award nomination, same scheme. The citation, which she drafted, says she cut corridor emissions by roughly forty per cent by deliberately prioritising decarbonisation in the design. Her team's note flagged that the emissions saving was a certain by-product of the bus priority — flagged in the same week, in almost the same words as the crossing warning — and when that was put to her she said the same thing again: not in it for the emissions, here for the target and the deadline. Our awards criteria require that the outcome was deliberately pursued, and the committee is minded to say the citation overclaims and strike that sentence out, on the grounds that the carbon saving was really just a windfall. She's already said she'll appeal the conduct finding, and our clerk has allready pointed out, helpfully, that an appellant gets to see the minutes of both items. There's a third item about somebody's late CPD return which is dull and you can ignore.

My daughter, who's doing a psychology degree, tells me the pattern in those two items has a name and a couple of decades of questionnaire studies behind it — the Knobe effect, something about a boss who's told a scheme will boost profits but harm the environment, says he doesn't care either way, and then gets called intentional by four out of five people when the side effect is bad and barely a quarter when it's good. My instinct is that we're just being sloppy and should be pushed into consistency: if foresight plus indifference is enough for the crossing, its enough for the emissions, and we should say so. But I want the paper to argue against me properly, because one of our members, a retired KC, thinks the two items aren't the same question at all — that you can deserve blame for what you foresaw and shrugged at without thereby deserving credit for what you foresaw and shrugged at — and he thinks the criminal law settled the point anyway with the virtual certainty test from the late nineties, which as I understand it is about what a jury may find rather than must find. So: could you draft the written opinion I'll circulate on Wednesday evening? It needs to land on an actual recommendation for each of the two items — uphold, dismiss, defer, whatever you think we should do, and in which order we should take them — a short section on how the published reasons should be worded so the two findings don't blow each other up on appeal, and two or three sentences of suggested wording for the code clause we could put to the institute's council for next year, since "deliberately" just hangs there undefined at the moment. Please take the retired KC's point seriously rather than knocking it down, because if blame and credit really do run on different rails, that's an argument for writing the code differently, not an argument for fudging. Two sides of A4 at most, and no reading list or references — we've got a dinner to book on Thursday and I need something seven tired people can argue with over coffee.
