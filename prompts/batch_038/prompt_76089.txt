I need help writing a eulogy, and I want the speech built out of my friend's own intellectual commitments rather than merely draped in them, because she would have scored every sentence and she would have known which ones I hoped to get away with.

Dorothea Ashworth — everyone called her Dot — died nine days ago at fifty-eight. She was an epistemologist who worked on accuracy-first epistemology, or epistemic utility theory: the view that the one fundamental epistemic good is the accuracy of one's degrees of belief, cashed out with a strictly proper scoring rule, and that the familiar norms of rational belief are not primitive but follow from it — credences that violate the probability axioms are accuracy-dominated, and so is any way of updating other than conditionalizing on what you learn. She was not a popularizer. She was the kind of person who spent a decade arguing that the quadratic score was the one worth living by precisely because it is bounded, so that one catastrophically confident mistake could not swamp the score of an entire life; the unbounded alternatives, she thought, made a person unlivable.

For twenty-two years she kept a prediction log: every call she made, the credence attached, the resolution. Each January we sat down together and computed her Brier score against the pooled benchmark of the forecasters she tracked. I am not an academic — I run a risk-analytics group and met her at a prediction meetup in 2004 — but that computation was ours. The twenty-second year of the log ended the week she died, so the last three months are full of open predictions that will now never resolve, and the final comparison can never be finished.

The service is Saturday at eleven, in the chapel at the edge of her campus. Half the pews will be her department and the accuracy people who flew in from three countries, and they will notice immediately if anything said from that lectern is confused. The other half are her mother, her two brothers, her neighbours, and the Wednesday-night meetup she co-founded — most of whom have never heard the word "credence." Her mother asked me last year, with Dot standing right there, whether her daughter had ever in her life been simply sure of anything. I did not answer then and I have to answer on Saturday, to both halves of the room at once.

Dot left a note with instructions, and I intend to follow them:

1. The eulogy is to contain exactly one number, and that number is to be one of my credences, reported honestly as of the moment I say it aloud. She wrote that she expected me to be tempted to report a credence I wished I had, or one that would be comforting, and that she would be able to tell.
2. Nothing unquantified that I do not actually endorse. If I say she lives on in us, I had better be able to say what that means and hold it.
3. She anticipated that I would feel obliged to address the afterlife question and told me not to waste the eulogy lying about it. My credence that Dot and I meet again is about 0.05; it has not moved in twenty years; she logged it in her own book after we argued about it in 2009.

The candidate numbers I actually have in hand are these. The 0.05, which she has pre-emptively excused me from. Roughly 0.9 that her twenty-two-year log beat the pooled benchmark — except that the final year is now uncomputable, so this is a claim about an unfinished calculation rather than a result. And a number about my own conduct going forward — whether I keep the log, whether I am still scoring myself five years from now — where I genuinely do not know, partly because of grief and partly because her own framework gives no clean verdict about what to report when you expect your future evidence and your future judgment to be impaired: deference to your expected future credences pulls one way, and the higher-order-evidence problems she worked on pull the other. There is a further wrinkle the experts in the front row will see without being told. At least six people in that chapel can predict me better than I can, and announcing a number about my own state in front of them is itself evidence arriving while I am speaking. The speech should not pretend otherwise.

One more thing, which you will have to decide how to handle. For eleven years there was a sealed envelope in her desk: the result of a predictive genetic test she had taken and never opened. She told me once that she did not really accept the standard result that free information can never be expected to lower your accuracy — that she accepted it in print and not in her life, and that this was the one inconsistency she was unwilling to fix. She died of the condition the test was for. I opened the envelope while clearing the desk, and it said what it said, dated eleven years earlier. Her family does not know. Whether to tell them on Saturday is not a question her epistemology can settle, because the theory governs what evidence does to accuracy, not whether to speak, and that silence is exactly where I now have to work. I want the speech to carry that gap rather than paper over it, but I do not want it to become a case study.

Write the speech: about nine hundred words, seven minutes, deliverable by someone who may need to stop and breathe, with one number and no more, no jargon that the surrounding sentence has not earned, no citations, nothing that reads like a conference paper in a suit. It has to make her mother understand why the spreadsheet was a form of love rather than an incapacity for it, and it has to survive being audited by people who could reconstruct the argument from my notes. Affection first: any sentence whose only job is to signal that I know what a strictly proper scoring rule is should be cut.

After the text, add no more than a short paragraph telling me which proposition you chose for the single number, and why each of the others would have failed her test.
