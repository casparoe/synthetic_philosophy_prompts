I am interviewing a philosopher next month at a public festival of ideas: forty-five minutes on stage, audience of philosophically literate non-specialists and graduate students. Her position is a radicalized version of the Strawsonian line from the 1962 essay Freedom and Resentment, which she calls constitutive responsibilism, and it rests on three commitments I want the questions to turn on.

First: an ascription of moral responsibility is made true by nothing over and above its being warranted by the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude, forgiveness — of competent participants in a moral community. There is no further fact, metaphysical or historical, in virtue of which such ascriptions are true or false. Second: those attitudes are criticizable and revisable, but only against standards internal to the practice of holding one another responsible — its own norms of fairness, proportionality, and excuse; no general justification of the practice from outside is either available or needed. Third: therefore nothing in the causal history of an action can defeat responsibility. She bites this bullet explicitly — an agent designed and covertly manipulated from birth, every disposition implanted by others, is just as responsible as anyone else, provided he has the ordinary capacities for reflective self-governance and our resentment tracks the quality of his will. Determinism, on her account, is not merely compatible with responsibility but beside the point.

Two further commitments of hers should be exploited. She holds that the objective attitude — treating someone as an object to be managed, cured, or trained rather than engaged with as a participant — is available in principle toward anyone, but that no one could be rationally required to adopt it wholesale, since the requirement would have to come from outside the practice and there is no outside. And she holds that the spread of neuroscientific and psychiatric explanation is producing what she calls exemption creep, a widening of the class of people we view rather than address, which she regards as a genuine loss of moral community rather than the correction of a past error.

Her reputation as an interviewee: warm, quick, and a master of the tactical example. Hand her an abstract objection and she will narrate a vivid case, answer that case charmingly, and the objection will have evaporated. Every question must therefore arrive with its case already loaded, and specific enough that she cannot substitute a friendlier one.

Write me eight questions, one for each of the following, in an order you choose and justify in a single sentence before you begin — start with something she can handle well so the room settles, build to the sharpest exchange somewhere in the middle, and end on the last topic:

1. The tumor case: a man whose impulse control changes after a lesion is discovered. On her account, is that an excuse, an exemption, or neither — and what is left of the distinction once the explanation is causal all the way down?
2. The variant community: one that resents people for misfortunes that merely befall them, or one that treats every harmful act as exempting. Are their ascriptions, on her view, equally true? If she dissents, what makes her community's internal standards authoritative for anyone else's?
3. The manipulated agent, pressed with the observation that if the world is deterministic, the manipulated man and the ordinary man have histories of exactly the same kind, so the intuition she is dismissing has nothing distinctive left to grab onto.
4. Resentment aimed at non-personal targets: a corporation that knowingly poisoned a river, a state, a parole algorithm, a person long dead. Does the quality-of-will analysis scale up to these, or is the attitude misplaced but socially useful?
5. Internal revision: whether we should stop blaming the addict and start blaming the executive who shelters behind a corporate structure — and what distinguishes a moral discovery from a change of taste when both happen inside the practice.
6. Exemption creep: on what grounds she can call it a loss, given that if the practice itself is shifting, the internal standards shift along with it.
7. Self-application: whether her own resentment functions, on her own account, as evidence about anything; and what in fact happens to her attitudes when she learns that the colleague who slighted her has a diagnosis.
8. A closing request for the single fact about ordinary moral life that, if it obtained, would show her account to be false.

For each question give me four labeled items: the question as I would actually speak it aloud (first person, roughly seventy words or fewer, any technical term glossed in a passing clause); one line naming the pressure point in her view it exploits; the answer she is most likely to give; and a follow-up keyed to exactly that answer rather than to a different one. Beyond the opening question, no softballs: nothing about her influences, nothing about the state of the discipline, nothing asking her to define her terms, and nothing she could dispatch by restating her first or third thesis. No question may presuppose familiarity with any particular piece of secondary literature.

Then commit, in prose rather than in a menu. Tell me which of her three theses you take to be false, and why. Identify the two questions above that you judge capable of doing real damage as opposed to producing a merely awkward pause, and state what a genuinely good answer to each would have to contain. Say plainly which of the remaining six a skilled guest survives, and how she survives them. Finally, flag one place where you think your own framing is unfair — a loaded case, or a term you have defined in a way that begs the question against her — and rewrite that question so that it is fair and still sharp. Do not hedge about which questions are strong, and do not leave me to choose between competing options: I want your assessment and your reasons for it.
