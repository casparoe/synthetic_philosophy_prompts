I am running a fifty-minute session for second-year undergraduates on the problem of logical omniscience. They have had propositional logic, an introductory probability course, and a knowledge-representation course in which an agent's beliefs were a set of formulas extended only by running inference. None have seen epistemic logic, and the only resources in the room are a whiteboard and what they already know. My goal is narrow: they should leave understanding that representing belief as a set of possible worlds is a substantive idealization, that it makes belief closed under known implication as a feature of the representation rather than of anyone's psychology, and that this closure costs something real — without concluding that the framework is therefore worthless.

I drafted the session around Lois Lane, who believes Superman can fly while failing to believe Clark Kent can, but I now suspect it teaches them something adjacent. Tell me whether to keep it, drop it, or hold it back for a particular purpose, and say which example should carry the main line instead — including what that example must exhibit and what it must not. I also cannot decide whether to begin with the possible-worlds representation or with the puzzle about arithmetically incompetent agents that it seems to generate; pick one and justify the ordering in terms of what each step requires from the last.

Then map the misconceptions. I expect three: that the problem is only about finite time and memory, so a resource bound settles it; that moving from outright belief to degrees of confidence dissolves it; and that no one ever claimed real agents were omniscient, so the objection attacks a strawman. For each, say where in the sequence it must be headed off and why it cannot be postponed. Tell me too what their knowledge-representation training gives me to work with — the instinct that a belief state is something you compute consequences from, rather than something that already contains them — and where leaning on that instinct will mislead them.

Finally, give me one closing question that separates students who have grasped the problem from students who can recite it, and a short list of what I should decline to explain at this level while saying so out loud.
