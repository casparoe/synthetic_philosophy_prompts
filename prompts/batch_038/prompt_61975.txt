Ive been in a three-day argument about whether King Arthur existed and realized nobody in the thread agrees on what "existed" would even take to establish. One guy says absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. Another says Gildas was basically a contemporary, describes Badon at length, and never once names Arthur. A third says there's a Roman Artorius in the inscriptions so obviously. I want to write this up as an essay but I don't want to write it until I have the spec, because right now all three of them are using "historical" differently and just yelling.

So: the requirements list. What does a body of evidence have to meet before you're licensed in saying a legendary story has a historical kernel behind it? Not "how do historians work" in general, the actual conditions. For each one, the case or consideration that motivates it, and a mark for whether it's non-negotiable or whether it's the pet criterion of one camp. I suspect a couple of the famous ones out of biblical scholarship, and possibly "contemporaneity" in general, are camp prejudices rather than real requirements — argue with me if you disagree, that's fine, I just want the flag raised.

Things I think the spec has to keep distinct, tell me if I'm wrong:

- site vs. story vs. person. That there's a destroyed citadel at the right place in the right century licenses something, and it isn't "the Trojan War happened." How much does it license, exactly?
- "someone like this existed" vs. "the narrative as we've received it happened," and how much of a story has to be true before the story counts as having a kernel at all.
- arguments from silence, which need two priors and not zero: would this have been written down if true, and would the writing have survived. Its why the Exodus silence and the Arthur silence don't carry remotely the same weight — half a million people plus livestock wandering a desert for forty years is the sort of thing that leaves stuff, and one warband leader in post-Roman Britain is precisely the sort of thing that leaves nothing. (And yes, I know the comeback: Egyptians didn't advertise their losses. But notice that the comeback is about the recording prior, not about the story, and I want the spec to make that move explicit rather than letting people smuggle it.)
- independence and descent. Geoffrey of Monmouth says he worked from a very old book in Welsh that someone handed him. The Welsh annals slot Badon in 516 and Camlann in 537, but they're a mid-tenth-century compilation surviving in a twelfth-century copy, and I gather the Arthur names in those two entries may be later insertions. So how do you count sources that share an ancestor, and what's a late source worth when it claims an early one you can't see?

Also worth having in there: the Merneptah Stele has "Israel" as a people in Canaan around 1208 BCE. That's doing something in the argument that "the Egyptians would have mentioned an exodus" isn't doing, and I want the spec to be able to say what.

What I most want, though: flag any pair or subset of your requirements that can't all be satisfied together. My suspicion is that the criteria doing the actual filtering — contemporaneity, written corroboration, multiple independent attestation — are only ever satisfiable by the powerful, the urban, and the literate, which means a spec strict enough to reject Arthur also rejects most of what genuinely happened to most people in the past. If that's right, is it a bug in the spec or a feature? Should one of the requirements get dropped, and which?

And give me your priors. Roughly what fraction of the well-known semi-legendary figures from antiquity should a calibrated person expect to have actually been real, given that legend-production is a completely normal, continuous social process and not a rare event? A number and a defense, not a shrug. Legend-making is cheap, which is the whole reason I want a spec instead of vibes.

Purpose, so you can calibrate: I need to be able to say with a straight face, in one essay, that the Trojan War probably had some basis, that Arthur probably didn't, and that the Exodus as the text describes it is very unlikely — and I need all three verdicts to fall out of a single consistent standard rather than three different moods. If your spec can't produce those three verdicts in that order, tell me which one it disagrees with and why.

Bottom line in the first sentence: name the requirement that's doing the most work and say whether you think the set is jointly satisfiable. Then the list, then a short pass over Troy, Arthur, and the Exodus showing which requirement is carrying the weight in each case. No preamble and no restating my question back to me. Table is fine for the list. No bibliography, no citations, no "consult so-and-so" — I want the argument structure, not the scholarship. And where you're not sure of a date or a manuscript detail, write "not sure" rather than making it sound confident.
