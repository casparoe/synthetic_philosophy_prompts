So I'm giving a 20 minute paper at a small workshop on reasons for the commandments in medieval Jewish philosophy, with a designated respondent, and honestly I don't need more help with the paper itself — I need a Q&A prep sheet for the hour afterwards.

My thesis: the standard way Maimonides on sacrifice gets taught is as a historical etiology. God let Israel keep burning animals because that's the only worship the generation that came out of Egypt could conceive of, and the specific rules exist to steer them off the surrounding idolatrous cults. I want to argue that reading is both too deflationary and too historicist. Taken as jurisprudence it's actually a general theory of legislative accomodation: the law deliberately lands short of the ideal because the addressees can't carry the ideal, and the shortfall isn't a defect to be explained away but part of what the commandment means. Second-best commandments — fully binding, fully meaningful, and not secretly pointing at their own abolition. That's also what lets him lean on the Jeremiah verse where God says he never spoke to the ancestors about burnt offerings when he brought them out of Egypt, while still writing a legal code that lays out the entire sacrificial order in exhaustive detail and expects the messianic king to rebuild the Temple and restore its service. Ramban's old charge — that on this account the Temple service is just divine stagecraft, an elaborate concession that tells you nothing about what God actually wants — is the thing I most want to defuse.
