I've been sitting in on a stat mech course this term (it fits my timetable and the prof doesnt take attendance) and last week we did the renormalization group and universality, and afterwards my friend — philosophy PhD student, works on exactly this stuff — and I ended up arguing about it at a bar for two hours. The case that got us going: the liquid-gas critical point of a simple fluid and the Curie point of a uniaxial ferromagnet come out with the same critical exponents and both sit in the same universality class as the 3d Ising model, even though the microscopic physics is about as different as you can get — molecules with dipoles and short-range repulsion versus spins on a lattice with nearest-neighbour coupling. Order parameter exponent around 0.33 instead of the mean-field 1/2, and above four dimensions they both go over to mean field. But the superfluid transition in helium doesn't share those exponents at all even though it's also a fluid, because the symmetry of its order parameter is different. Honestly I think I won on a technicality and I'm not sure I deserved to.

His line: this is the cleanest case of genuine emergence in all of physics, because there's a lawlike regularity at the higher level that survives the deliberate destruction of the micro details — you throw away most of the Hamiltonian in the flow and the fixed point doesn't move, which he says is the opposite of what a reduced law is supposed to look like. My line: it's the best reductionist result we've got, because the flow is derived from the microscopic theory, and the irrelevance of the details is a theorem about the microphysics rather than a limit on it; plus the fact that which class you land in is pinned down by the dimension and by the symmetry of the microscopic order parameter shows the micro facts are doing all the work. I've started calling my own view expendable reductionism and his fixed point realism, mostly as a joke, but those are the two positions I want actually defended — not the generic textbook reductionism-vs-emergence pair, which is what every version of this conversation I've read collapses into.

So: write it as a dialogue between the two of us. Five exchanges, numbered 1 to 5, one turn each per exchange, a paragraph or so per turn, and nothing before or after — no scene-setting, no wrap-up, no moral; just label the turns so it's clear who's who. A few things I care about. Keep it entirely in the physics: no minds, no consciousness, no biology, and neither of us is allowed to say "supervenience," "multiple realizability," or "downward causation." I know he's going to hit me with the fact that an RG calculation doesn't start from a real fluid's molecular Hamiltonian — it starts from a coarse-grained Landau-Ginzburg free energy whose form somebody wrote down by hand out of symmetry and locality, which looks like smuggling the higher level in through the back door — and I want my character to have a real answer to that (the exactly solved two-dimensional Ising model, or whatever else you think works), not a shrug about pragmatics or convenience. Symmetrically, let him press me on what "derivable in principle" is worth when the derivation is never actually carried out for anything as messy as water. I want him as good as I am; in most tellings of this debate the reductionist is a punching bag and I'm sick of it. And the fifth exchange has to end with one of us naming something concrete — a system, a calculation, an observation — that would come out differently if the other one were right, and the other has to say whether they accept that it would. If by that point one of us has come round to thinking the whole disagreement is verbal, that's allowed, but it has to be argued for rather than announced.
