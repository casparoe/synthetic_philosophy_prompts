I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine, but one of my side gigs is that I hold the "at-large" seat on the standards committee of a professional association for science writers — five of us, all volunteers, and because I'm the writer I'm always the one who ends up drafting the awkward decisions. We've got a case that's quietly turning into a civil war and I need help thinking it through before we vote Tuesday. It's an email vote, so whatever I circulate Monday night basically *is* the argument; people will read it once, on their phones, and then pick a side.

The facts. A freelance member — call her M — was found last month to have invented a source and two quotes in a long feature. Our schedule of sanctions calls for a $4,000 fine plus a two-year suspension, and the suspension matters far more than the money, because member outlets check our list before they commission anyone. Straightforward so far. Then three letters arrived, and our bylaws turn out to be silent on all three of them. (1) M's co-author on the piece asks to serve half the suspension himself, on the grounds that our own finding describes the failure as partly editorial, that he read the draft and could have caught it, and that if the blame is shared the sanction should be shared too. (2) A very senior member — big name in the field, decades of unimpeachable work — offers to be publicly named in M's place, arguing he has more to lose by it and she has two kids in college. (3) M's sister offers to pay the fine outright and points out, correctly and embarrassingly, that the bylaws say the fine is "payable within sixty days" without saying by whom, and that the association has accepted cheques from employers and relatives at least twice before without anyone giving it a thought. There's also a fourth thing nobody wants to say out loud: we already let members buy down part of a suspension with sixty hours of remedial training, and we forgive unpaid fines after five years of good standing, so the sanctimonious "the person who did it must bear it" line seems to have been abandoned years ago by people who are now invoking it.

So here's what I need. Play the role of a philosopher of punishment who has actually sat on real disciplinary panels — call yourself our outside counsel — and write the memo I'll circulate. Start with a short brainstorming section, five or six bullets, laying out the ways the question can be carved up before you commit to anything, because my own instinct keeps sliding between two different pictures and I want them separated: one where a sanction is basically a price on conduct, so who pays it is administratively irrelevant, and another where a sanction is a statement *about a particular person*, so handing it to someone else destroys the thing it was. Don't just adopt that distinction, though — pressure-test it, tell me where it breaks, and tell me whether the co-author's request is really the same kind of thing as the sister's, since half the committee thinks they're identical and half think they're nothing alike. Then give me a bottom line up front: which of the three petitions I'd grant, which I'd refuse, and on what condition, plus a concrete recommendation about what we do *before* Tuesday's vote (do we respond to the petitioners now, sit on it, or split the decision?) and a timeframe. I want draftable bylaw language with criteria a future panel could actually apply to a case nobody has imagined yet, not a principle so abstract that it licenses whatever we already feel like doing. And treat the strongest case against your own recommendation honestly and at length — I suspect it's the co-author's, and I suspect refusing it risks being squeamishness about collective responsibility dressed up as integrity — because the senior member of the committee will make exactly that argument and I don't want to be caught flat-footed. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list, please: I can't chase them down and in my experience half of them turn out to be invented anyway. Just make it tight enough that people read it all the way through — think carefully, and write like someone who knows these decisions have to be livable in three years when the next case lands.
