I'm in an interdisciplinary reading group that has drifted from philosophy of biology into machine learning, and last week the sentence 'survival of the fittest is a tautology' did an enormous amount of work in about ten seconds. One member — call him the deflationist — used it to dismiss a selectionist explanation another participant had offered of why a population-based search procedure over candidate designs (an evolutionary algorithm, so no biology involved) converges on the solutions it does: if the principle is analytic, he said, it cannot explain anything, and the whole account is a redescription dressed up as a mechanism. A second member replied that he was committing a category mistake, since analytic principles can be load-bearing — theorems are analytic and clearly do explanatory work — and that what matters is where the empirical content sits. A third invoked the propensity interpretation of fitness, on which fitness is a dispositional property of an organism, modelled roughly on solubility rather than on a count of actual offspring, and claimed that this dissolves the problem outright; I did not follow the argument and I am not prepared to accept it without one. Note also that the phrase itself is Spencer's rather than Darwin's, which I mention only because it seems to matter that everyone in the room was arguing about a sentence nobody could produce on request.

What I want is a menu of the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, not a survey of the tautology objection — I have read the standard version, in which fitness is defined by reproductive success and the principle therefore comes out trivially true, and I want to get past it. For each candidate reading, state it as a proposition specific enough to be assessed, pinning down two things at once: which proposition is in scope (the bare schema linking fitness to differential reproductive success; the whole Darwinian theory including its empirical claims about variation and heritability; the narrower claim that natural selection explains adaptation rather than merely describing it; or some general principle about selection processes that is meant to apply to algorithms and markets too), and what 'tautology' is being taken to mean (strictly analytic in the logical sense; a definitional truth following from how the terms were introduced; unfalsifiable in Popper's sense; empirically empty; trivially true yet informative once supplied with empirical premises; a theorem of a formal framework such as population genetics). Then attach a verdict to each reading: trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive and false, or incoherent because it slides between two of those notions without notice. There is one datum I would like the menu to be able to accommodate: in the algorithm case the fitness function is literally defined to be the objective the search optimizes, so the deflationist's premise is not merely plausible but close to guaranteed, and yet the explanation he dismissed still appears to tell us something about why the search behaves as it does. Any reading that cannot account for that combination should be flagged as unable to.

Finally, situate Popper in the grid. He complained at one point that the trouble with evolutionary theory was its tautological, or as he hedged almost tautological, character, and classified it accordingly as something other than testable science; a few years later he withdrew at least part of that assessment, saying that he had changed his mind about the testability and the logical status of the theory while retaining his view of natural selection as a research programme. Tell me which cell of the grid his original charge actually occupied, whether that charge was even available to him given his own distinction between statements that are analytic and statements that are merely unfalsifiable but metaphysically fruitful — the two are not the same thing in his framework, and I suspect the objection trades on collapsing them — and what exactly the later withdrawal gave up: the tautology claim, the demarcation verdict, both, or only the scope of one of them, given that a critic could say he merely changed the subject rather than changed his mind. Close by saying which reading, if any, licenses the deflationist's dismissal of a selectionist explanation of an evolutionary algorithm, and which licenses the second member's reply about analytic principles being load-bearing. I would like this as a list I can bring to the next meeting: readings, verdicts, and the two uses adjudicated.
