I'm helping run a debate at my uni politics society next week, motion roughly 'this house believes the EU is constitutionally incapable of reform', and in the run-up two people have leaned hard on the same four words to argue opposite conclusions. Our eurosceptic speaker says the treaties commit the EU to "an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe", so a federal end-state is baked in and no amount of voting or reform-from-within can stop it. Then someone on the other side told him that's rubbish, the phrase is just preamble poetry with no legal effect at all, and offered as proof that the UK apparently got an 'opt-out' from it back in the renegotiation before the 2016 referendum — you can't opt out of something, they said, unless it's already nothing.

Can you lay out for me the distinct precise claims that phrase could actually be making? Not a history of European integration and not a summary of the argument, a menu I can bring to the debate and hold both of them against. Specifically I want:

- the different places the wording sits in the treaties and whether being in a preamble versus being in an article changes what it does, or whether that's a distinction without a difference here
- what kind of speech act it could be: a commitment, an obligation, a statement of aim, a description of what's already happening, or an instruction to whoever ends up interpreting the treaties — and for each, who exactly it binds, if anybody
- the 'ever closer' as open-ended process with no destination versus 'ever closer' as a destination you can name, and what would have to be true of the second one for the eurosceptic to get his inevitability claim out of it
- whether the plural matters — a union among peoples rather than a union of states — and whether that does any work for or against the sovereignty version of the argument
- for each reading, a verdict: platitude that nobody would deny, substantive and genuinely contestable, or just false
- which of the readings actually supports the 'reform is impossible' conclusion, and which reading the other side needs to be able to call the whole thing empty

And then the opt-out business, which is the bit I find most confusing. What would it even mean to opt out of a phrase, and does the fact that member states bothered to negotiate one tell you the phrase was inert all along, or the opposite? If there's a reading under which the opt-out makes sense, say what that reading has to be.

Finish with a short section on the tell — how I can spot, in real time, which of these readings someone is smuggling in when they say the phrase, from what they do with it in the next sentence.
