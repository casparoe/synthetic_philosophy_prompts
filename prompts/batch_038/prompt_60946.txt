I'm putting together a half-day methods workshop for incoming grad students — they're split between sociology, economics, economic history and a couple of agent-based-modelling people — and I want to open it with a little diagnostic, something like "which side of the individualism/holism fight are you actually on". Because in my experience they all show up saying they're somewhere in the middle, and then it turns out they were answering different questions the whole time.

So: nine or ten questions, each one a concrete case rather than a slogan, two or three answer options apiece, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. Nobody should be able to game it by picking the label they like — no question may mention reduction, supervenience, emergence, "holism" or "individualism", and none of them should be answerable just from knowing which camp sounds more respectable in your home department.

The part I care about most: the questions have to probe seperately the things people constantly run together. Off the top of my head — (a) whether a social fact could hold even if nothing about individuals' beliefs, desires or dispositions made it true, (b) whether a good explanation has to bottom out in what individuals actually did, (c) whether collectives (firms, markets, states, classes) can have properties that no individual has or could have, and (d) whether you can even state an individual's attitudes without smuggling in social concepts. Somebody can plausibly say yes on one axis and no on another, and I want the instrument to catch that instead of averaging it away into "moderate".

Draw on cases like these (invent your own, but cover the territory): the bank run that happens purely because everyone believes everyone else is about to withdraw; a norm under pluralistic ignorance, where nobody privately endorses it and it still stays stable for decades; a pair of simulations, one where a macro pattern is reproduced from strictly local individual rules and one where you had to hand-set a parameter for "the prevailing norm" and never explain where it came from; statistical discrimination in hiring; and a sentence like "the market overreacted", where respondents have to say what they think is literally true there. At least two questions should be ones where the honest answer is that the vignette doesn't settle it — I'd rather show people that the cases are underdetermined than pretend otherwise.

Then the key. Lay out the whole answer space: which combinations across those axes are occupied by a real position and which aren't. Every occupied cell gets a name — use the standard label if there is one, coin a descriptive one if there isn't — plus one sentence on the commitment that's most awkward to live with. For the empty cells, say what kind of empty they are: logically incoherent, coherent but nobody wants to hold it, or just unexplored and worth a look. And be upfront about which pairs of leaves my questions can't really distinguish, since I'd rather tell the students that than have them argue about a difference the quiz never tracked.
