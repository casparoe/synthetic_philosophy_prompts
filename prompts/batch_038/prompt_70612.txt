I am a second-year doctoral student in political science, and this term I am helping to compile a departmental forecasting exercise on long-run great-power trajectories. One of our assigned questions asks for a probability that the currently dominant power loses its position at the top of the international system within the next twenty-five years. While reading around the question I came across a long essay in a general-interest magazine that claimed the problem is essentially soluble by a method it called the outside view, and it impressed my supervisor enough that I now have to address it in a memo before we do any modelling of our own. I would rather know how much of it to accept before building anything on top of it. Here is its central argument as I transcribed it:

'The first mistake we make about the future is to reason from the particulars. Ask what you know about this power — its institutions, its leaders, its present troubles — and you have not adopted a method, you have adopted a bias; Kahneman spent the second half of his career saying as much. The outside view is not a rough draft to be revised in the light of what you happen to know about the case. It is the estimate. Choose a reference class of comparable ventures, count how its members turned out, and let that count be your probability; any adjustment you make afterwards is simply a confession that you have drifted back inside. Consider the great powers of the modern system. On any reasonable list there have been eleven of them since 1500, and nine have already lost their primacy, most of them within two or three centuries of acquiring it. The reigning hegemon is not a special case. It is the tenth name on a list that is nearly finished being written. That is not history, it is arithmetic. And it explains the most embarrassing result in the study of forecasting: the area specialists who knew a country best turned out, study after study, to be worse at predicting its future than outsiders armed with simple algorithms, because intimate knowledge of particulars is exactly what drags you into the inside view. Those who object that no two empires are truly comparable have already conceded the point. The certainty that this time is different is not an insight; it is the disease for which the outside view is the cure.'

Two things trouble me about this, and I cannot tell whether they are real objections or my own confusion. First, if you genuinely set aside everything you know about the case, I do not see how you are supposed to decide which eleven powers belong in the class, when each of them began, and which of their endings count as losing primacy rather than merely transforming into something else — surely those decisions require precisely the knowledge the essay tells me to discard, which would make the eleven-out-of-nine figure a product of judgement rather than of arithmetic. Second, the tally counts only powers whose story has already finished, and that feels as though it rigs the sample, though my instinct says survivorship bias and I am not confident that is the right name for what is wrong here, or even that anything is wrong.

Could you work through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a serious defender of base-rate forecasting would happily sign, which are defensible theses pushed well past what their authors actually defend, and which are simply false? I would particularly like to know what happens to the arithmetic once the sample is repaired; what a distribution of lifespans among finished cases actually licenses you to say about a power that has already been at the top for three-quarters of a century, as opposed to a power at the start of its run; whether the essay's account of area specialists is a fair rendering of the forecasting literature or a distortion of a more complicated finding; and whether the closing move — treating every appeal to incomparability as motivated reasoning — is a legitimate point or a way of shutting down a genuine methodological problem. Finally, tell me what survives the corrections. My memo has to recommend a procedure rather than merely criticize one, so I would like a sketch of what a defensible version of this argument looks like, and an account of which kinds of case-specific knowledge it would need me to bring in, and at what stage.
