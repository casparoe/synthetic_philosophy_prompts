Two months ago I was appointed the patient-advocate member of my hospital system's clinical ethics committee, which is a paid lay seat, and last Thursday a palliative care physician who also holds a philosophy degree told me, in front of eleven people, that the recommendation I had drafted was a textbook naturalistic fallacy. My draft had opened with a purely descriptive paragraph — the diagnosis, the median-survival figures from the literature, the two failed lines of treatment, the family's report of what the patient had said about machines — and had closed with a sentence recommending against resuscitation attempts. There is no normative word anywhere in the opening paragraph. I trained as a clinical social worker and took exactly one ethics course twenty years ago, so I could not answer him in the moment, and I have been building a case list ever since: inferences from how things are to how they ought to be that I make at work and at home, with my honest verdict on each. I would like to know what principle, if any, generates those verdicts, because right now they look arbitrary to me.

Here they are. (1) From the full descriptive clinical picture above to the recommendation: fine, and I would sign it again tomorrow. (2) From a complete non-normative description of a person being held down and set alight for the amusement of onlookers, to that was wrong: fine — and I would say someone who accepted the description and withheld the conclusion is defective in a way that goes beyond missing a premise. (3) From human beings are naturally gregarious and cooperative to human beings ought to be gregarious and cooperative: not fine; this is the appeal to nature I keep meeting in patient-education pamphlets and I want no part of it. (4) From our intuitions about loyalty and disgust are shaped by kin selection and reciprocity to those intuitions are no evidence about what is right: fine, though I suspect I am quietly supplying a premise about what makes a belief justified. (5) From our risk model returns false negatives for the disadvantaged group at twice the rate it does for the advantaged group to the model is unfair and should be retrained: fine — this is literally what I was hired to say — and yet I cannot produce the normative premise, nor any premise that is not a restatement of the conclusion in a louder voice. (6) From Smith, playing by the rules, is in checkmate to Smith ought to move his king: fine, though I suspect this ought is a different word wearing the same clothes. (7) From Jones uttered the words I promise to pay you five dollars to Jones ought to pay five dollars: fine, but here I think I am genuinely smuggling something, namely that promises bind. (8) From the masses in this painting are balanced and its palette disciplined to this is a good painting: not fine until someone says what a painting is for, and fine afterwards — and yet in case (2) I feel no need to grant any parallel standard, which strikes me as inconsistent. (9) From water is H2O and there is water in this glass to there is H2O in this glass: trivially fine; and if wrongness just were identical to the production of suffering, case (2) would be the same trivial substitution — but then I cannot say what the gap in case (3) consists in beyond the absence of such an identity, and I do not know whether that absence is a discovery or a decision. (10) From the corridor lights are on to either the corridor lights are on or it is wrong to park in the ambulance bay: formally the conclusion is normative and follows from a non-normative premise, so this seems to demolish any prohibition stated purely in terms of logical form, and I want to be told why it is no derivation at all without my having to define derivation in a way that is obviously rigged in my favour. (11) From the evidence makes p considerably likelier than not-p to you ought to believe p: fine, and here I feel no gap at all — so why is the gap vivid in the moral case and invisible in the epistemic one? If the answer is that epistemic oughts are relative to the goal of truth, I notice the goal is doing all the work, in which case morality needs a goal too, and the appeal-to-nature objection comes back through the window.

My tentative principle, offered for you to attack: normative content is conserved across valid inference, so no conclusion can carry normative content that was not already in the premises, and therefore every one of the inferences I called fine is enthymematic — I simply declined to write down a premise because it seemed too obvious to state. Tell me whether that is coherent, and what notion of content it requires, since it plainly cannot be about which words occur, on pain of cases (9) and (10). Then state it clause by clause, saying which of my cases motivates each clause, and give me its verdicts on four I have not listed: from the statute requires X to you ought to do X; from smoking raises your risk of dying at sixty to you ought not to smoke; from she is my sister to I owe her more than I owe a stranger; from the distribution of income is more unequal than it was twenty years ago to the arrangement is unjust. Finally, be explicit about the cost: which of my eleven verdicts does your best principle force me to abandon? I expect it will be (5), and I expect I will want to resist, so tell me what I would have to believe in order to resist consistently. One last thing, since it bears on the embarrassment I am trying to undo: I have since learned that the charge my physician made names something different from the point everyone attributes to Hume in the third book of the Treatise, where he notices that authors proceed for a while with is and is not and then slide, without comment, into ought and ought not. The other charge concerns defining good in natural terms and the question that supposedly always stays open. Are those two objections independent, does either entail the other, and which one — if either — actually applies to the paragraph I wrote?
