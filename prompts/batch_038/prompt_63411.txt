I am drafting a term paper for a political philosophy seminar on the ethics of famine, and I would like to test the framing before I commit to it. My thesis is that famines fall on one side or the other of a sharp line separating natural disasters from political crimes, and that Sen's entitlement approach supplies the criterion for drawing it. As I read *Poverty and Famines*, Sen's central result is that famine mortality essentially never reflects an aggregate shortfall in food — the supply is there — but the collapse of the legal entitlements through which particular groups claim a share of it; so within his own framework a famine is always a case of some group being cut off from food that remained available to others. That seems to sort my three cases without further argument. Bengal in 1943 and the Great Leap Forward sit on the crime side, because the grain existed and identifiable decisions severed people's access to it, while the Irish famine of the 1840s sits on the disaster side, because it was precipitated by a genuine failure of the very crop its victims lived on and no one needed to have decided anything.

Two questions. First, does the entitlement framework actually license that partition, or am I reading considerably more into it than Sen offers — and if the partition fails, is there any part of it worth keeping? Second, if it does hold, what does the criminal charge require: merely that the deprivation have been foreseeable to those positioned to prevent it, or an agent who brought it about deliberately?
