DRAFT — "Two Kinds of Because"

After a bad fire the chief asks why the roof went. You can give two sorts of answer and they sound alike but they aren't.

Sort one: steel loses strength as it gets hot, the fire burned hot enough for long enough in the space holding the deck up, so the beams sagged and the roof came down. That answer would work on any building in any city. Almost nothing in it is about this roof.

Sort two: because the sprinkler main was shut off Tuesday for a repair and the tag that's supposed to hang on the valve never got hung, so the crew coming on at six didn't know. That answer is about this building on this night and it won't help you with any other one.

Here's a test I use to tell them apart. Take the because-sentence and swap the subject for a different one. "The empire fell because it could no longer keep paying its soldiers" — swap in any empire you like and it still reads fine, which means it was never really about that empire. It's a general claim wearing a specific costume. Now try "the empire fell because the rider carrying the recall order took the low road, and the low road was under water." Swap that and it dies. It only ever meant one thing.

My claim, and this is the part I'm least sure of, is that the second sort is the real explanation and the first sort is a fake. A general historical law is a vibe with a sample size of one. Nobody has ever watched two empires fall. What looks like a law is a pattern we noticed after the fact and then started saying in the future tense, and the reason people argue about it forever is that there was never anything there to check.

Take the summer of 1914. The sort-one answer is that alliances and arms races and nationalism made a big war likely. Maybe. But the war that actually happened came out of mobilisation plans built around railway timetables that couldn't be stopped halfway once they'd started. That isn't a law. It's a description of one machine in one year.

Last piece I want to put in. The interesting question usually isn't what caused the thing, it's why the thing that could have gone either way went that way. I call those fork explanations — you look for the moment where both branches were still open. Forks can't be tested, since you can't rerun 1914 with a different timetable. I dont know whether that's a defect or just what the subject is.

So — is the swap test worth keeping? And is "the first sort is fake" too strong, and if it is, what's the strong version I should say instead?
