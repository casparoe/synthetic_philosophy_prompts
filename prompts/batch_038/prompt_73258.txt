I spent four decades in an experimental physics laboratory, mostly making low-temperature measurements of no particular importance, and have come to philosophy only since retiring. A nearby department has asked me to lead one session of its continuing-education course in the philosophy of physics: eighteen adults, largely retired scientists and engineers, no formal philosophy training, one seminar room with movable tables, two hours including a break. My budget is paper, envelopes, scissors, and a whiteboard. I should like a complete and runnable activity on backwards time travel and the grandfather paradox — not a lecture with a puzzle attached, but something the participants physically do, in which the difficulty arrives before anyone has named it.

Please design to the following specification, and tell me where you have had to depart from it.

I want two phases with deliberately different degrees of control on my part.

Phase one: a closed loop that participants try, sincerely and with no rule forbidding the attempt, to break. They are given something — a message, an instruction, a sealed object, a number — that arrives as though from later in the session, and they are left entirely free to refuse it, alter it, destroy it, or simply walk away from it. Some of these attempts must fail, and the failure must be produced by the structure of the game rather than by improvisation on my part. What I want them to feel is that they did everything they could and the circuit closed anyway, and that the same person at the same moment both was able to act otherwise and was not, depending on how the question is put.

Phase two: a bootstrap. Some item of content — a melody, a drawing, a paragraph, a number sequence — circulates through a closed circuit within the room and is discovered at the end to have no origin: no participant composed it, and neither did I. Unlike phase one, I want this effect to survive the withdrawal of my control, so please specify exactly how much of the mechanism can be handed over to the group before the effect collapses, and what must remain in my hands.

Then give me: a minute-by-minute timeline; a materials list; the exact wording of whatever I must say aloud beforehand, which should be as little as possible, since the phrase grandfather paradox is not to be heard in the room until phase one has finished; and the debrief questions in the order I should ask them, mapping particular moments of the game onto the difference between changing the past and merely affecting it, the ambiguity in the word can, the bilking move of taking delivery of a message from the future and then declining to send it, Novikov-style self-consistency as a restriction on what the history of the world may look like, and Hawking's chronology protection conjecture.

Tell me also where a first run will come apart: the participant who spots the rigging within thirty seconds, and in my experience it is always an engineer; the person who declines to play; the group that finishes early; the odd number. I want something ready for each. And I want to know what to say to the participant who announces that all this is a card trick. I suspect they will be right, so please concede the point explicitly rather than fudge it, and then tell me what the concession costs and what it leaves standing.

One epistemic request, made because I am a scientist and have been misled before by confident summaries. Separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. If the diagnosis I have sketched — that the paradox rests on an equivocation in can — is itself a contested position rather than the standard resolution, say so, and say who resists it and on what grounds. Let nothing that is one philosopher's view arrive in my classroom wearing the costume of a consensus, and flag every point at which the physics has been stretched to make the pedagogy work.
