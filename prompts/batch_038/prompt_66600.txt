Three weeks from now I'm giving a talk at a decarbonisation-pathways workshop. The room is mostly energy systems modellers (capcity expansion and integrated assessment people), two economic historians of technology, and one guy seconded from a transmission system operator. Basically no philosophers, and nobody who thinks of this as a values debate.

My claim is going to annoy them. I'm arguing that the German exit — specifically the 2011 reversal of the 2010 lifetime extension — was net-positive for emissions, not despite killing the nuclear option but because it killed it. The mechanism I'm pushing is a commitment device: a polity only has so much administrative and political capital, and keeping a low-carbon option alive lets every government defer the build-out, the grid works and the permitting reform for another decade. France is my contrast case, not my villain: it kept the option, ran the fleet, and still hasn't produced the wind/solar fleet or the consenting machine to replace it. I want to be clear I'm not arguing nuclear is bad technology, I'm arguing option value and deployment rate trade off against each other inside a real government.

Prep me for the question period. I want the objections this specific room would actually make, in their register — with their modelling vocabulary, their counterfactuals, their favourite dataset — not the generic pro/anti-nuclear talking points you can find in any newspaper comments section. Rank them by how much damage they'd do. For each one give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds standing up, plus a blunt flag on where that reply is thin (I already suspect the counterfactual is where I bleed). Then tell me: what I should concede in my first five minutes so it can't be used as a gotcha later, what the single load-bearing claim is that I have to hold onto no matter how the session goes, and which question I should be praying nobody in that room thinks to ask.
