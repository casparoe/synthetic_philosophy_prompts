I help programme a small monthly film night in a community hall — forty-odd people, mostly retired, a handful of students from the technical college — and I have been asked to introduce Denis Villeneuve's Arrival next month. I have now seen it perhaps a dozen times, and the more I watch it the more convinced I am that it is a film about the thermodynamic arrow of time rather than about aliens or about language, which is not how it is usually described. I should say at once that I have no academic training in any of this: I read a couple of physics books written for general readers after I retired, got hooked, and have been worrying away at the same handful of questions for about four years. Please treat what follows as a set of hunches to be corrected rather than a position to be flattered.

My hunch runs like this. As I understand it, the striking thing about the arrow of time is that the underlying laws do not seem to have one; nothing at the microscopic level prefers a direction, so the direction has to come from somewhere else — from the fact that the universe began in an extraordinarily special, very low-entropy state, a "past hypothesis," I believe the phrase is. A great deal is supposed to follow from that, including why we have records of the past and none of the future: a memory, a photograph, a tree ring or a fossil is a present state standing in a very particular correlation with an earlier state, and correlations of that kind are only manufactured by processes that increase entropy. Reichenbach had a way of putting the point in terms of branch systems, and there is a line of criticism — Huw Price's, I think — to the effect that we cheat, because we explain the asymmetry of knowledge and causation by appeal to entropy running one way while simply assuming, without explanation, that the past is the low-entropy end. If I have garbled any of that, say so plainly. What puzzles me is that this picture makes knowing the past look like a physical achievement rather than a free gift — and Arrival appears to be about a character who has performed that achievement in the wrong direction. Louise's visions of her daughter are staged exactly as memories are staged: they feel like records, they carry the authority of records, and they are what enable her to act. Yet nothing in the film could have manufactured the correlation, because the events have not happened. That seems to me the real difficulty with the film, and not the one people usually raise.

So I would like a reading of Arrival — and, wherever it usefully differs, of Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life," which I have also read, and which builds its conceit out of Fermat's principle of least time rather than out of linguistics — that takes the arrow-of-time material seriously and does not let the film off easily. Concretely: are Louise's premonitions best understood as records running backwards, and if so what would that demand of the world (a second special boundary condition at the far end of time, a local entropy reversal, a brain that is an absurdly improbable fluctuation), or are they better understood as not records at all, in which case in what sense does she know anything rather than guess correctly every time? Does the film understand the difference between determinism and the direction of time, or does it quietly trade on the fact that audiences run the two together? A friend of mine who is doing a physics degree insists the picture has nothing whatsoever to do with entropy and is simply fatalism in a spacesuit, and I want to know whether he is right, because if he is, my whole introduction collapses. Does abandoning the variational principle in favour of the language-rewires-the-brain idea cost the story its one genuinely physical hook? I would also like you to face the ending rather than step around it: Louise chooses to have Hannah knowing Hannah dies at twelve, and Ian leaves her over it — if the future is already there in the way the film seems to think, is that a choice at all, and does the film mean her to come out admirable or monstrous? Finally, two smaller things on which I suspect I am fooling myself: whether the fact that the opening montage turns out to be a flash-forward is doing philosophical work or only a narrative trick, and whether the daughter's name, which reads the same backwards and forwards, is meant to signal anything or whether I am the sort of person who finds patterns in wallpaper.

Please close by saying what the film contributes to this debate that argument alone does not, and also where my reading strains the film and an honest critic would have to concede it. I do not want references or a reading list — I know you cannot look anything up and would have no way of checking them — and please do not quote the film or the story line by line; describe the scenes instead, and pitch the prose for an intelligent reader who has seen it once and has never read any philosophy.
