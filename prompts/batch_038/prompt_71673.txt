Last night I argued for four hours with a friend of mine — a Quaternary palaeoclimatologist who reads ice cores for a living, and whom I would describe as neither careless nor ungenerous — about the criterion for dating the Anthropocene that I intend to defend in public next month. He did not convince me, but he located several places where my formulation is genuinely exposed, and I would far rather walk into the room having rehearsed those than discover them in the fortieth minute.

The occasion. I am giving the opening plenary, forty minutes plus discussion, at a workshop jointly hosted by a Quaternary research institute and a history of science department. There will be about forty people: five stratigraphers and palaeoclimatologists, two of whom were close enough to the process that produced the Crawford Lake proposal to have settled views about its rejection in 2024 by the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy and then by the International Commission on Stratigraphy; six or seven environmental historians, most of whom hold that the epoch begins with the Columbian Exchange and who lean on the small dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 that Lewis and Maslin made famous; two philosophers of science; one Earth-system modeller who works on early-warning indicators for critical transitions and who will care about operationalisation considerably more than anyone else present; a scattering of graduate students; and one science journalist who will report whichever sentence is easiest to quote.

The thesis, as I will state it. I am proposing what I call a sustained-departure criterion:

1. A boundary belongs at the first *sustained* departure of the Earth system from the Holocene envelope, not at the first *legible* one. By legible I mean sharp enough, isochronous enough, and preserved in an archive we can actually core — a varved lake, an ice sheet, a reef. My claim is that legibility is an artefact of our sampling technology, and that treating it as constitutive of the boundary confuses how the world can be seen with how the world is.

2. Operationally: fix a panel of state variables (atmospheric CO2 and CH4, global mean surface temperature, ice volume, land cover, the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles, and a biosphere-integrity measure such as species ranges and assemblage composition); reconstruct the Holocene distribution of each; and define the departure date for a variable as the earliest year after which, under every counterfactual in which human forcing is frozen at its level in that year, the variable fails to re-enter its Holocene distribution within ten thousand years. The Anthropocene begins at the earliest sustained departure across the panel.

3. The consequence I endorse rather than apologise for: the variables do not depart together. The carbon variables depart late — plausibly the mid-twentieth century, at the earliest the nineteenth. The biosphere variable departs with the transposition of two hemispheric biotas after 1492, which is not recoverable on any timescale that matters, even though its atmospheric signature is that weak and contested 1610 dip, which runs *toward* the Holocene mean rather than away from it. So my criterion yields a start in the late fifteenth to early seventeenth century on the strength of the variable whose geological signal is faintest. I think that is the point, not an embarrassment: a golden-spike methodology, which demands one isochronous marker in one archive, systematically under-detects exactly the changes that matter most and arrive most slowly.

I also intend to say — and this is the part that will irritate the room — that the 2024 rejection did not settle the question but removed the body that had been settling it, so what remains is a question about who is entitled to date an epoch; and that the answer is not "only those who can core it."

My friend's best shot, which I want pressure-tested rather than answered for me: the panel is mine, the ten-thousand-year threshold is mine, the weighting across variables is mine, and the counterfactual ensemble comes from models whose structural uncertainty dwarfs the difference between 1610 and 1950 — so the criterion is not a criterion but a machine for returning whatever date I want. My reply is that freezing forcing at the candidate date makes the test hard to satisfy and so constrains the outcome, but I am not confident that reply survives contact with a modeller.
