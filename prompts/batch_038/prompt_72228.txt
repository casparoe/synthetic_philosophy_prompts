I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on consciousness and personal identity, and our current unit concerns the prospect of whole-brain emulation. A classmate of mine spent the summer interning at a startup that sells what it calls substrate-independent backup services — they will scan you, store the result, and promise to realize it later on hardware they have not yet built — and our discussions of that business model have made me acutely aware that I do not actually understand where the intuitive line between safe continuation and death is supposed to fall. Over the weekend I tried to reconstruct the gradual-replacement argument from first principles, in the hope of getting a clean statement of why replacing a brain neuron by neuron with functionally equivalent silicon is supposed to be harmless. Then I extended it, and the extension seems to prove a great deal more than I can swallow. My paper on this is due in two weeks, and I would much rather have a precise diagnosis of the argument than a survey of positions.

Here is the argument as I have written it out.

(1) Two systems with the same fine-grained functional organization — the same pattern of causal dependencies among sensory inputs, internal states, and behavioral outputs — are in the same conscious states. Call this organizational invariance; it is the premise I most want to keep.

(2) Suppose one biological neuron in a living, continuously functioning brain is removed and replaced by a silicon module that reproduces exactly that neuron's contribution to the overall causal organization. The rest of the organization is unchanged, so by (1) the system's conscious states are unchanged.

(3) No single substitution of this kind can be the difference between consciousness and unconsciousness. If it could, there would have to be some fact determining which neuron, which region, or which number of neurons mattered, and no such fact is available, since the neuron chosen in (2) was chosen arbitrarily. So each individual substitution is experientially neutral.

(4) Therefore, replacing all of a person's neurons one at a time, in any order, over any length of time, leaves consciousness intact at every stage: the endpoint is a wholly artificial system with the same conscious life the person began with.

(5) Note what did the work in (2) through (4): only the preservation of functional organization from stage to stage. Nothing appealed to the persistence of the original matter, and nothing appealed to the speed of the process, since (4) explicitly left the timescale free.

(6) So any sequence of states in which each state is functionally isomorphic to its predecessor preserves consciousness, however the later states are produced — by substitution inside a running brain, by scanning the state and rebuilding it somewhere else, by transmitting it to a machine on another continent, or by writing it to storage and realizing it a thousand years hence.

(7) Teletransportation is then just the limiting case of (4): compress the substitution sequence, relocate the realizer, and insert an interval. Since (4) left timescale and order free, and (5) says nothing else is relevant, there is no principled point at which such a process stops preserving a conscious life.

(8) Hence a person's conscious life can be ended only by destroying the functional organization outright — never by copying it, transferring it, or pausing it.

(9) But (8) has consequences I cannot accept. If two backups of me are realized simultaneously in two cities, (8) says both continue my conscious life, so survival on this account is not one-to-one and (8) cannot be a claim about my identity at all. And (8) implies that I survive a millennium in cold storage, and that someone destroyed and rebuilt a year later never ceased to exist, which makes survival so cheap that it stops looking like survival.

(10) So something in (1) through (6) is false. My seminar partner thinks (3) is a sorites premise and that the argument dies there. I suspect instead that (6) or (7) is doing something illegitimate with the idea of a limit, but I cannot say what.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look acceptable when I wrote it down? Please address these points in particular. State explicitly the principle you would need in order to iterate (2) into (4), and say whether that principle is independently plausible or merely true in the special case of a brain that keeps running throughout. Is (5) actually licensed by (2) through (4), or is it a fresh premise that smuggles in a claim about what is necessary on the strength of a claim about what is sufficient? Does (1) state a synchronic thesis about which states are conscious that is being asked to do diachronic work about persistence across gaps and across realizers? And if the step you reject is (5), (6), or (7), what survives of the gradual-replacement argument: does it still establish that substrate is irrelevant to consciousness, or does it reduce to a claim about continuously running systems only — and in that case, what does the functionalist owe by way of explaining what the extra ingredient in the continuous case is supposed to be? Finally, price the alternatives for me: for each of (1), (3), and (5), say what giving it up would cost, since I need to know which concession is cheapest before I commit to one in the paper.

Please write your answer in American English, and do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to works I should consult; I am interested only in your analysis of the argument as I have stated it, together with any distinctions you need to introduce in order to diagnose it.
