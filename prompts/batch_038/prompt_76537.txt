Our institution maintains a standing panel, the Evidential Standards Panel, whose only job is to decide whether a proposed justification may be admitted when the evidence offered for a procedure's accuracy is drawn from that procedure's own outputs. We do not rule on what is true; we rule on what counts as an admissible showing. Our charter states no general principle. What it contains instead is two decades of case notes, and I have been handed the six most recent as the working record for my first term as chair.

1. Weighbridge. A freight yard sought to certify a truck scale by re-weighing a set of reference loads and observing that the readings matched the loads' recorded weights. The recorded weights had been entered at commissioning from that same scale's readings. A customer had disputed a ticket the previous quarter. We refused to admit the report. Note: the comparison presupposed what it was offered to establish.

2. Cold-room thermometer. A laboratory sought to certify a logging thermometer against a reference standard traceable to a national metrology institute. A maintenance technician had earlier remarked that the unit might be drifting. We admitted the certification. Note: the standard was fixed without reference to the instrument it was used to check.

3. Duplicate assays. A quality-control lab proposed to certify a reagent's potency by running the same assay in duplicate on the same instrument and setting its acceptance threshold from the spread of those duplicates. A technician had flagged possible drift in that instrument. We refused. Note: repetition is not verification.

4. Field identification. A conservation unit asked whether a surveyor's unaided species identifications could be treated as established facts for planning purposes, where no independent verification was obtainable and no one had questioned her record. We admitted the identifications as facts, while expressly declining to treat that admission as certifying her reliability as an identifier.

5. Fuel gauges. After two units were replaced under warranty, a fleet manager asked whether the new gauges could be trusted. An operator submitted a reliability report constructed by treating each individual reading over one season as a small confirmation that the gauge was accurate, then pooling the confirmations. No independent check was available. We refused. Note: many weak instances of the same move do not compose into a strong one.

6. Image-reading assist. After a missed finding prompted an internal review, a clinic proposed to certify a computer-aided detection system by having a second reader re-read a sampled subset of images. The subset was drawn using the system's own confidence ranking, and the second reader had completed a training course whose exemplars were annotated by that system. We refused. Note: the auditor had absorbed the audited system's habits.

The matter now before us is this. A university-wide office uses an automated essay-scoring system, which I will call G, to produce grades for a large first-year writing requirement. A parents' association has filed a complaint alleging that G systematically under-scores essays by students writing in English as an additional language. The office proposes to answer the complaint by certifying G with an audit module, A: a different vendor, a different architecture, built without access to G's code, weights, or training data. A will re-score a stratified random sample of two thousand essays, and agreement between A and G will be reported as G's accuracy. The office reports overall agreement of 94 percent; it has not reported agreement within the complainant subgroup.

Two features emerged in discovery. First, A's scoring rubric was calibrated on a legacy corpus of essays for which the only available scores were G's, and the human raters who adjudicated A's calibration set were trained on exemplars that G had scored. That legacy corpus predates the current student population, comes from other institutions, and contains no essays by the complainant subgroup. Whether rubric calibration on G's outputs would carry over any subgroup-specific distortion in G, as against merely importing G's general stylistic preferences, is contested and, so far as we can tell, unsettled by the evidence before us. Second, the office intends the resulting certificate to establish two things at once: that G meets the accuracy threshold in our charter, and that this year's individual grades are correct.

I would like your analysis of the following.

(a) Reconstruct the candidate rules our record could be expressing. Give at least three, and state each one so that it could be applied mechanically to a new fact pattern rather than as a slogan. Candidate formulations might key on the provenance of the checking standard, on whether live doubt exists, on whether the errors of the checking procedure are correlated with those of the procedure checked, or on whether a failure of certification also undermines the first-order outputs; you may propose others, and you should say which formulations the record already rules out and which precedent rules them out.

(b) For each surviving rule, state the verdict it yields on the essay-scoring matter, and identify the feature of the new case that the verdict turns on. Where the record leaves a rule's parameters open, say so explicitly and give the verdict under each plausible setting. In particular, address how far up the chain a provenance-based rule must trace contamination before it bites, and whether our record fixes that distance.

(c) Give me your best prediction of what this panel will actually decide, and separately your recommendation as to what it should decide if it wants to be consistent with its own case notes. Identify the closest analog among the six precedents and the most important respects in which the analogy fails. If the honest answer is that our record underdetermines the matter, say what principle the panel would be adopting by ruling either way, including by issuing a split verdict that admits the individual grades while refusing the certificate.

(d) Describe one further case, concrete enough to go on next quarter's agenda, that would most sharply discriminate among your leading candidate rules. Say which rules it separates and what each predicts about it.

Please do not cite or recommend sources; we want the reasoning, not a bibliography. Write your response in American English.
