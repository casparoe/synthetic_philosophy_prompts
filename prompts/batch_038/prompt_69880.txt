So quick context — I'm not a philosopher. I run behavioral testing in a mouse neuro lab, and I've been reading philosophy of mind on the side for a couple years, mostly at night while protocols are running. Last month my sister's boyfriend made some remark about free will at dinner and it turned into a two hour argument, and in the middle of it I realized I keep running into a particular shape of argument that I can't place, and I want to know whether it has a name.

Here's the shape. Someone says: the pain caused the wince. Then someone else says — hold on, the physical story is already complete, because once you've specified which neurons fired, what the muscles did, and the laws, the wince follows whether or not you ever mentioned the pain, so the pain isn't doing any work, it's just along for the ride. And the first person answers: that's absurd, the pain isn't a second thing competing with the firing for the same job, it's the same thing described differently, so there's no leftover work for it to have failed to do. And at that point the argument just... evaporates. Not because anybody got refuted. It evaporates because it only ever looked like an argument under a tacit assumption nobody announced, namely that "the pain" and "the firing" are two separate candidates for one causal job. Switch to counting them as one thing under two descriptions and the question dissolves rather than gets answered. Nobody wins, the dispute was hostage to a slicing decision the whole time.

And the reason I think this is a general type rather than a quirk of the mind case is that the same shape keeps popping up elsewhere. Was it the baseball or the trillion little molecular impacts that broke the window? Was it the recession that closed my uncle's shop, or the several hundred individual decisions by creditors and landlords that added up to it being closed? Was it my wanting the light on that moved my hand, or the neurophysiology that was already sufficient for the hand moving? In every one of these, one side points out that the finer-grained level closes up all the gaps, and the other side points out that the coarse-grained item isn't a rival to it, and then the argument loses its grip — or seems to.

So: does this class have a name? I mean the meta-level thing, not the pain case itself — I'm aware there's a whole literature about mental properties getting "excluded" and about whether it's suspicious for everything to be caused twice over, and I'm not asking you to summarize that. What I want is whether there's an established concept for noticing that an argument's force depends entirely on an unstated choice about how finely you slice up events and properties, and how the diagnosis differs from just declaring the dispute verbal, or from calling it an equivocation on the word "cause" — because in my examples nobody is switching senses of "cause," they're switching how many things there are, which feels like a different failure. And if nothing established covers it, please coin a term and give me the definition plus the edges. Specifically I need to know what an argument of this shape would look like that does *not* dissolve when you re-slice, because I suspect some of them survive and some don't and I currently can't tell which is which, and without that boundary the whole move is just a way of dodging arguments you don't like. Bonus if you can say something about who in the literature has actually worried about the granularity question rather than about the mind case — I keep feeling like I'm reinventing something a real person already named and I'd rather not.
