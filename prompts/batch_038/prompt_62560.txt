I sit on the membership and standards committee of an association of about forty schools of public policy — mostly European, a couple in North America, one each in Istanbul and Tel Aviv — and I need help drafting the decision memo we'll put in front of the general assembly in early June, wich is not much runway. Our charter has a clause written in 1998 and never touched since: membership is open to schools that are institutionally autonomous and situated in a polity of good democratic standing. Nobody ever defined either phrase and for twenty-five years nobody had to. Now we do. The live case is Hungary. Over roughly a decade and a half the governing party replaced the old constitution with a new one adopted on a party-line two-thirds vote, expanded the constitutional court and filled it, rewrote the election law and redrew the districts, dropped the mandatory retirement age for judges and prosecutors by eight years so a couple hundred of them had to go at once, and saw most of the privately owned regional press end up inside a single pro-government media foundation. Opposition parties still exist and still run and still hold a few big cities; election observers keep saying some version of the same thing, that the voting itself is well run and competitive but that there is no level playing field; our member school's faculty are still publishing and still teaching and their faculty senate voted last month to stay in the association, warning that expulsion would land on their students and not on the government. There's also a second file behind it — a Warsaw school whose government changed hands recently and whose incoming government has been accused of using the same capture tactics in reverse to undo the last eight years, which is awkward for us, because it means the same member could be compliant or not depending on which month you look. And one of our American members has formally asked that whatever standard we produce be applied to their state too, where the legislature has cut early voting days, tightened purge and ID rules, and drawn congressional maps that the state supreme court declined to disturb; they've said, not unreasonably, that if our answer is "that's different," we owe them an account of why in words that don't just restate our own preferences.

The committee is split three ways and I need the memo to engage the split rather than paper over it. One faction thinks the whole exercise is a category error — that "democratic backsliding" is a term people invented to describe governments they dislike, that the thresholds are unfalsifiable and infinitely stretchable, and that an unelected body of academics with no mandate and no accountability has no business adjudicating the internal politics of sovereign states; on their view the right move is to strike the clause and admit we should never have had it. Another faction, which includes the dean of a school in a neigboring country that has been through much the same thing, says the opposite: that refusing to name what's happening is itself a political act, and one that favors whoever is doing it, and that "no level playing field" is a description rather than a slur. The third faction mostly wants to know what they're voting on and whether there's a procedure that won't make us re-litigate this every single year.

So: write it as though you're a political theorist who has done serious work on what democracy amounts to and on how institutions gatekeep, and who has also sat on enough accreditation panels to know how these documents actually get wielded afterwards. Think it through properly before you write — argue both sides for yourself, change your mind once if that's what happens — and then give me the finished memo. It needs a clear recommendation (suspend, expel, retain with conditions, strike the clause, defer, or some combination you actually believe in), the reasoning that gets us there, and a genuinely honest treatment of the strongest thing the losing side has going for it rather than a strawman we can knock over in one line. It also needs a rule the committee could apply to the next case, including the American one, that doesn't collapse into "we'll know it when we see it" or into a checklist a government could game by pacing itself. And tell us in a sentence or two what evidence would make you change the recommendation. Length: something readable aloud in about ten minutes, so call it 900 to 1300 words. Two hard constraints. First, don't cite anything — no index scores, no reports, no case names, no scholars' names, no country report from anywhere; I have the dossier and what I'm missing is judgment, and gesturing at sources is a way of hiding. Second, no formatting at all: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, no numbered criteria laid out like a slide — continuous prose only, the way you'd write a letter, because that's how this committee has always done things. And please don't split the difference just to be safe; if your honest view is that we should strike the clause and get out of the business entirely, say that and defend it.
