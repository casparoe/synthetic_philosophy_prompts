My dad died in the spring and left me and my two sisters the duplex he'd lived in for forty years, and honestly working through that mess is what got me paying attention, because about six weeks after the funeral I got seated on the board of the county housing trust, which runs our first-time-buyer grant pilot.

Quick context, because the details are doing the work here. The pilot is funded by a small surcharge on residential property transfers above a threshold. Eligibility used to be a plain snapshot: current income, current assets, no home owned in the last three years. Last year staff added a second layer they're calling the lifetime transfer ledger. Applicants self-report every transfer of value they've ever gotten from family — cash gifts, tuition paid, a down payment, a car, a loan that got forgiven, a lease somebody cosigned — and the reported total is subtracted from the grant they'd otherwise recieve, dollar for dollar. The pitch from staff was that two people with identical bank accounts today can have gotten to that point in completely different ways, and the pilot is supposed to care about that difference.

Now we have three appeals on next month's agenda and they're splitting the board badly:

1. A woman who inherited $180k from her grandmother about five years ago. She spent roughly $150k of it on her mother's assisted living and the rest on her brother's legal fees. Today she rents, has about $4k saved, and would qualify for the maximum grant if the ledger didn't exist. Her appeal says the money is gone and was never really hers in any meaningful sense.

2. A man whose ledger comes up empty — no cash gifts, nothing ever titled in his name. But his parents paid his in-state tuition directly to the bursar, he lived at home rent-free for three years after graduating, and he's now got $40k saved for a down payment from what that freed up. Staff only caught any of this because he volunteered the history in his personal statement, and he's arguing he should be scored on it like everyone else.

3. A man whose parents moved their house into a trust naming him remainder beneficiary, subject to his mother's life estate. The ledger counts the full appraised value. He can't sell it, can't borrow against it, doesn't live in it, and pays the property tax and the roof repairs out of pocket.

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the board before we meet. I need an actual recommendation on the policy — keep the ledger as written, amend it, or scrap it — plus how you'd rule on each of the three appeals, and a test staff can apply to the fourth case without phoning us. Give me a timeline too: what we adopt next month, what has to be phased in, and what the three appellants get told and when.

And I want the strongest honest case against my own instinct, which is that the ledger is basically right and these three are just edge cases we can patch. Argue the other side well enough that a board member who wants to kill the whole thing can't be waved off as squeamish about paperwork. There's also something I can't quite put my finger on about whether a program whose entire point is to help people is allowed to run a lifetime audit of what your family gave you — if you think that worry is real, or confused, or real but outweighed, say which and why.
