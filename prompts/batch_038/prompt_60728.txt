I teach in a history and philosophy of science department, though before crossing over I spent the better part of a decade as an applied statistician doing structural-break estimation and segmentation of economic time series, and I have never entirely left that vocabulary behind. This term I am running a graduate seminar on periodization — whether such labels as the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution pick out real discontinuities in the historical record or are artifacts of retrospective description — and I have become uncomfortably aware that I cannot think about the dispute except as a segmentation problem: a series observed through time, a small number of candidate breakpoints, penalized model selection over the number and locations of those breaks, a prior on break counts, the difference between testing a break at a date fixed in advance by tradition and estimating its location from the data, common-breakpoint models that ask whether many heterogeneous series break jointly rather than severally, and the dependence of every such result on aggregation windows, smoothing bandwidths, and the penalty term. I should like you to help me determine whether that is a prejudice or an insight. Restate the positions below in that language, as faithfully as you can, using whatever technical apparatus of changepoint analysis, structural-break econometrics, and discretization of continuous processes you think genuinely applies.

The positions I want translated are three. First, the constructionist case in roughly the form Le Goff gave it in his final book: that much of what is credited to the Renaissance as a rupture has medieval antecedents and much of what is deprecated in the Middle Ages persisted afterward, so that the apparent break shifts, weakens, or dissolves once one lengthens the series backward and measures variables other than the ones the nineteenth-century coiners of the term cared about — a long Middle Ages punctuated by several recoveries rather than one decisive changepoint. Second, the continuity thesis as deployed against the Scientific Revolution: that the fourteenth-century antecedents of the new mechanics are substantive rather than merely anticipatory, and that the impression of a sharp break is manufactured by the choice of a sampling window together with a handful of dramatic indicators. Third, Blumenberg's rejoinder to the secularization thesis: that the modern age is not a medieval model carrying new labels, but a different regime altogether — so that his quarrel with Löwith is not a quarrel about how to describe one and the same process, but about whether the process changed its character.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. For each of the three, say what translates cleanly, what you had to bend or narrow to make it fit, and what has no expression whatever in the segmentation vocabulary. I hold two suspicions and I would rather you adjudicate between them than average them. The first: that the constructionist and continuity theses, once translated, amount to familiar points about aggregation, window length, multiple testing, and the choice of penalty — true, but unremarkable, and generating metaphysical heat in my seminar room only because nobody states them that way. The second: that something genuinely inexpressible survives — most obviously, that a period term is not merely a claim about a series but is partly constituted by the interests of whoever is segmenting it, and by the self-understandings of the agents inside the series, who encounter the segmentation, believe it, and adjust, so that the process is not exogenous to the classifier in the way any changepoint model requires. Tell me which of the two you judge closer to right; and where you think the translation fails, say whether it fails because my vocabulary lacks expressive power or because the original claims were less determinate than they sound. Two ground rules. Do not smuggle the assumptions of the target framework in and present them as translation results — if a claim survives only by being converted into a statement about our descriptions rather than about the past, mark it as a substitution, not a mapping. And do not pad the response with historiography: I know the literature. I want the translation and its failures.
