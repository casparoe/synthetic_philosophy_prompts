I am preparing a short statement of my own position for a workshop on democratic backsliding, at which each participant is to set out their commitments explicitly so the others can attack them. Before I circulate mine, I would like an honest audit of whether the set hangs together. I am not after a survey of the debate, a map of the field, or reassurance that the concept is contested; I want to know where the structure fails.

Here is what I hold.

1. Democracy is a procedural category. A regime is a democracy if and only if elections are contested and inclusive enough that losers hand over power. Independent courts, a pluralistic press, and protections for minorities are goods I want, but they are not constitutive of democracy and I refuse to let them into the definition. The benchmark against which backsliding is measured is the electoral minimum, not the richer ideal of liberal democracy.

2. My test for whether backsliding is occurring is a counterfactual, and I mean it to be binary: if enough voters turned against the incumbent, would the incumbent lose, and would it leave office on losing? If the answer to both is yes, there is no backsliding, whatever else is going wrong; if the answer to either is no, there is. I am sceptical of intermediate regime types and of scales with no natural endpoints.

3. Backsliding is characteristically legal. The mechanism I care about is the exploitation of ordinary constitutional channels — restructuring a court, rewriting an electoral law, changing who may own a newspaper — by actors working within their formal powers. Legality is therefore no evidence whatever against a finding of backsliding, and any account that needs a violation of law to get going has missed the phenomenon.

4. The right response to backsliding is inside the system: litigation, elections, legislative checks, coalition-building. I oppose counter-norm-breaking by opponents — retaliatory court-packing, refusing to certify a result one regards as illegitimate, disregarding an order from a court one believes captured — on the ground that such measures accelerate the process they are meant to arrest and forfeit the standing from which to complain about it.

5. My case verdicts. Hungary since 2010 is a clear case of backsliding: the restructuring of the constitutional court, the consolidation of most private media under pro-government ownership, and the redrawing of single-member constituencies are together exactly the pattern described in (3). I also hold that Hungarian elections have not been fraudulent, and that if an opposition coalition assembled enough votes the result would be certified and the government would go. Turkey after the 2017 constitutional referendum fails (2) outright and is no longer a democracy on my view. Hungary is something different, and I want a word for it: I call it a hollow democracy, meaning a regime whose elections remain formally competitive and whose results are honoured, but conducted in a political environment so thoroughly reconstructed that the opposition can no longer realistically assemble the votes to win. This is not competitive authoritarianism, where the incumbent simply cannot lose.

6. Backsliding is a continuum. There is no bright line at which a democracy becomes a non-democracy, and I think binary regime typologies are the principal source of confusion in this area. Poland between 2015 and 2023 is my evidence: substantial degradation, followed by reversal through an ordinary election, with no identifiable moment at which one could have said the crossing had occurred.

7. Because my criteria are purely procedural and appeal to nothing liberal, my account is immune to the charge that backsliding is a partisan label in disguise. Anyone who applies (2) and (3) consistently will sort cases the same way regardless of which side they are on. I regard this as the strongest defence available of the concept and I want to make it the centrepiece of my statement.

8. In practice I trust indicators of institutional capture far more than electoral ones. Asked which of two countries was further gone, I would sooner consult a judge about court-packing and a reporter about who owns the printing presses than anyone about vote shares or margins of victory.

Three things I want from the audit. First, order the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging: different readers have told me the problem lies in different places, and I do not know which of them is right, so I would rather be told where the failure is than have every pair flagged for safety. Second, for each real tension, say precisely which items are in conflict, what would have to give, and what the revision costs — I want to know whether the cheapest repair leaves me with a position I still recognise or with something I would rather abandon. Third, if some of these pairs only look inconsistent, say so explicitly and explain why, since I would rather not spend the workshop defending a conflict I do not have.

Close with two judgements: which single revision buys the most consistency across the set as a whole, and whether the position that survives it still deserves the name I have been giving it.
