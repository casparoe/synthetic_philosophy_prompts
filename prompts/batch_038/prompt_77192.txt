I have just finished my first year on the Clinical Ethics Service at a hospital in a state where medical aid in dying is lawful, and I am writing about a case that will come before us next month. I should say at the outset why a sitting member is asking an outsider: the patient is my father. I have recused myself and will not be in the room. What I want is not to work the case on him, but to understand as precisely as I can how our own record is likely to resolve it, and whether I should expect consistency from us or improvisation.

Our service does not decide legality. It is consulted when a request is lawful but contested, or when it strains the statute's terms. Our statute, as we read it: adult patient, resident, capacity at each step, terminal illness with a prognosis of six months or less, and the medication must be self-administered. Two clinicians must concur and the request must be repeated.

Our record so far, in my own summary:

1. Advanced ALS, no longer able to swallow. We supported a plan in which the patient would start an infusion herself by pressing a button with her thumb, and we wrote that a nurse might set up the pump and remain in the room, but that a nurse who initiated the infusion would cross a line.
2. End-stage COPD, prognosis under six months, untreated major depression. We declined to endorse until the depression had been treated; the request was renewed afterward and we supported it.
3. Terminal pancreatic cancer, functioning well, no suffering she would have called unbearable. She wanted, as she put it, the medication in the drawer. We supported the prescription, noting that the statute requires a terminal prognosis and not unbearable suffering. She never took it.
4. A written statement made years earlier, while competent, that she would want aid in dying if she ever reached advanced dementia. Her family asked us to act on it after she lost capacity. We declined, holding that the statute requires a contemporaneous request. Several members recorded that they personally disagreed with the holding.
5. A patient whose adult son twice asked us when the process would conclude. We paused, re-interviewed her alone, supported the request, and required that no family member be present at any further discussion.
6. End-stage renal disease, dialysis continuing, request made while she still intended to keep going on dialysis. We held that we could not treat her as terminally ill while the treatment plan in force made her prognosis indefinite. After she stopped dialysis and the prognosis fell under six months, we supported the request.

My father's case. Glioblastoma, diagnosed fourteen months ago, now progressing; two clinicians put him inside six months. His capacity is intact today and expected to become unreliable — seizures, steroids, and then a decline nobody can date. He reports no unbearable suffering now. What he says he wants is the exit in his own hands while his hands still work. He asks for two things: that the medication be prescribed now and kept at home so that he may take it himself, at a moment he chooses; and, failing that — if he reaches a point where he cannot act for himself — that it be given to him then, by my mother, rather than be allowed to run out of reach. One further fact I will not conceal: my sister has said in front of him that she cannot bear to watch him linger, and has asked twice when the prescription might arrive. The phrase about not wanting to be a burden entered his vocabulary after that conversation.

Predict how our service will rule, and on which part of the request. Which of the six is the closest analog, and what turns on that choice? Set out the candidate principles our record supports jointly — I suspect more than one fits all six — and say what each implies here and exactly where they diverge. Then tell me what the service should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself rather than merely defensible, and be explicit about which principle we would be adopting by deciding either way. If our record genuinely underdetermines this, say so instead of manufacturing a determinate answer, and name the one further case that, had it appeared on our docket, would have settled which principle we are running.

Two requests by way of intellectual honesty. First, separate what is settled in this field from what is genuinely contested among people who work in it seriously, and tell me where I am at risk of mistaking a contested position for a consensus. Second, tell me whether there is a materially different way of describing my father's request that would bring it clearly under one of those principles — and whether that redescription would be honest, or advocacy in disguise.
