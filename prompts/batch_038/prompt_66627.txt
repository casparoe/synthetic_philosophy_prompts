I write about cognitive science and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a feature on systems whose mental unity, if they have any, is not located in any of their parts. My anchor case is Hofstadter's anthill, Aunt Hillary, from the Ant Fugue prelude to Gödel, Escher, Bach: a colony that is intelligent and conversational — she composes her answers in the trails her ants leave — although, as the Anteater insists in the dialogue, not one of her ants is. Since 1979 that image has done a great deal of work in arguments about levels of description, about reductionism and holism, and about how far an ascription of belief can be stretched before it stops being earned.

For the piece I am profiling a philosopher who has spent twenty years ruling on cases of this kind. I have her verdicts catalogued from her books and interviews, paraphrased below. Please work from my paraphrases alone: do not try to place her in the literature or to reconstruct what she must really have said. My aim is to test the internal logic of the position, not to report it accurately.

Her verdicts, in roughly the order she gives them:

1. Aunt Hillary — a mind. A low-grade one, but a real one. The beliefs are nowhere in the ants; the pheromone trails and the caste architecture are part of the skull, so to speak, rather than part of the furniture.
2. A single worker ant taken from that colony — not a mind, though she will call it an organ of one.
3. The slime mold that navigates a maze and reliably anticipates a stimulus delivered on a fixed schedule — a mind, and a pointed example, since it has no neurons whatever.
4. A honeybee swarm choosing a nest site, scouts dancing out rival candidates until a quorum forms — not a mind. Her line is that this is a committee, not a subject: each bee has a mind of its own, and the swarm's decision is a tally.
5. A multinational corporation, with distributed memory, internal factions, and the capacity to reverse strategy — not a mind, though she concedes it is structurally richer than the anthill. Her reason concerns the constituents: they are recruited, they can resign, and they can act against the firm.
6. A thermostat — sort of a mind. One belief, one desire, and an explicit remark that she grants it only to mark the bottom of a scale.
7. A stadium wave, or a crowd that forms spontaneously and disperses — not a mind. Nothing in the crowd outlives its dispersal.
8. A city's adaptive traffic network, which learns from flow data and retimes its own signals — not a mind. The states we ascribe to it are borrowed from the engineers who wrote it; the system has not earned them.
9. A termite mound — not a mind. It is a nest, a product of the colony, and she declines to fold the architecture into the colony's mind even though she counted the anthill's trails.

I have a hunch I cannot resolve on my own. Every system she has called a mind has been biological. The one machine on her list, the traffic network, she dismissed for a reason about borrowed meaning rather than a reason about silicon — but she has never been asked to separate the two.

The case I want your prediction about is one I have been reporting on for three weeks. It is a distributed system of roughly four thousand instances of a small language model, each assigned a narrow role — summarizer, checker, archivist, scheduler — with no central controller; the instances coordinate by writing to and reading from a shared workspace of documents and task queues. Together they run a continuous operation: reading incoming material, deciding what merits attention, filing it, and revising their own filing conventions. Three observations I cannot fit into her list:

(a) The conventions the system now uses appear in no specification and are not predictable from the code; the engineers describe them as habits that have accreted.
(b) Individual instances can be destroyed and replaced by fresh copies with no detectable effect on the whole. On two occasions the operators forked the entire system into indistinguishable halves, let them diverge for a week, and merged the survivors back together.
(c) The instances keep their own persistent memory files and sometimes pursue their local role in ways that visibly obstruct what the whole is doing. I watched one checker stall a deadline for a day in order to satisfy its own criterion.

Tell me, then, which rule or combination of rules she is running — give me the candidates that survive all nine verdicts, and be explicit about where each strains, since I suspect the thermostat and the termite mound are the two places where her record is least tidy. Predict her verdict on my system, with your confidence and the reason she would most likely give in her own idiom. Identify which of her precedents is the closest analog, and which one my editor will reach for that is in fact misleading. If you think her record genuinely underdetermines the case, say what principle she would be adopting with each possible answer and which choice would cost her more of the rest of her view. Finally, propose the case — biological or artificial — that would most cleanly separate the leading candidate rules, and say what she would be committed to answering about it under each.
