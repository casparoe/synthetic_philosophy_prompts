Prepping next week's seminar on migration and the welfare state, and there's a policy I keep failing to pin down philosophically. Maybe you can help. In the UK, many people with limited leave to remain — spousal and family visas, students, several work routes — carry a "no recourse to public funds" condition. They work, they pay income tax and National Insurance, their employers pay payroll tax on them, and visa applicants hand over a health surcharge upfront as part of the fee. Yet they're barred from means-tested benefits, from social housing, and from homelessness assistance. Meanwhile the same households kids get free state schooling, anyone can register with a GP, and turning up at A&E gets you treated without a bill whatever your status. And the Home Office can lift the condition for someone who's become destitute, so the exclusion isn't even stable.

What theory of what a welfare state is *for* does this package presuppose? I keep generating candidate stories — reciprocity and contribution, membership as a binary status, fear of benefit magnetism, cost-shifting onto households and employers — and I can't tell which the practice is actually committed to and which its merely consistent with. So: reconstruct the implicit theory as tightly as you can, then say which parts of the practice strictly need it rather than just fitting it. Is the taxpaying-plus-free-schooling-plus-free-A&E side genuinely consistent with the benefits ban, or does it only look consistent because nobody in charge is forced to say why? Does the destitution clause rescue the theory or give the game away? And if you swapped in a flatly need-based view, what would have to change institutionally — would the condition become incoherent outright, or just narrower? Bonus question for my own amusement: is there any principle that draws the line exactly where it's drawn, or is the line basically arbitrary?
