In three weeks I open a two-day workshop whose title is itself the question: *Found or Made? The Status of Features in Trained Neural Networks*. I have seven minutes for opening remarks before the first session, and I would like you to write them.

The room will hold about forty people. Some are interpretability researchers who spend their days on probes, sparse autoencoders, and activation patching, and who talk about features and directions and circuits as a matter of course. Some are engineers who ship models, who say "the network represents the user's intent" several times a day without embarrassment, and who regard our title as a waste of a funded afternoon. Three are philosophers of mind or cognitive science, who have seen this debate run once before under a different name. Two are program officers from the body that paid for the workshop, and they have a practical question that nobody else in the room quite shares: whether interpretability findings could ever support an assurance about a deployed system, or only ever a suggestive story about one. Roughly half the room reads papers in this area; the other half have never opened one, and several will be there because their name was on a list.

The speech should do the following.

**1. Organize itself around a disambiguation that the room has not performed.** At least three different claims are being run together under "the network represents X": a *tracking* claim, that internal states covary systematically with properties of the world or of the training distribution; a *functional-role* claim, that some internal state is used by the system in roughly the way a belief is used, so that its presence and its content make a difference to what the system does across situations, including situations it was not trained on; and an *ascription* claim, that "represents" is a description adopted from outside because it earns its keep in prediction and intervention, whether or not anything inside answers to it. The speech should let the audience see that a good deal of the workshop's loudest disagreement is disagreement about which of these is meant, while at least one dispute survives the disambiguation intact as a real dispute. It should say which is which, and why the surviving one is worth two days.

**2. Take no side, without being evasive about it.** Every assertion in the speech must be one that a committed realist about features and a committed instrumentalist could each utter without insincerity. I do not want this achieved by vagueness. Include at least three claims that will sound, on first hearing, as though they commit the speaker to one camp or the other, and then, after the speech, in a note to me of no more than two hundred words, say for each of the three what makes it acceptable from both positions and what a partisan would have to add to it to turn it into a claim the other side must reject.

**3. Carry one technical point by analogy.** Polysemanticity and superposition — the facts that a single unit can serve several unrelated purposes at once, and that a model can encode more distinguishable directions than it has units to put them in — are where the naive picture of interpretation breaks down, and the non-specialists need them. Build one sustained analogy that delivers both facts without using either word, and that a person who has never seen a neural network can follow. It must not be drawn from the standard philosophy-of-mind props: no brains in vats, no Chinese rooms, no homunculi, no zombies, no beetles in boxes. Something from maps, archives, storage, languages, cities, or instruments would suit the room better.

**4. Say something honest to the program officers.** The speech should acknowledge, without condescension and without drama, what interpretability could and could not presently support in the way of assurance about a system in use. It must contain no sentence they could quote afterward as a promise, and no sentence that implies their question is naive. Getting this right is more important than any joke in the speech.

**5. Leave the room one task.** End by posing the single question you think the two days can actually be organized around — something in the neighbourhood of what would make a proposed feature *wrong*, or what would make two candidate features *the same* feature — and set it up so that both camps can see their own view as answerable to it rather than immune to it. Do not answer it. Do not preview the sessions or thank anyone beyond a single brief acknowledgment of the hosts.

On manner: no quotations, no named philosophers, no cited papers or books — the concepts should arrive without their attributions, and the room will recognize them anyway. At most two moments of humour, neither of them at the expense of a subgroup present, and neither of them a pun on the word "feature." The register should be that of someone who is pleased the room is full and slightly suspicious of the title they chose. About nine hundred words for the speech itself.
