My reading group meets the week after Christmas and we have decided to watch Frank Capra's *It's a Wonderful Life* (1946) and then discuss the Pottersville sequence — the stretch in which Clarence grants George Bailey's wish that he had never been born and walks him through a town remade by his absence. I want to prepare a critical reading of that sequence in terms of the contemporary dispute between the Lewis-style counterfactual analysis of causation and the interventionist account associated with Woodward, and I would like your help writing it.

The two analyses of the sequence that I want tested are these.

First, Clarence's demonstration looks like an n=1 experiment. It supplies exactly one alternative world and no information whatsoever about how outcomes in Bedford Falls would vary as George's own conduct varies in smaller ways. On a Lewisian picture, where the truth of a singular causal claim turns on the nearest world in which the antecedent holds, that single world is precisely the right amount of evidence, and George's resulting confidence is rational. On an interventionist picture, where a causal claim is licensed by a relationship that is invariant across a range of interventions, a single data point licenses nothing: the sequence establishes that the world is worse in one counterfactual scenario, not that anything depends on George in the sense that matters. I want to know whether you think this asymmetry is real, and whether the film's emotional climax tacitly relies on the Lewisian reading of the evidence.

Second, and this is the part I am least sure of, almost the whole of George's causal contribution as the sequence reconstructs it is negative. He keeps his brother from drowning, and the brother later prevents the loss of a troopship and its passengers; he withholds a mislabeled prescription; he averts a run on the Building and Loan; he holds Potter in check so that a housing development exists instead of slums. What the tour shows, in other words, is a chain of preventions and double preventions, plus the standing absence of a countervailing force. The town's transformation is not attributed to any change in Potter, Billy, or the war, all of whom are held fixed; it is attributed to George's not being there. So the sequence appears to make absences and preventions the entire substance of a life's causal significance, which is exactly the territory where the counterfactual tradition is most embarrassed and the interventionist tradition most comfortable, since the latter can simply treat an absence as a value of a variable. Assess whether the film really does run on absence causation, and whether that helps or hurts it philosophically.

Alongside these, please attend to the mechanics of Clarence's device as an intervention. Is it surgical in the required sense, or fat-handed, given that removing a person at conception changes innumerable variables at once? Is the miracle that produces the sequence small and late in the way a Lewisian similarity ordering wants, or enormous and early with a divergence that never re-converges? What does the sequence implicitly hold fixed, and is it entitled to hold those things fixed? And does it commit a transworld identity error, in that George's nonexistence should make it unclear whose mother, whose brother, and whose wife the people he meets are?

I would like the piece to be faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the film to an illustration of either theory, and do not bend either theory to produce a neat verdict; where the sequence resists the causal reading, or where a philosophical point would require a different film altogether, say so plainly. Tell me directly where you think my second hypothesis overreaches. Please identify the specific scenes you are relying on rather than summarizing the plot, and end with a short section on what the film contributes to this debate that argument alone does not — whether dramatizing the difference a life makes does any philosophical work that a counterfactual conditional cannot. I do not need quotations from the screenplay, a bibliography, or a survey of the literature; I need a piece of criticism that could be handed to a mixed group of philosophers and non-philosophers and would leave both better able to argue about the film afterwards.
