Context: 3rd year in political theory, writing up my comps answer on the boundary problem, and I realised I've been describing my own view to people for a year without ever checking if it hangs together as a package. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The circularity worry is genuinely fatal at the constitutive level. You can't settle who's in the demos by taking a vote of the demos, because the vote already presupposes the answer — that's the Whelan/Dahl point and I think it's right, not just a cute puzzle.
2. So the boundary question has to be settled by pre-political normative principles, and democracy comes downstream of that. The demos doesn't get to constitute itself.
3. The right principle is an all-subjected / all-coerced one rather than an all-affected one: what generates a claim to a say is being subject to a state's coercive rule, not merely being causally affected by what it does.
4. Part of why I like (3) over all-affected is exactly that all-affected blows up — causal interdependence runs off in every direction, there's no natural stopping point, and you end up either with a world demos or with something so indeterminate it can't discipline anything. I want a principle that gives you a bounded constituency.
5. But I also accept the Abizadeh-style claim that a state's border regime is a paradigm case of coercion, and that the people it coerces aren't inside the territory. So on (3) they have a claim to a say over the border decision itself.
6. At the same time, I don't think the demos has to be cosmopolitan about membership. Once there is a demos, it has a lot of ordinary discretion over admission, and I don't want my view to imply that every outsider gets a vote on every admission decision in the routine sense.
7. And long-term resident non-citizens should be enfranchised domestically — I think doing that by ordinary democratic decision is completely unproblematic, no circularity issue at all, it's just legislation like anything else.
