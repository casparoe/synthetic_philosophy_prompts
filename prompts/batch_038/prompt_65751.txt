I have worked as a clinical embryologist in an NHS fertility unit for eleven years, and last autumn my mother told me that I was conceived by donor insemination. The clinic's records for that year were lost in a building move, so the identity of the man whose sperm I came from is, so far as anyone now knows, unrecoverable. I have been processing this in the only way available to me, which is a part-time MA in philosophy; my dissertation is on the metaphysics of origins, and my supervisor has asked me to set out on a single page the position I am actually committed to before I build anything on it. She has now told me twice that the package below is unstable. She has not said where, and I would considerably rather hear it from you than from her.

Here is the package, as honestly as I can state it. (1) I am an origin essentialist in the strict sense: there is no possible world in which I come from a different sperm or a different egg — not even a sperm differing from the actual one at a single nucleotide — and my reason is precisely that any toleration of small differences leaves me with no principled stopping point. (2) I am a four-dimensionalist: I am a spacetime worm, and I reject mereological essentialism about myself, since that worm could have had a different heart, a different liver, fewer fingers, or somewhat more mass at thirty. (3) I am not comfortable with transworld identity: when I say that I might have been a better swimmer, what makes this true is that some counterpart of mine in another world swims better, and I think all de re modality about myself works that way. (4) I think identity can be indeterminate — in cases such as gradual teletransportation, or the splitting of an early embryo, I am willing to say there need be no fact of the matter as to whether the later person is the earlier one, and I do not accept that every such question has a sharp answer of which we are merely ignorant. (5) Two verdicts from the clinic that I am not prepared to give up: a tetragametic chimera, where two zygotes from two eggs and two sperm fuse early and develop into a single body, is one person rather than two people sharing a body and rather than strictly nobody; and in mitochondrial donation, where the nuclear DNA comes from the intended mother and the mitochondria from a donor, the resulting child has two parents, the donor not being a parent in any sense that matters. (6) Finally, origin is for me necessary but not sufficient: if that very sperm had fertilised that very egg and the resulting zygote had then split, I do not think it follows that I would have been one of the two children; and I might never have existed at all, since in the overwhelming majority of worlds in which my parents had children, there is no one who is me.

Please treat this as an audit rather than a tour of the literature. Go through the pairings and tell me which are in genuine conflict and which only look conflicted, ordering the genuine ones by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and saying for each what would have to be abandoned or weakened and what that abandonment costs me elsewhere. I suspect that at least one of the pairs I would myself have flagged turns out to be a pseudo-problem, though I may be wrong about which; equally, I suspect I am missing at least one that you will not. Where a principle of mine fails to deliver a verdict it ought to deliver — in the cases from (5), for instance — say so plainly and say what the principle would have to become in order to deliver it. Above all, I want a committed response: take a definite position on which of these is least survivable, commit to your ranking, give your reasons for it, and tell me which single revision buys the greatest coherence for the least, rather than laying out the available reconciliations and leaving me to choose among them.
