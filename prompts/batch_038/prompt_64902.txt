I work on the inference side of an LLM product — mostly plumbing: batching, timeouts, a "thinking budget" knob that caps how many tokens an agent gets to churn through before it's forced to answer. Over the past year I've been collecting incidents that all look like the same underlying thing, and I don't have a word for it, so I figured I'd ask here before bugging my friend who's in a philosophy PhD program (she keeps telling me to read stuff and I keep not having time).

Three of the incidents:

1. Same model, same weights, same prompt, same everything except the budget: one run gets 512 tokens, the other gets 32k. The long run correctly answers a question that is a direct logical consequence of a spec we pasted into the prompt itself — not hidden info, literally entailed by stuff the model was handed. The short run doesn't get it. Nobody would say the short run was missing *information*. It was missing compute.

2. We cache and we restart things, so a job that gets killed and relaunched with a smaller budget stops knowing claims the same system produced yesterday. Nothing was forgotten in the normal sense — no state lost, no evidence retracted, identical weights, identical prompt. The knowledge went away because we stopped paying for it. That breaks the mental model I have where knowledge goes up when information comes in and stays up unless something is withdrawn, instead of oscillating with a cost knob.

3. The one that actually annoys me: to decide whether to spend budget reasoning about X, the system would have to already know what it would conclude about X, which is exactly what it cant know before spending. So the choice of what to think about is made under ignorance of what thinking would yield. We paper over it with heuristics (if the diff touches auth, always think longer) and I suspect theres a real principled problem hiding there rather than just bad engineering.

So: does this have a name? I've bumped into the fact that possible-worlds semantics for knowledge makes an agent automatically know everything that follows from what it knows, and I gather people consider that a known defect, so maybe what I'm describing is just that with extra steps. But if so I want to know what the neighborhood looks like and how my version differs, because the part I care about isn't "agents fail to be omniscient," which is just a bug report against an idealization. It's the specific structure: what-an-agent-knows is indexed to a budget it allocates itself; knowledge can go *down* when the budget gets cut, with no change in evidence; and the agent has to pick what to think about without knowing what it would find. Is that bundle captured anywhere — some nonstandard epistemic logic, something in decision theory about the value of computing, a distinction between kinds of belief I've never heard of? Relatedly, I have a vague sense there's a problem in Bayesian-land where your probabilities are supposed to shift even though no new evidence arrived, because you merely noticed what your old evidence already implied. Might be the same thing in a different hat, might not be.

If it turns out there's no single established term, please don't just list five half-relevant concepts and shrug — I want the carving. What exactly is this phenomenon, what is it *not* (I keep suspecting it gets lumped in with generic bounded-rationality complaints and with plain old "models are inconsistent"), and where are its edges? Also tell me whether my three incidents are one phenomenon or three that just rhyme. And if it needs naming, coin something and give me a definition tight enough that I could check whether a new case I hit next year falls under it.
