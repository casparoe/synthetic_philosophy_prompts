I've just been seated on the collections ethics committee of a mid-sized ethnographic museum — around sixty thousand objects, three curators, one provenance researcher on a fraction of a post, and a public funder whose next grant cycle requires both guaranteed public access and a written restitution policy we can actually apply. Our first meeting made clear that we don't disagree about cases so much as we disagree about what counts as a reason. The director keeps invoking the welfare of visitors; the curator of African collections says the only question that matters is how an object left its place of origin; the head of conservation says we are both ignoring the fact that some claimant institutions cannot hold metalwork at stable humidity; and I left convinced that all three of us were stating positions rather than criteria. Before we draft anything, I want the specification. What must an adequate normative theory of the restitution of colonial-era collections deliver in order to be usable by an institution like ours? Please write the desiderata as a numbered list — no more than nine items, and cut anything that sounds elevated but does no work — giving for each the case or consideration that motivates it, a tag saying whether it is non-negotiable, merely negotiable, or actually a camp commitment disguised as a criterion, and a sentence on what a theory that fails it looks like when a small staff has to apply it on a Tuesday.

I'd like the list built against our own material rather than the two famous ones everyone reaches for, so here are six composites from our files, anonymized and simplified: a carved throne bought in 1898 from a regional ruler at a price everyone at the time thought fair, four months after a punitive expedition had burned his capital, with account books in our archive showing the money went to ransoming hostages; a set of regalia taken by force in 1897, sold at auction in 1954, and bequeathed to us in 1991 by a collector who apparently never knew how it had left, now claimed simultaneously by a national museum, by a traditional council insisting the objects belong to an office and can only be held by its current occupant, and by a diaspora association in our own city arguing they should stay where they are; a bundle of ritual objects the claimant community asks us not to photograph, display, catalogue online, or describe in print, under a funding agreement that requires public access and with our own scholars warning that an object nobody can see drops out of the historical record; an object taken by one regional polity from another in the 1860s and then by Europeans in the 1890s, now claimed by the successor state of the intermediate polity, which was itself an imperial power in that region; some four thousand everyday objects whose acquisition records say only "purchased," or nothing at all; and one object that nobody claims, whose makers left no written record and no identifiable descendant community.

Then tell me whether any subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect the demand that past transactions be judged by norms available at the time, the demand that colonial legality not function as a shield, and the demand that our decisions be predictable enough for a three-curator staff to apply cannot all be met; if there is an impossibility there, I want it stated as the headline result rather than buried in a caveat, and I want to know which requirement has to go. Commit also to the single requirement that knocks out the most otherwise respectable views, and say why it does the most work. Finish with a short scorecard — a couple of sentences each, a table is fine — on how the corrective-justice and property account, the state-succession and legalist account, the custodial-welfare account on which collections belong to humanity wherever they are best held, and the account on which sacred objects are not property at all but persons-in-relations do against your list. Keep the scorecard brief: the weight belongs on the criteria.

I want a position, not a menu. Tell me plainly which of the things people demand of a restitution theory are genuine requirements and which are retentionist or repatriationist prejudices wearing the costume of criteria — including the popular appeal to the joint statement by the directors of several large encyclopedic museums defending their holdings as belonging to all humanity, which I suspect is a claimant to be evaluated rather than a criterion, but which our director treats as one. If you think one of my composites is misdescribed — that the ransom-money throne is not a hard case at all, say, or that the unclaimed object is really a question about custody rather than about justice — say so and argue it rather than quietly accommodating it. Don't hedge with "it depends on your values," and don't quote: if you want to gesture at what a thinker or an institution has said, paraphrase it, since I'd rather check sources myself. Aim for something in the region of fifteen hundred words.
