I'm auditing a political epistemology seminar this term and our reading group passed around a one-page handout last week called "epistemic democracy in seven steps" — actually eight, the guy added a coda — written by one of the older PhD students. Everyone in the room nodded along, me included, and three days later I still can't stop picking at it. I've tried to restate it the way he'd defend it rather than as a strawman, and I've kept his numbering:

(1) Condorcet's jury theorem: if the members of a group each answer the same yes/no question, each has probability p of answering it correctly where p is greater than a half, and their answers are independent, then the probability that the majority answer is correct is higher than p, and it approaches 1 as the group grows. He did the arithmetic in the margin for p = 0.6 and n = 1,000: the chance of a wrong majority comes out around one in a billion. And he noted that the same thing happens for p = 0.51, just slower. (2) On questions of public fact — will this policy push unemployment up, is this drug safe, did the bridge fail at the welds — ordinary citizens are better than chance. His evidence: the ox-weight guessing contest at a country fair that Galton wrote up, where the crowd's pooled estimate landed within a percent of the animal's actual dressed weight; prediction markets beating pundits; and the fact that referendums and elections usually track how the economy subsequently does. (3) So on those questions, the majority verdict of a large electorate is more likely to be correct than any single citizen's verdict. (4) And nothing in the theorem privileges the specialist. An expert's judgment is just one more draw, with some p short of 1, and the theorem's guarantee holds for any p above a half however slightly, and for any n, so the majority verdict of a million laypeople is more likely to be right than one expert's verdict on the same question. (5) He anticipated the correlation objection — voters aren't independent, they all watch the same channels — and answered that positive correlation only slows the convergence down rather than reversing it, and that an electorate of a hundred million always contains plenty of people who never talk to one another, so the theorem still bites. (6) Where one procedure is more likely to be right than another about the very question you're trying to settle, you should go with the more reliable one; that's all there is to having a reason to defer. (7) Therefore on any question of public fact I ought to defer to the majority verdict rather than to my own judgment, including when my own judgment is the informed one. (8) Coda, which he thought was the clincher: the theorem runs backwards too, since if p is below a half the majority converges to being wrong with probability approaching 1. So either the electorate is asymptotically infallible or asymptotically and reliably wrong; the second is absurd, so the first stands.

The problem is that I can't live with (7) and I don't think he can either. It says that when a million people who all read the same two headlines outvote one person who actually read the study, the million are the better bet — and that is precisely the case my thesis is about, and it's obviously false. It also seems to say that the large confident majorities that were later shown to be flatly wrong (everyone in the seminar had a favourite example, nobody wrote theirs down) were nevertheless the most reliable procedure available at the time, and I can't figure out what "they got it wrong" could mean after that. And (8) bothers me in a seperate way: it's clearly meant to block the escape route where you just say the electorate's p is under a half, but I can't tell whether it actually blocks anything.
