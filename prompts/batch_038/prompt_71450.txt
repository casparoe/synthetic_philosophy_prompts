In about three weeks I'm giving a paper at a law-and-econ workshop — sort of an interdisciplinary crowd, law school hosted, and it's my first time defending this thing outside my own department so I'm nervous about the Q&A specifically. The thesis: for mediated settlements, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the better normative model than Nash bargaining, not because its axioms are "truer" but because it's more honest about the work the aspiration point does. Nash quietly makes the ceiling of what each side could ever hope to get irrelevant (that's what IIA buys), while KS puts it on the table and says: when one side's best achievable outcome improves, they should get proportionally more of the surplus. Which is, I want to argue, roughly what mediators are already doing when they get parties to reframe what the deal could look like at its most ambitious and then split the improvement proportionally.

The argument runs in four steps and it'd help to have them in front of you so the objections have something to target. (1) In a mediated settlement the disagreement point is the expected litigated outcome, and it's usually better specified than people think — probability-weighted judgment plus costs. (2) The utopia point isn't arbitrary or made-up, because the parties' opening demands are already on the record as damages figures or reservation prices on injunctive relief, so the ceiling is something they've asserted rather than something I've imported. (3) Equal proportional gains is what makes that ceiling normatively load-bearing in a way parties can actually argue about: you can contest the other side's demand without being accused of just grabbing. (4) So the "manipulation" worry — that a party inflates its stated maximum to drag the solution its way — is not a bug but the theory correctly modeling what high anchoring does, and it's constrained by the fact that an absurd demand moves the aspiration point without moving the Pareto frontier, so it can't buy unlimited gains. I'll also lean on the observation that in fixed-split bargaining experiments people seem to gravitate toward the midpoint of the frontier rather than the Nash shares, though I'm told that literature is messier than the textbook summaries make it sound. I do NOT claim KS is more fair, and I don't want to have to defend a general theory of interpersonal utility comparison — my claim is deliberately narrow, it's about what makes a settlement procedure justifiable to the parties in the room.

The audience is maybe fifteen people: two or three formal bargaining theorists who live in NTU games and axiomatics and will know the KS material cold, one experimental economist who runs negotiation labs, a civil-procedure/settlement professor who thinks most bargaining theory is fiction dressed up as prediction, two practicing mediators who care only about what happens at the table, and a philosopher of social science who will go straight for is/ought. So give me the prep sheet, not a defense of my paper: the eightish objections I'll actually get from this room — phrased in their words, the way the game theorists would phrase them versus the way the mediators would, since they'll object to completely different things — with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, what I should concede up front, what I absolutely cannot give up, and a note on where each reply is soft or where I'm leaning on something contestable. Rank them by how much damage they'd do to the thesis if they land unanswered. Then tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — my guess is something about utility units, or about the solution being non-additive across linked sub-games, since those are where I'm weakest, but you may see a worse one. Two meta things I want you to be straight with me about rather than reassuring: if you think my ranking is wrong, say which objection you'd move and what would change your mind about that ordering, and for each reply tell me where I'd most likely get caught bluffing by someone who knows the literature better than I do and should just say "I don't know, here's what I'd have to check" instead.
