**The position I hold.** Stated as four claims, in increasing order of how exposed I suspect they are:

- **P1.** In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between programs with read-access to each other's source, mutual cooperation is attainable as an equilibrium, and the Löbian construction — an agent that cooperates exactly when it can prove its opponent will cooperate — attains it in a way that is robust, in that it does not depend on the two agents' code being identical, and safe, in that the agent never cooperates with an opponent that defects.
- **P2.** The earlier constructions, which relied on tests for program equality (Tennenholtz, and the Fortnow variant), were fragile precisely where the Löbian approach is not: they turned on exact code identity, which is a knife-edge condition.
- **P3.** What remains is a resource problem rather than a conceptual one. Bounded proof search threatens the Löbian trick, but under a suitable parametrisation of proof length the reasoning can be recovered, so boundedness is a technical hurdle on the way, not a refutation.
- **P4.** Consequently, as deployed systems become more mutually legible — published weights, signed attestations about a build, machine-checked claims about a policy's behaviour — the cooperative dynamics that program equilibrium predicts should begin to show up in practice. On this view P4 is an empirical claim with a timeline, not a thought experiment.

Where my formulation of the technical content is loose or outright wrong, correct me rather than reasoning around the error. I am particularly unsure whether P1 conflates two different agent properties that the literature keeps apart.

**Two things I want you to take as given.** First, I recall that in a later revision the authors of the modal-agent paper appended a remark to the effect that bounded analogues of their results had yet to be proved. Tell me how much that concession costs P3, and whether it is the sort of admission a defender can absorb. Second, the research programme has continued into settings with private information, using commitment devices that conditionally disclose information and yielding approximate equilibria in a program-equilibrium sense. **A concrete case to reason about.** Call it the Attestation Setup. Two deployed systems, A and B, each consist of a trained policy network plus a separate proof-checking module with a fixed resource budget k. Each publishes a signed hash of its deployment build and an attestation of the form: for any opponent satisfying a published specification, I cooperate provided your attestation checks out. Three complications. The hash covers the quantised production build, not the research code that was verified. The checker is a different component from the policy, trusted as an oracle, and is sound only on a restricted fragment of the language in which the attestation is written. And each side's budget k is private information, though both know the other's is of a similar magnitude. What does my position predict here? What happens as k shrinks relative to the complexity of the attestation? And which of P1–P4 does a failure in this setup actually falsify, as opposed to merely embarrassing?
