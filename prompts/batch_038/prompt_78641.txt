stuck on something in Montesquieu, Spirit of the Laws, Book XIV, chapters 1–2 (Nugent translation, footnote markers stripped out), and I can't tell whether I'm missing an argument or reading one in. Here's the bit:

> If it be true that the temper of the mind and the passions of the heart are extremely different in different climates, the laws ought to be in relation both to the variety of those passions and to the variety of those tempers.
>
> Cold air constringes the extremities of the external fibres of the body; this increases their elasticity, and favours the return of the blood from the extreme parts to the heart. It contracts those very fibres; consequently it increases also their force. On the contrary, warm air relaxes and lengthens the extremes of the fibres; of course it diminishes their force and elasticity.
>
> People are therefore more vigorous in cold climates. [...] This superiority of strength must produce various effects; for instance, a greater boldness, that is, more courage; a greater sense of superiority, that is, less desire of revenge; a greater opinion of security, that is, more frankness, less suspicion, policy, and cunning. In short, this must be productive of very different tempers. Put a man into a close, warm place, and for the reasons above given he will feel a great faintness. If under this circumstance you propose a bold enterprise to him, I believe you will find him very little disposed towards it; his present weakness will throw him into despondency; he will be afraid of everything, being in a state of total incapacity. The inhabitants of warm countries are, like old men, timorous; the people in cold countries are, like young men, brave.

So on one hand he's offering what looks like a genuine mechanical story — air temperature acts on the fibres, the fibres act on the heart and on sensation, sensation and vigour act on character — and then he walks straight from that to things like frankness, low cunning, timidity. On the other hand, a few chapters later in the same book he says (paraphrasing, since I don't want to butcher the wording) that bad legislators are the ones who go along with the vices the climate produces and good ones are those who resist them, and he repeatedly treats laws, religion and customs as able to correct what nature has set up.

That's where I get tangled. Call it, for lack of a better name, the mediator problem: if climate acts on temperament, and law also acts on temperament, and the law is itself supposed to be chosen *in reaction to* the climate — which is literally the instruction in chapter 1 — then how much of a climate's contribution to any national outcome is even a determinate quantity? It depends on which political response the place got. Two warm countries with different legal systems could come out any way at all, and the theory never loses; but you also couldn't in principle separate "geography" from "institutions," because institutions sit on the causal path running out of geography. Which makes me wonder whether he's really a geographic determinist at all, or whether climate is functioning as a residual term that gets absorbed by whatever the legislators do.

Three things I want help with:

1. Is that tension actually load-bearing in his system, or am I dragging a 20th-century identification worry back into an 18th-century text that was doing something else entirely (e.g. just giving a naturalistic warrant for legal pluralism)?
2. Does the "put a man into a close, warm place" move do more work than it looks like? It reads to me like he's quietly swapping an individual-level manipulation (same man, warm room, faints at the thought of a bold enterprise) for a population-level comparison (inhabitants of warm countries are timorous), and using the first to license the second. Is that fair, or is the warm-room case meant to establish the mechanism only, with the cross-country claim resting on something else he gestures at in the same passage?
3. Taken at his word, is his position better described as climate causing institutions, or climate causing a *distribution* of dispositions which institutions then sort through? The second reading seems to make him an early institutionalist who just has a weird physiology bolted onto the front end, and I want to know whether that's a defensible reading of these chapters or a charitable distortion.

Also, and this matters to me more than the answers themselves: tell me plainly what would change your mind about your reading here. Which consideration or which feature of the text would push you to the opposite verdict on (1) and (3), and where do you think your own interpretation is most likely to be wrong — especially since I've only given you two chapters and you're partly reasoning about the rest of the book.
