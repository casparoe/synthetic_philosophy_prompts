My roommate in the physics dept has an argument that chaos theory kills determinism, and I can't find the flaw — every step looks fine on its own but the conclusion is way too strong to be true.

(1) Determinism says the exact state of a system at a time, plus the laws, fixes exactly one future.
(2) But "fixes" can't mean anything over and above "makes derivable in principle." If a future were fixed in some way no derivation could ever reach, the word wouldn't be doing any work at all.
(3) In a chaotic system, errors grow exponentially: an initial error δ becomes δe^{λt}. Toy numbers — say your error doubles every two days.
(4) Any specification of an initial state is finite: a string of digits, a formula, a physical record. So it carries some nonzero error against the real-valued true state.
(5) Therefore from any specification you can derive the system's behavior only out to a horizon T ≈ (1/λ)ln(ε/δ), where ε is the error you're willing to live with. Doubling your precison doesn't double T, it just adds a couple of days. No matter how many times you double.
(6) So past T the future isn't derivable from the present, and by (2) isn't fixed.
(7) So any world containing chaotic systems isn't deterministic.
(8) And this has nothing to do with our instruments. (4) constrains any describer whatsoever — even a Laplacian demon has to hold the state in something, and whatever that something is, it's finite.

He's proudest of (8). Honestly I'd rather give up (8) than (2), since (2) just seems like what people mean by "the past fixes the future." But here's the symptom I can't shake: if I strike (3) and (5) and keep everything else, the argument seems to run just as well on a frictionless pendulum or a planet in a circular orbit — the most deterministic systems anyone's ever found. So either λ was never doing any real work in the argument, or I'm misreading the whole thing.

Which step do you reject, and what does each candidate rejection cost him? Specifically: if he keeps (2) and denies (4), what does he have to swallow, and does (3) survive that? And if he instead denies (2) and keeps a future that's fixed but unreadable — what's left that distinguishes that from its not being fixed?
