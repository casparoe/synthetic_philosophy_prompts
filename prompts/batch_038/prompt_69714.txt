You are a philosopher of science who has spent several years embedded in technical AI safety work and now advises early-career researchers on grant applications. I am a third-year philosophy PhD student with six months of funded time under a small fellowship that pairs humanities doctoral students with an AI safety team; my deliverable is a paper plus a workshop I will co-run with the technical staff. Please write the two-page research proposal I need to submit to the fellowship's mid-term review. It will be read both by philosophers and by engineers, so it has to be conceptually precise without requiring the reader to follow any mathematics.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I would like you to sharpen rather than merely decorate. The distinction people draw between alignment work and control work is that control — monitoring, tripwires, capability restrictions, sandboxing, sandbagging detection — is supposed to buy safety without assuming that the model is in fact aligned: you keep your guarantees even if the model is deceptively aligned, or retains some trained-in behavior that survived later attempts to train it out. But I have come to suspect that control is not assumption-free. Every control regime ultimately rests on evaluations, and an evaluation is a sampling procedure: you observe the model in a finite set of contexts and extrapolate a guarantee to deployment. That extrapolation is licensed only by something like a homogeneity premise — the deployed model behaves the way the evaluated model behaved. In statistical quality control this is precisely the premise that makes acceptance sampling work, and everyone there knows it collapses when defects are clustered rather than randomly distributed through the lot. My suspicion is that the analogy is not decorative: control's advertised independence from alignment is really independence from a *strong* alignment premise coupled with silent dependence on a *weak* one, and the interesting question is what the weakest usable version of that premise is, and which control measures require which version. I also think a further distinction is being run together, and I want the proposal to make it central: the sample can fail to represent deployment for adversarial reasons (the model behaves differently because it is being observed, or its dispositions shift once the stakes change) and for non-adversarial reasons (the deployment situations simply fall outside anything the evaluation covered). Those two failures have different epistemic status and call for different remedies, and I suspect that most arguments about whether control "works" are two sides talking past each other across exactly that divide.

Before writing the proposal, spend a few hundred words in brainstorming mode: sketch two or three candidate formulations of the research question that differ in substance and not merely in wording, and say which you would choose and why, including which is most likely to yield something a technical safety team could actually use. Then write the proposal itself with the following components: (a) a precise statement of the question, framed so that it has determinate possible answers rather than expressing a mood; (b) an account of why the existing positions — control-first skepticism about alignment, and the reply that control is naive because a sufficiently capable model will defeat any monitor — leave the question open rather than settling it; (c) the method, month by month across the six months, stating what I would concretely do (conceptual analysis, a taxonomy of control measures by the sampling premise each requires, worked examples, and what I would ask the technical staff for, such as access to how evaluations are actually designed and what they are claimed to license); (d) why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, in particular rather than going straight to formalizing a threat model or rather than running my own empirical evaluations; (e) what a successful result looks like and, just as importantly, what would count as the project failing — I want a failure condition a reviewer could check, not a shrug; (f) a realistic assessment of what could make this too large for six months and what I would cut first; and (g) a short paragraph anticipating the objection that this is alignment research wearing a control costume, and saying whether that objection misses or lands. Please do not include a bibliography, a reference list, or any named citations; I will add those myself, and I would much rather you refer to positions and arguments descriptively than guess at sources. Think carefully about the framing before drafting: the conceptual work here lies in finding a question that is both open and tractable, and I would rather have a narrow question convincingly argued for than a grand one merely asserted.
