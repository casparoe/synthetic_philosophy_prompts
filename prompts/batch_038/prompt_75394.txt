I am a second-year graduate student in a philosophy of mind program that shares a methods seminar with the vision science lab across the hall, and I have been asked to run the session on whether perception is cognitively penetrable. The problem I want to solve is not that the literature is unfamiliar to us — most of the room has read the same three or four review papers — but that two people can read the very same study and honestly report that it settles the question in opposite directions. My own supervisor's working view is that essentially every candidate effect in this literature dissolves into response bias, demand characteristics, or a memory distortion that was never perceptual in the first place; my view is that at least one or two of the paradigms survive all of that. What I want, therefore, is an instrument that makes disagreements like ours locatable: a decision tree that a reader walks down using only what a paper's methods and results sections tell them, arriving at a verdict about what kind of effect, if any, the study reports. The deliverable is the tree itself, not a survey of the debate, and the tree should be built for first-year students, several of whom have no vision science background, so every node needs a sentence saying what a positive and a negative answer would concretely look like in an experimental report.

Please give me six to eight branch nodes and seven to ten leaves. Every node must be a question about the design or about what the effect would have to consist in, phrased so that a reader could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to — nothing of the form "do you think perception is encapsulated?" The axes I want represented, at minimum, are these. First, whether the manipulation changes what participants say about a stimulus or what the stimulus looks like: does the effect appear in measures that do not require an explicit judgment about the property in question, such as adaptation aftereffects, rivalry dominance durations, perceptual matching as against identification, or action-guiding measures? Second, whether the effect survives controls for expectation and demand — for instance a condition in which the participant holds the same belief but the belief is rendered irrelevant or is disconfirmed, or in which the inducing manipulation is never noticed as such. Third, whether what is doing the work is the content of the cognitive state or merely its arousal, valence, or salience: that is, whether the penetrator is semantically related to the perceptual content rather than being a general alerting condition, which I suspect is where a great many claimed effects quietly fall out. Fourth, whether the whole effect could be explained by attention reallocating which inputs reach a processing stage that remains sealed off from belief — the familiar move of treating selection of input as something other than penetration — and what could tell that apart from penetration proper. Fifth, at what level the penetrated processing sits: low-level feature encoding such as color, orientation, size, or slant; object identification and categorization; or multimodal scene understanding. Sixth, whether the resulting experience is less accurate than it would otherwise be, equally accurate, or more accurate. Among the leaves I want: no real effect, or artifact only; a real effect that is post-perceptual, residing in judgment, memory, or decision; attentional gating without penetration; penetration of low-level feature encoding; penetration confined to high-level categorical perception; a predictive-processing leaf on which the penetration–encapsulation contrast is not carving anything real; and, if you can separate them, penetration that is real but epistemically harmless versus real and epistemically corrosive. If you judge that last contrast to belong on a second tree rather than this one, say so and explain why rather than forcing it into a leaf.

Three further things. First, flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and for each say either what evidence would separate them or why you think the distinction is not worth drawing — my suspicion is that the attentional-gating leaf and the low-level-penetration leaf are in danger of collapsing, given that endogenous attention is itself under cognitive control, and I would rather see that confronted than hidden. Second, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: set out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, locate the actually defended positions in that space, and identify at least three unoccupied combinations with a one-line diagnosis of each — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. One cell I particularly want diagnosed is a top-down effect that systematically improves accuracy rather than degrading it, which strikes me as either empty or oddly under-theorized, since memory-driven color effects of a constancy-like sort are often defended precisely on the grounds that they make perception more veridical. Third, apply the finished tree to four paradigms I will describe in my own words: participants judge a disc of exactly matched chromaticity as more saturated when it is shaped like a familiar fruit than when it is shaped neutrally; hearing or reading a category label makes a heavily degraded image of an animal from that category easier to detect than an equally degraded image of something else; a bistable figure that can be read either as a letter or as a numeral is reported according to which reading is currently being rewarded, with the reward contingency learned rather than instructed; and estimates of the slant of a hill, or of distance to a target, shift with bodily state such as carrying a load or with how desirable the target is. For each of the four, tell me which leaf it lands on, which single node does the decisive work, and at which node my supervisor and I actually part company — because I suspect it is not the same node in all four cases. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reference list, and do not invent one: describe studies and positions in your own words, name individual researchers only where you are confident you have them right, and end with a brief assessment of what the shape of the tree implies about whether this is an empirical dispute at all or a dispute about what the word perception is being asked to cover.
