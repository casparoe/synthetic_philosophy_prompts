A lawyer friend writes a monthly column on language and the law, and last month's piece on political spin contained this gloss of the philosophical debate:

"Philosophers define a lie as an assertion that is false and known by the speaker to be false. So when a witness or a politician answers with something literally true but calculated to point the listener toward a false conclusion, that is misleading rather than lying, and the difference is verbal rather than moral. Nothing false has been said, so nobody has been deceived about the facts; the listener has simply drawn the wrong inference on his own. That is why the law keeps the two apart — the Supreme Court has held that a literally true but unresponsive answer under oath cannot be perjury — and why Kant was so fixated on the literal words that he would rather let a murderer find his victim than tell a lie. Misleading is a victimless technicality; lying is the real wrong."

I am inclined to think a calculated literal truth is just as culpable as an outright falsehood, so I would like this summary to be wrong, but I cannot say where. Please audit it claim by claim: which parts a philosopher working on this debate would accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which are mistaken. In particular, is the definition offered the one actually in circulation, and is it even agreed that lying requires the assertion to be false? Does saying nothing false rule out deceiving someone? And do the legal point and the Kant point support the moral conclusion drawn from them, or does the argument quietly slide from what a word means to how bad an act is? Finally, set out what the strongest argument for a genuine moral difference between lying and misleading looks like once the summary is corrected, and test it against a case of his own kind: a reference letter for a job candidate that mentions only her punctuality and her legible handwriting.
