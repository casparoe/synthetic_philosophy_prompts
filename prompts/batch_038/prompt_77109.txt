Taking a social choice seminar this term and we just did Sen's impossibility result — the one with the prude and the lewd guy and the single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. Two weeks later discussion drifted into a right-to-be-forgotten style proposal (a legal right to make platforms delete material about you), and this one guy in the seminar — smart, zero chill — cut across the whole thing with basically: "that's already settled, there's no such thing as a Paretian liberal. Individual rights and Pareto efficiency are jointly impossible, it's a theorem, so any deletion right makes someone worse off and that's the end of it." Half the room nodded and we moved on. I left feeling like something had been slipped past us but I cant say what.

So what are the different precise claims that "there's no such thing as a Paretian liberal" could be standing for? I want a menu, not an essay that picks one reading and runs with it. For each: (i) what liberty/rights amounts to on that reading — decisiveness over some pair of social states, a protected choice, a constraint ruling outcomes off the table, something else; (ii) which Pareto condition is doing the work — weak, strong, indifference included or not, and whether it's a condition on the social ordering or a claim about realized outcomes; (iii) whether the reading is a platitude, a genuine theorem, or just false; and (iv) whether it licenses the move he made. Order them roughly from most trivial to most radical.

Two things I especially want pressed on. First, how much of the impossibility comes from allowing every preference profile — i.e. from people being permitted to have meddlesome preferences about what others do in their own private sphere — and how much of the result survives if you restrict that. Second, whether modelling a right as "person i is decisive over the pair (x, y)" is really the same thing as modelling it as "certain outcomes are not available for the social ranking to pick, whatever anyone prefers," and whether the contradiction goes through on the second version.

Then separately: what would have to be true on top of Sen's theorem to get to "a deletion right makes some third party worse off, therefore it's ruled out"? Be explicit about the gap — I think he skipped at least two or three distinct steps and I want them named.

And finally, which reading do you reckon he was actually invoking, versus which one a room full of people hears when that slogan gets dropped into a policy argument?
