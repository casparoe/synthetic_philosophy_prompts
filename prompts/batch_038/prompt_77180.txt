You are a senior philosopher of physics who has written on the twentieth-century quarrel between working physicists and the philosophy of science, and who is equally at home with the technical content of quantum foundations and with the dialectics of that quarrel. Please think carefully and begin with an explicit brainstorming stage before committing to any conclusion.

I am a doctoral student assembling a chapter on the "physics versus philosophy" polemic — Weinberg's essay "Against Philosophy," Hawking and Mlodinow's opening declaration in The Grand Design (2010) that philosophy has died and left physics to carry on alone, the ornithology comparison usually attributed to Feynman. Rather than treat Weinberg's stance as a slogan, I want to reconstruct it as a track record of verdicts and test it on a case he never ruled on. The verdicts I have extracted, in my own paraphrase except where quoted:

1. Philosophy of science is at best "a pleasing gloss on the history and discoveries of science"; it cannot tell working physicists how to proceed or what they are likely to find.
2. Physicists nonetheless carry a working philosophy — for most, "a rough-and-ready realism" — but it is learned from doing research, rarely from philosophers.
3. Philosophers have occasionally benefited physics, "but generally in a negative fashion—by protecting them from the preconceptions of other philosophers."
4. The Copenhagen interpretation is a philosophical straitjacket that has blocked the measurement problem for decades; Everett-style realism about the universal wavefunction deserves serious attention.
5. Aesthetic criteria — beauty, symmetry, simplicity — are indispensable to theory construction, yet cannot be grounded from above: physicists are "more like hounds than hawks."
6. Anthropic and landscape reasoning counts as legitimate physics despite the absence of any near-term decisive test.
7. Reductionism he defended argumentatively against philosophical critics rather than ruling their objections out of bounds.

The unadjudicated case: QBism. A philosopher of physics submits to a mainstream physics review venue a defense of QBism as a resolution of the measurement problem. It denies that the quantum state is an objective physical condition, treats it as an agent's personal degrees of belief, borrows openly from Jamesian pragmatism, promises no new predictions, but claims to dissolve the paradoxes — and it grew out of work in quantum information by practicing physicists, not out of metaphysics.

Deliver: (a) a brainstorm listing at least three genuinely distinct rules that fit all seven verdicts, not relabelings of one another; (b) each rule's predicted verdict on the QBism manuscript and why; (c) your single best prediction, with a confidence level and an account of which verdicts carry the most weight; (d) one further case, real or constructed, that would most cleanly separate your top two rules, with the expected split; and (e) any verdict above you suspect I have mischaracterized. Please do not supply citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — reason from the positions as I have summarized them, and say so if a rule you propose depends on a reading of Weinberg that my summary cannot support.
