I have been a volunteer on a restorative-justice circle at our community college for three years, and for most of that time the circle has been chaired by a woman I'll call Miriam, who writes up every ruling in a shared log. One recurring question in her write-ups is whether a person who comes forward demanding that someone be held accountable is entitled to lead that process — she calls it having "standing to speak," and she distinguishes it sharply from whether the accusation is true. I'm trying to work out how she'll rule on a case coming to us Thursday, partly because I have a vote and want to be consistent with our own record rather than with my gut. Here are her rulings as I have them logged:

1. Two students in the same study group both submitted essays written by the same paid tutor. The one who came forward first admitted her own use of the tutor without offering excuses and asked that she and the other student be held to account together. Miriam ruled that she retained standing and could participate fully, and the log says the reason was that she had not placed herself above the rule she was invoking.
2. A professor pressed hardest for censure of a student who had bought a term paper. It later emerged that the professor had, years earlier, put his name on a chapter substantially drafted by a postdoc. Ruling: he could give information as a witness, but he could not lead the process or sign the accountability agreement.
3. A groundskeeper who was habitually late himself complained about a colleague's lateness. Ruling: his complaint about the lateness as such was set aside, but he was still permitted to describe how the absences had affected the shared rota. The log is explicit that the loss was limited to the fault he shared.
4. A parent with no child enrolled and no connection to anyone involved demanded censure of a student for behaviour at a private off-campus gathering that harmed nobody in the college community. Ruling: the circle declined to convene; Miriam wrote that it was "not ours to hear."
5. A resident adviser who had never met the accused student personally raised a matter that occurred on the floor she was assigned to. Ruling: standing granted, on the ground that the role, not the acquaintance, was what mattered.
6. A longtime volunteer, himself impeccably reliable, blamed a student for breaking a commitment without ever having asked the student for an account. It turned out the student had been at a parent's bedside. Ruling: standing lost. I should flag that the write-up here is ambiguous: in one passage Miriam says he condemned what he had not bothered to understand, and in another she says he had spoken for a community he had not consulted — I have never been sure which was doing the work.
7. An alumnus and donor, blameless and well informed on the matter, dressed down a student in a way that made plain he did not regard the student as his equal, and refused the student's request to reply. Ruling: standing lost notwithstanding his blamelessness.

Thursday's complainant is a faculty member, Dr. Fischer, who sits on the committee that awards a first-year writing prize. She read the winning essay, became convinced that most of it was produced by a generative writing tool, and confirmed her suspicion by reading an email that was not addressed to her, in which the student said as much to a friend. She has come to us asking that the student be held to account and that the prize be withdrawn. Three features make her case unlike anything in our log. First, she is personally beyond reproach on the specific charge: she has never used ghostwriters or text-generating tools for her own work, she has said in writing that she would accept the same censure if the standard were applied to her, and she has volunteered her own drafts for inspection. Second, her published work over many years has depended on unnamed undergraduate research assistants who drafted passages she then rewrote and published under her own name — a practice she considers entirely ordinary and professional, and one she does not regard as comparable to anything the student did. Third, she has not spoken to the student, and we have not established whether the course in which the essay was assigned permitted tool use; her standing to speak about the prize is also arguably different from her standing to speak about the student's honesty, since the essay was written for someone else's class.

What I want from you is a prediction and a recommendation. Which general principles are jointly consistent with rulings 1 through 7 — noting that at least one familiar principle looks like it fits the early cases but is refuted by ruling 1, and that ruling 6 is compatible with more than one reading? State each surviving principle, say what it implies about Dr. Fischer, and pay attention to whether it implies a flat yes, a flat no, or a split ruling in which she may speak to one issue but not another. Then give me your best single guess about Miriam's verdict, with the reasons you would weigh one principle over the others given the wording of the log, and identify the closest analog among the seven precedents and where the analogy strains. Tell me also what our circle should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, and be explicit about which principle we would thereby be committing to and which we would be disowning — I suspect that either choice silently settles a question we have never voted on. Finally, describe one further hypothetical case, of the kind Miriam might be presented with, that would discriminate between the principles you have identified better than Thursday's case does. You don't need to cite any literature or name particular philosophers' works; if a principle here matches a distinction you already know, say so in a clause and move on, because what I need is the reasoning about our own record.
