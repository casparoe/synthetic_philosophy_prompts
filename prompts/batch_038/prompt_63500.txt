So, three weeks out I'm giving a talk at a local aesthetics reading group — small room, maybe fifteen people: two faculty who work in the analytic aesthetics tradition (Kant, Hume on taste, the usual), one Russian-lit person who I'm told has read What Is Art? in the original and does not enjoy watching amateurs paraphrase it, a sociologist of culture who does Bourdieu-flavored distinction work, and the rest grad students of mixed and mostly undeclared commitments. My thesis is deliberately provocative and I'd rather find out where it bleeds from you now than in the room. I've been sitting on this since a long LW-style argument with a friend about whether Goodhart's law applies to art, so treat it as a steelman request, not a defense request.

Thesis sketch. Everybody treats Tolstoy's infection criterion as the embarrassing part of the 1897 book — the crank's test that licenses him to denounce Shakespeare, Wagner, Beethoven and half his own output. I think that's backwards, and the machinery is in chapter 15: infection is the "one indubitable indication distinguishing real art from its counterfeit," the degree of infectiousness is "the sole measure of excellence in art," and the three conditions he gives for it are individuality of the feeling, clearness of transmission, and sincerity — which he says is the most important, and which is "always complied with in peasant art" but "almost entirely absent from our upper-class art, which is continually produced by artists actuated by personal aims of covetousness or vanity." Notice what sincerity actually is: a claim about the producer's optimization target. He spells out the failure mode — once the receiver senses the author "is doing it for him, the receiver," then "the most individual and the newest feelings and the cleverest technique not only fail to produce any infection but actually repel." That's a capture story about a signalling channel whose audience became its own in-group of connoisseurs, academies and conservatoires, which he says teach people "how to counterfeit art": the metric keeps improving after the payload stops being transmitted. Chapter 14 is basically a base-rate argument, and a good one — of the million-plus works a year, "scarcely one in a hundred thousand proceeds from an emotion felt by its author," and worse, "the counterfeit is often more effective than the real, and its subject more interesting," i.e. the counterfeits win on every proxy the in-group actually measures. So: infection = channel fidelity, counterfeit = proxy-hacking, and the only part of the theory that's really a content filter is the "religious perception of the age" business in chapter 16, which appraises the feelings transmitted rather than the transmission itself. My move is to amputate that part, keep the channel theory, and swap in a weaker operational test — a work transmits to the degree it lands on receivers outside the producer's own status group, without preparation — and then argue his own surviving examples (Uncle Tom's Cabin, Adam Bede, Les Misérables, the Dickens novels he allows, the story of Joseph, that Bach violin aria and the Chopin nocturne) pass it while King Lear and late Wagner fail. I'll say out loud in the talk that this is reconstructive surgery and not exegesis, but I know that disclaimer is also exactly the motte-and-bailey hinge.
