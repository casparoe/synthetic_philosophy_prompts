I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and once a year I also mark the take-homes for a science-writing MA module on evolution — I'm the "real world" marker, not the biologist, which is normally fine but has landed me in a spot this term. One answer I have read three times and I still cant tell whether it's strong work with a couple of rough edges or whether there's a load-bearing crack running through the middle that I'm sailing right past. I don't want to guess, and I don't want to grade it up just because it reads confidently.

The assignment I set was:

"A magazine essay argues that the units-of-selection debate was settled in the 1970s. Genes are the only things that get copied; bodies are throwaway containers that carry them; therefore any talk of group selection, or of the individual organism as a unit of selection, is at best a loose way of talking about genes. Assess this argument. Say explicitly whether 'unit of selection', 'replicator' and 'vehicle' pick out the same thing, and whether recent multilevel-selection modelling reopens the debate. 800 words max."

Here's the answer, verbatim apart from a few spelling fixes:

---

The essay is half right, and the half it gets right is the half it doesn't actually need.

Start with what's solid. Genes get copied; bodies don't. An organism is a one-off combination assembled out of two other combinations, and it gets taken apart at meiosis before it can pass the whole thing on, so nothing at the level of the body persists across generations in the sense that matters. A gene, by contrast, can sit in an unbroken line of copies for millions of years. That asymmetry is real, and it's why Dawkins split the replicator — the thing that gets copied — from the vehicle, or as he later renamed it, the interactor: the thing whose properties decide which copies get made.

It's also fair to say the old good-of-the-species stories were bad biology. A population of birds that lays fewer eggs for the sake of the branch isn't stable, because a single mutant who lays more takes the whole branch, and then the next, and the restraint disappears. Whatever you call that, it isn't an explanation.

But the essay's conclusion doesn't come out of those premises. Selection, in the ordinary sense of some things surviving and reproducing more than others, happens to vehicles. It's the deer that gets eaten, not the deer's alleles. What flows down the generations is gene-level; what gets filtered along the way is body-level. So the claim that only genes are units of selection quietly does two different jobs with one word. If the essay were right, the replicator/vehicle distinction would have no content at all — you'd only ever need one of the two terms.

The same swap infects the standard recipe for selection: variation, heritability, differential success. Nothing in that list mentions a level, which is why you can apply it to genes, to cells inside a body, to organisms, and in principle to groups without changing a word. Groups usually fail the heritability clause, since a herd doesn't bud off daughter herds that resemble it. But usually isn't never.

Which brings me to the multilevel modelling, and here the essay is simply out of date. There are two kinds of case, MLS1 and MLS2, and the difference between them is bookkeeping rather than biology. In both, what matters is whether the between-group part of the variance does more work than the within-group part; whether the groups themselves happen to reproduce is a choice the modeller makes when she writes the equations down. So any trait that spreads because of what happens between groups counts as group selection, and the essay's insistence that only genes count is just an unfashionable way of describing the same arithmetic.

The clincher is that kin selection and group selection turn out to be intertranslatable — you can rewrite either as the other inside the same models. When two descriptions convert into each other at will, the choice between them isn't a matter of fact. There's no answer to which level selection really acts at, only a choice of notation.

So: the essay is wrong that the debate was settled, and right that the cumulative record of evolution is written in genes. Both of those can be true.
