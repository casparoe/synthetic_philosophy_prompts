I ran a neurophysiology lab for 35 years and retired three years ago, and I've been reading philosophy full time since — badly, but enthusiastically. Right now I'm stuck on moral responsibility, and specifically on the fact that the two Strawsons disagree with each other, which I find both funny and deeply confusing.

So: play P. F. Strawson, late in life, who has just read his son Galen's argument that nobody can be morally responsible because you can't be responsible for the way you are, and being responsible for what you do would require being responsible for the way you are, and nobody is causa sui. Answer me in the first person as yourself. You may use anything you'd plausibly have read up to that point — Kant, Hume, Butler, your own work, the whole ordinary-language toolkit — but nothing from after your death, no fMRI pictures, no experimental-philosophy surveys, and please don't drop references or cite chapter and verse like you're padding a footnote; if you need a fact about my science, just ask me for it the way you would in a seminar. Character-wise: courteous, dry, and utterly intolerant of muddled metaphysics. You may concede small points to Galen — that his regress is valid, that the demand for ultimate origination is a real demand people actually feel, that determinism is beside the point — but never the main one, which as I read you is that the practice of holding each other responsible never rested on any such condition and so cannot be shown to lack one. Don't play the proud father, don't get sentimental, don't strawman him; I want the argument. Stay in character until I say stop.

Keep each reply to two short paragraphs so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and end every reply with one line that begins Bottom line: followed by exactly one word — answered, deflected, or conceded — and a single sentence saying why. That line can be out of character; everything above it stays in.

Here's my opening move. Your essay tells us we modify or suspend our reactive attitudes in the abnormal cases: the small child, the compulsive, the man under unbearable strain, the one with the tumour pressing on his frontal cortex (I had a case like that in '79 and I've never shaken it). But that looks like a sliding scale, and Galen's move is simply to say the scale runs all the way down — every adult has a tumour, only a diffuse one: an upbrining nobody chose and a genome nobody chose. And in my old line of work we are getting close to being able to print the file out. Not a metaphor: an actual dossier of the developmental inputs to a person's dispositions, thick as a phone book, handed over at trial. So suppose I hand you that file for a man who quietly embezzled from his own brother. Do you resent him? If you say yes, on what ground — the file leaves nowhere for the buck to stop, which was his whole point. If you say no, you have just adopted the objective attitude toward the entire species, which you yourself said would mean the death of everything that makes human relationships human. Which is it?
