I lead a small team that builds internal developer tooling, and a large share of my time goes into test infrastructure — mutation testing in particular. I have no formal philosophy background beyond a couple of half-read books, so please treat what follows as an amateur's hypothesis that needs to be argued properly rather than validated.

The hypothesis, stated in my own vocabulary first. Mutation testing works by taking a program under test and mechanically generating variants of it — mutants — through small syntactic perturbations: flip a comparison operator, negate a branch condition, change a constant, shift a loop bound by one. You then run the existing test suite against each mutant. A mutant is killed if at least one test fails, and it survives if the entire suite still passes. A surviving mutant means the suite fails to discriminate the correct program from that perturbed one. The mutation score — killed over killed plus surviving, excluding equivalent mutants, which are variants that are semantically identical to the original and therefore unkillable in principle — is treated as a far better measure of test quality than coverage, because a suite can execute every line and still be nearly worthless.

The parallel that will not leave me alone: a Gettiered believer is a suite that passes for the wrong reason. Smith's belief comes out true, and the process that produced it did in some sense run and report a pass, but that same process would also have reported a pass in nearby variants of the situation in which the belief is false. On the mutation-testing picture, that is not a defect in the pass/fail output at all; it is a defect in the discriminative power of the harness — a quantitative property, measured over a neighborhood of perturbations, that a single binary verdict cannot express.

What I want is an argument for the following position: that "justified" in the justified-true-belief analysis is being used as a stand-in for something a binary predicate over belief-states cannot capture, namely the error-discriminating power of the belief-forming process across the relevant neighborhood of ways the situation might have been slightly different; and that the correct repair to the Gettier problem is therefore to replace the binary justification condition with a graded one defined over that neighborhood, rather than to bolt on a no-false-lemma clause or to retreat to a causal or virtue-theoretic account.

Please make it a genuine argument and not a restatement of my analogy. Specifically:

- Lay out the mapping between the components explicitly — belief, truth, the process, the mutant set, the score, the equivalent mutant — and say both where it holds and where it visibly strains.
- Show whether the view delivers the right verdicts on the standard cases: the two applicants and the coins in the pocket, the Ford-or-Barcelona disjunction, the county full of barn façades with one real barn, and the clock that stopped exactly twenty-four hours earlier. If it gives a wrong or deeply unintuitive verdict on any of them, say so plainly; I would rather learn that than hear a tidy story.
- Engage the two objections I suspect are strongest: that a graded justification turns knowledge into a matter of degree and so cannot respect the flat, unqualified denial that Gettiered subjects know anything; and that selecting which perturbations count as relevant nearby variants simply reintroduces the generality problem that already plagues process reliabilism, leaving nothing gained over a sensitivity or safety condition wearing engineering clothes.
- If what I am describing already has a standard name in epistemology, give me the name and a one-sentence definition. I am not asking for sources and I would rather you not guess at them.
