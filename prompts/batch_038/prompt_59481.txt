I'm working on a long feature about end-of-life care in this country, and for the past few weeks I've been riding along with a home hospice team in the Midwest. The institutional machinery has struck me as philosophically peculiar in ways my interviews haven't been able to articulate. As I understand it, admission to the Medicare hospice benefit requires that a patient be certified — by two physicians, and renewed every benefit period — as having a prognosis of six months or less if the illness runs its normal course, and that the patient give up treatment directed at curing the terminal illness. The provider is then paid a flat per-diem rate rather than for services rendered. If the patient stabilizes, or improves, or simply decides to go back to treatment, the hospice discharges them alive — a category the program tracks, and which now accounts for something close to a fifth of all hospice discharges. Meanwhile the standard instrument for measuring whether the care is working, the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System, consists of nine items rated from zero to ten: pain, tiredness, drowsiness, nausea, appetite, breathlessness, depression, anxiety, and one labeled well-being. Every item is scored so that zero is the best possible answer, and they are summed into a total symptom burden whose floor is also zero. Music, a chaplain's long conversation, a dog brought in from home, a last glass of whiskey, a drive to the lake — these are funded largely by the hospice's foundation and its volunteers, and they appear in the paperwork as amenities rather than as outcomes.

The label I keep reaching for, without being sure I'm entitled to it, is tranquilism: the view that what ultimately matters is the absence of suffering and of craving, and that once suffering is gone there is nothing further to be added — the good, on this picture, has a ceiling, a saturation point exactly at zero. It's an old idea wearing new clothes, as I gather: Epicurus's claim that once pain is removed pleasure cannot be increased in magnitude, only varied, and the Buddhist diagnosis of craving as the origin of suffering, with cessation rather than fulfillment as the goal. So my question is what the practice above presupposes, and whether it presupposes it consistently. Does an institution whose success measure bottoms out at zero, and whose extras are voluntary, embody something like tranquilism at the level of design — or does it merely look that way because distress is the tractable part of the problem? I'm particularly bothered by the six-month gate, which makes eligibility turn not on how much a person is suffering but on how soon they are expected to die: on its face, a person with appalling, refractory suffering and two years to live is not entitled to what a person with no symptoms and five months is. Is the practice therefore not really about suffering at all, but about a judgment that life's value stops outweighing something at a fixed horizon? And what theory of value renders the live-discharge rule coherent rather than perverse — the sicker you are, the more comfort you're owed; the better you get, the less?

What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, keeping strictly apart what the practice requires from what it merely fits comfortably. Which rival accounts of well-being does it quietly rule out, and which could license it just as well — negative hedonism, a two-level or lexically prior view on which relieving suffering trumps but does not exhaust the good, a desire-fulfillment view, a capability or flourishing view, or the World Health Organization's insistence that health is not to be defined as the mere absence of disease? Where do the parts of the practice presuppose incompatible things? Then tell me what would have to change concretely — the eligibility criteria, the payment model, the assessment instrument — if we substituted a view on which positive goods admit of no ceiling, and, in the other direction, what would change if we took tranquilism entirely at its word: would a hospice then have to regard a dying patient's loss of appetite for life not as a symptom on a scale but as an achievement? There is one further wrinkle I can't reconcile. These units are famously permissive in ways the rest of medicine is not: patients smoke, drink, eat whatever they want, keep their pets, decline their medications. That looks like the deliberate indulgence of craving inside an institution devoted to the removal of suffering, as though the practice were tranquilist about distress and anti-tranquilist about desire. Can any single theory of value hold both halves together, or is this the point at which the honest answer is that the practice is philosophically underdetermined? Finally, since I'll have to write around them: what are the two or three things this analysis rules out as readings — the sermon about a death-denying culture, the gotcha about hospice as a rationing scheme — and what does the practice know about the value of a life that those framings, and perhaps the theory itself, miss?
