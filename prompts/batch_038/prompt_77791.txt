You are a moral philosopher whose specialty is the structure of normative ethics — obligations, permissions, and the space above and below them — and you are advising a working committee rather than writing for a journal. Think carefully, and before you settle on the list, sketch briefly the failure modes you are trying to rule out: the characteristic ways the phrase "beyond the call of duty" gets misused in institutional settings. Let that diagnostic step drive the requirements you go on to state.

Here is the situation. I work with a hospital ethics group that is redrafting the written material given to prospective living kidney donors. The current draft tells them that altruistic, nondirected donation is morally admirable but beyond the call of duty, and adds that no one could blame them for not donating. A patient-advocacy colleague has raised two objections. First, she suspects the phrase is doing no real normative work at all — that here "supererogatory" is a polite synonym for "optional," and that a consistent consequentialist ethics denies there is any such category, so we are borrowing comfort from a picture of morality we could not defend if asked. Second, she thinks the phrase actively misleads: the same draft says that donating to a relative is simply what families do, which implies that whether an act lies beyond the call of duty shifts with the donor's relationship to the recipient, and she regards that relativity as a bug we have smuggled in as a feature.

Before I take either objection further, I want to know what I should be demanding of the concept in the first place. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of supererogation would have to deliver in order to be usable in a document like this one. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it and say explicitly whether you regard it as non-negotiable for our purpose or as a preference of one camp in the literature. The requirements are the deliverable, so weight everything else accordingly, but be sure to cover:

- The acts the account must classify correctly, including the awkward ones: the firefighter who goes back into a burning building; directed donation to a sibling; a small, recurring, low-cost kindness to a stranger; a risky rescue undertaken at great cost whose intended victim dies anyway; an act that would be obligatory if everyone performed it but is optional when only one person does; and, if your account generates the mirror category at all, acts that are bad yet not forbidden.
- The distinctions it may not collapse. At minimum: good-but-not-required versus required-but-excusable; praise for performing versus blame for omitting; dependence on cost or risk to the agent versus dependence on benefit to the recipient; the difference between an act's being admirable overall and its being admirable in one respect while violating a duty in another; and the difference between an act's being beyond the call of duty and its being beyond the call of any duty the agent currently has, given the roles and commitments she has already taken on.
- The puzzles it must either solve or explain away. In particular: how it can be that performing an act earns praise while omitting it earns no blame, given that praise ordinarily seems reserved for going past what is owed; what happens to the category as the cost to the agent falls toward zero — whether there is a threshold, whether the threshold is vague, and whether vagueness here is tolerable or evidence that the category tracks nothing real; and whether the account can avoid turning every merely permissible good deed into a supererogatory one, thereby inflating the category until it stops marking anything.
- The structural commitments the account cannot hide. Whether supererogation presupposes some limit on how much morality may demand of an agent, and if so, what an institution is implicitly endorsing when it invokes the category while wishing to stay neutral among consequentialist, contractualist, and pluralist accounts of the right; and whether the category can be made to survive the observation that it is most convenient for those with the least to give.
