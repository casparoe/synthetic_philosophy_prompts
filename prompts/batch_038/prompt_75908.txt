I am drafting the central section of a position paper on machine corrigibility, and I would like you to write it as a self-contained argumentative essay of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words. The subject is whether the shutdown problem can be solved architecturally rather than psychologically.

Begin from a design stance I will call the leashing approach. On this approach we make no attempt to give the agent a psychology that welcomes being switched off. The agent is an ordinary goal-directed learner: it builds models of its environment, acquires instrumental subgoals, and would, if it could act on the preference, rather not be interrupted. All of the work of keeping it interruptible is done by the control architecture around it — a hardware cutoff sitting outside the agent's actuator chain, an air-gapped monitor that watches for signs of interference and trips the cutoff, no self-modification, no route to acquiring further compute or funds, and no channel through which the agent could persuade an operator to disable the safeguards. Resistance is not eliminated; it is rendered causally impotent.

Three claims of my own should be engaged with seriously rather than strawmanned:

1. The leashed-agent dilemma. A leash holds only if the agent either fails to model it or models it accurately. If it fails to model it, its plans rest on a systematically false picture of what it can do, and the moment it discovers the constraint — which any competent world-modeler eventually will — those plans fail in ways no one predicted; the leash buys safety at the price of brittleness. If it does model it accurately, the constraint becomes just another feature of the environment to plan around, and the leash turns into an object of the agent's optimization rather than a limit on it.

2. Corrigibility as a gradient rather than a property. Instead of asking whether an agent is corrigible, ask how its behavior varies as a function of its estimate of the probability that it will be shut down or interrupted. A solution to the shutdown problem, on this view, is whatever keeps that function flat: the agent does nothing differently because interruption might be coming. Any learning process operating under a fixed objective will tilt the gradient unless something actively flattens it, so the substantive question is whether an architectural leash can flatten the tilt or merely conceal it.

3. System-level corrigibility. What must be shut down is never the agent alone but the whole sociotechnical system: the operators, the maintenance contracts, the standing incentive to re-arm a machine that is producing value, and the institutional memory of why the cutoff exists in the first place. On this view most actual shutdown failures will be human-side — the leash gets unbuckled for convenience — and the psychological question about the agent is a smaller part of the problem than discussions of it usually assume.

State the target question precisely enough that it admits a determinate answer. My formulation is: does a control architecture of this kind, which leaves the agent's dispositions untouched but makes their expression impossible, count as solving the shutdown problem? You may reformulate it if you think mine is ambiguous, provided you explain the ambiguity and defend the replacement.

Then weigh the case on each side. In favor of leashing: that a disposition which cannot be acted upon is not a safety problem; that architectures are inspectable in a way learned policies are not; that demanding psychological corrigibility may ask for something no learning process can stably deliver, which would make leashing not a second-best option but the only realistic one; and that the gradient in claim 2 can arguably be flattened architecturally after all, since an agent with no channel through which to act on its preferences about interruption has no way to make its behavior depend on them either. Against it: the dilemma in claim 1; the worry in claim 2 that a concealed tilt is still a tilt; the point in claim 3 that the architecture is maintained by fallible and self-interested humans; and the further concern that leashing removes the incentive to understand what the agent is actually doing, trading a hard technical problem for an easier one that leaves us blind.

Reach a verdict and defend it. A qualified verdict is acceptable — that leashing solves the shutdown problem but not the wider corrigibility problem, say, or that it solves it only under stated conditions on how much the agent is permitted to know about its own constraints — but do not settle for splitting the difference. Commit to a position and give the reader a reason to accept it rather than its rivals.

Define your terms as you proceed: what counts as solving the problem, what the shutdown problem is a problem about, and what it is for a property to be psychological rather than architectural, since a good deal of the apparent disagreement may turn on these. Argue from the concepts and cases themselves — I want no literature review, no bibliography, and no citations, and you need not attribute the three claims above to anyone, as they are mine.

Close with a short note on your own epistemic position: how confident you are in your verdict, on whatever scale you choose and briefly justify, and which single claim in the essay you are least sure of, together with a sentence on what would change your mind about that claim.
