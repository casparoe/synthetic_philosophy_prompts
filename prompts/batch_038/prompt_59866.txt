I'm a third-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and the chapter I'm stuck on is about Kepler's astrology — specifically how he tries to reform it in the *Tertius Interveniens* (1610, where he steps in as the third party between Roeslin and the physician Feselius) and then again in the *Harmony of the World*. Setup for the chapter: Kepler throws out most of what the working astrologers of his day actually did — the twelve houses, planetary rulerships over signs and body parts, most individual prognostication — and keeps essentially just the aspects, the angular relations between the light rays of two planets, which he then re-derives from the polygons you can construct with ruler and compass. That's how he ends up licensing new ones like the quintile, the biquintile and the sesquiquadrate. His causal story, as I read it, is that the sublunar world and the earth in particular have a kind of perceptive faculty that senses these angles and reacts to them, and that human souls are tuned the same way — so the influence is geometrical rather than a mechanical push-pull. I've also got a section on the awkward fact that he kept casting nativities for money, including for Wallenstein, and on that little line everyone attaches to his name about astronomy starving unless her silly daughter astrology earns the bread. Please don't get hung up on whether that saying is his in those words; it's just flavor for how people talk about him.

At a colloquium last month a visiting speaker who was pushing the "one continuous rational project" reading of Kepler offered this analogy: Kepler's reformed astrology is like a physician who has given up humoral theory but keeps the bedside routines the humors originally justified — seasonal bloodletting, certain diet rules — because those routines were calibrated on generations of case observation and kept working after the theory died. He used it to argue three things at once: (a) Kepler wasn't two-minded or moonlighting, the astrology was a live research program continuous with the astronomy; (b) it's anachronistic to treat the practice as a lapse in rationality just because the framework is now gone; and (c) his dropping of houses and rulerships while keeping the aspects was a data-driven theory choice, exactly what the good physician does when he strips out the humors and keeps the parts that predict.
