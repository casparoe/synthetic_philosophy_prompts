I am a doctoral student in cognitive science, and I run learning simulations that bear on the structure-dependence argument for linguistic nativism. My setup is this. A sequence model is trained by maximum likelihood on positive examples only — declaratives such as The man who is tall is in the room, together with a large number of simpler yes/no questions — and is then probed on the crucial case it has never encountered, to determine whether it generalizes to Is the man who is tall in the room? rather than to the linear-order alternative *Is the man who tall is in the room? A control condition trains the same architecture on the same positive examples plus a small set of explicit contrastive pairs, in which the linear-order variants are marked as ill-formed.

At a recent lab meeting, a senior colleague dismissed both conditions in a single breath with the slogan "there's no negative evidence." His remark appeared to do two jobs at once: it ruled the control condition inadmissible as a model of the child's epistemic situation, and it supported the further claim that no learner confined to distributional information over positive data could arrive at the structure-dependent rule, so that rule must be built in rather than learned. I do not think those two jobs can be discharged by one sentence, but I want the sentence taken apart carefully before I answer him.

Please lay out, as an organized menu rather than as a single preferred explication, the distinct precise claims that "there is no negative evidence" could be making. For each reading in the menu, give me:

1. The kind of claim it is — for instance, a descriptive claim about what caregivers in fact say, a claim about what is in principle recoverable from the input even if never stated, a formal claim about the identifiability of some language class, or a claim about the actual developmental trajectory of a particular learner.
2. Its epistemic status on your assessment: platitude, empirically well supported, empirically shaky, plainly false, or question-begging in this particular argumentative setting — together with the reason for the verdict.
3. Whether, if true, it would in fact support either of the two jobs the slogan was doing in my colleague's mouth, or neither.

In constructing the menu, please attend to distinctions I suspect are being collapsed: evidence against a particular utterance as opposed to evidence against a candidate rule or hypothesis, and the consequent problem that correcting a single string need not identify which generalization is at fault; the mere presence of corrections in the environment as opposed to their reliability, consistency, and timeliness relative to the child's own errors; the absence of relevant data as opposed to the presence of data that says no; and the claim that no negative evidence exists as opposed to the claim that no learning procedure could succeed without it. Say explicitly, for each reading, whether it is a claim about the child's environment, about the child's use of the environment, or about the space of learning procedures, since I believe the equivocation runs along exactly that axis.

Two further questions deserve their own section. First, what is the status of a maximum-likelihood objective trained on positive examples alone? Because the model's probability mass is normalized, exposure to what does occur seems to penalize mass assigned to what does not, and in a corpus of realistic size a construction that never appears in a context where a competing construction does appear becomes progressively less likely. Is that negative evidence, indirect or statistical negative evidence, or neither — and does the answer change depending on whether we characterize negative evidence in terms of the data supplied or in terms of the learner's update rule? If the characterization is data-relative, tell me whether my positive-only condition is genuinely on the same side of the line as the child. Second, if my colleague were compelled to choose the one reading that would legitimately render my simulation irrelevant to the nativism debate, which reading should he choose, and what additional premises would he have to supply to get from that reading to the conclusion that the structure-dependent rule is innate rather than acquired? I want to know whether the missing premises concern the quantity and quality of the input, the size and shape of the hypothesis space, or the learning mechanism itself.

Write it as a formal summary I could circulate to the group: numbered readings, a verdict on each, and a short closing section identifying which readings are load-bearing for the argument I was actually met with.
