For twelve years I have read the rationalist blogosphere closely enough to watch the same argument about the direction of time get restarted twice a year, in comment threads and reading groups, by people who are numerate, well-read, and mutually unintelligible. I now facilitate one such group — fifteen regulars, mostly self-taught, one working physicist, two philosophers, several programmers — and our last three sessions on time asymmetry ended with each participant privately convinced the others were committing an elementary error. Those communities were always building self-sorting quizzes, so I want to build one before our next meeting: an instrument that locates each of us in the logical space by what we say about concrete cases, not by which label we would choose for ourselves.

What I would like is a decision tree of eight or nine questions that carries a reader from a shared starting point — that the microscopic laws we use for gases, radiation, and gravitation are, to an excellent approximation, indifferent to the direction of time, while entropy in ordinary closed systems nonetheless rises — to a named position on where the asymmetry comes from.

The requirements:

1. Every node must be answerable by someone with no physics beyond secondary school, in fifteen minutes total, without knowing which branch the answer leads to. No node may use the vocabulary of the debate: no talk of coarse-graining, boundary-condition hypotheses, retrocausation, freak observers, or two-headed arrows. Phrase the nodes as situations — a film of cream dispersing through coffee; the fact that you remember last Tuesday and not next Tuesday; why we take ourselves able to influence how a wager turns out but not where a crater is; why a shattered glass reassembling would astonish us while a planet orbiting the other way would not; what to make of a region in which entropy is falling. Give two or three options per node, each of which is a live answer somebody in my group actually gives.

2. Order the nodes so that the most discriminating questions come first, and show the branching explicitly rather than in prose.

3. Each leaf carries the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the question its advocates least enjoy being asked.

4. These must appear as leaves, described here without their names because I want your naming rather than mine: a view on which the early universe's unusual state is an unexplained initial condition rather than something physics derives; a view on which entropy rises in both temporal directions away from a state of minimal entropy, so that the "past" points toward the middle from either end; a view on which the asymmetry is tied to the difference between what an agent can intervene upon and what an agent can only observe; a view on which the early universe's specialness is specifically gravitational — matter distributed unusually smoothly rather than unusually clumped — and this smoothness is what demands explanation; and, if you judge it a genuine occupant of the space rather than a confusion, a view on which the expansion of space does the explanatory work all by itself.

5. Separately, the tree must distinguish three people currently in my group: one who says the arrow is nothing over and above the fact that space is stretching; one who says entropy increase is a claim about descriptions rather than about the world, so that a sufficiently fine-grained account shows nothing ever increasing, and who takes this to dissolve rather than answer the worry about observers who should be wildly outnumbered by their fluctuated counterparts; and one who treats that same worry as a decisive reductio of certain cosmologies and wants the rest of us to feel its force.

6. Show which combinations of answers your questions make reachable but leave unoccupied, and diagnose each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

7. Flag every pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate, and say whether some further question would separate them or whether they are the same position under two descriptions.

8. Finally, run the instrument on three invented respondents whose answer patterns you specify, including one whose pattern lands them in a position they would indignantly deny holding. Say what in the tree produced that result, and whether the fault lies with the instrument or with the respondent.

Two caveats. First, most of my framing above was acquired from blog posts of uneven quality, so if a premise in my setup is mistaken, or if one of the three group members I described is not stating a position at all but a confusion in a position's clothing, say so plainly instead of accommodating it in the tree. Second, please append no reading list and no citations; a good deal of what circulated under authoritative names in those communities turned out to be misremembered, and I would rather have the reasoning stand on its own than chase references I cannot verify.
