I'm the director of a small municipal museum in a northern European port city — forty staff, a lot of shipping history, and one object in the building that anyone outside this town has ever heard of. On Thursday we hand over a brass plaque from Benin to a delegation from the Nigerian national museums commission. It reached us in 1899 through a local ship's chandler who bought it in Hamburg, and its been in a case at the top of the main staircase more or less ever since. I give the opening remarks: four minutes, standing, no lectern, before the head of the delegation speaks and before the crate gets closed.

I don't want the usual thing. Here's what I keep coming back to, and I want the speech built out of it rather than decorated with it — nearly everybody in this debate talks about return as restoring a relationship. We'll stay in touch, we'll lend each other things, common heritage, this isn't goodbye. But I think that's a comfort Europeans tell themselves. A return can just as easily be a severance: the last act of a relationship rather than the first act of a new one, an account settled so that nothing is outstanding between the parties afterwards. Which one Thursday is hasn't been decided and I'm not going to pretend it has. So write the speech so that it takes a position — you pick — but make the choice visible structurally, in what the speech commits us to and what it refuses to commit to. If it's a severance, the speech can't ask for anything: no loans, no joint projects, no we look forward to. If it's the start of a relationship, then it has to name what we're actually undertaking, in a room where my board has agreed to undertake nothing beyond the handover itself.

Second thing. The plaque was made to be looked at. These things were nailed in runs onto the wooden pillars of palace courtyards, where they were read in order by people walking past, not hidden from outsiders as sacred. So the tired argument about access doesn't bite here. What was destroyed wasn't visibility, it was placement — one plaque out of a long ordered run, taken out of position, with everything on either side of it elsewhere. Giving one back doesn't repair that, and keeping it doesn't repair it either. I want that said carefully, so that nobody in the room can hear it as an argument for keeping it.

The room: the delegation, five people, two of whom have been doing this for twenty years and are thoroughly tired of European directors perfoming feelings at them. My board chair plus three trustees who argued against the return, lost, and will be sitting there anyway. Our retired conservator, 84, front row — forty years on that metal, she knows its surface better than anyone alive, she wanted it to stay, and she is not a villain, so don't write her as one. Two classes of eleven-year-olds from the school down the road who've drawn it for years. Local press. And a handful of people who wrote to the paper saying we're emptying out the town's history to buy ourselves a quiet conscience.

Constraints, please honor them. Name the 1897 expedition and what it was, plainly, in one sentence, then move on — my worry is that a long recital of our own wrongdoing at a ceremony we're hosting turns out to be about us. Don't quote anybody: no proverb, no poem, no famous line, no "as so-and-so put it," I've sat through too many of these speeches and it always sounds borrowed. Don't use the words gift, generosity, reconciliation, dialogue, or shared heritage, and don't smuggle those ideas in wearing other clothes. Speak as the institution, first person singular, but let exactly one sentence be visibly mine and not the museum's. Address the conservator once, by name — call her Marit — in a way that doesn't turn her into our defense. And it has to let people in that room feel something other than relief: if it ends on a note where everyone can exhale and drift off toward the wine, I've done it wrong.

Give me the speech, around 550 words, then two or three sentences after it telling me which of the two positions you took and pointing to where in the text I can see it.
