And here's their answer, lightly condensed but I haven't changed or added any of the claims:

The intuitive starting point is that if a couple has a child whose life is happy, the world is better off. Nobody is made worse off, somebody gains, so this looks like a pure improvement, and the subsidy is just a way of getting more of it. Parfit's challenge to that intuition is the mere addition paradox, better known these days as the repugnant conclusion. Start with population A: ten billion people, all with very high quality of life. Now add ten billion more whose lives are worth living but not as good. The result, A+, is not worse than A, because the extra people are glad they exist and no one else loses anything. Keep trading quality for quantity in small steps and you arrive at Z: a vast population whose members have lives only barely worth living, but which contains more well-being in total than A did. The total view is therefore committed to saying Z is better than A, which is repugnant. Notice that this depends on 'not worse than' being transitive across the steps, which is one place you could push back. Since Parfit thinks the conclusion has to be rejected but every escape route carries costs of its own, he put forward Theory X, the view that quality can matter more than quantity in a way that isn't just a matter of adding up, so that Z is not better than A after all. The most popular response is to drop the total view for the average view: rank outcomes by average well-being, and huge-but-miserable populations automatically lose out. This also sidesteps the sadistic worry people sometimes raise, since the average view is neutral about population size — it just tells you not to add people who would drag the average down. A different response is to change your account of well-being rather than your account of aggregation. The repugnant conclusion only bites if you're a hedonist, because you need well-being to be a single measurable quantity that can be summed across lives. On a preference-satisfaction view, a life barely worth living is one in which almost none of the person's preferences are satisfied, and there's no sense in which trillions of such lives outweigh ten billion flourishing ones. Either way the policy implication is clear. The subsidy assumes the government can rank whole populations by size and happiness, and since it can't, there are no grounds for the claim that more happy people makes the world better, so the payment is unjustified — even though, as I said at the start, a couple having a happy child obviously makes the world a better place.
