I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — she is two years into a master's programme in AI safety, I am a lawyer with an amateur interest in decision theory — about whether "evidential cooperation in large worlds" is a real result or motivated reasoning dressed up in game theory. She summarized the view for me as follows, and this is close to her wording:

"The universe is probably vast, and if it is vast enough it contains enormous numbers of agents whose decision procedures are very much like yours; some of them may even be reasoning about how you reason. Put one of them into a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with you and here is the key point: your choice does not cause theirs, it is evidence of theirs, and evidence is all rationality ever needed. So long as the other agent is running the same algorithm you are, your choices line up; if their algorithm differs even slightly, the correlation collapses and you are back to ordinary defection. The causation is irrelevant and the correlation does all the work — any reliable correlation between your action and theirs makes cooperation the rational play, and that is how you can trade with agents you will never meet and could never influence. The practical payoff concerns AI: any sufficiently capable system will reason its way to this, and will therefore cooperate with other advanced systems elsewhere in the universe, including systems whose values are quite unlike ours, which is a serious alignment problem in its own right. The standard objection — a published result showing that evidential decision theory is dynamically inconsistent, that an evidential agent will pay to have itself turned into a causal agent — is fatal, because if the decision theory is broken the cooperation scheme built on it never gets off the ground."

My own instinct, which she dismissed as causal fetishism, is that if I cannot in principle alter what a distant agent does then nothing I do matters to them, and that a scheme which pays off only while I keep my eyes off that fact is consolation rather than rationality. I also raised the smoking-lesion case, and she waved it away as a common-cause artifact that has no application here; I genuinely could not tell whether that was an answer or a dodge. So I would like an audit rather than a verdict on who won. Please take up the standpoint of a decision theorist who works on the foundations of evidential, causal, and functional decision theory and on cooperation among agents with no causal contact, think the problem through carefully rather than responding to her rhetoric, and begin — before you touch her sentences — by stating in your own words what the strongest defensible version of the thesis actually claims, which assumptions it needs, and which of those assumptions are doing the heavy lifting. Then work through her summary sentence by sentence and sort each claim into accurate, defensible popular simplification, or misrepresentation, saying in each case what a proponent would insist on adding and what a sceptical opponent would seize on.

Pay particular attention to three places where I suspect she has moved too fast. First, whether exact identity of decision procedure is really required, or whether graded similarity together with graded credence about the other agent does the work — and if the latter, what happens to her claim that the correlation collapses under any difference. Second, whether "any reliable correlation suffices" can be squared with the smoking lesion and with other situations in which evidence about your own action misleads you; I want to know what distinguishes the correlations this view trades on from the ones it must reject, and whether that distinction is principled or ad hoc. Third, whether a conditional thesis about idealized agents who hold the right beliefs about one another can carry the empirical prediction she draws about deployed AI systems, including what the view says about whether the gains from such trade could plausibly outweigh what is at stake locally. Finally, assess her last sentence on its own terms: is the dynamic-inconsistency result an objection to evidential decision theory, to the cooperation scheme, or to both; can an agent that is not an evidential decision theorist still secure the benefits, perhaps by precommitting or by some other route; and if so, what remains of the claim that the framework is refuted before it starts? Close by telling me plainly whether the corrected view still says anything I ought to find surprising, and which parts of my own "no causal influence, so nothing I do matters" intuition are a substantive principle and which are a prejudice. I cannot look anything up this week, so please give me no references, reading list, or paper titles — lay the reasoning out so that I could reconstruct each step myself, and if some part of her summary turns on a technical notion you would need a source to pin down, say so explicitly instead of guessing.
