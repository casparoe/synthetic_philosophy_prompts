Ok so — long story short, I've been reading the rationalist and EA blogs since around 2011, and somewhere along the line that turned into me actually sitting on the grants committee of a small longtermist fund. Five of us, roughly $6M a year to give away. This cycle we've got a proposal that's split the committee 2-2 with one person waffling, and I volunteered to write the memo we'll circulate before Thursday's vote. I want to hand you the situation and get back a draft I could actually put my name to.

The proposal: ~$430k over two years from a two-person research outfit plus a part-time comms person. They want to do what they call suffering-risk work, defined as work on scenarios where advanced AI leads not to extinction but to futures containing enormous quantities of intense suffering — either (a) humans and other beings subjected to large-scale suffering by a misaligned or captured system, or (b) the creation of vast numbers of digital minds that are themselves suffering. Listed deliverables: a scenario analysis that tries to show these are structurally different from standard x-risk cases rather than a subset of them; a survey instrument for measuring how many people at AI labs think digital minds could suffer, and which architectures they think would matter; and a policy track aimed at getting a paragraph on "risks of large-scale suffering" into national AI safety frameworks.

Where the committee is stuck:

- Two of us (me included) think it's genuinely neglected, nothing else in our pipeline covers the digital-minds half, and the scenario analysis might actually change how people reason about post-transformative futures.
- Two think it's x-risk work in a different coat of paint — that the scenarios will turn out to be misalignment scenarios with adjectives added, and the survey will produce numbers with no decision-relevance. Thier sharper worry is that the proposal smuggles in a strong ethical view, something like "the badness of a future is dominated by how much suffering it contains," or even that creating more minds is net-negative, and that funding it lends our name to a contested moral position we've never endorsed. Our charter says we exist to reduce the risk of astronomically bad outcomes and says literally nothing about how to weigh suffering against other things worth having.
- The fifth is mostly bothered by the epistemics of the survey: self-reports about whether machines can suffer, collected from people with career incentives in both directions.

What I want from you: a decision memo, call it 700–1000 words, that I can circulate as-is. Fund, decline, or fund with conditions — pick one and commit, with a number attached, and if you go conditional, make the conditions specific enough that we could tell by month twelve whether they'd been met. It needs to do four things. First, give an actual test for when suffering-risk work is a distinct thing worth funding versus a relabeling of x-risk work, and then apply that test to this proposal rather than leaving it as a principle floating in the air. Second, treat the strongest case for the losing side honestly — if you recommend funding, take the smuggling-in-a-contested-ethics objection seriously as a real threat to the fund's legitimacy, not as a strawman to knock over in one sentence. Third, write it so the two dissenters can respond to it, with claims they could dispute rather than a vibe. Fourth, say in a couple of sentences what we'd be implicitly committing ourselves to by approving, because this is the first of at least three s-risk-flavored proposals in our pipeline and whatever we do here becomes the precedent.

Please don't just tell us to commission more analysis or defer to next cycle. We have to vote Thursday, and deferring isn't free — the group has told us they'll take a smaller offer from elsewhere if we don't move.
