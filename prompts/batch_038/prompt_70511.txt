So I'm in a seminar on Politics Book I and I've been building a table of Aristotle's calls on who can be ruled and how, and I want to stress test it before Thursday. Here's what I've got:

- war captives from a barbarian people, where he thinks the war itself was just: slavery is fine, but he's clear the capture isn't what makes it fine — an unjust war makes unjust slaves, and he won't allow that a well-born Greek could be a natural slave no matter how he ended up captured.
- people enslaved purely because the law says the loser belongs to the winner: rejected, even though everybody does it and everybody thinks it's normal.
- wives: ruled, but politically rather than master-over-slave, because on his account they have a deliberative part that doesn't get the final say.
- children: ruled the way a king rules, deliberative part present but not developed yet.
- animals: ruled despotically, no share in deliberation at all.
- natural slaves: also ruled despotically, but unlike an animal they can follow reason coming from somebody else, they just don't have it themselves. Supposed to be marked out by bodies suited to necessary labour and by being unable to plan a life of their own — tho he admits nature often fails to deliver that, so you get slavish bodies with free souls and the reverse.
- master and slave: meant to have a common interest, and he'll allow something like friendship with the slave in so far as he's a human being, just not in so far as he's a slave. He also gives slaves a bit of virtue, enough for their tasks, and says the master's part is basically just to issue the orders.
- artisans and hired labourers: free, definitly not slaves, but their way of life makes them unfit for citizenship in the best city and hostile to virtue.
- slaves ought to come from slaves, nature intends it, but again he concedes it frequently doesn't work out.

If he's coherent, all of that has to fall out of one rule or a small set, and I can already see at least three candidates that reproduce everything on my list. Thursday's case is one our tutor made up, loosely on the slave stewards and slave-run moneylending that actually existed: a slave who manages his master's whole estate plus a lending operation on the side. He sets the work rota, hires and disciplines the other slaves, keeps the accounts, negotiates contracts, plans two or three seasons ahead, and has a reputation in the deme for good judgement. He owns nothing, can be sold at any moment, and never proposes anything the master doesn't want — tho the master admits he mostly just signs off on what the steward had already decided.

Which way does he call it?
