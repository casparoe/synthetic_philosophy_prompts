I've been going around in circles on Shubik's dollar auction and I want to pressure-test it on a real case instead of another toy. Concorde. Everyone waves at it as the escalation-of-commitment poster child — there's literally a fallacy named after the thing — but waving isn't analysis, and I'm not convinced the standard gesture survives contact with the actual history. Here's what I want.

Facts I'm supplying so you don't have to guess (you can't look anything up, so treat these as given): treaty between the UK and France signed 1962, original programme estimate around £70 million; first flight 1969; entry into service January 1976; by 1976 development costs had run to roughly £1.5–2.1 billion; 20 aircraft built, of which 14 went into airline service — seven each to British Airways and Air France, and not one other airline ever took delivery, against a market forecast of a few hundred; both fleets retired in 2003. Meanwhile the US ran a competing supersonic transport programme, the Boeing 2707, which Congress killed in 1971 before any prototype was finished, and the Soviet Tu-144 carried passengers for about six months and was out of passenger service by mid-1978. One more, shakier: my understanding is that the 1962 treaty contained a cancellation clause that made unilateral withdrawal very expensive for whoever pulled out, and that Britain did try to get out in the late sixties and ended up staying. Flag it if you think that's wrong, but I'd rather you not build anything load-bearing on it.

First, do the mapping properly. Who are the players, what is the prize, what counts as a bid, and — the part I actually care about — where is the loser-pays feature? The trap in the dollar auction isn't sunk cost in general; it's specifically that the runner-up forfeits their whole bid while the high bidder gets the prize, so once you're behind the marginal cost of raising is lower than the cost of passing. Concorde is two governments who are not competing with each other. So either there's a rival pair hiding somewhere in the story (UK against France? Europe against the US against the USSR? the government against its own earlier self?), or the loser-pays structure is being supplied by something else — the cancellation clause, domestic political accountability, supplier and employment commitments. Say which, and show the work rather than asserting it.

Second, work with a variant, because I think the plain auction has a feature Concorde lacks. Call it the decaying-prize dollar auction: same rules — alternating raises, high bid takes the prize, the other player loses everything they've bid — except the prize is worth strictly less at every round than it was at the previous one, exogenously, whether or not anybody bids. Work out what changes. Does decay shrink or expand the range of bids at which raising is still rational? Is there a round past which passing is dominant for both players, and if so does the game then have an equilibrium that ends early? And what is the analysis once decay has driven the prize's value through zero and made it negative — at that point is this still an auction, or has it turned into a contest over who eats the smaller loss? I want the reasoning spelled out, including the cases where you're not sure.

Third, apply it. Somewhere around 1973 — oil shock, widebody economics, no overland supersonic routes — the prize arguably decayed past zero, and by then there was no American rival to beat and by 1978 no rival at all. Both governments kept paying for another three decades, subsidising their airlines to fly the thing at a loss. Which parts of that does your decaying-prize model explain, which does it misdescribe, and which is it simply silent about? The silences matter to me more than the fit. Specifically: what can a competitive-auction model say about escalation that continued for 25 years after the last competitor was gone?

Fourth, the direction I think is most interesting: what does the case do to escalation-of-commitment theory as a theory? My worry is that the only way to keep Concorde inside a rational-choice story is to keep enlarging the prize — prestige, jobs, technology spillover, treaty credibility, not being the minister who killed the national aircraft. But if the payoff is allowed to be whatever it takes to make the observed behaviour rational, does the theory have any content left? Tell me what observation would falsify an escalation account of Concorde. If you can't name one, say so plainly instead of fudging it.

Finally, the contrast case: the US SST was the same kind of game in the same decade with arguably a bigger prize, and it stopped. Whatever you conclude about Concorde has to be able to say why the 2707 died and Concorde didn't, or it hasn't explained anything.
