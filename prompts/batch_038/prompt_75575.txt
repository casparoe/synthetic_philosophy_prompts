I've spent about a decade doing low-level reverse engineering — firmware images, stripped binaries, occasionally a horrifying pile of undocumented C that somebody's compiler mangled. No philosophy background whatsoever, I haven't taken a humanities class since high school, so please don't assume I know any of the jargon.

Lately I've been sitting in on the interpretability reading group at my company, because half of what they do looks like my job with different words. But they argue in circles about one thing and I can't tell if the argument is real. Roughly:

Camp A says that when their sparse autoencoder picks out a direction they nickname something like "is this text about dogs," that direction is a genuine feature of the model — a fact about the weights — and a better method would converge on the same thing. Like how a function is really in the binary even when my decompiler hands back 400 lines of goto spaghetti.

Camp B says the finding is mostly an artifact of the instrument. You chose the dictionary size, the sparsity penalty, the prompt distribution you collected activations on. Change those and you get a different bestiary of "features," so calling any of them *the model's representation* is like saying Orion is really up there.

My buddy on the infra side and I came up with what we call the recompile test, and we think it settles the whole thing: a candidate feature counts as real iff it (a) reappears across retraining runs with different seeds and a shifted dataset, and (b) survives intervention — you patch it, the behavior changes in the predicted direction, and the change generalizes off-distribution instead of falling apart. Camp B says this is circular because the test is defined in terms of our own tooling. I don't buy it but I can't say why.

So here's what I want. Restate this entire debate in the vocabulary I actually use — disassembly vs decompilation, symbol stripping, struct and function-boundary recovery, calling conventions, ABI vs implementation, obfuscation, undefined behavior, the gap between what the source said and what the binary does. Map each side's claims onto things I'd literally say about a binary, as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report:

1. Which claims translate cleanly.
2. Which ones you had to stretch my vocabulary to fit — and name what you stretched.
3. Which ones have no counterpart at all in reverse engineering.
4. For each of those: is it because my vocabulary is missing something the model genuinely has (no source, no spec, no compiler, weights aren't instructions, and whatever "superposition" is seems to mean one variable carrying several unrelated meanings at once), or because their disagreement is less substantive than it sounds and they're really just fighting over a word.

Tell me straight whether the recompile test is a legit analogue of my stability intuitions or whether its doing something sneaky. And the bigger question: in RE there's always a ground truth, somebody wrote the source. Does realism about representations mean anything once you delete that fact, or does "is the feature really there" just collapse into "does it predict the model's behavior"?

One ground rule: don't smuggle your own framework's assumptions in and label it translation. If a mapping only works because you quietly assumed the weights are a program, flag it.
