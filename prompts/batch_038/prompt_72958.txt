Last night two friends of mine turned my kitchen into a debate hall for about two hours and I mostly just did dishes and got more confused the longer it went, so I want somebody else to referee. It started because I mentioned my brother-in-law Sam, who is certain he mailed an important form before the deadline — he remembers walking to the corner mailbox in the rain, in some detail — and the agency has now sent a letter saying nothing arrived and that he's late. Sam's position is that he knows he mailed it, so the letter has to be wrong, mail gets lost, forms get misfiled, and he's not going to spend the weekend digging through his records or "entertaining" the idea that he didn't. One of my friends, Ines, said Sam is actually onto something, and worse, that it generalizes: if you know p, then you know that anything appearing to show not-p is evidence for a falsehood, and you can know that from the inside without further investigation, so a knower is rationally allowed to close their ears. She wrote it on the back of an envelope as a chain — I know p; knowing p entails p; so any evidence against p is evidence of a falsehood; and since I know p I'm in a position to know this evidence is misleading; therefore I may dismiss it. (I think there's a name for this puzzle but neither of them used it.)

Theo's first response was that this is a self-sealing trick, because run it and nobody ever has to change thier mind about anything, which cant be right. Ines said that isn't an objection, it's a grimace: he hasn't said which premise is false, and the argument is permissive anyway, it says you may ignore the evidence, not that you must. Theo then said the break is in the last step, since to know a piece of evidence is misleading you'd need some idea why it's misleading, and Sam has no idea at all — "mail gets lost" is a shrug, not a reason. Ines shot back with a case: she knows the sun is up because she looked out the window ten minutes ago, and if you texted her a photo that was pitch black she'd know the photo is misleading without being able to say whether it's a lens cap, an old shot, or something doctored — not knowing the mechanism is completely normal, so Theo's demand is too strong. Theo said the cases aren't alike, because her window evidence is still standing untouched while what Sam remembers about the mailbox is exactly what the agency's letter is calling into question, and memory about dropping something in a box isn't a great source; he wanted a distinction between evidence that p is false and evidence that your route to p was shaky, saying the chain only has any bite against the first kind. Ines's reply was that if the second kind always gets a hearing then the principle is empty, because in real life almost all evidence against an ordinary claim works by shaking the route rather than by contradicting the claim. They looped on that three times, Theo at one point saying the whole thing equivocates on "knows" — you have to already have ruled out being wrong in order to claim knowledge, and the argument then uses that claim to rule out the very thing you were supposed to have ruled out — and Ines saying that's just a description of what makes the argument interesting rather than a refutation of it. Neither budged and I have no idea who won.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step? What I want: the verdict first, in one sentence — who came out better overall, or a draw if that's the honest answer — then each move in order with a rating from clearly wrong to clearly right plus a line or two of justification, then whatever the two of them both missed, including whether they were even arguing about the same thing by the end and whether Sam, the actual guy holding the letter, is being rational. Don't restate my setup or warm up to it, just start with the verdict.
