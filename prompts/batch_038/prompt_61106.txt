I spent the better part of four decades in a crystallography laboratory, and only since retiring have I had the leisure to read the philosophy of science properly rather than in the margins of methods papers. My progress has been uneven: I find the historical arguments against realism intuitively compelling in a way that my working life never suggested to me, and I find the responses to them strangely unsatisfying, though I cannot yet say why. Last month I bought a general-audience collection of essays on the current state of the physical sciences, and one chapter — written, I should say, by a physicist rather than a philosopher, and by a competent and lucid one — offered an overview of the position known as structural realism. I have copied out the relevant passage below, and I would very much like to know how much of it I may safely believe.

The passage runs as follows. "Structural realism is the moderate and defensible position in the debate about scientific realism. It goes back to Poincaré, who noticed that when one theory displaces another — Fresnel's wave optics giving way to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory being the classic instance — the equations of the older theory are carried over intact into the newer one, while the older theory's account of what the world is actually made of, the luminiferous ether in this case, is simply discarded. On that basis Worrall argued that a realist ought to commit himself only to the structure of a theory, by which is meant the web of mathematical relations its equations encode, and not to the intrinsic nature of the things standing in those relations, since relations alone prove stable through theory change. Structural realism is thus the one form of realism that the pessimistic induction cannot touch: past theories were all wrong about what there is, but the successful ones were right about how things are related, and the structural realist simply confines his belief to that surviving residue. The more thoroughgoing structural realists draw the obvious further conclusion, that there are no objects at all but only structures, and quantum mechanics confirms it, since identical particles cannot be told apart even in principle and so are not individuals in any classical sense. On this view structure is not merely a feature of the world but the whole of it, and the natural objection — relations between what? — evaporates, there being nothing further for them to hold between."

Please answer as a professional philosopher of science who works on scientific realism, and think carefully before you begin; if it would help, open with a short map of the family of positions this passage is gesturing at, so that I can see which boxes exist before I am told which ones have been mislabelled. Then take the passage claim by claim — you may work sentence by sentence if that is tidier — and assign each a verdict: accurate as it stands, a harmless simplification for a general audience, a distortion that changes what the position actually says, or false. Where the passage runs together claims that this tradition keeps carefully apart, name the distinction and explain why the tradition bothers to make it; where it overstates a modest thesis, state the modest version. For each distortion, tell me what a defender of the view in question would say by way of reply, and be even-handed: I am not looking for a demolition, since I suspect the author is broadly sympathetic to the view he is summarising. Two further things I care about particularly. First, whether either of the passage's two headline conclusions — the uniqueness of structural realism as an answer to the pessimistic induction, and the dissolution of the worry about what the relations are relations between — survives once the passage is corrected, or whether they depended on the distortions. Second, whether there is a serious standing objection to the position that the passage omits altogether, especially one that turns the passage's own reasoning against it; my own instinct from the bench is that the historical premise is being asked to carry more weight than it will bear, because I have watched mathematical structures abandoned outright rather than merely reinterpreted, and I have watched relations survive only in a shrunken corner of their original domain — but I do not know whether that instinct corresponds to an objection anyone has actually pressed. I should add, since you may be tempted to it, that I want no citations, no bibliography and no suggested reading list; I no longer have library access and a list of titles I cannot obtain is of no use to me. Reason it through from the ideas themselves.
