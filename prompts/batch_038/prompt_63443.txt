I'm a third-year PhD student in environmental history, and I'd value a referee's eye on an argument that played out in my department last week between a stratigrapher who is a postdoc in the geoscience building and a historian of science who sits on my committee. I tried to stay neutral and just take notes; here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) It began when the historian said that the 2024 decision by the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy to reject the Anthropocene as a formal epoch settles nothing: formalization is a bookkeeping convention about how geologists label time, whereas whether human activity has restructured the Earth system is a matter of evidence, not of a vote. Her conclusion was that we should keep using the term descriptively and stop treating the commission's ruling as though it had epistemic authority.

(2) The stratigrapher replied that this is precisely the evasion the procedure exists to prevent. Without a ratified boundary, "Anthropocene" has no determinate extension: in the same week she had heard it applied to 1610, to 1800, to 1950, and to the Neolithic. Each dating implies a different agent and a different moral — colonial land use, industrial capitalism, nuclear weapons, agriculture — so picking a start date is already picking a culprit, and the only way to keep that from being done by fiat is to force the choice into a public procedure with shared evidential standards.

(3) The historian said that inverts the story. The requirement of a globally synchronous marker is itself a political filter: it privileges the one signal, bomb-test radionuclides, that a handful of states happened to produce within a decade, and it systematically disqualifies slower, regionally staggered phenomena such as the post-1492 collapse of Indigenous American populations and the land-use change that followed it. Her claim was that there is no neutral date, so the appeal to procedure is a way of laundering a preference.

(4) The stratigrapher said that move is self-defeating: if every candidate date is political, the historian has no non-political ground for preferring 1610, and the dip in atmospheric CO2 associated with the depopulation of the Americas was chosen precisely because it points at a particular injustice. Procedure, she added, is not a claim to value-freedom but a way of making a contestable choice contestable on evidence anyone can inspect. She also noted that the base of the Holocene is itself pinned to a single Greenland ice core, so the objection that the Anthropocene proposal rested on one lake in Ontario is not really an objection to how boundaries get defined at all.

(5) The historian's last move: fine, but that concedes her real point. A spike is an event and the Anthropocene is a process — diachronous, cumulative, still unfolding — so the mismatch is not between competing dates but between the geological concept of a boundary and the kind of thing being bounded. On that reading the commission is not the wrong authority exercising power badly; it is the wrong kind of authority for the question, and the whole framing of who gets to decide should be dropped.

They did not budge, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. Could you go through moves (1) to (5) in order and give a verdict on each — clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification, and then say where the two of them are talking past each other and what the exchange leaves out entirely?
