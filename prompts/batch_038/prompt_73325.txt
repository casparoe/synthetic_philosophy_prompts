Three areas of intellectual property law treat the same fact — two people arriving independently at the same thing — in three different ways, and the differences look arbitrary once they are set side by side. In copyright, independent creation is a complete defense, yet a songwriter was held liable in 1976 for a melody he had absorbed so thoroughly that he no longer intended to borrow it (the ruling against George Harrison over "My Sweet Lord"). In patent law, independent invention is no defense: the later inventor may be barred from practicing what he thought up for himself, subject only to a narrow right to carry on a business already begun in good faith. In trade secret law, an identical result reached by one's own efforts or by reverse engineering is wholly lawful, and liability attaches only to a breach of confidence or to improper acquisition. Meanwhile the strongest of these rights runs twenty years from filing, and the weakest an author's lifetime plus seventy.

What theory of the ownership of ideas does this patchwork presuppose? Say what it takes the owned object to be — a mental content, a piece of labor, a relationship of trust, an entry in a public register — and which rival accounts of intellectual entitlement the pattern rules out rather than merely fails to support. Then separate what these practices strictly require from what only fits them: could a purely instrumental, incentive-based story license all three attitudes with no theory of ownership behind them at all, and is any of the difference a genuine contradiction rather than an apparent one? Finally, suppose a legislature imposed a single rule across the three regimes — entitlement tracks causal contribution, so that a person owns what they actually produced and is liable only for what they actually took. What would each regime have to give up to comply, and which existing doctrine is hardest to reconcile with that principle?
