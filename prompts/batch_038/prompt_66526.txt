So I got into it with a friend of mine last night — three hours, two of them at the kitchen table with a notepad, and we both still think the other one is being dense. He does data work for a city agency, I don't, and it started because I asked him a simple question about a program he's working on and it turned into a fight about what "fair" even means when your model is bad at predicting for everyone but worse in specific ways.

The program: a utility-arrears thing. Households apply for a one-time grant to cover an overdue bill so they don't get shut off. There's way more demand than money, so they score applicants on "will this household still end up in shutoff territory six months after we pay this off" and fund the highest-risk ones first. Two neighborhoods in the city have much higher measured rates of that outcome than the rest, and one of them has maybe 200 households in the whole dataset so any rate you compute for them is basically noise. His position: the score has to be calibrated, because a number that doesn't mean what it says is a lie, and if the model says 60% it better be that about 60% of the people it says 60% about end up shut off, in every group. My position: if you fund by a single cutoff on a calibrated score, you reject a way bigger share of the high-base-rate neighborhood, and their rejection rate and the false-alarm rate they eat are both wildly worse, so you've built a machine that reproduces the gap. He says then I want him to fund someone at 70% predicted risk and deny someone at 45% purely on the basis of which group they're in, which is treating an individual as a group member, which is the actual thing everyone agrees is wrong. I said the 70% and the 45% aren't comparable because the label is dirty — the shutoff history in those two neighborhoods is partly the product of the utility's own past practices, so what he's "calibrating to" is partly a record of prior decisions, not a fact about households. He said fine, then go get a better label. I said there isn't one. We looped. Twice.

Here's what I want you to make out of this, because I think our argument was actually two different arguments stapled together and I want a tool that unstaples them for other people before they waste an evening.

Build me a diagnostic — a decision tree or a short quiz, whichever fits better — that takes a practitioner who says "I just want my model to be fair" and lands them on a named position about what to do when the metrics genuinely can't all hold. Say eight to twelve questions or nodes. Every one of them has to be a concrete choice about concrete people, ideally drawn from the arrears program above or from mini-cases with the same shape, with two or three options each. Not a single node may name a metric, or ask which definition someone prefers, or require knowing any jargon to answer. The sort of thing I mean: "two applicants, same score, different neighborhood, one grant left — who gets it, and what would make that answer change?" A person should be able to fill the whole thing out and only find out at the end what they believe.

Then the scoring key. Map answer patterns onto positions, and make sure these show up as leaves: the score-must-mean-what-it-says camp; the equal-error-rates camp; the equal-outcomes camp; the "your metrics are all fine, your target variable is the problem" camp; the "prediction is the wrong frame entirely, publish the decision rule and the cost of each error and let people fight about that" camp; the group-blind camp that thinks all of this is a category error; the camp that thinks any gap in measured base rates is itself the injustice, so a metric that takes base rates as given inherits it; and the deflationists who think the impossibility stuff is technically true and practically irrelevant because you can get close enough. Give each leaf a name a normal person would say out loud in a meeting — not a citation, not who proved what, I don't care and neither does anyone in that room.

Three things I specifically want handled, because they're where he and I kept sliding past each other:

First, show me the answer combinations that can't be occupied. If someone says both "the number has to mean what it says" and "the error rates have to match across groups" while also conceding the base rates really do differ, that region is empty — say so, and say why. Then diagnose each empty region separately: is the combination incoherent, merely unstable in practice, or does it only look empty because of a hidden assumption, like assuming the model is nearly perfect, or assuming the cutoff has to be the same for everybody, or assuming "the groups" are fixed rather than something you chose to measure? That last distinction is the one I want the whole thing to hinge on.

Second, at least one node has to be about the contaminated label — what you do when the outcome you're predicting is partly manufactured by earlier decisions in the same system. The tree should route people to genuinely different destinations depending on whether they think the fix is a better label, a different target, or no target.

Third, work in the two wrinkles I mentioned: the neighborhood with 200 records, where every rate you could equalize is within the noise, and the fact that the same score gets used by two different people with different costs for the two error types — a caseworker rationing a fixed budget, and an auditor who only cares that we didn't hand money to households who'd have been fine anyway. I suspect those two wrinkles are where a lot of our fight actually lived and neither of us noticed.

Finally, be honest about the limits. If there are two leaves your tree can't really tell apart from the inside, say which ones and what question would separate them if I had it. I'd rather have a tool with visible seams than a clean-looking one that quietly lumps together two views that fought for three hours last night.
