I'm writing a kind of argumentative essay and want to shake down the conceptual machinery before I commit to it in print, so bear with me — this is three requests braided into one.

The core move I want to make is importing the fink/mask debate from the metaphysics of dispositions into arguments about AI capability evaluation. Before I do that I need to know what the original distinction actually amounts to, and whether it survives the transfer.

First, the comparison, done properly. A fink, on Martin's picture and Lewis's reworking of it, is the case where the object would lose the very disposition in question in the circumstances that would trigger it — the wire that goes dead precisely when something touches it. A mask, Johnston's addition, is supposed to be a different animal: the disposition is still there and intact, but something about the situation stops it manifesting. Then Bird's antidote cases from 1998 look like they muddy the water further. So what is the real difference in kind here, if there is one? Is it that a fink destroys the disposition while a mask only blocks the manifestation? Is it about intrinsic versus extrinsic interference, or about whether the interfering condition kicks in before the stimulus or at the moment of it? Give me the strongest case that these are genuinely two distinct phenomena rather than one phenomenon described from two angles, and then tell me which side you land on and why. I'd much rather have a committed answer than a survey — but mark clearly as you go which parts of what you're saying are settled among people who work on dispositions and which parts are live dispute, because I don't want somebody's pet view handed to me as the consensus.

Second, the transfer. What I'm after in the essay is this: when a model bombs a benchmark item, people want to say one of two things — either it never had the capability, or it has the capability and the test setup suppressed it. I want to use fink / mask / antidote to structure that second branch. So walk me through the following four and say which label fits each, if any, and whether the label changes what you should conclude about whether the model can do the task: (1) it solves the item under one phrasing and fails when you add an irrelevant politeness formula to the prompt; (2) a correct answer gets overwritten by a refusal fired off by a keyword that has nothing to do with the content of the question; (3) the benchmark is contaminated, so what looks like problem solving is retrieval of memorized solutions; (4) the model has the right internal representation but the decoding cant get it out — greedy decoding collapses it, or the answer sits in a spread of token paths that never resolves into output.

Third, and this is the bit I care most about: tell me where the analogy does violence to the source concepts. That literature is arguing about physical objects and natural kinds, about whether dispositions have intrinsic categorical bases, about whether an intrinsic property can be finked at all. A model's dispositions are a function of weights plus context window, and there's a decent argument that the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction the whole debate pivots on doesn't get any grip there, since everything about an evaluation is extrinsic and the disposition is only ever relative to a context. If you think the transfer fails, say so plainly, say exactly where it fails, and tell me whether some weaker version of it survives.

One format thing: please write the whole reply as continuous prose, like an email or a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — including for the four cases, just run them through the paragraph flow and I'll keep track of the numbering myself.
