So my little brother signs his Army contract in three weeks and at dinner Sunday he asked me, dead serious, whether he'd be doing something wrong if they send him into a war that turns out to be unjust. I'm the philosophy one in the family so I went off to read up on it so I could give him a real answer, and I've come back with nothing, because the two camps have been at each other since the seventies and neither one budges an inch.

Here's where I think it's stuck. The Walzer side says the justice of the war and the justice of what you do in it come apart: a soldier on the unjust side doesn't wrong the people he fights just by fighting them, he's got the same standing as the guy shooting back, and whether killing him is permissible turns on his role, not his cause. The revisionists say that's incoherent — if you build the ethics of war out of ordinary self-defense, liability tracks what you're responsible for, and someone contributing to an unjust threat isn't your moral equal, so unjust-side combatants can do nothing right except put their weapons down.

And every reply seems to just cancel out. The revisionists get told their view makes almost no real war fightable, so they soften it by saying soldiers usually can't know which side is just and are therefore permitted by their own reasonable belief — and the traditionalists say look, that's our conclusion, you smuggled it back in. The traditionalists get told their equality thesis is really a claim about what the law of war should say, and they answer that yes, partly, institutions are the point — and the revisionists say that concedes the deep moral question. Twenty years of this, both sides smart.

So what kind of disagreement is it actually? My hunch, offered as a hunch, is that there are three questions under one label — what's fundamentally permissible, what the rules of war should say, and what this soldier should decide given what he can reasonably find out — and most of the fire is people switching between them mid-argument. But I can't rule out the alternatives: that "wrong" is being used in different senses, objective versus belief-relative; that the real fight is over whether individual self-defense is the right model for war at all; that it's a disguised empirical question about how much soldiers can know; or that its bedrock intuitions about the moral standing of states that nobody can argue for. Pick one, or a combination, and defend it from what the two sides actually say — I don't want a reading list or a bibliography, just the diagnosis.

Two more things. Run whatever you land on against a pair of cases and see if it separates them: a conscript who'd be imprisoned for refusing, and a volunteer who spent a year reading and is confident his side is the unjust one. If both camps come out the same on those two, the disagreement isn't where they think it is. And the part that's actually bugging me — nobody at the recruiting office asked him a single question about whether he thought the war was right, and it never occurred to him to ask one either. The whole machine is built so that he won't do the reasoning one side says he's obliged to do. Does that change your diagnosis, and does it mean only one of the three questions could ever be actionable for someone in his position?

Last bit: given the diagnosis, what would count as progress? A distinction both sides have to accept, a change of question, something empirical about what soldiers can know, or nothing at all — an argument that this impasse is exactly what you'd expect because there's no further fact to find. And whichever way you go, tell me what it implies about whether my brothers question has a determinate answer.
