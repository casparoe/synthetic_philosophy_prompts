I'm a third-year econ/philosophy double major, and the seminar I'm in this term has spent the last three weeks on Bayesian agreement — Aumann's result, then the stuff about what happens when two people keep announcing their posteriors to each other back and forth, which I mostly followed. But something from my other life keeps not fitting and I don't have a word for it, so I figured I'd ask here instead of derailing the seminar. I help run my uni's prediction club and I also write a small monthly newsletter about campus and national politics for maybe 200 subscribers, which means some of the people who read my numbers are also people who could act on them. Last month a guy in the club put 80% on the student senate passing a fee hike. Someone leaked the writeup to the student paper, opponents screenshotted it and ran "the prediction club already thinks this is decided" as a mobilization line, turnout at the vote was way higher than usual, and the fee failed. Then there was a two-hour argument about whether he'd been wrong. One camp said obviously yes, it was an 80% and the 20% came in, calibration is calibration. The other camp said no: he was right about what he knew, and the only reason it came out the other way is that the number went public — so the thing he was estimating wasn't a fixed target that he missed, it was a target that moved when he aimed at it.

Here's why I think that second camp is onto something but can't articulate it. How far the target moves depends on how many people see the number, and the direction isn't fixed: sometimes announcing a forecast makes it come true (everyone believes 6% inflation next year, wage demands go up, so it happens), and sometimes announcing it makes it come false (the same forecast gets taken seriously by the central bank, which tightens hard, and it doesn't happen). Same forecaster, same data, same model, two different outcomes, with the difference being nothing but whether and how loudly the number was released. So I genuinely can't tell whether there's supposed to be a stable correct answer to "what is the probability of X" in cases like these at all, or whether the question is only answerable once you specify an announcement policy — who hears the number, when, and with what credibility. It also feels like there should be some cases with no stable answer whatsoever, where any number you publish changes things in a way that makes the published number wrong, but I have no idea how you'd even show that.

So, two requests. First: does this configuration have a name, and if the honest answer is that there are several near-misses but nothing exact, can you lay them out — self-fulfilling prophecy, whatever the mirror-image one is called, the Lucas critique (which people reach for in the inflation case but which seems to be about estimated parameters breaking when policy changes, so not quite it), anything from the ML or market-design side you think is closer, maybe something in the public announcement logic literature? For each one I want to know how what I described differs from it and where the boundary is, including whether a thermometer that raises the temperature of a tiny sample counts or whether that's just measurement and there's a principled line somewhere. If nothing fits, coin something and define it with boundary cases, because I'm definately not satisfied with a bare label — I want the carving. Second, and this is the bit I actually want to bring to seminar: is any of this a counterexample to Aumann? My club president thinks the two camps in the fee-hike argument are just being irrational and would converge if they pooled everything, and my gut says the theorem is being applied to a situation where one of its assumptions quietly fails, but I can't say which. Is it the common prior, the common knowledge, the requirement that the state space be fixed and exogenous, or something about what counts as an announcement? If its the last one, tell me what the state space is supposed to look like once the announcement is itself part of the world being forecast, and then whether two rational people who have fully announced their posteriors and still say different numbers are irrational or just estimating different things.
