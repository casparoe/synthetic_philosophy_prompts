I am enrolled in an upper-level seminar on the paradoxes of knowledge, and this week we are working on the dogmatism paradox, which Kripke arrived at as a byproduct of his treatment of the surprise examination. My professor has asked each of us to prepare a short "informal exposition" of the puzzle — a statement of it that uses no logical notation — and I have decided to do mine as a joke, on the theory that the humor would have to land in exactly the right place for the exposition to count as accurate.

For reference, the reasoning I want the joke to engage with is this. Suppose I know that some proposition is true — say, that the water from a certain well is safe to drink. Then I also know that any evidence I might later acquire against it would be misleading, since it would be evidence for something false. But if I know that any such evidence would be misleading, it seems I am entitled to disregard it in advance, whatever form it happens to take. Knowledge thus appears to license a standing deafness to counterevidence, and to license it in proportion to how much one knows, which is absurd; yet each step looks valid.

Please write the joke, subject to the following requirements:

1. Keep it short — no more than a dozen lines, ideally a compact exchange between two characters — and give it an actual punchline rather than a wry closing observation.
2. The punchline must turn on the content of the paradox itself, not on philosophers being pedantic or out of touch with ordinary life. Specifically, I would like it to exploit the gap between the standpoint from which the argument is run (before the counterevidence has arrived, when I still know the proposition) and the standpoint of a character who has just been handed that counterevidence (at which point it is far less clear that I still know it, and hence far less clear that I may brush aside what I am being shown).
3. Make it intelligible to someone who has heard of the surprise examination paradox but has never read anything on dogmatism: no technical vocabulary, no symbolism, no naming of the paradox or its author inside the joke.
4. The two characters should be roughly evenly matched in cleverness, so that the joke does not depend on one of them simply being obtuse.
5. It should be good enough that I would be willing to read it aloud at the start of my presentation.
