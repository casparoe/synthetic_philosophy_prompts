So last night I got into a two-hour argument with a friend of mine — she's a clinical psych grad student, I'm in philosophy — about Hacking's looping effects, and I still can't let it go, so I've decided the only way to settle it is to make my students feel it. You're going to help me build the thing. Act like you're someone who works at the intersection of philosophy of psychiatry and science education: you know Hacking cold, but you've also actually run a room full of undergrads and you know what falls apart the second you start handing out index cards.

Setting: I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of psychiatric classification. 16 students, one 80-minute session, next Thursday. They've done the reading already — interactive versus indifferent kinds, the idea that classifying people changes the people classified, which in turn changes the classification, so the target keeps moving under your feet. They can all recite it. Nobody has felt it.

The argument I want the activity to adjudicate: she says the looping effect is basically proof that DSM categories are social constructions rather than real kinds, because anything that mutates when you point at it can't be tracking something in nature. I say that's neither Hacking's view nor a good view — the kinds are real, they're just the sort of kind that runs a two-way street with the people inside it. She also said, somewhat unfairly, that you could never show any of this in a classroom because the effects need years and whole institutions to show up.

Here's what I want you to design. Runnable, not a sketch:

- Invent a completely fake category. She and I came up with one over drinks: cognitive latency divergence, or CLD, defined by how long someone takes to commit to a first answer on a set of low-stakes puzzles relative to everyone else in the room. Tell me honestly whether a measure like that will give me a usable spread in about 15 minutes with 16 people, and if it won't, propose a better proxy for the same underlying idea.
- Two rounds of the same instrument, with a labeled group and an unlabeled control arm, so the class can see a shift in the distribution rather than just trade anecdotes.
- The part my friend will say is missing: a third round where a small criteria-revision panel of students gets to re-threshold the category — build in an incentive so they definitely will — and then everybody gets reclassified. I want the room to watch the extension of the category move because the criteria moved, and then watch the behavior move again because the criteria moved. Two-sided loop, not one-sided.
- A scoring scheme that doesn't need real money but where the points actually matter to how people play.
- Materials list, minute-by-minute timeline, and the literal text I should print on the cards and the category fact sheet.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the view, including where the two of us each get something right.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with 16 undergrads, and what I do about it in the moment.
- The demand-characteristics objection. Half my class will say what they saw is just people performing because they knew they were being watched, which is not the same thing as a looping effect at all. Build something into the design that lets them adjudicate that themselves, and if you can't, say so plainly instead of hand-waving.
- Ethics. Nobody gets told a made-up diagnosis is going to follow them out of the room, everybody learns in-session that it was invented, and I need an opt-out that works for a student who has a real diagnosis or just doesn't want to be sorted — without singling them out or making them lose the experience.
- Be frank about what an 80-minute game can't reproduce: identity, institutions, time, the fact that real people build lives and paperwork and communities around these labels. Tell me what I have to supply in words afterward because the game structurally can't.

Process, please: think carefully and start with some brainstorming. Before you write the activity, sketch 3 or 4 genuinely different mechanics it could run on, then pick one and tell me in a few sentences why it beats the others. Then give me the full thing. Don't explain Hacking back to me and don't hand me a bibliography — I want card text, payoffs, and minutes, not a lecture.
