Since my father died in the spring of 2019 I have kept a notebook, originally of jobs and quotes, which has gradually turned into a record of decisions I could not make cleanly. I run a building firm of about thirty people near Wakefield with my brother; my mother is eighty-four and still lives in the house I grew up in. What has struck me over the last few years is that almost none of the hard choices I face are really about weighing consequences against one another. They are about who somebody is to me. I have also been listening to a good deal of radio and reading a translation somebody lent me, and I came across an idea that I do not know the proper name for: that a person simply is his roles, that there is no "me" left over underneath being a son, a brother, an employer and a neighbour which could stand outside all of them and choose between them. When I try to picture myself with those stripped away, I find nothing there, which is why the idea has taken hold of me. I gather there was a very old argument in Chinese thought between people who held something like this and people who insisted that one ought to care for everyone impartially, and that the first group thought the second were pulling up something by the roots. I cannot tell which side I am on, and I would rather work it out from my own reactions than from a theory somebody hands me. So here are the reactions, and they are not tidy.

1. My mother needs increasing help. I do not experience this as one claim among others competing with the claims of strangers; I experience it as mine, in a way that does not seem to admit of being outweighed. I would not sell the house she lives in even if the money would plainly do far more good elsewhere, and I am not particularly ashamed of that.
2. My brother has wrecked two businesses through his own choices. He asked me for a substantial loan last year. I gave it, and I would give it again, but I know perfectly well that I would not have given a tenth of it to a far more deserving stranger.
3. An apprentice of mine, no relation, worked harder than anyone on site and I paid for his qualifications and put him in charge of a small job ahead of my own nephew, who was less keen. I felt no conflict at the time. My nephew's claim simply did not register, and afterwards I was not sorry.
4. My daughter now wants a job in the firm. She is good but she is not the best applicant. I think I should not give it to her, and I would feel like a fraud if I did — and yet I also think that if I were standing where she is standing, my father handing the job to a stranger would feel less like fairness than like being abandoned.
5. An employee's father died in the middle of a season when we could not afford the time. He took three weeks and I paid him in full. It never occurred to me that I was making an exception to our rules; the rules seemed beside the point rather than overridden.
6. A cousin of mine was cruel to my mother for years, and now needs money. I feel almost nothing. An unrelated neighbour who has driven her to appointments for a decade asked me for a favour last month and I felt more obliged to him. That verdict frightens me, because it suggests the tie only counts when it has actually been lived.
7. A long-standing friend asked me to lie to his wife about where he had been. I refused, and I do not feel I betrayed the friendship. I feel the friendship is what required the refusal.
8. My uncle, the eldest of that generation, expects to direct me on the strength of who he is, and I resent it and think his expectation is hollow. My mother expects nothing and directs me constantly, and I obey her without the slightest resentment, though she has no more standing than he does.

What I want is a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, stated in plain language, that delivers as many of these verdicts as it honestly can. For each clause of it, tell me which case forces that clause in. Then tell me what it says about cases I have not listed: a promise I made to my mother while she was well, to do something after her death that I now think wrong; whether the principle treats a role I chose, such as employer, differently from one I never chose, such as son; and a parent who has committed something criminal and asks his child to help him escape the consequences. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which one you would tell me to abandon. I suspect the real choice is between a simple principle on which the tie holds whether or not it is honoured — which would oblige me to give up the cousin and the apprentice — and a more elaborate one on which a role binds only in so far as it is actually being lived, which troubles me for the opposite reason: if the tie depends on conduct, it stops being something I am and becomes something I can hand back whenever the terms disappoint me. Tell me which of those two is nearer the thing I have been circling, and whether the second is genuinely available or whether it quietly collapses into ordinary impartial morality with a discount for relatives. I am not after references or the names of books; I want the reasoning laid out so clearly that I could put it to my brother across a table in the pub and he could tell me where it fails.
