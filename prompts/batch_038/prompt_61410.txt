I've been writing down my gut verdicts on definite-description cases for a while now and I can't get them to line up, so I want to know what principle — if any — is actually underneath them. Here's the list.

**1.** Smith has been murdered. Everyone in town is sure the insane butler did it, but it was actually a perfectly sane burglar. Someone says "Smith's murderer is insane." I think that sentence is just false. Not gappy, not true-of-the-butler — false.

**2.** At a party I say "the man drinking a martini over there is a famous poet," pointing at a man holding a glass of water. I think what I said is true. It's about that man, he is a famous poet, nobody is misled or talking past anybody.

(1) and (2) look like the same shape to me — the description doesn't fit the person I have in mind — and I give opposite verdicts. That's the first thing I need sorted out.

**3.** "The present King of France is bald." I don't think that's false in the way (1) is; it's not true, but flatly calling it false sounds wrong to my ear. And yet "the present King of France is bald or the present King of France is not bald" reads to me as obviously true. I know that's embarrassing for a gap view. It's what I hear.

**4.** The scope ambiguity in "the King of France is not bald": I cant hear it. If you tell me there's a reading on which it comes out true, I'll nod along as a technical matter, but left to myself only one reading is live for me. So whatever principle explains the rest of this shouldn't require that I'm unconsciously tracking two readings one of which I have no access to.

**5.** "The average taxpayer has 2.4 children." Not about anybody, not literally true, but I also don't want to call it a failed reference or a falsehood. It seems to be doing an entirely different job. Same with "the American teenager spends six hours a day on their phone."

**6.** "A man walked in. The man was carrying a wet umbrella." Totally fine, and clearly about that guy — but before the first sentence there was nothing unique in the world fitting "the man." Uniqueness got manufactured by the conversation. Now: "every student who read the novel enjoyed it. The novel was 900 pages." I find that acceptable-ish and I can't tell you what "the novel" picks out. If different students read different novels it sounds worse but not flat-out wrong.

**7.** "The lion is a solitary hunter" — true, despite counterexamples and despite there being no single lion. "The Americans invented jazz" — true, and it isn't about every American. Neither of these feels like the same phenomenon as (2), and I don't want them folded in with it.

**8.** "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" — true. "The present King of France does not exist" — true. "Smith's murderer does not exist" — false, someone killed him. This is the one place where the Russellian machinery seems to give me exactly what I want for free.

**9.** "I thought the ship was bigger than it was" — true, and about that very ship. But in "George IV wanted to know whether Scott was the author of Waverley" I don't think you can substitute "the author of Waverley" for "Scott" without changing what's said, even though it's the same man. So co-referential descriptions both do and don't substitute, and I don't know what the switch is.

My own tentative repair, which I suspect is broken: *a description picks out the speaker's intended target when that target is the unique satisfier of the description as restricted to what's mutually relevant in the context; where no restriction delivers uniqueness, you fall back to the literal Russellian analysis and the sentence is false.* The obvious problem is (2) — no amount of restricting to the people I can see turns the water-drinker into a martini-drinker, so the restriction clause doesn't actually rescue the verdict I care about most. Maybe the description's job there is to fix attention rather than state a condition, but I don't know how to say that precisely without it collapsing into "the speaker means whoever they mean."

So: give me the principle, or two or three rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a brand new case without asking you anything. For each clause tell me which of my cases forces it in. Then tell me which of my verdicts it has to overrule — I'd rather lose one verdict than carry a baroque theory, but say which one and why that one rather than another. Give me three or four cases I didn't list, including at least one where you think I'm committed to a verdict I'd hate. And say whether it matters if the mismatch is my private mistake versus a community-wide one.

The case I keep going back and forth on: I tell a cop "the car that hit me ran the red light." I never actually felt or saw the impact — I saw a blue car swerve through the intersection and then I was on the ground. Is my sentence about the car that really hit me, or about the blue car, and does the answer change if the blue car turns out to have been parked three blocks away the whole time?
