I'm finishing a co-authored chapter on social learning for a volume on epistemic institutions, and my coauthor has drafted the following argument to close a section we'd been calling "Are Cascades Irrational?" He thinks it's a *reductio* of the standard rational-herding account, and he has been reading it aloud at seminars to good effect. I don't think it works, but I have not been able to say where it breaks, and I don't want to send it back to him with only a shrug. Here is the argument as he has it, with the numbering he insists on keeping:

(1) In the standard sequential-learning setup — the urn-and-signal models behind Banerjee's and Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch's treatments — agents choose between two actions under a payoff-relevant unknown state, each receives a private signal, each observes every earlier action, and each maximizes expected payoff given a common prior. No agent is assumed to be irrational, and none is assumed to follow a crowd against their own evidence: an agent who ignores a private signal after observing two identical predecessors is doing precisely what the posterior demands.

(2) A cascade begins exactly when the public evidence summarized in the action history outweighs the agent's private signal. That later agents ignore their own signals is therefore not an epistemic failure but a consequence of correct conditionalization.

(3) Every action in the history was itself chosen on the basis of that agent's private signal together with the history available to them. So the complete public history encodes, recursively and without loss, the private information of the entire population.

(4) If (3) holds, then an agent who conditions on the full action history conditions on something at least as informative as the pooled private signals of the group — a sufficient statistic for society's evidence.

(5) Hence in a cascade the consensus is the best-supported belief the group can form: it coincides with what a hypothetical aggregator who saw all the private signals would also conclude, because the individual agent's posterior just *is* that aggregate. Any dissenter is either less informed than the crowd or failing to conditionalize correctly.

(6) Nothing in (1)–(5) is peculiar to urns and restaurants. It requires only a common prior, sequential observation of actions, and Bayesian rationality — so it transfers unchanged to markets, juries, peer review and citation practice, clinical guidelines, and any other consensus formed by people watching each other act.

(7) Therefore, wherever those three conditions hold, stable social consensus is by construction the most accurate belief available to the group, and lone dissent is irrational in the technical sense.

(8) But (7) is demonstrably false: bubbles burst, settled scientific views turn out to have been wrong, and in the laboratory urn experiments whole rooms converge on the wrong urn, where anyone who had simply trusted their own draw would have been right.

(9) So (1) is false, and what the literature calls rational herding must in fact involve irrationality.

I reject (9) and I think he knows it, but he says I have to give up (1) to avoid it. What I would most like to keep are (1), (2), and (8): I want to defend the claim that herding agents are doing textbook Bayesian reasoning — that is the genuinely interesting content of this literature — and I also want to keep the empirical fact that groups converge on the wrong answer with alarming regularity. If those two cannot both survive, tell me which of (3) through (7) is doing the real work, why that step sounds unobjectionable when spoken quickly, and what precisely I pay for rejecting it. Please be concrete about the payment rather than general: I want to know how the diagnosis interacts with the known result that unbounded private beliefs rule out the sort of cascade step (3) depends on, and with Aumann's agreement theorem, which (5) seems to me to be smuggling in by the back door and which (8) does not obviously contradict. If it turns out that the right response is not to reject any single premise but to locate an equivocation or a scope slide between two of them, say so and mark the exact seam; if instead the argument is valid and the question is only which plausible premise is cheapest to abandon, rank them.
