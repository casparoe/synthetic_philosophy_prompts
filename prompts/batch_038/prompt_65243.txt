ok so I'm prepping a couple of writing exercises for a philosophy-of-science reading group, half of whom have never met grue in their lives and half of whom have been arguing about it for weeks, and I want the piece to work for both — funny on the surface, but the payoff lands harder if you know what's going on.

Can you write me a parody government web page, about 650 words, formatted the way those service pages actually are: a "before you start" box, numbered eligibility criteria, a fee table, an FAQ, and a small-print section at the bottom. The premise is that there's a national registry (call it the Bureau of Entrenchment unless you can beat that) which issues certificates of projectibility for predicates, so citizens know which generalizations they're legally entitled to draw about unobserved cases. Two applications have been sitting in the queue for years — you'll guess which — and the entire page is about them without ever once arguing with Goodman.

The jokes have to come from the machinery being exactly right, not from anyone winking:

- the eligibility criteria should be ones that visibly assume the thing in dispute. Like, require the applicant to show a track record of the predicate having been successfully projected into the past, and require the applicant to state, in advance, which future cases the certificate will cover.
- there's a mandatory checkbox asking whether the predicate is purely qualitative, with a footnote that concedes, in flat administrative language, that the answer depends on which language the form happens to be filled out in.
- an appeals section where a petitioner from a community that speaks the other language complains that their own predicates are far better entrenched locally and that ours are the gerrymandered ones — and the form has no field in which that complaint can be entered.
- somewhere, ideally buried in the small print, the Bureau explains that its certification standards are themselves justified by reference to the record of past successful certifications, and states this as a feature.

For one denied application, invent a brand new time-indexed predicate of your own rather than recycling Goodman's examples, and write the refusal notice so it's technically airtight and completely unanswerable. Maybe throw in a processing-times line about applications having to be lodged before t and what happens to the ones that aren't, if you can make that land.

Deadpan all the way through. No character ever says the words circular or question-begging, no editorial asides, no one steps out of the register to explain the joke. Someone who knows the new riddle should be able to tick off every standard objection to entrenchment from the page alone, without a single one of them being stated.
