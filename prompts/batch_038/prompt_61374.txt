I am a doctoral student in philosophy, and this term I am teaching-assistant for a third-year philosophy of science course on scientific reasoning and the a priori. I have done the first marking of the timed midterm essay. Our department requires an independent second read before grades are released, and I would like yours. I have deliberately not said what I made of the script, because I want your assessment to be genuinely independent rather than an adjustment of mine.

**Conditions and marking scheme.** Students had fifty minutes, closed book, no notes, with a ceiling of 1,200 words. Thought experiments in the natural sciences were one of three units; the two weeks devoted to them were built around the exchange between James Robert Brown and John Norton. Mach, Kuhn, and Sorensen were mentioned in lectures, and students were told explicitly that they were not required. Marks are out of 100: 70 and above is a first, 60–69 an upper second, 50–59 a lower second, 40–49 a third, below 40 a fail. The published criteria for this essay were (i) accurate reconstruction of the two positions, (ii) a worked example from the history of physics handled in enough detail to test those positions against it, and (iii) an evaluation that identifies and presses the real point of dispute rather than listing contrasts.

**The essay question was:**

> A thought experiment appears to tell us something about the natural world without supplying any new empirical data. Explain what makes this puzzling. Then set out Norton's response to the puzzle and Brown's rejection of that response, and assess whether Norton's response succeeds. Illustrate your discussion with at least one thought experiment from the history of physics, traced in enough detail that we can see what its reconstruction would have to look like.

**The script, transcribed as written:**

> A thought experiment is puzzling because it seems to produce knowledge of the world without new experience of the world. When Salviati asks Simplicio to consider two stones of different weight tied together and dropped, nobody goes outside and drops anything: the conclusion is reached by considering the scenario. Yet the conclusion is a claim about falling bodies, not a claim about our concepts. So either the thought experiment is not really giving us knowledge of nature, or the empiricist picture of how we know nature is wrong. That is the dilemma.
>
> Norton takes the first horn. On his account a thought experiment is nothing but an argument: the imagined scenario is a vivid and memorable way of presenting premises and a chain of reasoning that could equally well be written out as a plain inference. His thesis is that any thought experiment can be reconstructed this way — the premises can be laid out and shown to be drawn from ordinary experience, and the reasoning shown to be ordinary deductive or inductive reasoning. If that is right there is no special epistemological problem, because there is no special source of knowledge; the novelty is in the packaging. Norton's motivation is a thoroughgoing empiricism: he holds that there is no such thing as a priori knowledge of nature, so any account that requires such a thing is disqualified at the outset. He is therefore best described as an eliminativist about thought experiments. Strictly speaking there is no such thing as a thought experiment, only arguments dressed up in narrative form, and the pictures we form are rhetorical aids that help us keep track of premises we already accept.
>
> Brown takes the second horn. He thinks some thought experiments really do give us knowledge of nature a priori, and that the only honest way to explain this is Platonism about laws: laws of nature are relations among universals which exist independently of us, and in the right sort of thought experiment we perceive them directly, in an act of intellectual intuition analogous to seeing but not sensory. On Brown's view, then, the thought experiment is not an argument at all, and its conclusion is not inferred from premises but grasped; and because what is grasped is a law that holds of necessity, the grasping cannot mislead us. A physicist who fails to see the law has not really performed the thought experiment. There is no such thing as a faulty intellectual perception of a law.
>
> Take Galileo's argument in the Discourses Concerning Two New Sciences. Suppose, with Aristotle, that heavier bodies fall faster. Take a heavy stone H and a light stone L and tie them together. Because L falls more slowly than H, L will hold H back, so the tied pair falls more slowly than H alone. But the tied pair is heavier than H, so by the Aristotelian principle it should fall faster than H alone. The same assumption yields two incompatible results, so the assumption is false. Norton's reconstruction shows that this argument is valid: the premises it needs are empirical ones about weights, about how bodies behave when they are bound together, and about how speeds combine, all of them available from everyday experience of lifting, dropping, and dragging things, and the conclusion follows. Brown objects that the reconstruction imports premises that were not in the thought experiment, but this misses the point, because what a reconstruction is supposed to show is precisely which premises were doing the work implicitly, and once they are made explicit we can see that they are ordinary empirical claims. Since a reconstruction of this kind can always be produced, Norton's account dissolves the puzzle.
>
> It also tells us what thought experiments can and cannot do. If every thought experiment is an argument whose premises come from experience, then no thought experiment can ever yield new knowledge of nature; it can only re-present, in a more vivid form, consequences of what observation has already told us. The sense that a good thought experiment is revelatory is therefore purely psychological — it is the pleasure of drawing out a consequence we had not bothered to draw out. Einstein chasing a light beam, or Einstein in the falling lift, look like knowledge of nature gained from an armchair, but on Norton's account they are inferences from the empirical content of Maxwell's theory and from the observed equivalence of gravitational and inertial mass. This is a satisfying result, because it preserves empiricism without denying that thought experiments are useful. It simply denies that they are a source of knowledge in their own right.
