ok so comparative federalism seminar (US / Germany / Switzerland / EU) and I've hit a wall I can't get past.

My model of subsidiarity: decisions belong at the lowest level that can handle them, so the further up you go the more justification you owe. Fine. On that model the EU looks like a total failure case — Brussels regulates working time, data protection, product standards, professional qualifications, and as far as I can tell not one single measure has ever been struck down for breaching subsidiarity. Meanwhile Germany and Switzerland look like successes: the federation there simply can't take over schools, Switzerland has subsidiarity written into its constitution as a rule about the allocation and performance of state tasks, and Germany has that necessity test governing when the Bund may legislate in concurrent areas.

So: subsidiarity is supposed to be a legal constraint, so why is it unenforceable in the EU but effective in the two actual federations? Is it the drafting — the EU test is a mush of "cannot be sufficiently achieved" stapled to "better achieved at Union level" — or is it that the ECJ just won't touch it, or that the national-parliament yellow card is a consolation prize dressed up as enforcement? And the one time the EU really lost, when Germany got the tobacco advertising directive annulled, I'm fairly confident that was a subsidiarity win, which makes the rest of the record even stranger.

I think I've got the fix, and I want you to develop it rather than just agree. Treat it as two seperate questions that everyone runs together: the competence question (whose power is it) and the exercise question (given that it's yours, should you use it here). Subsidiarity, on this view, is only ever an answer to the second one, which is why it feels toothless — it never decides who holds a power in the first place. Push on that: does the distinction hold, or does it collapse in practice, becuase if the EU's treaty powers (internal-market harmonisation above all, plus the flexibility clause when nothing else fits) are elastic enough, the competence question gets answered for Brussels almost automatically and subsidiarity ends up carrying all the allocation work anyway. Which would mean my complaint is right but for the wrong reason.

Second, test my analogy: subsidiarity in the EU is basically a non-justiciable principle, political-question-doctrine style, a norm everybody invokes and no court applies. Check that against what actually happens — the eight-week reasoned-opinion window, the one-third threshold, the fact that the institutions review and can then carry on regardless, and whatever the Court does when a member state actually brings an annulment action. Does the analogy survive contact?

And make it concrete so this isn't armchair: suppose the Commission proposes a directive harmonising the licensing of dog groomers across the member states, on an internal-market legal basis. Walk me through the real analysis — which questions get asked in what order, who bears the burden, what counts as evidence, where national parliaments and then the Court come in, and at which step the thing is most likely to die. Then tell me whether the identical proposal in the US or in Germany would even be the same kind of question.

No reading list and no case citations, please — I want the structure and the reasoning, not references I can't check. Last line should begin "Verdict:" and name in fifteen words or less the single load-bearing mistake in how I set this up.
