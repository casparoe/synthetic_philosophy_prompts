so I'm a junior and I've fallen down a rabbit hole about how much we should discount the future — climate mostly, but also just my own life — and I wrote down what I actually believe so my cousin who studies philosophy could poke holes in it. he flaked. can you do it instead? here's the list, roughly in the order I thought of it:

1. pure time preference should be zero. the bare fact that something happens later isn't a reason to care about it less. to me that's just the same principle as saying the bare fact that somebody is far away isn't a reason to care about them less.

2. but for my own life I think wanting the sooner version of a good is fine and not a bias. I'd take $100 today over $110 in a year and I don't feel like I owe anyone an argument for that. I know the usual comeback is uncertainty or that I could invest the hundred, and maybe some of it is that, but I don't think that's the whole story — I think a life is just the kind of thing where earlier counts.

3. I'm a reductionist about personal identity, Parfit style: me-now and me-at-60 are held together by psychological connectedness, that comes in degrees, and there's no further fact of identity underneath it. some people say that view makes it easier to reject the bias toward the near, and other people say it does the exact opposite, that it lets you care less about your far future precisely because there's less connectedness there. honestly I lean toward the second one.

4. I also don't think "it's mine" is a special reason. my future welfare isn't worth more than a stranger's just because it's mine. I try to actually think this way and not just say it.

5. related: I think caring way more about my family than about random strangers is obviously fine and is a totally different kind of thing from caring more about now than later.

6. I'm ok discounting a little for uncertainty — like the chance there's nobody around in 2300 to receive the benefit, or some catastrophe wiping out the plan. what I'm not ok with is the other move, where economists say we should discount because future people will be richer than us so a dollar of damage matters less to them. that one seems like selfishness in a lab coat to me, because its still discounting by date of birth, just with extra steps.

7. hyperbolic discounting is irrational and the right normative model is exponential, even though actual brains are hyperbolic.

8. two cases I have firm verdicts about. imagine a dial that sets how much you care about future generations relative to your own generation, and a second, identical dial that sets how much you care about your own future relative to your present. turning the first one down is a moral failure, the kind of thing I'd criticize a person for. turning the second one down is a personality quirk — nobody owes anybody an explanation for mostly caring about their next ten years.

is this a coherent package, or am I helping myself to something? I want an audit, not a lecture: which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, how bad each real tension is (rank them, don't just dump them all as equally bad), what the cheapest fix for each one is and what that fix costs me, and then which single change would clean up the most mess. I'm genuinely fine being told that three of these have to go — I don't know which ones and I'm not going to hint.
