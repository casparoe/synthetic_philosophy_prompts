THE QUESTION (second of three, closed book, 45 minutes, no texts allowed, worth a third of the paper, word limit 800):

"Hardin's argument is usually boiled down to the claim that shared resources get overused. Explain the actual structure of his argument, saying what sort of thing the pasture is supposed to be. Then explain what you take Ostrom's case studies to have shown about that argument, and say whether you think she refutes it or shows that it was never about what people now use it for."

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

Hardin starts from a problem he borrows from the nuclear strategists: some problems have no technical solution, meaning no fix that leaves our values and our institutions as they are. The commons is his example. He asks us to picture a pasture open to every herdsman in a village. Each herdsman asks himself what he gains from putting one more animal out to graze. The gain is his alone, the whole benefit of the extra animal, whereas the cost of the extra grazing is real but shared with every other herdsman using the pasture, so his share of the cost is only a fraction. Rational herdsmen therefore keep adding animals, and since they are all rational they all do it, until the pasture is ruined. His slogan is that freedom in a commons brings ruin to everybody. The striking thing about this argument is that nobody has to be a bad person: the ruin comes out of the structure rather than out of greed, which is also why Hardin thinks appeals to conscience cant work, since conscience is self-eliminating. The men who ignore the appeal take more of the resource and do better, so the conscientious get selected out over time. That leaves him with only two options, enclosure, meaning you privatise the pasture so the full cost lands on the owner, or coercion that everyone has agreed to in advance, like licensing or taxation. He then uses the same structure for population growth and for pollution, where the commons in question is a sink rather than a source.

The pasture is a public good in the strict sense, non-excludable and non-rival, and that is why exactly the same structure generates the free-rider problem for things like clean air and a stable climate. In each case the individual's contribution disappears against the whole while the benefit of defecting is private, so the tragedy of the commons is really the free-rider problem writ large. That is also why Hardin's argument travelled so far beyond pastures: it was never actually about pastures.

Ostrom's fieldwork is usually said to refute this. She looked at cases such as a Swiss alpine village that had held its pasture in common for centuries, mountain hamlets in Japan doing the same with their wooded slopes, the irrigation systems in Valencia and in the Philippines, and a fishery on the Turkish coast, and in all of them the users had avoided collapse without either privatising anything or being policed by a state. What she gets out of them is a list of design principles: clear boundaries between who is a member and who is not, rules that fit local conditions, users involved in making the rules, monitoring carried out by the users themselves, graduated sanctions rather than expulsion first time, cheap ways of settling disputes, and a tolerance from the wider authorities of the right to organise.

The way I'd put her challenge is that Hardin assumed the herdsmen were selfish maximisers, and that assumption is just false of real communities. Once you see that people are capable of reciprocity, of shame, of caring what the group thinks of them, the arithmetic that makes adding one more animal rational doesn't go through, so the tragedy never gets started in the first place. On this reading she does refute him, but only because he made a claim about human nature that the case studies disprove. There is a limit to it though: her cases are all small and pre-modern, places where everybody knows everybody and can watch each other. At global scale, with the atmosphere, you can't have that, so the tragedy comes back, which is why climate policy ends up looking like Hardin's mutual coercion after all, i.e. treaties with sanctions attached.
