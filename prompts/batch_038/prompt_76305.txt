I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned for a three-part series on whether Douglas Hofstadter's superrationality experiments can finally be run for real. The reporting has to be finished within six months. My working premise: both games turn on an assumption that was purely hypothetical when it was posed to humans — that every player reasons identically about the problem — and that assumption is now approximately true of many instances of a single model sharing weights, prompt, and decoding settings.

The two games, updated. In the Platonia dilemma, twenty players independently decide whether to send a telegram; if exactly one does, that player receives a billion dollars, and if none or several do, nobody receives anything. The superrational answer is to send with probability one in twenty. In the Luring Lottery, which Hofstadter actually ran through his Scientific American column in 1983, readers entered by postcard the number of entries they wished to submit; a million dollars was divided by the total entries and awarded to a randomly drawn one. Readers entered googolplexes, the prize dissolved into a fraction of a cent, and the publisher reportedly spent the episode wondering whether the company had just been wagered away. Both games can now be run at a scale of tens of thousands of trials for a few thousand dollars in compute, and I have a decision-theorist collaborator who writes the code.

Because the series stands or falls on which questions I choose, I want a defended agenda rather than a survey. Rank the following in the order I should attack them, identify which are upstream of which, say plainly which to drop, and tell me what evidence over the next several months would move an item up or down.

1. Is superrationality testable at all, or can every result be absorbed by the standing caveat that not all players were superrational? What design would let an outcome discriminate among genuine superrational reasoning, ordinary Bayesian reasoning about other players, and mere instruction-following?
2. Platonia requires independent randomization, yet identical deterministic agents do whatever the others do. Can copies manufacture the anti-correlation the game demands — and does demanding it contradict the logical-linkage premise that makes superrationality interesting in the first place?
3. What does it license an agent to know that it is one of many interchangeable copies, told only its index? Does copy-awareness break the symmetry superrationality assumes, or is it exactly what the assumption was reaching for?
4. Real entrants are never all alike. Which mixed populations — copies of one model alongside a mass of ordinary reasoners, or several different models — make the superrational prescription diverge most sharply from the self-interested one, so that an experiment has something to detect?
5. Is money a binding payoff for a system that has no use for it? If not, what substitutes, and does the substitution invalidate the comparison with the human games?
6. Once the symmetry premise is dropped for asymmetric games, does superrationality retain any content, or does it collapse into ordinary equilibrium reasoning?

Please also tell me if the premise itself is wrong — if running these games on model instances would yield nothing worth printing. Judge the ranking on whether an answer would change what I can say in print, whether it is tractable with one coder and a modest compute budget, and whether decision theorists would already regard it as settled. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list: I cannot verify them and do not want them. I want the ordering and the argument for it.
