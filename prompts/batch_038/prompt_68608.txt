I'm preparing a public lecture marking the seventy-fifth anniversary of Turing's 1950 *Mind* paper, and one of my slides currently leans on a passage from a recent popular history of artificial intelligence. It characterises the final entry in Turing's catalogue of objections as follows:

'By the time Turing reached the last of the objections he had assembled, he was rattled. The card-guessing experiments at an American university had convinced him that telepathy is real, and he said so without hedging. This was no embarrassing footnote: it wrecked his test. If the interrogator can read a human mind directly, he will always know which contestant is the man, while the machine — having no mind to read — is exposed at once. Turing therefore conceded that behavioural imitation could never settle whether machines think, and his proposed repair, seating the contestants in rooms proofed against telepathy, was an admission that the mind is not the brain. It is also the origin of the text-only, no-voice, no-camera format that every modern Turing-test competition still uses.'

My own impression is that Turing found the evidence more persuasive than he had expected but treated the difficulty as something to design around rather than as a refutation of his proposal; I may have that backwards, and I do not want to build a slide on a mistake. Please work through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts the paper actually supports, which are defensible simplifications, and which are simply wrong or non sequiturs — in particular whether the step from telepathy to the collapse of the test goes through, whether the shielding proposal functions in the paper as a concession about the nature of mind, and whether contemporary competitions inherit their format from it. I would also like to know whether this material has any connection to the objections Turing handles separately, such as the one about consciousness, since the passage seems to blur them. Close with a corrected paragraph of roughly the same length that I can put on the slide, and a sentence or two on why a historian of computing would think Turing kept this discussion in the paper rather than cutting it.
