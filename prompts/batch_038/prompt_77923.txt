Six years ago I retired from a career in analytical chemistry, the last fifteen of them spent running the instrument-development group at a national measurement institute, where my working life consisted of calibrating instruments against reference materials and of writing procedures that other laboratories would follow without understanding them. For four years now I have been reading philosophy seriously, chiefly the philosophy of biology and of science, and I have acquired the beginner's habit of wanting my ideas stress-tested rather than applauded. I have one such idea, and I should like a case built for it to order.

Let me state the idea plainly, since the case must be judged against it. What I have come to call a free-floating rationale is a consideration that (a) genuinely explains why a system, procedure, or arrangement has the features it has rather than others; (b) belongs in the normative register — it says what must be preserved if a certain end is to be achieved — and correctly grounds criticism of something a person did; and yet (c) is not, and at the relevant time could not have been, anyone's reason. Not the designers', who worked from habit and specification sheets; not the users', who follow the procedure on authority; not the system's, which is not the sort of thing that has reasons; and not a group's, since I want no corporate agent in the story. Dennett, as I read him, will ascribe rationales to designs and to whole systems without requiring anyone whose reasons they are; Millikan makes a trait's rationale depend on a history of reproduction; Scanlon holds that reasons are not psychological states at all, so that there can be reason to do something without anyone's having that reason before their mind. I want to know which of these positions my idea actually belongs to, and whether it belongs to any of them.

Please construct a single thought experiment, to the following specifications.

1. Setting: an ordinary technical workplace — a laboratory or a small engineered plant of the kind I worked in. Realistic in its detail; no brains in vats, no zombies, no stipulated omniscient observers, no fiction about future technology.
2. Length: the scenario itself in one paragraph, no more than about 250 words, with the technical specifics concrete enough that I can tell whether you understand the apparatus.
3. The rationale must be statable in one sentence, in normative form.
4. It must do real explanatory work: give me at least one prediction it licenses about a design that was never built and never considered by anyone in the scenario — the mark, for me, of a rationale rather than a redescription of the mechanism.
5. It must ground a criticism: identify a decision made by a named person in the scenario and say why it was a mistake in light of the rationale. And the criticism must not reduce to a conditional about that person's own aims; that things would have gone better had they acted otherwise is available to anyone and proves nothing. Tell me what makes it criticism rather than a hypothetical imperative.
6. No one may have it. This is the hard constraint, and I want it met rather than asserted: the rationale must have been undetectable at the time by anyone, given the data, instruments, and concepts then available, and you must tell me specifically why — who had access to which subsystem, what was and was not logged, what could not yet be measured. Merely unnoticed will not do; unnoticed is easy. That it is later recovered by someone with better tools is not only acceptable but should be part of the case: the discoverer merely reports it and does not thereby become its owner.
7. Forbidden routes: no natural or artificial selection of any kind (no breeding programmes, no directed evolution, no trial-and-error optimization across generations of designs); no goal-directed or self-modifying software; no group agent with a decision procedure, so that the List and Pettit machinery is out; no homunculus, no talk of what the institution believes, no organism metaphors.
8. Do not smuggle in an idealized reasoner — a fully informed observer, a hypothetical rational agent, an "as if" designer — to hold the rationale on someone's behalf.

What the case is for, since this fixes what counts as cheating: I am drafting an essay for a philosophy-of-science reading group in which I argue that a great deal of functional explanation in the working sciences commits us to rationales that no one has, and that this is uncomfortable for any view on which reasons are essentially reasons *for* an agent. The discomfort must be generated by applying the positions to the case. If the description simply announces that no one has the rationale, or if that verdict is a brute stipulation rather than a consequence of the concrete arrangement of who knew what, when, and with which instruments, the case fails, and I would rather be told so.

Deliverables, in this order:

(a) The scenario, one paragraph.
(b) The rationale in one sentence; the prediction about the design never built; and the criticism of the named person's decision, with the answer to specification 5.
(c) The audit. For each of four positions — Dennett's intentional stance and derived intentionality; Millikan's etiological account of proper function; Scanlon's non-psychological normative reasons; and a reasons-responsiveness view of the sort Fischer and Ravizza defend, on which reasons must be capable of being an agent's — say whether the case contains a genuine reason, whose it is if anyone's, and what that position takes my case to show. No labels instead of arguments; and where a position has internal resources to absorb the case, such as a distinction between explanatory and normative reasons or between a rationale and a reason proper, deploy them on its behalf.
(d) The load-bearing feature. Name the single feature of the case doing the most work, then run the ablation: what verdict each position gives once that feature is removed. If removal collapses the case into ordinary design intention, into a selection story, or into a mechanistic redescription, say which.
(e) The strongest objection available to whichever position handles the case worst, put as that position's advocate would put it, together with your assessment of whether it is a challenge to my case or a concession about the position.
(f) A verdict on feasibility. If you judge that any two of my specifications cannot be satisfied together — I suspect specification 5 and specification 6 may be in tension, though I cannot see why — identify the pair, say which should be relaxed, and say what is lost by relaxing it. I would far rather have that than a case which quietly bends one of them.

Please keep to the case and the audit. I have no need of a survey of the literature, and none of a bibliography; I need the design to work and to be shown to work.
