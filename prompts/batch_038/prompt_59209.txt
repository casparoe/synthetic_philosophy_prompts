I'm giving a talk at an early modern workshop in six weeks. Thesis: Berkeley's master argument — the "try to conceive of a tree existing unconceived" move — is not a free-standing reductio against matter. Its a parity argument, and it only has teeth on an opponent who has already conceded that colors, sounds and tastes are mind-dependent while insisting shape, size and motion aren't. So what it licenses is narrower than people assume: not esse est percipi across the board, but the collapse of the opponent's own primary/secondary split. My sketch: if you can conceive of X existing unconceived, then in conceiving it you conceive it; so nothing is conceivable as existing unconceived; and that inference only transfers to real mind-dependence if conceivability is already doing metaphysical work for the opponent — which they let it do for secondary qualities and refuse for primary ones.

The room: mostly Locke and Hume scholars, several holding representationalist readings of Locke, who'll say Berkeley miscasts what Locke actually conceded (powers to produce ideas, not ideas themselves). Plus two Reid people who think common-sense realism settled this in the 1760s and want to know why we're still talking.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet. Six objections I'll realistically get, roughly in the order they'll arrive, phrased in the vocabulary these people use — not the "Berkeley was a solipsist" internet register. For each: a 90-second reply, plus an honest flag on where that reply is thin or where I'd be caught bluffing. Then the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Commit to a ranking by how much damage each objection does and say why; don't lay out options and leave me to pick. Whole thing under 900 words.
