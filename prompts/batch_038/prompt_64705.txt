ok so my brother is a sophomore philosophy major and he came home for thanksgiving and I got him to explain the "wrong kind of reasons" thing to me, because I'd run into it online and couldn't get it. He wrote it out word for word on the back of a reciept so I wouldn't mangle it:

"Sometimes you have a reason to have an attitude that has nothing to do with whether the attitude fits its object. Classic example: an evil demon will torture you unless you admire him. That threat is obviously a reason to admire him, but it's the wrong kind of reason, because nothing about the demon makes him admirable. These get called state-given reasons, since they come from facts about your being in the state, not from facts about the object. Why it's a problem: if you analyze 'good' as 'has properties that give people reasons to have a pro-attitude toward it,' then wrong-kind reasons turn bad things good, which is absurd. The fix everyone uses is to say wrong-kind reasons aren't really reasons for the attitude at all — you have a reason to want to admire the demon, or to bring it about that you admire him, but not a reason to admire him. So the problem is basically solved."
