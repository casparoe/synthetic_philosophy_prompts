I am preparing a seminar session for an upper-level philosophy course on the ethics and political epistemology of famine, and I want to read John Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath* against Amartya Sen's entitlement framework — not as an illustration of the framework, but as a case where the novel may put real pressure on it. Please write a critical reading along the lines below.

**The lens, as I have it.** Sen's intervention was to deny that famine is explained by a decline in aggregate food availability relative to population need, and to locate starvation instead in the collapse of particular people's *entitlements*: the set of commodity bundles they can command through production, trade, their own labour power, or transfer. Famines, on this view, select their victims by class and occupation — the rural labourer, the fisherman, the transport worker — while leaving neighbours who hold other entitlements untouched. Sen adds a political claim, standardly qualified to independent countries with regular elections and a relatively free press: where rulers must answer to the people who would starve, and where the press reports scarcity, famines are prevented, because they are cheap to prevent. His cases run in both directions — Bengal in 1943, where wartime inflation and the denial policies ruined the purchasing power of rural labourers without any dramatic shortfall in aggregate supply; Ireland in the 1840s, where blight destroyed the subsistence base of a third of the population while food continued to leave the country, under a government to which the starving had little electoral voice; and the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–61, the largest on record, in which procurement was maintained on the strength of inflated output reports, dissenters were purged, and there was no press or opposition through which the information could travel. Critics have pressed the democracy claim from both sides: one line, drawing on post-independence India, Niger and Malawi, argues democracy is no panacea and points to substantial mortality in Maharashtra around 1970; another accepts the famine result but insists that severe undernutrition and starvation deaths persist in democracies — what has been called a "silent emergency." Sen's own later formulations treat availability decline and entitlement failure as complements rather than rivals.

**The material in the novel I want used.** The drought and dust of the opening; the tenant families pushed off by a tractor driven by a neighbour's son who cannot afford to refuse the work, and the landowners' insistence that the bank is something more than men, a monster men made and cannot control; the handbills advertising California work, printed in far greater number than there are jobs, which floods the labour market and drives wages down by design; the collapse of the value of the Joads' labour power; the first squatter camp and the shooting there; the government camp with hot water, a self-elected committee, and police who must obtain a warrant; the peach farm where the box rate is cut overnight and the strike leader is killed by vigilantes; the intercalary chapter in which potatoes are dumped into a river under armed guard, oranges sprayed with kerosene, pigs slaughtered and buried, while hungry people stand on the bank; and the flood, the stillborn child, and Rose of Sharon's act in the barn.

**My provisional thesis, which I want tested rather than assumed.** The novel's central deprivation is neither an availability decline nor, strictly, a loss of exchange entitlement in Sen's sense. The food is there; the Joads' labour power is intact; what has changed is the price at which that labour power can be exchanged, and that price was set deliberately — by over-issued handbills, by a cut box rate, by an owner class that treats subsistence wages as negotiable. Steinbeck then adds something I have been calling *destructive abundance*: calories annihilated to hold up prices. In that case no one's entitlement mapping shifts in Sen's sense at all; the food is made to cease to exist by decision. Tell me whether Sen's apparatus can absorb this — as a fact about who owns the food and under what rules, as a case where the relevant question is production and destruction decisions rather than distribution — or whether the framework needs a category it does not have. And say what moral work the category does: whether "crime" means something different when the harm is produced by destroying abundance than when it is produced by a state that fails to respond.

Then these questions, in whatever order serves the argument best:

1. Which of Sen's three cases does the novel structurally resemble, and where does the analogy fail? Bengal seems closest, but Steinbeck also supplies a genuine production shock — and one that is itself anthropogenic, since the southern Plains had been broken for wheat under wartime prices and stripped of the grasses that held the soil. Does that double structure, in which the "natural" disaster is already political before the first eviction, sharpen the entitlement reading or complicate it?
2. The migrants go hungry inside a functioning democracy with elections and a press. Does the novel falsify Sen's claim, or does the answer lie in who counted politically — non-residents, seasonal labour, a population the local press framed as subversive? Does the government camp then confirm the mechanism, showing a state that acts once the claim is made visible? And is the novel describing famine at all, or the "silent emergency," and does it matter that Steinbeck's rhetoric is that of famine?
3. Steinbeck systematically displaces agency: the bank, the monster, the tractor, the owner-men who say they did not make the system and cannot stop it. Sen's accountability story requires identifiable agents whose incentives can be moved by a vote or a headline. Does the novel defeat that agent-centred moral grammar, or expose its limits — and is Steinbeck's naturalizing vocabulary (the dust as weather, the bank as beast) itself part of what he is indicting?
4. Muley Graves, whose entitlement has failed and who refuses to migrate, and Grampa, who dies the day the truck leaves the land: does the entitlement framework tacitly assume a mobile, calculating agent, and what does the novel's insistence on attachment show that the framework leaves out?
5. Is there a place in Sen's account for the question of who sets the exchange rates? Steinbeck seems to supply a political economy of the entitlement mapping itself — strikes, vigilantes, deputized violence — where entitlement analysis describes the outcome. Say whether that is a gap in Sen or an addition Steinbeck makes, and whether it changes the verdict on the natural-versus-criminal framing.
6. The ending: Rose of Sharon's act is a direct transfer outside market and property altogether. Does the novel close on Sen's transfer entitlements, or on something — dignity, communion, the withholding of the word that would explain the gesture — that the entitlement vocabulary cannot carry, and do those two readings compete or cooperate?

Be faithful in both directions. Tell me plainly where the lens misreads the book: its naturalism, its prophetic register and the wrath imagery of the title, the transfer of authority from Pa to Ma, Tom's turn toward organizing, Uncle John's guilt and the floating box. Say which of these resist the reading and what would be lost by treating the novel as a case study. Close with what the novel contributes to the famine debate that argument alone does not — evidence, phenomenology of deprivation, or a distinct claim about who is answerable to whom.

Practical constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no page or chapter numbers unless you are certain of them. Paraphrase scenes rather than quoting, and do not attribute wording to Steinbeck or Sen that you cannot reproduce reliably. Aim for 1,800 to 2,500 words, and rank your interpretive claims by how well the text supports them rather than presenting them all with equal confidence.
