Been arguing with a friend for like three hours about Lewis's counterfactual account of causation versus the interventionist stuff (Woodward, Pearl's do-operator) and neither of us budged. His line is that the two are notational variants: any counterfactual claim can be read as a claim about what a hypothetical intervention would change and vice versa, and the closest-world similarity metric is just an unanalyzed stand-in for "holding the background conditions fixed," so the whole debate is like arguing about whether to write coordinates as (x,y) or (y,x). My line is that there's a real difference, since an intervention is itself defined counterfactually — a change that would set the variable without otherwise disturbing the system — so interventionism is either circular or it isnt a metaphysics of causation at all, just a methodology for building models, and the stalemate is because everyone's talking past each other about what the job even is.

Who's right, or is that the wrong question? I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this verbal, a clash about what a theory of causation is supposed to deliver, a difference about which primitive you're allowed to leave unexplained, or a hidden formal issue doing the work? And then give me a concrete case where the two accounts actually come apart and yield different answers — non-modular mechanisms? omissions and absences as causes? the worry that two distinct graphs can encode the same dependency structure but disagree about what caused what? — or, if you think no such case can exist, argue for that and say whether it means the debate is dead or just that its hard.
