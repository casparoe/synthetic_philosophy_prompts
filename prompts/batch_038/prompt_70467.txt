I cover behavioral science and social research for a weekly magazine, and an argumentative pattern on my beat has begun to bother me enough that I want to know whether it has a name. I will describe it concretely rather than in the abstract, because the concreteness is where the difficulty lies.

A profile piece establishes that people born within a particular eighteen-year window rarely answer unsolicited phone calls. The explanation offered is a formative one: this cohort came of age when a ringing phone was already an intrusion, or when texting had become the polite default. So far, an observation with a plausible story attached. But three paragraphs later the same piece predicts that this cohort will make poor delegators as managers, on the grounds that delegation requires unstructured real-time contact. Nothing about managing has been observed or reported. The warrant for the second claim appears to be inherited entirely from the first.

A second instance. A columnist argues that the cohort which entered the job market during a severe downturn is permanently cautious about money, and then, within the same argument, has them marrying later, having fewer children, founding fewer businesses, and drifting toward whichever party promises stability. Again the only disposition actually checked against evidence is one; the remainder are presented as following from it, as though a single well-documented habit were a window onto a settled character.

The part I have no language for is what happens when those downstream predictions fail. A member of the cautious cohort founds a startup, or a member of the phone-averse cohort turns out to talk on the phone constantly, and the label is not adjusted. Either the case is treated as an exception that proves nothing, or the person's membership is quietly revised: they are an old soul, they were really on the cusp, they belong to the adjacent cohort in temperament. The boundaries shift to absorb the disconfirmation, and because they can shift, the label is never at risk. Yet throughout, the label is being used the way one would use a claim about a substance — as though knowing which cohort someone belongs to told you something reliable about their unobserved properties.

I am aware that this sits near well-trodden ground: the philosophical question of what licenses inferring the unobserved properties of a thing from its category, and the social-psychological literature on how a generalization about a group responds to counterexamples. But every term I have reached for in those areas seems to cover only a slice of what I am describing, and none of them captures the combination.

So, three things. First: does this configuration have an established name of its own? Second: if it does not, coin one and define it precisely enough that I could distinguish it from an ordinary group generalization being applied — which at least remains revisable — and from a legitimate inference about a category that genuinely does support projections of this kind. I would want a boundary case on each side, and an account of what makes the difference between them. Third, and most important to me: is this move defective only because generations are probably not real kinds, or would it remain defective even if they were? Suppose we had good evidence that cohorts do share durable dispositions traceable to common formative circumstances — would the pattern above then be vindicated, or would something still be going wrong? I want an argument on that question, not a label.

If it strikes you that I have actually bundled two distinct phenomena that deserve separate names and separate treatments, say so and divide them. I do not need a bibliography; I will track down sources myself. What I need is the carving.
