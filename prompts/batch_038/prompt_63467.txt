No philosophy background here — I work part time at a tiny charity that hands out emergency grants, rent arrears and car repairs mostly, so people don't lose their jobs over one bad month. On my own time I've been reading about fairness, luck and desert for a couple of years now, whatever's free online, and lately I've been reading the stuff that says the difference that matters is whether your bad situation is down to luck or down to your own choices.

There's a pattern I keep hitting at work and I cant find a word for it, and I've started to suspect nobody has one.

The whole test we run is basically that one: bad luck, or the fallout of your own decisions? And three things happen every single time we try to apply it.

First, the more carefully you check, the worse it is for the person being checked. Proving you weren't at fault means narrating your worst decisions out loud to a stranger across a desk, with paperwork, and the ones who come through it fine are the ones who've learned how to tell it. I've watched regulars get visibly better at performing helpless over two years.

Second, the people who'd most hate doing that don't come. So the screening doesn't just measure who's responsible, it selects for who's willing to be measured, and the fund ends up going disproportionately to the performers and not to the proud, and the proud are often the ones with the least. Which then makes the whole thing look like it's working when you check the numbers, because the numbers only contain the people who agreed to be counted.

Third — this is the bit that really gets me — the question we're asking doesn't seem to have an answer we could ever reach, even in principle. The plant closed and he lost his job: bad luck, or he wouldn't take the transfer to another state? Her car died: bad luck, or she should have been saving for repairs instead of what she did spend it on? Every case I've ever sat through can be told both ways with equal honesty, and which telling wins depends on the mood in the room. So we're not tracking a real distinction, we're choosing a sympathetic description and calling it a finding. And all of that costs money and goodwill out of a fund that was already smaller than the demand.

So: does this have a name? I know the neighbours. I know means-testing and its stigma, I know moral hazard and adverse selection, I know there's some argument floating around about whether a theory of who deserves what can be run by an actual institution that has to decide about actual people. But each of those only gets one of my three items, and none of them gets the shape of the thing, which is that the distinction the approach depends on can't be drawn by anyone who's obliged to draw it, and the trying makes the honest ones leave and the performers stay.

Is that a recognized problem in its own right, with its own name? If it is, I want the name and the standard replies to it. If it isn't — if people have only ever noticed the pieces separately — then name it for me, and define it tight enough that I can tell it apart from means-test stigma and from moral hazard, and give me the edges: what would a case look like that seems like this but isn't, and where does it stop applying? A bare label won't help me, I need the carving.
