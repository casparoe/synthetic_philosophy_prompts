I'm a third-year PhD student in normative ethics, and in the spring I'm running a six-week graduate reading group on the aggregation problem within Scanlonian contractualism — the question of how a framework that assesses principles by comparing individuals' claims one at a time, rather than by adding across persons, is supposed to handle cases where the numbers seem to matter. For the opening session I want a diagnostic instrument rather than another summary of the debate: a decision tree that takes a participant from a shared starting commitment to a named position on whether, when, and how much the numbers count.

Please build the tree around two anchor cases that I will supply, and which you should describe concretely rather than by their usual labels: (A) we can either prevent one identifiable person's death or prevent five other people's deaths, with no further difference between the six; (B) we can either prevent one person's death or prevent a billion people from each receiving a brief, painful but trivially minor shock. Everyone in the room is expected to reach a verdict on both; the interesting work is in what has to be true for those two verdicts to be jointly principled.

The tree should have nine to twelve decision points, and it must cover at least the following axes, in whatever order you judge best: whether a complaint that differs in kind from another has any exchange rate against it at all; what the unit of complaint is — the person, the whole life, or the momentary stage; whether the number of people holding a claim ever carries independent weight or only breaks ties between claims that are already equal; whether a burden can be too small to register as a complaint at all, and whether that threshold is about magnitude or about kind; whether a policy that gives each of a million people a one-in-a-million chance of death generates a complaint in the risk itself or only in the outcomes it might produce, and whether comparison is made before or after the uncertainty resolves; and whether the prohibition on pooling claims is grounded in the separateness of persons, in the public justifiability of principles, or in nothing deeper than the mechanics of pairwise comparison.

Two constraints on phrasing. First, every node must be answerable by someone who has never seen the literature: pose each as a case, a choice, or a concrete judgment, and do not let any node turn on a technical term. You may use the specialized vocabulary freely in your own exposition — the individualist restriction, pairwise comparison, the priority view — but not inside a question the participant answers. Second, build the space before you populate it: give me first the axes you are using, with a sentence each on why none is entailed by the others, and only then the tree.

At each leaf, provide a short label for the position (a philosopher's name in parentheses is fine where it is the standard shorthand — Taurek's coin-flipping view, Broome's fairness-based equal chance, Parfit's priority view, Scanlon's tie-breaking account, and so on — plus any leaf that has no defender), a one-sentence statement of its core claim, what that view is committed to saying about both anchor cases and about the risk case, and its single most awkward commitment. The leaves must include at least the following: a view on which numbers never count; a weighted-lottery variant; unrestricted additive aggregation; priority-weighted aggregation; pairwise comparison with numbers only as tie-breakers; a de minimis view on which sub-threshold complaints drop out entirely; a capped or limited-aggregation view on which numbers count up to a point and then stop; and contractualism with the individualist restriction abandoned outright.

Finally, three pieces of housekeeping that matter more to me than the tree itself. Identify every unoccupied combination of answers your axes permit, and say for each whether it is empty because the view would be incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply explored it yet. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate — I suspect the priority view and capped aggregation will be extensionally equivalent across a wide range of cases, and I want to know where they diverge, if anywhere. And state plainly where the tree fails the test that two participants giving the same answers at every node reach the same leaf. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the point is the structure of the space, not who occupies it.
