I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, and I'm preparing a short internal talk for a software team on the way moral evaluation leaks into judgments that feel purely descriptive. My hook is the familiar asymmetry in ordinary judgments about intentional action: when a foreseen side effect of a profit-seeking decision is bad, people tend to call it intentional, and when it is good, they tend not to. I don't want to restate that result. I want a case that runs the other way.

Design a matched pair of vignettes in the same plain corporate style that reverses the pattern, so the good side effect is judged intentional and the bad one is not. Constraints: an ordinary organizational setting with no science-fiction machinery; the two vignettes identical except for one feature, which you must name; the same foresight, indifference, effort, skill, and causal contribution in both; and the reversal must not come from altering how good or bad the side effects are, from making the agent secretly want either outcome, or from turning one side effect into a means rather than a byproduct. Any of those would defeat the point of the talk. Keep each vignette under roughly 100 words.

Then show the design works. Name the manipulated variable and the mechanism by which it should flip the intuition. State how three accounts of the standard effect fare: the view that the word is doing moral work, the view that it tracks whether an outcome expresses attitudes the agent reflectively endorses, and the view that the whole effect is a pragmatic artifact of how the question is put. Finish by saying which feature carries the weight, what happens to the case if you delete it, and how someone convinced the asymmetry is purely blame-driven would attack your vignettes. If you conclude a genuine reversal is unattainable under these constraints, build the strongest case available and identify exactly which constraint blocks the flip.
