I'm in a philosophy-of-social-science seminar this term, and last week's session was on falsification, auxiliary assumptions, and long-run prediction. Afterwards two of the other students — I'll call them Owen and Camille — got into a prolonged argument in the common room about Malthus, specifically about whether he has been refuted or whether he is better described as "right but early." They asked me to referee. I took notes as they went, I could not decide, and I would now like a proper adjudication rather than another round of the argument.

You are to act as a philosopher of science who also has real training in the history of economic thought, and I want you to think carefully and treat both of them as intelligent rather than as foils. Please do not summarize Malthus's life or the Essay for me, and do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I have the book and I know the history. What I want is judgment on the arguments as they were actually made.

Here is the exchange, reconstructed from my notes in eight moves.

(1) Owen opened by saying the theory is straightforwardly falsified. Malthus's core claim was that population, left unchecked, grows on a geometric progression while the food a given territory can yield grows only arithmetically, so that any improvement in output is eaten by the additional mouths and wages are dragged back to subsistence. Since 1800, per-capita food supply and real wages in the industrializing world have risen by multiples, and fertility fell precisely as incomes rose — the mechanism did not merely fail to fire, it ran in reverse. A prediction whose predicted causal process was observed to operate backwards is a refuted prediction.

(2) Camille said that this refutes a theory Malthus never held, because the claim was never unconditional. What he described is the behaviour of a population pressing against a fixed state of the arts. The entire post-1800 record is a record of the state of the arts not being fixed, so the escape clause was engaged rather than violated. And where the condition did hold, the dynamics did run: for centuries before 1800 real incomes in the most advanced parts of Europe were essentially flat, and famine, epidemic disease, and late marriage did the checking work. On her account the theory is confirmed across its entire actual domain of application, and merely inapplicable outside it.

(3) Owen replied that this is textbook immunisation. A theory that is confirmed by flat wages and excused by rising wages cannot lose, and an argument that accommodates every possible outcome by retreating into an unstated clause has not been corroborated, it has been rescued. Worse, he said, the clause is not in the text and cannot be. The Essay was written against Godwin and Condorcet, whose whole thesis was that human ingenuity would progressively remove scarcity; Malthus's point was exactly that ingenuity cannot keep pace, because of the geometry of mouths against the arithmetic of land. If the fixed-technology reading were correct, the Essay would have had no target and no argument at all. He then read aloud the closing passage of Chapter VII of the 1798 edition: "The power of population is so superior to the power of the earth to produce subsistence for man, that premature death must in some shape or other visit the human race. The vices of mankind are active and able ministers of depopulation. They are the precursors in the great army of destruction, and often finish the dreadful work themselves. But should they fail in this war of extermination, sickly seasons, epidemics, pestilence, and plague advance in terrific array, and sweep off their thousands and tens of thousands. Should success be still incomplete, gigantic inevitable famine stalks in the rear, and with one mighty blow levels the population with the food of the world." That, he said, is not a conditional claim about a hypothetical fixed technology; it is a claim about what must happen.

(4) Camille said he was conflating a forecast with a mechanism, and that the mechanism should be judged on its own support. It is just the logic of any species in a bounded niche, which is why both Darwin and Wallace said that Malthus gave them the struggle-for-existence idea — a mechanism strong enough to found a theory in another discipline is not an abandoned forecast. She added that the later editions introduce moral restraint, the deliberate postponement of marriage, as a preventive check alongside misery and vice; the demographic transition is that check operating at civilizational scale, adopted for the reason Malthus identified and averting the catastrophe he feared. On the "right but early" reading, what failed was his estimate of how quickly the checks could be taken up voluntarily, not his account of what the checks were.

(5) Owen said that this compounded the problem. First, the Darwin-and-Wallace point cuts against her: a mechanism that holds of bacteria in a flask is silent about the timing of human history, and "true, but says nothing about when" is precisely the unfalsifiable position he is objecting to. Second, "right but early" is available to the loser of every long-run prediction ever made, which is a good reason to think it is not a verdict. He cited the 1980 wager between Paul Ehrlich and Julian Simon on the inflation-adjusted price of a thousand dollars' worth of chromium, copper, nickel, tin, and tungsten: Ehrlich chose the metals and lost, since all five fell by 1990 — but the same wager placed in 1960 rather than 1980 would very likely have been won by Ehrlich, given how those metal prices moved through the sixties and seventies. If the outcome of a fixed-date bet depends on which decade you happen to select, the theory never contained a date; and a theory with no date has nothing that could refute it.

(6) Camille granted part of that and turned it around: the sensitivity to the window is an argument for evaluating theories by their mechanisms rather than by dated forecasts, which is her position. She then charged him with an asymmetry in his ledger. He accepts "refuted" the instant a forecast fails, but refuses "confirmed" when the mechanism keeps operating, calling that vacuous. And it keeps operating: the resource-constraint modelling published by the Club of Rome in 1972 was the same ratio logic with the environment substituted for farmland as the fixed factor. A theory that you credit only when it wins and discredit when it does not is being scored with a rigged book.

(7) Owen said the environmental substitution is a replacement, not a confirmation. The original mechanism was about land and food; swapping in carbon capacity while retaining the label is how a dead theory buys an afterlife under its own name. If any bounded-resource model whatsoever counts as a vindication of Malthus, then Malthusianism is not a theory but a metaphor — and metaphors cannot be refuted, which is his complaint in the first place.

(8) Camille said his side was doing the same thing. He counts the demographic transition as a refutation, but fertility falling as income rises is what the preventive-check story predicts once the check is chosen rather than imposed. So he treats the transition as evidence against Malthus when discussing the transition, and as evidence of rescue when Malthus's own mechanism is invoked to explain it. He cannot, she said, use the same historical episode twice in opposite directions.

Neither budged, and the porters closed the room.

What I would like from you:

1. Before scoring anything, spend a few sentences diagnosing what kind of disagreement this actually is. Say what would have to be true for Owen to win outright, what would have to be true for Camille to win outright, and whether there is any reading on which they are not in fact contradicting each other. If you think one of them has silently shifted the subject partway through, identify where.

2. Then go move by move, (1) through (8), and rate each one on a scale of clearly mistaken, partly right but overstated, roughly right, and clearly right. For each, give two or three sentences justifying the rating, and state explicitly where a move's force depends on a premise that is itself contestable or on an equivocation between two senses of a term. I want you to be as willing to penalise Camille's moves as Owen's, and to say when a move is a legitimate hit rather than a rhetorical one. Move (8) in particular deserves care: determine whether Owen really is using the demographic transition in two incompatible ways, or whether there is a distinction available to him that Camille has collapsed.

3. Then give an overall verdict. Who is more right, and about what specifically? If the honest answer is that they are talking past each other, say so and reconstruct the dispute they should have been having — but do not settle for a both-sides shrug; I want a ranking of the moves and a stated winner, with the margin explained.

4. Then do the general work, which is what I actually need for the seminar. Formulate a criterion that distinguishes a legitimate "right but early" verdict from an immunising rescue: what has to be true of a theory, and of the later evidence, for it to be correct to say it was right ahead of its time rather than merely unfalsifiable? Make the criterion concrete enough that it could be applied by two people who disagree and yield the same answer. Then test it on a case that seems to be a genuine instance: Aristarchus's heliocentrism was rejected in antiquity partly because no stellar parallax could be detected, and the objection was only dissolved when Bessel measured the parallax of 61 Cygni in 1838 with instruments unimaginably better than anything available to the original critics. Say whether that case satisfies your criterion, and then say whether Malthus does or does not, and locate precisely where the disanalogy lies — if you think there is one.

5. Finally, tell me what both of them missed. I have a suspicion that neither addressed the reflexive character of a prediction of this kind: the Essay was enormously widely read, and may have helped motivate the very responses — agricultural research, the census, and later the advocacy of contraception — that averted the outcome it forecast. Work out whether a prediction that defeats itself by being published should count as confirmed, refuted, or as belonging to a third category, and say which of the two, if either, has the resources to handle that. Note also anything else material they both left on the table, including whether a theory can be true and yet fail to earn the title of a scientific theory simply because it does not constrain what may happen.

Please keep the whole thing disciplined: no throat-clearing, no biography, no list of further reading, and no hedged summary at the end restating what you have already said.
