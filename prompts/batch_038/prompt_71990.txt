I'm drafting a paper on why the illusionist-versus-realist dispute about phenomenal consciousness has not moved in thirty years, and I'd like a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure rather than another summary of the positions or a verdict on which side is right.

Here is the shape of the stalemate as I see it. The illusionist side — Dennett, and Keith Frankish in his more recent systematization — holds that introspection is a kind of inner detection whose deliverances can misrepresent, that the properties which supposedly make experience feel like something from the inside are posits doing no explanatory work, and that once you strip them out nothing remains that science cannot handle. The realist side — Chalmers most prominently, with older allies in Searle and in Georges Rey's opponents — replies that the argument never engages the datum: whatever else experience is, there is something it is like to have it, and a theory that says this seeming is systematic misrepresentation has explained the appearance of phenomenal character only by presupposing the very thing it denies. Both sides then trade the same two moves. The illusionist points to the empirical record of introspective failure — change blindness, inattentional blindness, the gap between the felt richness of the visual field and what subjects can actually report — as evidence that inner detection is unreliable in principle. The realist answers that these are failures about the world-directed content of perception, not about phenomenal character, and that the illusionist has no comparable case in which a subject is wrong about how an experience feels.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that the dispute has two separable layers. The first is largely verbal: "phenomenal property" is stipulated by the illusionist to mean something intrinsic, ineffable, and incorrigibly known, precisely so that it can be eliminated, while the realist uses it for the residue common to all cases of experiencing, which no stipulation reaches. The second layer is substantive and concerns whether introspection has any authority that is not parasitic on the reports it generates. I also suspect, though I can't tell whether this is an insight or a confusion, that the whole debate has a self-undermining structure: the illusionist's evidence consists entirely of introspective reports, which is the very instrument her theory impeaches, making the position reflexively awkward in the way skepticism about perception is.

Please tell me what kind of disagreement this really is. Are the two sides answering different questions under one label, disputing the semantics of "seems," disagreeing about what counts as evidence, or resting on bedrock intuitions neither can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, and if so, name it precisely enough that I could see what result would settle it? To make the abstraction concrete: suppose a subject is trained over months to report a faint tint on blank screens, comes to do so reliably, and can discriminate cued from uncued screens better than chance, though nothing of the sort is on the screen. Does that vindicate illusionism about one particular quale, or does it show only that reports are trainable — and if the latter, why does the same conclusion not generalize to every introspective report? Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a change of question, or nothing at all, and explain why.

Begin with your bottom line — a single sentence naming the kind of disagreement you think this is — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of my question. And if you think my framing is itself confused, for instance because the verbal-versus-substantive split I'm proposing doesn't carve this dispute at any real joint, or because a diagnosis of this sort is a way of dodging a position one ought to take, say so plainly instead of working politely inside my assumptions.
