I am drafting a novel about a woman who, after a head injury, loses the capacity to forget, and I have been setting Borges's "Funes the Memorious" beside von Neumann's The Computer and the Brain to decide what such a mind would actually be like. What I want is a reading of the story through von Neumann's account of the nervous system as a peculiar kind of computing machine, and above all the places where the two come apart. Please address four of them: Funes seems to have only the innermost layer of a memory that von Neumann pictures as organized in tiers, small and fast near the arithmetical organ and vast and slow further out; his system of enumeration, which gives every number its own arbitrary name, read against von Neumann's claim that the nervous system speaks a statistical idiom of few symbols and shallow logical depth, nothing like the language of mathematics; his gift for naming the hour to the minute without a watch, given that von Neumann expects precision to be bought out of many unreliable, parallel channels rather than produced directly by any of them; and von Neumann's estimate that only a minute fraction of the brain's storage ever reaches consciousness, which recasts Funes's condition less as a surplus than as the collapse of an ordinary filtering. Then tell me what in the story this idiom cannot absorb, and what it therefore puts into the philosophical question that argument alone would not reach.

Give me exactly five numbered points, the first four answering the four frictions above in order and the fifth on the excess, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
