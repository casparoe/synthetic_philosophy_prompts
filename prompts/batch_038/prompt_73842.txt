I chair the clinical ethics consultation service at a teaching hospital, and we have to decide our hardest advance-directive case in four years. I want you to reason from our own file rather than from general principle, because we will be asked afterwards why we decided as we did.

Our record, abbreviated:

1. A man with advanced ALS whose directive refused mechanical ventilation. He was ventilator-dependent after an acute crisis but able to signal refusal consistently through an eye-gaze device. We honored the directive and withdrew ventilation.

2. A woman who at 62, fully competent, refused artificial nutrition and hydration once she no longer recognized her children and there was no reasonable hope of recovery. Nine years later she met that description; she appeared contented, smiled at music, showed no signs of distress. When she developed dysphagia we honored the directive and declined a feeding tube. Her family told us she would have found the alternative unbearable.

3. A woman whose directive refused any life-prolonging treatment if she became, in her words, a shell of herself. She had always been easygoing and took pleasure in small things; in the year before the consultation she had told staff repeatedly that she was happy and delighted in visits from her grandchildren. We declined to honor the directive and authorized antibiotics for her pneumonia.

4. A patient with schizoaffective disorder who between episodes signed a directive asking to be medicated even if she objected when acutely ill. When the episode came she objected strenuously and was in obvious distress. We honored the directive over her present objection.

5. A man who at 45 refused dialysis if he ever lost the ability to make his own decisions. At 78, with moderate dementia, he could no longer manage his finances but recognized his wife, asked about his garden, and enjoyed visits. When his kidneys failed we declined to honor the directive and began dialysis.

The new case: a woman diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's at 58. At 59, competent and after two counseling sessions with her husband present, she refused antibiotics and hospital transfer for any intercurrent infection once she could no longer hold a conversation with someone she loved and could no longer read. She crossed that threshold two years ago. She is not distressed: she gardens with a volunteer, sings weekly, and greets her husband warmly, though she no longer knows his name. She now has pneumonia. Her husband tells us that this is precisely the moment she described, and then adds that the woman who wrote the directive is not the woman sitting in the chair, and that the woman sitting in the chair seems glad to be alive.

Please give us: (a) which precedent is the closest analog for honoring the directive and which is closest for declining it, with the respects of similarity and difference stated explicitly; (b) two or three general rules that would explain all five of our past rulings, each stated so that it does genuinely account for all five, and each paired with a note on what it makes decisive — the fit between the condition described and the condition present, the balance between the interests the directive protects and the interests the patient now has, the persistence or rupture of the valuing self, or something else you think is doing the work; (c) what each rule implies for the new case, including any rule that comes out indeterminate here and why; (d) your recommendation to the committee, with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others given how we have actually ruled rather than how we might have; and (e) one hypothetical case that has never come before us which, had it arisen, would have forced our record to discriminate between these rules. Aim for roughly 900 words. No bibliography or references — we want your reasoning about our file, not a survey of the literature.
