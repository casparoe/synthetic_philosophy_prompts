I spent thirty-four years in observational geophysics, retired three years ago, and have since been doing what retired scientists are supposed to do: reading philosophy, badly and out of order. Lately I have been reading about archives — who appears in them, who does not, and what historians think they are entitled to conclude from an absence. Two things from my former life keep intruding. The first is that much of the long sea-surface temperature record I used to work with was assembled from the logbooks of merchant and naval vessels, which means the ocean is sampled thickly along the shipping lanes that empires found profitable and almost not at all elsewhere; the Southern Ocean is close to blank, and which blank patches have since been filled in depended largely on which national meteorological offices funded digitization. The second is that in an observatory logbook a blank field is ambiguous between "no observation attempted" and "observation attempted, nothing to report," and the whole of your inference turns on which it was. It seems to me that a great deal of what is now written about archival silences is gesturing at exactly this structure without ever being obliged to state it precisely, and I would like the specification before I read another page of it.

So: write the desiderata for a theory of archival silence — by which I mean a theory that tells us what may and may not be inferred from what a body of records does and does not contain, and what role power plays in determining that. For each requirement give the statement, the case or consideration that motivates it, and whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference characteristic of a particular camp. Do not simply adopt the following typology I have sketched for myself; test it, and revise it if it cuts the phenomenon at the wrong joints. My draft distinguishes five mechanisms behind a gap: *generative* silence, where no record was ever made because nobody in a position to record was present or recording was forbidden; *custodial* silence, where records were made but discarded, never collected, or lost, so that the archive's boundary was drawn somewhere else; *classificatory* silence, where records survive and are held but are filed under headings that render them unfindable or unreadable as what they actually document; *credibility* silence, where the record is findable but its maker is structurally discredited as a witness, so that it cannot do evidential work of the kind a comparable record would do; and *hermeneutic* silence, where the record is present, findable and credited, but the concepts required to recognise what it testifies to did not exist then or do not exist now — the complaint entered in a ledger as insolence that a later vocabulary would read as something else entirely. I am not confident the fifth is a genuine kind rather than a subspecies of the fourth.

Among the requirements I would expect you to consider, and to accept or reject with reasons: that the theory let a practitioner discriminate which mechanism produced a given gap using evidence available from inside the archive together with independent sources, since a typology that cannot be applied is decoration; that it state when an absence raises or lowers the probability of a hypothesis and roughly how much, and how it copes with the awkward case where the recording process is itself the object of inquiry and so cannot be independently characterised; that it be applicable reflexively to the evidence the theorist uses to establish that silencing occurred, including their own preferred sources; that it not entail that every archive is unusable or that nothing can be known of the silenced, since that would defeat the recovery work it is meant to serve; that it keep distinct the claim that an archive is a biased sample from the claim that it is an instrument of power, and say whether either entails the other, because my impression is that the literature slides freely between them; that it handle both a single fonds and a multi-repository corpus spanning centuries, and also born-digital holdings where deletion is trivial and the metadata is often the record; that it specify what would count as evidence against its own diagnosis of a particular silence; and that it be operable by a working historian with a month in a reading room rather than only by a philosopher with a thought experiment. Then tell me which subset of these, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect that licensing quantitative inference from absence and treating absence as evidence of domination pull in opposite directions, and if the list itself is the result I would rather know that than receive a harmonised one. Finally, and briefly, how the main families of approach actually on offer fare against the list; describe them by what they claim rather than by whom they were said, and give me no bibliography, no citations and no attributions, since I cannot check them and I would much prefer an unnamed account to a guessed one.

One request about manner. I would rather be corrected than accommodated. If you think the framing is a category mistake — if treating silence as an inference problem is precisely the way to miss what is at issue, or if a natural scientist's instinct to reduce all of this to selection effects and detection limits is the wrong instrument here — say so plainly at the outset and tell me what I should be asking instead, rather than producing a careful answer to a question you think should not have been put that way. Please number the requirements, tag each as non-negotiable or camp-relative, and end the response with a final line in exactly the form Verdict: followed by the label of the single requirement that does most to exclude candidate theories.
