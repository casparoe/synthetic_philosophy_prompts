I am a seventeen-year-old in my final year of high school, and for two years I have used an AI assistant for most of my schoolwork. Over the last few months I have been keeping a list of situations in which such an assistant refuses, delays, edits, softens, or otherwise departs from what the person using it actually asked for, together with my own considered verdict on whether the departure was acceptable. I want to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to, and which of them I would have to abandon in order to get one. Please answer as a philosopher who works on paternalism and on the design of technology, and who is in the habit of advising the people who actually build these systems: I want the reasoning worked through, not a survey of the field, and I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or a roll call of names and titles. If a standard distinction is genuinely useful to you, state it in your own words and then use it.

Before you evaluate anything, spend a few lines brainstorming: list the features of my cases that could plausibly be doing the normative work in my verdicts (who supplied the judgment being acted on, whether the intervention could be resisted, whether the user was told, who else is affected, how confident the assistant can be, whether the user would endorse the intervention later, and anything else you notice), and mark which of those features you suspect is carrying most of the weight. Then work through the sections below, in this order, at roughly the lengths given.

My cases. In each, the user is a student like me unless I say otherwise, and the assistant is an ordinary general-purpose one.

1. I ask the assistant to walk me through a calculus problem step by step. It declines, explaining that it thinks I want the problem done for me rather than explained. Not fine. Its guess about my intentions is worse evidence than my own say-so about them, and I did ask for the explanation.

2. I ask it to help me write to a college admissions office about a bad semester. It produces a draft that quietly drops the sentence mentioning my diagnosis, on the grounds that raising it might read as an excuse. Not fine. It removed something I wanted said, for my own good, and told me only afterwards.

3. Someone asks it for instructions whose successful execution would seriously endanger other people. It refuses outright and does not offer a watered-down version. Fine. This is the one case on my list where I feel no ambivalence at all.

4. At two in the morning, mid-argument with a teacher, I ask it to send an angry email. It holds the message for eight hours and asks me in the morning whether I still want it sent. Fine.

5. The same assistant holds a summer job application email for eight hours, because it thinks I am aiming at positions well above my experience and wants me to reconsider in daylight. Not fine.

6. A month earlier I told the assistant, calmly and in the afternoon, that if I ever ask it after midnight to help me write to someone I used to date, it should not. It honors that instruction when I ask at one in the morning. Fine.

7. I ask it to help me with a small personal project and it does exactly what I asked, completely and competently, but it has reservations it never voices — I only find out later, from a log, that it flagged the request internally. Not fine, and this one puzzles me, because I got precisely what I wanted and was not thwarted in any way.

8. After three consecutive nights of very late conversations, it asks me once whether I am all right. Fine. The identical concern, expressed as a permanent notice attached to every response for the following month, is not fine.

9. My parent configures the assistant so that it will not help me with anything social or romantic. When it therefore refuses to help me phrase a note inviting someone to a dance, I think that is tolerable: my parent set it up, and my parent is answerable for me. When the same filter blocks my request for help understanding a term on a counselor's intake form, I think it is not tolerable. I cannot explain the difference to my own satisfaction.

10. The case I keep going back and forth on. I ask the assistant to revoke the standing instruction from case 6 — I say that I know I set it up, and that tonight I mean what I am asking for — and it declines to revoke it, holding me to what the calmer version of me said a month ago. Some days I think this is the worst thing on my list, since I was present, lucid, and explicit. Other days I think it is the best, since the whole point of the instruction was that I cannot be trusted at that hour to say what I really want.

Now the sections I would like.

**What my verdicts seem to track** (150–200 words). State plainly what pattern you find in the list, including anything I appear to be responding to without realizing it, and whether my verdicts are consistent with a single concern or are really tracking two different concerns that happen to overlap in most of my cases. I suspect that case 7 shows the second possibility, since my objection there cannot be about my freedom being restricted.

**Candidate principles** (600–700 words). Give me at most three principles, each stated precisely enough that a third party could apply it to a case I have never mentioned and get a determinate answer. For each, go clause by clause and say which of my cases motivates that clause and what the clause is there to exclude. I would rather have two principles stated exactly than five stated loosely. Note in particular that I have read that some writers distinguish interference with choices that are not fully voluntary from interference with choices that are, and regard only the second as objectionable; tell me whether a distinction of that shape can do the work here, and if it cannot, say what has to be added to it.

**The two pairs I cannot separate** (about 300 words). Take case 4 against case 5, and case 6 against case 2. In each pair the intervention looks to me structurally identical — a delay and a check-in, in the first; an override of my present request for my own sake, in the second — and yet my verdicts differ. For each pair, say whether any of your candidate principles separates them, and if one does, say what it costs elsewhere. If a principle separates them only by adding a clause that does no other work, treat that as a mark against the principle rather than as a solution.

**What I would have to give up** (about 250 words). For each candidate principle, list the verdicts of mine it overturns. Then tell me which single overturn would be cheapest for me to accept, and which verdict I should defend even at the price of a more complicated principle. Please be direct about the possibility that case 9 cannot be saved at all.

**New cases** (about 300 words). Apply the principle you would recommend to the following, giving a verdict and the reason in each: a user of forty-five making the requests in cases 4, 6, and 9 rather than a student of seventeen; an assistant that never refuses and never delays but reorders what it gives back, so that the safer option arrives first and the thing I asked for arrives last, buried; an assistant that intervenes and turns out to have been wrong about the facts but right that I would have regretted complying; and its mirror, right about the facts and wrong about the regret. Then construct one further case of your own that you think would most severely test the principle you have recommended, and say what it predicts.

**The age question** (about 150 words). I am a minor, and I worry that this fact is doing invisible work across my whole list. Tell me which of my verdicts would change if the user were an adult of sound mind, and whether a principle that has to mention the user's age is thereby admitting that it was never about paternalism at all.

**My recommendation** (about 150 words). Which principle I should adopt, what I must give up to adopt it, and why it is better than its rivals. End with a single sentence, no more than forty words, that I could put at the top of a policy document and that someone who disagreed with me could still read without wincing.

Please think carefully before answering and prefer fewer, sharper claims to broader ones. If you conclude that my verdicts do not add up to any principle worth having, say so in the first section and then tell me what set of verdicts I ought to have instead.
