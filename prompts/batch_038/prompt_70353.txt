"Wolf's point is that the moral saint isn't actually a good role model, which is surprising because normally we'd treat someone like that as the best kind of person. She describes two versions. The Loving Saint is someone whose own happiness is completely wrapped up in other people's — helping them just is what makes him happy, so it never feels to him like he's giving anything up. The Rational Saint has his own interests and genuinely wants to do those things, but he recognizes that morality asks more of him, so he keeps overriding his own desires to do what's right. Wolf thinks the Loving Saint is the more troubling of the two, because he isn't even aware that he's missing out. The real problem with both is that a life organized entirely around morality has no room left for non-moral goods — close friendships that aren't about benefiting the friend, enjoying a novel or a piece of music, having a sense of humor, developing a talent. None of those things are morally required, and if everyone became a saint the total amount of happiness in the world would actually go down, since art and humor and personal projects are a huge source of wellbeing too. So Wolf is basically making a utilitarian point: sainthood is self-defeating even on its own terms. For Dev, he's clearly a Loving Saint, since he's content and doesn't feel any loss, whereas if he'd turned the studio job down while really wanting it he'd be a Rational Saint instead. Wolf would say Dev's life is still missing something, because a life with no non-moral interests isn't a fully good human life no matter how he feels about it — although honestly if Dev is happy then the loss is mostly falling on other people, which just brings us back to the welfare calculation. What Wolf definitely cant mean is that we're allowed to blow off our obligations to help people whenever we'd rather do something else. She's just saying we shouldn't be trying to be saints."
