I'm the reliabilty engineer at a small company building heavy-lift cargo drones — about forty people, and I'm the only one who does this part. Every subsystem that's new to us has to come with a probability of a hazardous failure per flight hour before the safety case can even start, and our internal template says anything worse than 1e-7 per flight hour for a hazardous failure is a no-go and anything worse than 1e-9 for a catastrophic one is a stop-the-program. Roughly three or four subsystems a quarter need a number, and about half of them are genuinely novel — no field history, no vendor reliability data, sometimes no test hours yet. So I've been making up priors, and I've started to suspect I'm making them up badly.

Here's the thing that set this off. For a new release mechanism I wrote "failure rate uniform between 1e-7 and 1e-3 per hour." A colleague pointed out that's exactly the same as saying "mean time between failures uniform between 1000 and 10 million hours" — except it isn't, those two statements put their mass in completely different places, and the first one makes a 1e-4 rate look likely while the second makes it look almost impossible. Then he asked which one I'd still have written if the template had asked for hours-between-failures instead of per-hour rates. I had no answer. Somebody in the review said I should have heard of something called a Jeffreys prior and that it fixes this; somebody else said my prior is just my guess wearing a lab coat.

What I want is a written procedure — ordered steps, the way you'd write a maintenance checklist — that takes "we know nothing about this part" and turns it into a number, or an interval, or an explicit refusal, that I can defend to an auditor and hand to a junior engineer to run without me. Specifically:

- the questions to ask in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each before you're allowed to move on;
- which quantity you should state the prior over, and why that one, and how to check you haven't just moved the same arbitrariness somewhere else;
- what to do when you have partial information rather than none — a count of physical failure modes, data from a cousin part in a different industry, three near-misses in 500 hours of ground test;
- where the procedure stops and outputs "no defensible number, test it / redesign it" instead, and where it hands the call to a human because it's a question about how much risk to bystanders we're willing to carry rather than about facts;
- whether I'm allowed to submit a range or a set of distributions instead of a point, and what that does to the pass/fail gate above.

Constraints: I have a spreadsheet and Python, no specialist software, and it has to take under an hour per subsystem. Every step needs to produce a sentence I could paste into a document a skeptical non-statistician reads.

Then run it on three cases. (1) The pallet release mechanism: zero data, one failure mode that could drop a load early, and a vaguely-similar winch in offshore marine use whose history I don't really understand. (2) A battery tray meant to contain thermal runaway: no failures in 600 hours of ground test, and management wants to claim we've thereby demonstrated something better than 1e-5 per hour. (3) The one I probably won't like: the 1e-7 and 1e-9 numbers in our own template, which I've been treating as fixed targets rather than estimates. Tell me whether they survive the procedure, and if they don't, tell me what to do Monday.

Finally, be blunt about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, what this whole approach cannot do, and how the procedure itself will misfire if someone runs it carelessly.
