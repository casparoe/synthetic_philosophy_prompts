so i sit on the science advisory panel of a private foundation (about $40M a year) and in three weeks the full board votes on one flagship grant in searching for life. It's the only line item big enough that we're picking one of three finalists rather than splitting it, which is making everyone cranky.

The finalists, all with real teams and real costings behind them:

**A — deep listening survey.** Five years, ~$18M. Buy observing time on existing radio arrays and stare at roughly 4,000 nearby sunlike systems looking for narrowband signals, plus a pipeline and a small team to triage candidate hits and rule out terrestrial interference. Nothing broadcast, no new hardware beyond receivers.

**B — active beacon program.** ~$12M. Build and operate a high-power targeted transmitter, on the order of 10^13 W EIRP, aimed at about 30 systems chosen for likely rocky planets in the habitable zone, repeating an encoded message annually for a decade so the same transmission sweeps past each target several times over.

**C — biosignature false-positive program.** ~$22M. Lab work and modeling on all the abiotic routes by which a rocky planet can accumulate oxygen, methane, or whatever else we'd be tempted to call a biosignature, ending in an instrument concept for some future flagship mission.

The panel is split in a way I find unproductive. The B people say a successful contact would be the largest single event in the history of the species, that the probability someone is out there listening is not zero, and that the "hostile listeners come and get us" scenario is science fiction with no evidence on either side — plus they say we're being asked to make an irreversible-by-omission choice: if nobody ever transmits, the question never gets answered at all. The anti-B people say the entire case for B rests on quantities nobody can even in principle estimate — the chance a receiving civilization exists, the chance it's within range and paying attention, the chance it responds rather than exploits — and that multiplying an astronomically large payoff by a number you invented isn't a calculation, it's a mood with a decimal point. They add that once the signal is away you can't take it back, and that a one-in-a-million chance of civilizational-scale harm is not something a foundation can absorb just because the expected-value arithmetic looks tidy. Meanwhile the Fermi-paradox reasoning gets invoked by both camps to support opposite conclusions, which nobody has bothered to sort out.

Two things I want the memo to genuinely engage, because they got hand-waved at the last meeting:

1. What does our total failure to detect anything so far — no signals, no artifacts, no unambiguous biosignature — actually license us to conclude about how common life and intelligence are? I want the board shown the difference between treating the null result as evidence about the universe and treating it as evidence about how weak our searches have been, and what each reading implies for A versus B versus C.

2. How do we decide when the key numbers aren't merely uncertain but arguably unlearnable — when no experiment we could plausibly fund would ever narrow the range? I need an actual position on that, not a shrug and not a spreadsheet with made-up decimals.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate? Take a clear position: fund one of them, or say explicitly why we should restructure the round instead. Give reasons the losing side can actually respond to, and don't strawman them — give the best version of the case against your recommendation its own real paragraph. Around 900–1200 words.

Then after the memo, give me a JSON object our secretary can paste into the minutes, in exactly this shape:
