Last month I read an encyclopedia entry on aesthetic testimony and it kind of wrecked my afternoon. The shape of the debate as I took it away: pessimists say you can't get genuine aesthetic knowledge from "pure testimony" — someone just telling you the Matisse is beautiful with no reasons attached — and that what's missing is acquaintance, i.e. you have to have had the experience yourself; there's a related distinction between believing that something is beautiful and actually finding it beautiful, and a further claim that first-hand experience has value even when the testimony would have gotten you the right answer anyway. Optimists say we defer to critics about film and food and wine constantly and there's nothing mysterious about it. I want the whole debate restated in my framework, claim by claim, as honestly as you can manage, and then a residue report: for each claim, does it translate cleanly, does it only translate if you bend something, or is there no slot for it in my toolkit at all. And please don't smuggle your own assumptions in and label them translation — if the only way to make the pessimist's view expressible is to already assume an aesthetic judgment is a forecast about a fixed quantity that individual raters are noisy estimators of, flag that loudly, because that assumption might be the entire issue.

Here's the concrete thing that started this, since I think the philosophers are describing it and I can't locate it anywhere in my own machinery. Panel of 40, 60 wines, tasted blind twice three weeks apart. Intra-rater correlaton between the two sittings was about 0.2 — basically noise. But every individual rater's scores correlated about .58 with the panel consensus, and the consensus held up when we re-ran with a different 40 people. By my own reliabilty standards the consensus is a much better instrument than any member of it, which licenses deference to the consensus in a way it doesn't license deference to any single taster. And yet when I tell a friend "trust the panel, this bottle is genuinely great," and she believes me and reports that yes, it's great, I have this stubborn intuition that she hasn't judged anything at all — she's echoed me.
