I am preparing a graduate seminar session on the adaptationism debate, and I want to test a specific claim: that the Gould–Lewontin critique has empirical bite, rather than amounting to a standing reminder to consider alternatives. To settle that, I need a designed case in which a researcher restricted to the ordinary adaptationist toolkit — comparative correlation across a clade, an optimality model, engineering analysis of fit to function — arrives at a confident and well-evidenced but incorrect verdict.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

**Scenario.** One paragraph of roughly two hundred words, describing a single heritable morphological trait of an ordinary extant animal or plant. You may invent the species, but keep it inside a real and well-known clade so that its development and ecology are of familiar kinds. The trait must be genuinely useful and apparently well engineered: no sloppiness, no vestigiality, no mismatch between form and function, no cost to carrying it, no pathology. The paragraph must state the developmental or architectural mechanism by which the trait arises as a structural consequence of two other features, each of which is independently selected for a different function — in the way that a triangular panel is forced wherever a round dome comes to rest on adjoining arches. It is not enough to assert that the trait is a byproduct: the geometry, mechanics, or growth pattern that necessitates it must be concrete enough that a biologist could check it.

Do not use any of the standard textbook illustrations of non-adaptive byproduct traits (the navel, the redness of blood, male nipples), and do not build the case on a single pleiotropic gene, on mutation order, or on genetic drift alone. No lineage known only from fossils, no genetic manipulation, no exotic chemistry or physics.

Then, in separately headed sections:

1. **Evidence inventory.** Describe what an adaptationist would actually collect: a phylogenetically controlled comparative study across roughly ten species of the clade, an optimality model predicting the trait's dimensions, and an engineering account of how well the trait performs its apparent function. Make the correlation tight and the optimality model numerically accurate, and then explain, for each item, why that pattern arises in your case without any selection on the trait itself. Say explicitly which shared ecological driver is doing the work.

2. **Verdicts.** What a strong adaptationist, a pluralist in the Gould–Lewontin vein, and a defender of structural necessity as the default hypothesis each conclude from precisely that evidence, and why.

3. **Discriminating evidence.** Name the one observation that would settle the question, and explain why it discriminates. It must be of a kind an adaptationist accepts in principle, not a device that assumes the answer.

4. **Load-bearing feature and removal test.** Identify the single structural feature of the case that generates the disagreement, then report what happens to each verdict if that feature is weakened or removed.

5. **Counterattack.** Give the strongest available adaptationist response — including, if it applies, the charge that the byproduct has since been co-opted and refined, so that the distinction you drew no longer holds — and state how much of the case survives it.

A case that merely stipulates the trait's status, or that gets its verdicts from labels rather than from the stated mechanism and the evidence you inventory, does not meet the specification. Where the honest answer is that the case is delicate or partly question-begging, say so plainly rather than papering over it.
