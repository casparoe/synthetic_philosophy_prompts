I retired four years ago after a career in experimental high-energy physics and have since taken up philosophy in earnest. Next month I present to a joint reading group: philosophers of physics, two of whom are structural realists and hold that fundamental physics delivers structure and no individuals at all; philosophers of mind, mostly physicalists, who regard the argument that physics is silent about intrinsic natures as either question-begging or a relabeling of causal roles; and two value theorists who work on aggregation, and specifically on whether a great many small harms can ever be weighed against a few severe ones.

My thesis is this. If the categorical bases that physics leaves unspecified are proto-experiential, and if valence is not an addition to experience but constitutive of it, then the physical world carries a negative hedonic load dwarfing anything biology produces; and one can have reasons to reduce that disvalue without first solving the combination problem, because those reasons do not depend on identifying the subject who bears them. I intend to concede that proto-experiential properties are not themselves experiences, and to insist that the concession costs me nothing ethically — which I expect is precisely where I will be attacked.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this room would raise, in its own vocabulary, rather than the ones a general audience would. Give exactly five numbered items, ranked by the damage each would do, with no preamble and no closing summary; each item is to be at most two sentences, the first being the objection as the questioner would actually put it, the second my best reply together with an explicit flag on the point at which that reply gives way. Indicate which of the five is the one I should be hoping nobody asks.
