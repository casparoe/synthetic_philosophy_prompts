So I'm co-teaching the philosophy-of-science seminar with the engineering faculty this term, and next Tuesday is my one shot at the interpretations of probability — the third-years have had a single lecture each on frequentism, on the subjective/coherence view, and on propensities, and nothing else, so anything I hand them has to be readable cold. The usual in-class example (a coin, a die, a screening test with a base rate) is useless to me because they've all seen it a hundred times and they pattern-match to the standard punchline instead of thinking. What I actually want to argue in the paper section this is drafted for is stronger than the familiar claim that the three interpretations disagree about what probability *is*: I want a case where they disagree about what to *do*, in an ordinary non-exotic setting, where each interpretation's own verdict points to a different action and no one has to be an idiot or a villain for the disagreement to bite.

Here's the seed, feel free to adjust the numbers but keep the shape. A short-sea car ferry operator runs 200 identical hydraulic actuators on its bow doors, and over fifteen years logged eight seizure events across roughly 2,000 crossings. Post-mortems attributed seven of the eight to salt water freezing in a seal groove and one to a fatigue crack in the piston rod driven by a harmonic at a particular actuation speed. A retrofit — new seal compound and groove geometry, plus a control profile that keeps the actuator out of that speed band — has now been running on 25 units for a year, 250 crossings, zero seizures. The operator's reliability manual tells engineers to always work from the widest homogeneous reference class for which they have at least a hundred recorded trials, so the frequentist in the story is on a leash and can't just shrug. Question at issue: probability that the actuator on door 3 seizes on tonight's crossing. Now build me the case to spec: all three interpretations have to deliver a *determinate* number for that one single event, the three numbers have to be distinct, and they have to straddle two decision thresholds so that the three verdicts recommend three different actions (sail as normal / sail with an added sensor and a watch officer / hold the vessel in port) — you pick the payoff figures, but state them and show the arithmetic. No coins, dice, cards, urns, quantum events, medical base rates, no appeals to infinite sequences by fiat, no perfect predictors, and the subjectivist must be fully coherent: her credence has to differ from both the others without violating any Dutch-book constraint. Most important — the numbers have to be *generated* by each theory's machinery, not declared. A case where you just assert "frequentism says zero, propensity says one in eight hundred" fails, because the seminar will smell it immediately.

Give it to me as a single paragraph labelled SCENARIO (no more than about 180 words, and it has to stand alone), followed by exactly five numbered items, two or three sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion. Item 1: the frequentist verdict — which reference class she's forced into by Reichenbach's homogeneity rule and why the pooled fifteen-year class is disqualified. Item 2: the propensity verdict — what single-case physical disposition is being claimed here, and why 250 clean crossings don't drive it to zero the way they'd drive a frequency to zero. Item 3: the subjectivist verdict — what her evidence and prior do, and specifically why coherence and Cromwell's-rule considerations leave her credence free of the propensity figure rather than snapping her onto it (this is the bit my students always get wrong, they think believing the vendor's propensity report just *is* having that credence). Item 4: the one feature of the case doing all the work, and what happens to the divergence if I delete it. Item 5: the best attack available to whichever theory comes out worst — and if that attack is actually good, say so plainly, I'd rather find out from you than from a student.
