I serve on the grants committee of a private family foundation — endowment roughly $90M, annual disbursement about $4M — whose charter directs the trustees to fund work "on behalf of those who will come after us." The phrase was written by the founder in 1998 and has never been construed, so over the years it has supported everything from watershed restoration to a small lab working on the governance of frontier technology. This cycle two finalists are competing for a single $600,000 three-year award and we are deadlocked 3–3. Proposal A funds a research program on reducing the risk of permanent civilizational collapse; its impact case rests on the claim that a durable future could contain on the order of 10^16 lives, so that even a small reduction in the probability of collapse is worth more than anything else we could buy. Proposal B funds lead-abatement and developmental screening across three counties, with a cost-effectiveness analysis our program officer calls the most defensible document she has seen in five years.

The deadlock is not really about the two programs. One trustee, a software engineer, has circulated a two-page argument that Proposal A's expected-value case dissolves under the simulation argument — Bostrom's trilemma, on which either almost no human-level civilizations reach a posthuman stage, or almost none of those that do are interested in running ancestor simulations, or else we are very probably in one now. She presses three claims. First, simulations get switched off: the far future inside a simulation is truncated by a termination date set by people whose reasons we cannot model, so the astronomical horizon A counts on may not exist at our level of reality. Second, if our world is a simulation, our forecasts about our descendants inherit the arbitrary choices of the simulators, which makes a figure like 10^16 epistemically worthless rather than merely uncertain. Third — and this is the one I have not been able to answer — a nesting worry: if posthuman civilizations run ancestor simulations and the simulated civilizations go on to run simulations of their own, the same vast future is replicated down many levels, each level's share of the total value shrinks accordingly, and we have no way of knowing which level we occupy; so treating *our* level's future as overwhelmingly important looks arbitrary. Another trustee, a retired judge, thinks this is sophistry and argues the other way: if we are in a simulation, then some civilization did survive to the posthuman stage and did choose to run ancestor simulations, which is at least weak evidence that the transition Proposal A fears is survivable; and, he adds, if our descendants turn out to be implemented in silicon rather than carbon, that is no reason to count their suffering less.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — fund A, fund B, split the award, or fund with conditions — with the dollar allocation and a time frame for committing it; state, in terms a trustee with no philosophy background can follow, how much decision-relevant weight the simulation hypothesis deserves and why; take a position on each of the three claims above rather than summarizing them, including whether the nesting worry can be answered or must simply be priced in; do justice to the strongest consideration on the losing side of whatever you recommend; and close with two or three criteria this committee could apply to future applications that lean on very large far-future numbers, so the question does not have to be relitigated next cycle. Keep it under 1,100 words, argue from first principles, and include no citations, references, or reading list — several trustees read these on a phone.

After the memo, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "decision" (string); "allocation" (object with numeric values for "proposal_a", "proposal_b", and "unallocated", in dollars); "simulation_weight" (string, one of "none", "low", "moderate", "high"); "simulation_weight_reason" (string, one sentence); "weakest_link_in_A" (string, the single premise of A's case that most deserves scrutiny); "hardest_claim" (string, one of "termination", "unmodelable_forecasts", "nesting") and "response_gist" (string, how the memo answers it); "strongest_case_against_decision" (string); "conditions" (array of strings, empty if none); "revisit_trigger" (string, the evidence or event that should reopen the question); "confidence_0_100" (integer).
