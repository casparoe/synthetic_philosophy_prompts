I'm a novelist, and I'm about six months into a third book that keeps turning back toward Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* — not because I want to imitate it, but because it solved a structural problem I can't solve. My novel concerns the long-term cohort of a fertility clinic: people whose existence is owed to a program that will also cut their lives short, narrated by one of them in middle age. I want a close reading of Ishiguro's novel through population axiology — the Mere Addition Paradox and the Repugnant Conclusion as Parfit formulates them, the non-identity problem, and the distinction between a life that is worth living and a life that it would have been right to start. What I'm after is not the familiar bioethics reading of the book as a parable about cloning and consent; I want the book treated as a work that is doing population ethics, badly or well, in the shape of its plot.

Here is my working hypothesis, and I would like you to test it rather than flatter it. The Hailsham project, as the elderly guardians finally explain it, looks on a totalist reading like an engine for mere addition: it rescues no one from a worse fate, because the students would not otherwise have existed at all, and what it actually produces is a larger number of lives worth living inside a system that ends them early — and then presents itself, in an elegiac register, as a moral achievement. Does the novel know that? Does it let the guardians' defense stand as the book's emotional summit because Ishiguro accepts it, or because he wants the reader to feel the pull of an axiology the novel declines to ratify? And where does Kathy's narration sit in all this? I have come to think of her flatness as a kind of retrospective neutrality: she reports everything and adjudicates nothing, she never once says whether it would have been better had none of them been made, and her satisfaction with her own lot may be exactly the adaptive preference the system was designed to induce. I cannot tell whether that evenhandedness is a philosophical commitment to the intuition of neutrality about adding lives, or simply an aesthetic of restraint that I am over-reading into the prose. Tell me how you would distinguish the two from inside the text, if it can be done at all.

Build the case out of four or five scenes you describe rather than quote — Miss Lucy's blunt classroom disclosure and her dismissal, the stranded fishing boat in the marsh, the failed plea for a deferral, Madame watching Kathy dance to the Judy Bridgewater tape, the closing field in Norfolk where the lost things of the world are supposed to wash up — and say what each contributes to or against the reading, including whether Tommy's deferral theory is doing philosophical work of its own, as a person-affecting hope that the system's impersonality then extinguishes. I also want the honest part: where this frame distorts the book. Ishiguro's preoccupations are memory, self-deception, and the ethics of looking away, and you may well think that turning the novel into a Parfit case study is a category mistake; if so, say which portions survive the objection and which don't. Please give the anti-natalist gloss a single sentence and set it aside — everyone reaches for it, and it does not touch the question I care about, which is what the reformers owe the people they choose to bring into being rather than whether anyone should be brought into being at all. If you bring up the 2010 film adaptation, flag anything you aren't confident you remember accurately; I would much rather be told you're unsure than handed a confident error, and I have my own suspicion that the screen version converts the final explanation into something closer to a verdict than the novel permits. No bibliography and no page references, please — I know the secondary literature and will handle citations myself. Close with the part that is actually useful to me as a working writer: what is the narrative device by which a novel can withhold an impersonal judgment about a population without reading as evasive, and what is the characteristic failure mode when a book attempts it and gets caught?
