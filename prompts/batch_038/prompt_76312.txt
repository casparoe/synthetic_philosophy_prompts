You are a professional philosopher who works in aesthetics and in the nineteenth-century philosophy of art; please think carefully, weigh several candidate diagnoses of the impasse described below before settling on one, and treat my own account of the disagreement as data to be scrutinized rather than as a summary to be endorsed.

Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about Tolstoy's What Is Art? and made no progress whatsoever. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on Tolstoy.

First, the terrain as I understand it. Tolstoy's positive account is a transmission theory: art is a human activity in which one person deliberately conveys, by means of certain external signs, a feeling he has himself lived through, so that others are infected by that feeling and live through it too — feeling standing to art roughly as thought stands to speech. On top of this he ranks degrees of infectiousness, governed as I recall by the individuality of the feeling conveyed, its clarity, and above all the sincerity of the artist; and he then adds a further clause requiring that the feeling transmitted be one that unites people in a manner consonant with the religious perception of the age. That last clause is what generates his notorious verdicts. As I remember it — correct me if I am wrong, though my question does not turn on these details — he rejects Shakespeare and late Beethoven, praises Dickens, Hugo, Uncle Tom's Cabin and his own short moral parables, and is prepared to condemn most of what educated Europe calls art, including a good deal of his own earlier fiction.

My friend's position is that the whole edifice falls to counterexample. Infectiousness cannot be a criterion of arthood, since plenty of non-art infects us powerfully — a rousing speech, a panic in a crowd, a lullaby that works — and since plenty of acknowledged art leaves a given spectator stone cold, which on Tolstoy's own account would make that work not-art for that spectator and therefore would make arthood a property of audiences rather than of works. Sincerity, clarity and individuality cannot be criteria either, because they are matters of degree and can be scored however the desired verdict requires. His conclusion: the book is not a definition of art at all but a moral polemic wearing a definition's clothes, and he calls the move definitional imperialism.

My position is that his counterexamples attack the wrong mechanism. Tolstoy is not offering necessary and sufficient conditions to be tested against intuitions about marginal cases; he is asking what art is for and then identifying art by that function, so something that infects without uniting is not a counterexample but a different practice doing superficially similar work. And on my reading the rejections of Shakespeare and Beethoven follow from the religious clause, not from the infectivity clause, so an inventory of works Tolstoy condemns tells us nothing about whether infectivity is a plausible criterion.

We tried to settle it and failed. We agreed to look for cases in which the two clauses come apart, and we constructed two. Case A: a technically masterful, wholly sincere work that infects a large audience reliably, but whose dominant communicated feeling is triumphant national superiority or obsessive erotic longing — something Tolstoy's religious clause would condemn. Case B: a work that conveys exactly the brotherly feeling Tolstoy wants but conveys it feebly, without individuality or fire, so that it moves essentially no one. My friend said Case A shows infectivity is not sufficient for arthood and Case B shows it is not necessary, since Case B is bad art but still art. I said Case A is simply not art under the full theory, which includes the religious clause, and Case B is a failed instance of art rather than a non-artwork, so neither case is decisive. We each then accused the other of moving the goalposts, and that is where the evening died.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that we were never disagreeing about Tolstoy but about what a theory of art owes us — extensional adequacy to ordinary use versus an account of the point of the practice — and that the word art was doing two jobs in our argument, one classificatory and one laudatory, which neither of us separated. Tell me whether that is right, or whether the real joint lies elsewhere: in the audience-relativity of a transmission model, in whether a functional account can be refuted by counterexample at all, or in a straightforwardly verbal dispute about the scope of infectiousness. Address specifically whether my friend's charge of unfalsifiability is correct or whether my reading leaves the theory determinate content, and whether the goalpost accusation is symmetrical between us or whether one of us is doing something dialectically illegitimate.

Finish by saying what would count as progress here — a distinction that separates the fused readings, a reformulation of Tolstoy's criterion that survives Cases A and B, a change of question, or nothing at all — and if your answer is that no progress should be expected, argue for that rather than announcing it. Do not survey the book chapter by chapter, do not offer a reading list or bibliography, and do not adjudicate Tolstoy's broader claims about art or morality; I want the structure of our disagreement.

Begin your reply with your bottom line in a single sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
