**Question.** In 1798 Malthus argued that population, when unchecked, increases in a geometric ratio while the means of subsistence increase only in an arithmetic ratio, and that population is therefore kept pressed against the level of subsistence by checks. World population has since multiplied roughly tenfold, and across much of the world real incomes have risen rather than fallen. It is said either (a) that Malthus was falsified by this record, (b) that he was falsified but that the falsification was postponed by auxiliary hypotheses, or (c) that his theory is flexible enough to accommodate any demographic history whatever, and so was never falsifiable at all. Some of his defenders reply that he was "right but early". Explain what "right but early" would have to mean in order to be a genuine epistemic possibility rather than a way of evading refutation, and assess whether it can bear that weight in this case.

**Submitted answer.** Malthus's theory is not a vague prophecy. It states a mechanism — unchecked population doubles in about twenty-five years, and food production does not keep pace — and a consequence, that population is held at the level of subsistence. Because it says something that could have come out otherwise, it is falsifiable in principle, and it deserves to be taken seriously in a way that many grand theories of history do not.

The trouble is that it has come out otherwise. Two centuries of rising population accompanied by rising real incomes are precisely what the theory rules out. On Popper's account a theory is refuted when observation contradicts it, and here observation has contradicted it repeatedly; the honest verdict is that Malthus was falsified, and that everything since has been commentary.

Defenders do not accept this. When the evidence turned against the theory, it was revised: in a later edition Malthus added moral restraint — late marriage and celibacy — as a third check alongside misery and vice, and twentieth-century Malthusians appeal to the Green Revolution to explain why the arithmetic ratio never bit. Lakatos would describe this as a paradigm whose protective belt of auxiliaries absorbs the anomalies. On his view a paradigm degenerates when its hard core stops delivering predictions and the theorist is reduced to patching it, and that is what has happened here: each patch was designed after the event, in order to save the core.

That last point is decisive, and it decides the question the essay asks. If a theory can always be patched, then no possible evidence counts against it, and "right but early" ceases to be a claim about the world. It is a device for moving a theory out of the reach of refutation while retaining the credit of having made a prediction. The phrase does real rhetorical work — it converts a failed prediction into a deferred success — but it carries no content, because there is nothing that could show the deferral to be unwarranted. Anyone who says a theory is right but early owes us a date; and a theory that supplies a date is no longer the same theory.

So "right but early" is not a genuine epistemic possibility. It is the verbal form of an ad hoc rescue. Malthus was not right and early. He was wrong, and his wrongness was concealed for two hundred years by a series of revisions that were never themselves placed at risk.
