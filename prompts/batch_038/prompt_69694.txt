I have been working through the doomsday argument — the claim that, because I know roughly where I sit in the ordering of all human beings who will ever live, I should expect that total to be within a modest multiple of my own birth rank — and more generally through how indexical evidence is supposed to bear on belief. I have reached the point where I trust my verdicts on individual cases considerably more than I trust any slogan I could state, and I have a tentative principle that I suspect is broken in more than one place. What I want from you is the reflective-equilibrium work: identify the account, or the small set of candidate accounts, that best fits my verdicts, and tell me plainly which verdicts I have to abandon if I want the account to stay simple.

Here are the cases, in my own words, each with my verdict.

1. Urn. A sealed urn holds balls numbered 1 through N, where N is unknown to me. I am told that one ball was drawn uniformly at random from the urn and that it is the ball numbered 57. Verdict: I take this as strong evidence that N is on the order of tens rather than millions. I also take it to be plain Bayesian inference from an explicitly stated sampling procedure. Nothing anthropic is involved, and no special assumption about how I should conceive of myself is required.

2. Restricted amnesia. I wake with no memory of history and I am told exactly three things: that the two live hypotheses are that the human race ended shortly after my cohort, with about a hundred billion people ever born, or that it continued for a million years, with about a hundred quadrillion; that these two were equally likely beforehand; and that my birth rank is roughly sixty billion. Verdict: this is close to decisive for the first hypothesis, and I feel no resistance at all. On the face of it, this is the doomsday argument in its purest form, and I accept it without complaint.

3. Real-world doomsday. I know roughly where I stand in human history, somewhere around the sixty- to hundred-billionth person ever born. Verdict: I resist. I do not think this should shift my expectations about how long humanity persists by much. My reasons are inchoate: nobody arranged my position in the ordering by a random draw; the total is not a fixed quantity sitting in an urn but the upshot of decisions nobody has yet made; and insofar as the conclusion has a determinate magnitude, that magnitude looks to me like an artifact of whatever prior over total population I happened to bring to the question.

4. Bare existence. A fair coin is flipped. On heads, ten people are created; on tails, a thousand. I find that I exist, and I have no other relevant information. Verdict: my credence in heads should stay where it was. Merely being an observer is not evidence about how many observers there are.

5. Populated theory. One physical theory implies a universe containing something like a trillion trillion observers; an empirically equivalent rival implies about a billion. Verdict: the first theory is not thereby better confirmed. I do not want a theory to earn credence by populating the universe, and the consideration that I would have been likelier to exist were the first theory true seems to me to carry no evidential weight here at all.

6. The sleeper. A subject is put to sleep and a fair coin is flipped. On heads she is woken once; on tails she is woken twice, with her memory of the first waking erased. Verdict: on waking, her credence in heads should remain one half. The additional waking does not count for anything.

7. Copies. A machine produces a million exact duplicates of me, each of whom wakes and reasons. Verdict, and this is where I am least confident that I am coherent: in matters of belief I am willing to let the existence of many duplicates bear on my reasoning, at least in cases like 1 and 2, where I have information about which duplicate I am. In matters of value I am not willing to let duplication multiply what is at stake. A procedure that destroys one randomly selected duplicate does not strike me as much worse when there are a million duplicates than when there are ten; in each case it is one of me. Counting, apparently, matters for my credences but not for how bad I take outcomes to be.

8. Earliness among the stars. I am an early member of an early technological species: the universe is roughly fourteen billion years old and could remain hospitable for vastly longer. Verdict: my earliness is not, I think, evidence that technological civilizations are short-lived, or that some stage in their development is nearly impossible to survive. Whatever force the doomsday argument has inside the human species, I do not feel it transferring outward to the class of observers in the universe at large.

My tentative principle, which I expect you to attack: update on my position in an ordering only when the ordering is genuinely chance-driven, meaning that my occupying that position is the upshot of something I can model as a random draw, and only when the reference class is fixed by a natural kind that I could have specified before learning how many members it turns out to have. Bare existence never counts, and neither does the mere number of beings like me. Rank counts only insofar as it is information about which member I am; it must not be permitted to smuggle itself in as information about how many members there are.

What I would like back:

(a) State the account as precisely as you can manage — precise enough that two people who both accepted it could still disagree about a number. That means the update rule itself, the sampling premise written out explicitly, and a procedure for selecting the reference class that someone could apply without already knowing which answer they wanted.

(b) Work through cases 1 to 8 and say which verdicts your account reproduces and which it contradicts. For each contradiction, say what would have to be amended to save the verdict, what that amendment costs elsewhere, and whether on balance you think I should keep the verdict or give it up.

(c) Cases 1 and 2 look to me like the inference that case 3 refuses to make, and cases 3 and 8 look like the same inference applied to different reference classes. Diagnose each pair. Is there a real difference that does not simply assume my preferred conclusion, or am I merely uncomfortable? If there is a difference, put it in a form I could apply to a case whose answer I do not already know.

(d) On case 7: can counting bear on belief but not on the badness of outcomes, or must one of those two verdicts go? If they can come apart, explain what makes belief and value responsive to numbers in different ways.

(e) Apply your account to three situations on which I have no verdict, and tell me what I ought to think. First: the future members of the reference class are mostly uploaded or synthetic minds rather than biological humans — does my rank still bear on the total? Second: I learn my approximate birth rank only because a historian happens to be studying my cohort, so that the rank was not sampled even though my being told about it was — does that change anything? Third: the total is partly mine to determine, since a decision I am about to make regarding the expansion of a colony will settle whether the eventual total is nearer to a hundred billion or a hundred quadrillion — does your account give me a reason to decide one way rather than the other, and if so, is it a reason I should accept?

(f) Finally, tell me which single amendment to my tentative principle buys the most consistency for the least complexity, and name the verdicts it forces me to surrender.

Please do not survey the literature, and do not give me citations or a reading list: I have no way of looking anything up, and labels are not what I am after. If your account turns out to coincide with some named position, say the name once and move on. The deliverable is the rule, the verdicts on my cases and on the three new ones, and the reasoning about what has to go.
