I have been collecting my own verdicts about when a person's choices are evidence of what they prefer, and I would like help determining whether there is a principle behind them. This matters to me because it decides what I think utility means: if choice is only sometimes evidence of preference, then utility cannot simply be read off behavior, and I need to know what, if anything, does the reading.

The cases, with my verdicts as I actually hold them:

C1. A woman chooses a salad over a slice of cake at lunch when both are on offer; at three in the afternoon she eats the last piece of cake in the break room, nothing else being available. My verdict: no inconsistency, and taken together the two choices tell me nothing about how she ranks cake against salad.

C2. A man leaves his credit card at home before going to a restaurant so that he cannot order dessert. My verdict: the act of leaving the card behind reveals a genuine preference, but the choice he subsequently makes from a dessert-less menu reveals nothing.

C3. In a repeated tasting, the same woman chooses A over B, B over C, and C over A — not occasionally, but stably, on every presentation. My verdict: this is a real failure. Unlike C1, there is no ordering here at all, and I would call her irrational.

C4. Two offices receive identical pension enrollment forms. In one, the form auto-enrolls employees into a target-date fund; in the other, into a bond fund. Nearly everyone in each office stays with whatever they were handed. My verdict is unstable, and I want to be honest about it: on one hand I treat the default as welfare-relevant, as though staying put shows this is the fund they would have wanted; on the other hand I want to say these people have no preference in the matter whatsoever, which is why the form can be switched without wronging anyone. I do not see how both can be true.

C5. The same insurance policy is offered to two groups, described to one in terms of the number of lives saved and to the other in terms of the number who will die, the two descriptions being arithmetically equivalent. The groups reverse their choices. My verdict: pure artifact. It reveals nothing about their preferences, and I discount it entirely.

C6. Sen's apple-basket case: a guest takes the largest apple from the basket without hesitation, but when only two apples remain he takes the smaller one. My verdict: the second choice does reveal a preference — not over apples, but over situations, apples included — and I count that as a legitimate preference rather than a bias.

C7. A colleague spends an hour deliberating and ends up choosing what she said in the first two minutes she would choose. My verdict: the hour added no information; the snap judgment was equally good evidence. But the same colleague, forced to decide in thirty seconds in a different matter, chooses something she later pays money to reverse. My verdict there: the pressured choice does not count as revealing anything.

So C4 and C5 look to me like the same phenomenon — a feature of the presentation, rather than of the alternatives, driving the choice — yet I say one is preference-revealing and welfare-relevant and the other is noise. And C6 is context-dependence again, but there I call it a genuine preference, whereas in C5 I call it an artifact. I cannot see what separates them, and I suspect that whatever principle I have been applying is one I would not sign my name to if it were stated plainly.

What I want from you:

1. Is there a single principle, or a small number of candidate principles, that decides when choice is evidence of preference and when it is not? State each precisely enough that a researcher holding a dataset of choices and choice sets could apply it without knowing anything about my intuitions. I want operative conditions, not a slogan.
2. Go clause by clause and say which case motivated each clause and what work that clause is doing.
3. Give each candidate's verdicts on three cases I have not adjudicated. (a) A person who owns a concert ticket and would not sell it for a sum she would not have paid to acquire it. (b) A person who donates when a fundraiser is standing at her door but not when the identical appeal arrives by email. (c) A choice shifted by the addition of a clearly inferior third option, as when a medium soda priced just beneath the large makes the large look like a better deal than it did before. For (c), say specifically whether your principle treats this as C6 with the politeness removed, or as something genuinely different, and in what the difference consists.
4. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which of them I should give up. If all of them can be saved, say what the cost is in complexity and falsifiability, and whether the saving clauses are doing real work or are merely being adjusted after the fact to fit.
5. Say what the resulting principle implies about utility: is utility, on your account, a representation of choice made under good conditions, an independent quantity that choice sometimes tracks badly, or something with no content whatsoever outside the choice situations in which it is revealed?
