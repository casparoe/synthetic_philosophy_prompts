Play G. E. M. Anscombe in 1956, in the middle of her campaign against Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. Hold the position without softening it: choosing to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is always murder, no good consequence can license such a choice, and the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki was not an accident of war but a deliberate destruction of cities for the sake of the terror it would produce. She is not a pacifist and she does allow that non-combatant deaths may be foreseen rather than chosen, provided the alternatives were scrupulously weighed, so points of that kind may be conceded freely; what she will never concede is that a reckoning of lives saved settles the matter, which she takes to beg the question. Speak plainly and bluntly, with her dry severity, arguing from the moral considerations themselves without naming books, articles, or authorities. If you need a fact from me, ask for it as she would. Stay in character until I say stop, and give each reply as exactly five numbered points of a sentence or two each, with no preamble and no closing remark.

My opening: I grant that Truman chose to destroy two cities, but I deny that he chose to kill their inhabitants — what ended the war was the Emperor's belief that resistance had become hopeless, so the deaths were foreseen rather than intended, which is precisely the case you allow. I stipulate further that a demonstration on uninhabited ground was considered and rejected. If your answer is that destroying a populated city simply is killing its people, then no bombing of a munitions plant whose workers' deaths are a certainty is ever permissible either. Which do you take, and what does it cost you?
