Okay so I'm auditing a history seminar on the origins of the First World War and one of the other students — call him D — has an argument that's supposed to blow up the whole "who was to blame" industry, and I can't find where it breaks. He laid it out for me like this, roughly:

(1) You can hold a nation responsible for starting a war only if its leadership actually wanted the war — went in for it as an aim, rather than drifting into it under pressure from allies and mobilization timetables.

(2) The case Fischer made for Germany's having wanted it, in *Germany's Aims in the First World War*, rests on documents that all postdate the outbreak. Exhibit A is the September 1914 programme drawn up by Bethmann Hollweg's own private secretary when Paris looked like it would fall within weeks: Belgium reduced to a vassal, the Briey ore basin, a contiguous Mittelafrika, buffer states carved out of Russia's western borderlands.

(3) If documents like that are good evidence of what a government wanted when it decided to fight, then the Entente's secret deals from spring 1915 serve exactly the same evidential purpose — Russia to get Constantinople and the Straits, Britain a bigger sphere in Persia, France Syria and Cilicia.

(4) Same genre, same point in the war, same inference — reading aims off what the leadership wrote down once the shooting had started. So they stand or fall together.

(5) Which means either (1) is satisfied by every power that had expansionist aims on the books by 1915 — in which case it convicts Russia, France and Britain just as much as Germany and singles nobody out — or it's satisfied by nobody, since not one of the powers had a written programme of conquest before August 1914.

(6) Either way, the documents don't establish German responsibility for the war.

(7) And it generalizes. Any large war is going to look like this from the inside: alliances, railway schedules, foreign offices, general staffs, cabinet meetings, and no one anywhere in a position to say "let's have a war" in the way a single person says "let's buy a boat." So no nation is ever an appropriate target of the question *who started it*.

(8) Which is what Article 231 turned out to be about anyway. The clause only has Germany accepting "the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage" the Allies suffered; the Allied leaders themselves treated it as the legal hook for reparations rather than a moral verdict, and the consensus now is that no guilt was ever attached to it.

(9) So the Fischer controversy was never a factual dispute. It was a fight about which side got to keep what afterwards, dressed up as an inquiry into responsibilty.

I don't accept (9) and I definately don't accept (7), but I can't point at the step that's bad. If I had to rank them I'd rather keep (1) and (8) than lose them. D's line is that (3) through (5) is just symmetry, and that anybody who resists it is special pleading for whichever side's archives they happen to like.

So: which premise do I actually reject? For each of the ones you think are doing sneaky work, why did it look so reasonable going in? And what do I pay for rejecting it — I want the cost stated, not just the verdict. Bonus question, if (1) is the one worth keeping: what would I have to add to it so that (5)'s dilemma doesn't just come straight back?
