so im putting together a session for a small interdisciplinary reading group (economists, a couple of philosophers, one historian) and the gimmick is: we hand people a made-up country and ask what a dead thinker would say about it. no web access for anyone, me included, so dont bother with citations or a reading list — you'd just be guessing and i'd have no way to check. i want reasoning, not references. and if you're not certain of Keynes's exact wording somewhere, paraphrase it, do not invent quotes.

the setup i've written:

**Veldmark**, a small rich country, in 2041. stipulated facts, take these as given:

- real GDP per capita is roughly 8x its 1930 level — the top end of the range Keynes sketched for his grandchildren
- average annual hours worked per worker: about 1,350, down from ~2,000 in 1930
- median life satisfaction has been flat since 1980; time-diary studies show people have more free hours than their grandparents did but report *more* felt time pressure
- the top 1% wealth share has doubled since 1990; wage share of GDP at a record low
- most of the last decade's growth came from a handful of asset-heavy firms whose revenue is machine-generated content and software
- the government is debating a **compute dividend**: levy compute directly, pay every adult a flat citizen dividend
- a booming private market in optimization services — sleep coaches, hobby coaches, itinerary planners, "reading goals" apps

two coinages of mine that i want you to actually engage with rather than politely ignore:

1. **performed leisure** — free time that is produced for an audience and scored. the audience is permanent and the scoring is public, so rest becomes a deliverable.
2. **prestige migration** — once material goods get cheap, status competition moves to goods that can't be scaled up (attention, provenance, credentials, taste, authenticity). felt scarcity stays constant no matter how much is produced.

the question i want the group arguing about afterwards is whether prestige migration is just Keynes's relative-needs category getting wider, or whether it's something that breaks the category — because relative needs used to be partly satiated by growth trickling into conspicuous consumption, and non-scalable goods structurally can never be satiated by growth. if that's right, does his whole 1930 optimism quietly depend on a premise he never stated?

now the task. give me predictions for five issues that did not exist in 1946, and for each one split the answer into **the Keynes who wrote the 1930 essay** and **the Keynes of the mid-1940s** — after Bretton Woods, after years of institutional negotiation, and after his late memoir piece where he turns on Bloomsbury's early ethics and admits that a morality built on prized states of mind and personal relations, with no general rules of conduct underneath, was reckless. where those two men diverge is the interesting part, so tell me when they diverge and why.

the five:
1. legislating a four-day week — would he want hours pushed down by law, or would he think the change has to come through wealth and habit?
2. the compute dividend — given his full-employment commitments and his long-standing hope that the rentier would be gradually squeezed out, does a levy on compute and a flat cash payment look to him like a fix or like a surrender?
3. performed leisure — is Veldmark evidence he was wrong, or evidence he was right but incomplete (he did worry that the ordinary person, having no special talent to fall back on, would face a genuine problem of how to live)?
4. someone tells him a country could reach 8x in thirty years instead of a hundred. he was a gradualist who distrusted revolution and thought institutions get changed slowly and reluctantly. does the *speed* alarm him independently of the level?
5. given the Bloomsbury self-critique: would he say Veldmark is what happens when you tell people they're released from purposiveness but hand them no structure to put in its place?

be fair to him, not a caricature — he wasn't just a technocratic optimist, and he was also a man of his class with real blind spots about who is equipped to use free time well. include that honestly instead of sanding it down.
