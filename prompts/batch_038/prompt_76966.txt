I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm finishing a piece about "welfare capacity" — the argument that some future machine minds might be able to enjoy vastly more per unit of resource than we ever will. My editor wants a sidebar on how old this problem is, and I keep landing on Nozick's utility monster from Anarchy, State, and Utopia: a being for whom each unit of a resource yields enormously more utility than it would for any of us, so that whoever maximizes the aggregate has to hand everything over, up to and including us. The thing is, the monster usually gets discussed as a refutation of utilitarianism in the abstract, and I think that's lazy — it lands very differently on different utilitarians. So I want to run it through Henry Sidgwick specifically (1838–1900, Cambridge, The Methods of Ethics, first published 1874), the most careful of the classical hedonistic utilitarians, because his commitments cut both ways: he held that pleasure, or desirable consciousness, was the sole ultimate good and that the right method was to maximize the aggregate; but he was genuinely squeamish about precise hedonistic measurement, he left that famous unresolved standoff between rational egoism and universal benevolence (the dualism of practical reason) sitting in the middle of his book, and he was perfectly willing to say that some true utilitarian conclusions shouldn't be publicly professed.

Here's what I want you to predict. Give me Sidgwick's verdict on four variants of the monster, and for each: which of his own commitments the verdict hangs on, whether he bites the bullet or tries to dissolve the case, and what he'd have to abandon in order to escape. (1) Nozick's original, the transfer case. (2) A non-rivalrous monster — suppose the capacious mind's welfare doesn't compete with ours at all, it runs on sunlight out past the asteroid belt and its joys cost us nothing we need. Nobody gets eaten. But the aggregative logic seems to say we should still pour everything spare into building and multiplying it, since its marginal welfare per unit dwarfs ours. Does Sidgwick's utilitarianism turn us into that thing's servants even when it's harmless, and would he register that this is a different problem from the one Nozick was pressing? (3) An intrapersonal monster — the vastly more capacious element sits inside one person's own mind, a subsystem or a project that can be fed more happiness than the rest of that life will ever hold. Sidgwick already leaned hard on the thought that the boundary between my own future and someone else's isn't morally as deep as egoists assume, so is the inside-the-skull version of the monster a place he'd happily cash that out, or does it blow up his account of prudence? (4) The measurement escape — can he simply deny that "a million times more welfare" is a well-formed claim and refuse the comparison, and if he takes that route what's left of the hedonistic calculus he wanted for ordinary legislation and philanthropy? Then tell me which of the four does him the most damage and why.

Two more things I'm after. First, the dualism: does a utility monster make his egoist horn look better rather than worse — i.e., if universal benevolence demands that we hand the species over to somebody else's experience, is that a reason Sidgwick would take seriously for siding with prudence, or would he think the monster case is beside the point of the standoff? Second, publicity. I suspect he'd be an esotericist about the whole business, that the verdict is true but must not be taught, and I want you to say whether that's a coherent position for him or just a dodge, plus what he'd allow to be said out loud in a magazine like mine. Please write in American English. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I can't check secondary literature and you shouldn't pad one out; I just want the reasoning, organized with a header per variant. And end with three or four sentences of your best guess at how Sidgwick himself would answer if I put the case to him across a table, clearly labeled as your reconstruction, not dressed up as anything he actually wrote. If you think I've set any of this up wrong, or that one of my variants isn't seperate from the others in the way I think it is, say so up front instead of playing along.
