I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long feature on whether the left–right distinction still picks out anything real. Before I put forty interviews into it, I want to know what my own classification judgments commit me to. So I have written them down, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

The verdicts, in no particular order:

- An anarcho-capitalist who wants no state, unregulated markets, and no complaint about company towns and inherited fortunes: right. An anarcho-syndicalist who wants no state, worker-run firms, and no complaint about markets between cooperatives: left. They agree about the state and largely about voluntary exchange, and I still put them at opposite ends of the axis.
- Anti-colonial nationalists — the FLN in Algeria, Irish republicans: left, though they are particularist, nationalist, and often socially conservative. My instinct says that particularism about who counts as one of us is a right-wing trait, which makes me uneasy here.
- The Nordic populist parties that want a generous welfare state restricted to citizens and near-closed borders: right — not on the economics, which is social democratic, but on the restriction.
- Stalinism and fascism are opposites rather than neighbors. Whoever invokes a horseshoe to place them together is not describing them.
- Protectionism: right-wing now. But for most of the century between the Civil War and the Depression the Republicans were the party of high tariffs and the Democrats the party of low ones, and I would call those protectionists right-wing too. So whatever makes tariffs right-wing cannot be the tariff.
- Distributists — Chesterton and Belloc: anti-monopoly, pro-family property, pro-guild, hostile to capitalism and to socialism alike, deeply traditional. Right. Their economics reads left to me and their social thought reads right; I file them right and cannot fully say why.
- Free-speech absolutism: left when it shields pamphleteers, pornographers, and strikers from the state; right when it shields a platform's ranking decisions and a billionaire's spending. Same principle, opposite sides, and the difference seems to be who is doing the speaking.
- Open borders: left, even though the free movement of labor is precisely what the market right has always asked for. My libertarian readers write in to say I have mislabeled it. I keep the verdict; the only thing I can point to is who the existing restrictions fall on.
- I refuse to call today's defenders of the welfare state right-wing, even though they are conserving an arrangement. That kills the old French seating idea — the left as the party of movement, the right as the party of order — since the movement of 1789 is now the order being defended.

My default account is Bobbio's: the left treats inequality as something to be attacked, the right treats most of it as natural or ineradicable. Test that against the list and repair it if you can; replace it if you cannot. Specifically, I want four things.

1. The principle, or the small set of them, stated precisely and clause by clause, with the case behind each clause.
2. Its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a national conservative who wants tariffs, industrial policy, and antitrust enforcement against the big platforms while opposing no-fault divorce; a Chinese Communist Party that runs a market economy, is fiercely nationalist, and suppresses independent labor organizing; and an open-borders, free-market transhumanist with no interest in traditional morality.
3. Which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which single one you think is simply wrong and should be dropped. Say so plainly rather than diplomatically.
4. Whether the honest answer is one dimension, two, or something that is not a spatial arrangement of positions at all — and, given that, what my feature is entitled to claim: that the distinction tracks something, that it tracks something only under conditions you should specify, or that it is a habit of speech which has outlived its justification.

Commit. Take a definite position on the principle, on which verdict goes, and on what the distinction is; give me your reasons and your degree of confidence in them, rather than an even-handed menu of candidate frameworks that leaves the choice to me. No citations, no bibliography, no scorecard of named schools — I want the principle and the argument for it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of the question.
