I am drafting an audit protocol for a single narrow question: whether a deployed language system holds information that its own outputs systematically fail to disclose, and whether a resulting false self-report is deceptive or merely erroneous. Before committing to any detection machinery, I want the specification of what would count as a successful detection. Write the desiderata for an account of latent knowledge — of what it is for such a system to know something it does not say, and of when a procedure has genuinely elicited that knowledge rather than manufactured it.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it: the failure mode it rules out, the phenomenon it would otherwise misdescribe, or the verdict an auditor would get backwards. Address at least the following, while judging for yourself which are genuine requirements and which are local commitments of particular research cultures (the interpretability culture, the behavioral-testing culture, the honesty-and-deception culture):

1. that a positive result not be reproducible by the detection channel alone, given that the channel is itself a trained behavior of the system under test;
2. that possessing information be distinguished from being able to deploy it in one task format, and that the known dissociations be handled — internal-state probes recovering what the system's generations deny, and the converse;
3. that a failed elicitation be interpretable, so that absence, inaccessibility, and unwillingness are separable rather than observationally equivalent;
4. that measurement be distinguished from construction, since asking can create, alter, or destroy the state asked about;
5. that the account say what happens when information is held by one subsystem and unavailable to the subsystem that speaks — the hinge on which any judgment of deception rather than error turns;
6. that it not presuppose introspective access, on pain of settling in advance that no artificial system qualifies;
7. that it remain applicable under black-box conditions, where no internal states are available at all.

Mark which of these you treat as non-negotiable for this purpose and which as the preferences of a camp. Then identify any pair or subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and show why; if the list amounts to an impossibility result for the audit I am trying to build, that is the finding I most want. State which single requirement is most exclusionary — the one that otherwise-plausible accounts most often fail — and which requirements are hostage to an antecedent decision about whether this sense of knows is a psychological attribution or a purely dispositional one, and whether that decision can be deferred until the protocol is fixed.

Close with at most one line per family of existing approach — self-report-based, internal-state-based, purely behavioral — naming the requirement each is structurally unable to meet. No scorecard beyond that: the criteria are the deliverable.
