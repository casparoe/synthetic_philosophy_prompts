I run a second-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility — 16 students, one 75-minute class — and I want to open the Strawson session with an activity rather than the paper. What I want them to hit is the regress: being responsible for what you do seems to require being responsible for how you are, and that requirement never bottoms out. They should collide with it themselves; nobody says the words "basic argument" until the debrief.

Design a credit-allocation game that generates the regress out of its own scoring rule, not out of me asking leading questions. Here's the shape I have in mind, but improve it or throw it out: teams do a short production-chain task where each member's output is the next member's input, then the group has to divide 100 points among themselves, and the points buy people out of the tidying rota at the end — so genuine stakes, no money. The catch I'm after is that any allocation rule they can actually write down keeps forcing them upstream, to how the earlier members were positioned to do well, and there's no rule that closes the search off.

Give me: materials, minute-by-minute timing, roles, and the exact wording of the only two or three sentences the facilitator is allowed to say (I want to be nearly silent throughout). Then the debrief, mapping particular moments in the game onto particular steps of the argument. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover — especially the student who says "but I still chose to do it my way", which someone always does. And be frank about which feature of the argument the game cant reproduce, so I know what I have to supply by talking.
