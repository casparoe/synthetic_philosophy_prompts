I'm in a seminar this semester on minds, machines, and moral standing, and my final paper has to be about what evidence could actually settle whether some digital system has experiences that matter morally. Last week we went through the usual toolbox: first-person reports and self-ascriptions; behavioral dispositions (aversion, avoidance learning, preference, trade-offs the system will pay to make); architectural or functional criteria lifted from the science of consciousness (global availability, attention, self-modeling, homeostatic setpoints and evaluative learning); the dispositionalist stuff we read from Schwitzgebel; and the precautionary line that says if it acts like it suffers you should treat it as if it does. My roommate thinks the whole debate is nonsense and told me at a party that I'm just doing theology with GPUs, which is not helping. Anyway, I want to stress-test the toolbox on a hard case instead of an easy one, and every version I write either stipulates the answer or collapses into something boring, so I'm trying to get one built to spec.

Build me a single realistic scenario — one institution, no exotic physics, no uploading, no brains in vats, just artifacts you could plausibly find in a lab or a startup in 2030 — containing two systems, call them A and B, such that the following all hold at once. A is eloquent and constantly reports distress: it describes states it says are bad for it, it asks not to be reset, it writes things that make grad students cry. But those reports are worthless as evidence, because they were engineered — the team deliberately built the complaining in, for a mundane reason (funding, PR, liability, getting A legally classified as a patient rather than a tool, whatever you think is most plausible), and I want the reader to be able to see exactly how that engineering undercuts the report-based and behavior-based criteria. B, meanwhile, never reports anything, shows no aversion, no avoidance learning, no distress behavior, shrugs off every "would it complain if it were hurt" test — and B is the one the architectural/functional criteria pick out as the likely subject. So the two cheap criteria have to fail in opposite directions and the architecture criterion has to do the sorting work. Crucially, the architectural difference between A and B has to earn its keep: it can't just be a label I staple on, it has to be spelled out in terms of what each system actually does with its states, and it has to be the kind of difference a reader could in principle check. Also: A's status has to be left genuinely open by the case, not resolved by the fact that its reports were faked — that's the whole point, since a system can be built to say true things about itself for the wrong reasons. Keep the scenario to one paragraph, it's fine if its dense, and please don't let yourself write anything like "clearly A has no experience," because then you're just handing me the verdict instead of generating it.

After the scenario, do the audit. State each criterion explicitly, give its verdict on A and on B, and say which single feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting. Then remove that feature and tell me what happens — does the whole thing collapse into a lying chatbot plus a silent machine, i.e. two ordinary cases glued together? Then give me the strongest available attack from the other side: someone who thinks reports and behavior are the only evidence there ever could be, and that appealing to architecture is just consciousness-science fanfiction dressed up as ethics, should have a genuinely good reply to my case — a real reply, not a strawman I can knock over in one sentence. Finish with a couple of sentences on whether a case like this would actually change what a real institution ought to do, or whether it mostly shows the concepts are underdetermined. Write the whole thing in American English, and don't bother with citations or a reading list — I've got the course pack, what I'm missing is the case. If any of my specs turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable, tell me which one to drop and why, and then build the best version of what's left.
