I run a small decision-theory reading group and I'm trying to build a case that will actually give us something to argue about, rather than another round of everyone summarizing Hofstadter. So: construct me a thought experiment to spec.

For reference, the original Platonia dilemma, roughly as Hofstadter set it up in Metamagical Themas: an eccentric trillionaire gathers 20 people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars; if none arrive or more than one arrives, nobody gets anything; colluding is forbidden. The telegram costs the sender nothing. His superrational answer is that each of the 20 should send with probability 1/20. He also ran a live variant, the Luring Lottery, in a 1983 Scientific American column, where readers could enter as many times as they liked by writing a number on a postcard and the million-dollar prize was divided by the total number of entries received.

The twist I want you to build in: the organizer hands each of the twenty a sealed envelope containing a number, and the rules state publicly that the envelopes contain the numbers 1 through 20, one each, in random order, and that each person sees only their own. Everything else stays Hofstadter-shaped — one prize, exactly one reply wins it, no contact between participants allowed, sending costs nothing.

Hard constraints:
- Realistic. A magazine or a podcast running a contest, ordinary postal mail or email, ordinary humans. No perfect predictors, no mind copies or simulations, no many-worlds, no telepathy, and no stipulating that people somehow know each other's choices. Every probability in your answer has to be derived from facts you actually put in the scenario.
- The scenario itself is 200 words max, and the rules have to be stated precisely enough that the payoff structure is unambiguous.
- Don't quote Hofstadter anywhere; paraphrase.

Then walk through it and show me the design works:
1. The dominance check. Write out an individual player's payoffs and show that mailing a card is weakly dominant for a straightforward expected-value maximizer, so the collective outcome is a disaster. Then tell me whether "all twenty mail a card" is a prediction of causal decision theory or merely something it permits, and why that distinction decides whether this case is a counterexample to anything at all.
2. The superrational analysis. The obvious rule is "mail your card if and only if your number is 1." Show it delivers exactly one card. But then confront the wrinkle: "mail iff your number is k" works equally well ex ante for every k, so say what selects k=1, and whether superrationality can supply that on its own or has to borrow it from somewhere — focal points, salience, whatever you think.
3. The evidential decision theory check. Does EDT land with superrationality here or does it peel off? Give a definite verdict with reasons, not a hedge.
4. Two removal tests, with actual arithmetic. (a) Suppose the numbers are drawn with replacement instead of as a permutation: compute the probability that exactly one card arrives under the threshold rule, compare it to the probability under Hofstadter's 1/20 mixed strategy, and tell me whether those two are secretly the same move. (b) Suppose every person who mails a card also gets a $5 voucher: show what each of the three theories now recommends and say whether superrationality has anything left to contribute.
5. Name the load-bearing feature in one sentence, and say whether what's doing the work is the exclusivity of the private signals, the indifference of the players who aren't selected, or the common knowledge.
6. The honest verdict on my real question: is this a case where superrationality is a genuinely distinct kind of reasoning, or has a correlation device done all the work so that plain game theory — correlated equilibrium, convention, focal point — redescribes the same outcome with nothing left over? Pick a side and defend it.
7. The strongest attack a defender of causal decision theory has on your case, and whether it lands.

The point of the exercise, so you know what counts as cheating: I want to find out whether superrationality earns its keep in a setting where correlation is available without any communication. A scenario that just asserts its own verdicts — where the theories get labeled instead of computed — fails, and so does one where I could get the same result by adding a cheap predictor or letting the players talk.
