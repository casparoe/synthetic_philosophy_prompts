I am a fifth-year doctoral student in philosophy of science, and this term I am the instructor of record for an upper-division seminar on the metaphysics of space and time — twelve students, mostly seniors, plus two MA candidates. They have worked through the Scholium that follows the Definitions in the *Principia*, the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence, Mach's reconstruction of the bucket, and the modern literature on the hole argument and on gauge redundancy; their differential geometry is informal at best. The final is a closed-book, three-hour paper. I have already written the question on determinism and gauge freedom, and I need a second essay question, on substantivalism and relationism, that will not reward recitation.

Here is the difficulty I want the question to exploit. Every version of this question I have seen in past papers from other departments asks students to explain the bucket argument and then to assess Mach's reply, and a student who has memorized the shape of that dialectic can produce a competent-looking essay without ever grasping why rotation is supposed to be metaphysically different from velocity. I want to build the question around a case of my own devising that removes the empirical handle the standard argument depends on.

**Case R.** A Newtonian world containing exactly one material object: a thin, perfectly rigid, homogeneous ring. There is no other matter anywhere, no bucket, no water, no distant stars. Consider two such worlds. In W₁ the ring does not rotate; in W₂ it rotates uniformly about its axis at constant angular velocity. Because the ring is perfectly rigid, its rotation produces no deformation, no equatorial bulge, no differential strain, and no change in the distances among its parts at any time. W₁ and W₂ therefore agree on every intrinsic property of the ring and on every spatiotemporal relation holding among its parts, and there is nothing else in either world to which the ring could stand in relations of rest or motion.

**Variant R\*.** As R, except that each world also contains a very distant, extremely massive spherical shell of matter enclosing the ring — counter-rotating in W₂, at rest in W₁.

The question should require students to say what a substantivalist and what a relationist are each committed to about W₁ and W₂ under R, and which view the case favors. But the crux I want them to reach is whether R describes a possible world at all, and what follows for each view on either answer. A relationist who declares W₁ and W₂ to be one and the same world has applied the Leibniz-shift strategy to rotation rather than translation, and students should notice that this is the same identification general covariance licenses for diffeomorphic models — so accepting it here appears to concede the relationist thesis in precisely the case where Newton took himself to have refuted it. A substantivalist who resists must explain why the identification is legitimate in one setting and not the other. A student who never asks whether perfect rigidity together with uniform rotation is coherent has missed the point.

Please write:

1. The exam question itself, with R and R\* stated for the students exactly as they should see them on the paper, and with sub-parts that make the intended crux unavoidable without naming it. It should be answerable in about ninety minutes in roughly 1,200 words.

2. A model answer of about 650 words that argues rather than surveys. It should discharge both horns of the possibility dilemma; say what positive account of rotation the relationist owes in R, given that relative motion is unavailable and that buying relationism about position by positing absolute acceleration as a primitive property of the ring is a retreat rather than a solution; and use R\* to test whether the Machian strategy explains rotation or merely relocates the unexplained element into the shell.

3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks, with each must-hit move named and priced, and the deductions stated as the specific errors that should cost marks — among them: conflating absolute rotation with absolute velocity; treating Mach's principle as a theorem rather than a research program; collapsing the epistemic question (could an inhabitant of W₂ detect the rotation?) into the metaphysical one (is there a fact of the matter?); reading the hole argument back into Newtonian space as though the two settings were interchangeable; and inferring from undetectability that the W₁/W₂ distinction is merely instrumental. Award no marks for naming philosophers or citing texts: the paper is closed-book, and I want the case to do the work.

4. A short paragraph distinguishing an A answer from a B answer and from a C answer by the moves each contains. I expect the A/B line to fall on whether the student recognizes that R may be impossible and then works out the consequences for both views anyway.
