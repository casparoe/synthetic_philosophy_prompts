I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and next month's issue carries a long feature of mine on the prehistory of the infographic — the Social and Economic Museum in Red Vienna, where Otto Neurath, the artist Gerd Arntz, and Marie Reidemeister (later Marie Neurath) built a method of pictorial statistics between 1925 and 1934 that was eventually renamed Isotype. The art desk is running three of the original charts beside the piece, and my editor has left a gap at the close of the article for something that is neither a pull-quote nor a summary. I would like a poem for that gap, and I want to be precise about what I am commissioning, because the obvious version of this poem — a sentimental one about turning numbers into pictures — is precisely what I do not want.

Write it in the voice of the transformer. That was the job on the Vienna team: the person who took material from specialists and turned it into a chart a layperson could read, a post Neurath described as the trustee of the public. The speaker is therefore someone professionally obliged to simplify, to decide what a single sign stands for, and to answer to readers rather than to experts.

The governing constraint should be Isotype's first rule: greater quantities are shown not by enlarging a symbol but by repeating the same symbol at the same size, each repetition carrying a fixed value the reader can count if need be. Neurath's reason was that a viewer cannot reliably compare an area against a length, whereas a repeated sign of constant value can be counted. I want that rule to be the poem's form rather than its subject.

- A single refrain line, word-for-word and punctuation-for-punctuation identical on every appearance, occurring exactly ten times. This is the unit sign. It may not be varied, intensified, inverted, truncated, or echoed; no adjective or adverb may attach to it, and no later occurrence may be permitted to mean more than an earlier one. Choose a short, concrete line — the verbal equivalent of one of Arntz's woodcut figures, not a maxim — capable of standing for the same determinate quantum each time.
- The ten occurrences should fall into two unequal groups, and which group is larger must be discoverable only by counting. The groups may be marked by stanza breaks or white space, never by arithmetic: no digits, no cardinal number-words, no ordinals, anywhere in the body of the poem.
- Any turn in the poem must happen in the lines between occurrences. The refrain stays fixed while the world around it moves; that immobility is the argument.
- One line, and only one, may function as the caption — the verbal element Isotype always required, the single place where the poem may say what the signs cannot say on their own. It must not be allowed to do all the work.

On content: the two series being compared should be one and the same sign put to two uses. The first is the museum's own purpose — a chart made for a workers' city, legible to people who had arrived there speaking different languages, the promise behind Neurath's slogan that words divide and pictures unite. The second comes later, and I will leave you to decide how explicit to be; the historical fact I want the poem to lean on is that when Otto and Marie Neurath reached England in 1940 they were interned as enemy aliens, and that the classification which put them in that category was itself a uniform sign applied to a counted population. If the refrain can serve both authorities without changing, the poem will have done what I need. If it has to soften one of them in order to work, tell me so.

Roughly thirty lines, readable by someone who has never heard of any of this, and the body should not name Neurath, Isotype, Vienna, or the method. Give it a title that does not name the method either. I would rather it not rhyme than rhyme badly.

After the poem, add a note of five or six sentences telling me where it cheats: which recurrence you were tempted to vary, whether the count actually delivers the comparison, and whether identifying a unit sign with a poetic refrain holds up under scrutiny or is a convenience you were glad of. Treat that note as a place to disagree with me rather than to reassure me. In particular, if you think the slide from the museum's charts to the internment register rests on an equivocation — that a sign devised for voluntary public education and a sign used for compulsory classification are not the same operation, and that a poem implying otherwise draws its force from a false analogy — say so at the top of the note and either decline the commission or write a different poem and explain the substitution. I would much rather be told the brief is unsound than handed something that flatters it.
