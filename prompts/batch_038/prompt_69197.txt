I am a junior, and last week our AP Environmental Science seminar got into an argument I have not been able to leave alone. We were discussing how much weight to give forecasts of resource collapse, and a classmate of mine — a sharp one, who reads widely — announced that Malthus was never refuted at all: "Malthus was right, he was just early." He then pushed the slogan further, and this is the part I want to deal with. His claim was that because a famous long-run prediction of scarcity looked flatly false for two centuries and then turned out to be merely premature, the same benefit of the doubt must go to today's predictions about fisheries collapse, aquifer depletion, and topsoil loss even though they too keep missing their dates; dismissing such forecasts as alarmism, he said, is precisely the mistake Malthus's critics made. I tried to object and got nowhere, partly because I could never pin down what his sentence was actually asserting.

Some background I can supply, because I think the readings need to be tested against the real mechanism rather than the cartoon. Malthus's claim was that population, if unchecked, grows geometrically while the food supply grows only arithmetically, so that subsistence presses back on numbers through preventive and positive checks; he wrote partly against the perfectibilists of his own day and against English poor relief, and he revised the book substantially in later editions, adding moral restraint as a check and softening the argument. The subsequent history is what my classmate leans on: real wages for English laborers barely moved for decades after the essay appeared, then industrial fixation of nitrogen, mechanization, and the mid-century gains in crop yields pushed food output far faster than he allowed, and fertility fell as incomes rose instead of climbing back toward the subsistence ceiling. Later versions of the same forecast — the population-bomb warnings of 1968, the growth-limits modelling of 1972, and the 1980 wager on the price of five metals — went the same way.

What I want is a disambiguation, not a verdict on Malthus. Please separate the distinct precise claims that "Malthus was right, he was just early" could be making, from the ones that are barely more than a truism to the ones that would amount to a substantial and controversial thesis about how long-run predictions can be confirmed or rescued, and say for each whether it holds and whether it does the two jobs my classmate needed it to do.
