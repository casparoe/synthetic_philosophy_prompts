I work in claims processing at a mid-sized insurance firm, and for about two years now I have been reading about AI alignment on my own — no degree, no coursework, mostly online essays and forum threads, plus a Tuesday-night video call with five or six other self-taught people where we argue about a topic someone brings. I am reasonably handy with scripts but I have never trained anything larger than a fine-tuned classifier. Last spring I built a small pipeline that turned out to be more philosophically interesting than it was commercially useful: a modest open-weights model reads a claimant's statement and produces a draft summary for the adjuster, but instead of asking it for the summary directly, the job is decomposed into pieces — pull out the dates, pull out the medical terminology, pull out the monetary amounts, flag internal contradictions — each piece is shown to a human reviewer next to the relevant passage of the source text, and only then are the approved pieces assembled into the final report. What I noticed after a few months is that the individual pieces almost always pass review, while the assembled report occasionally says something no reviewer would have approved had it been shown to them as a whole: a contradiction gets resolved in the wrong direction, or two separately accurate date extractions combine into an implied sequence of events that the statement does not support. That observation sent me back to the amplification-and-distillation line of thinking, which I understand — possibly wrongly, and please correct me if I have it wrong — as the proposal that decomposition lets a weak model together with a human evaluator perform and check work that neither could handle alone, and that the checked, assisted process can then be trained back down into a cheap model that does it unaided.

Trying to make my pipeline experience into a general objection, I wrote out the following, and now I cannot tell whether it is a genuine dilemma or a piece of motivated reasoning with a hole in it. (1) The point of decomposition, in that scheme, is that each subtask is one a weak model can perform and a human overseer can reliably evaluate. (2) To evaluate a subtask reliably, the overseer has to know how that subtask bears on the finished output; without that, the overseer is merely checking a string against nothing. (3) An overseer who knows how every subtask bears on the finished output, and who is competent to evaluate each of them, is in a position to evaluate the finished output. (4) So whenever decomposition makes a task evaluable, the task was already evaluable without it: decomposition relocates effort and attention, but adds no evaluative reach. (5) It follows that the assisted, "amplified" human is not more capable of oversight than the unaided human in any sense that matters, and training a model to imitate the assisted process therefore inherits no oversight that was not already available to the unaided human. (6) Yet my pipeline, and I suspect most working pipelines of this shape, produces assembled outputs that reviewers would reject if they saw them whole even though every component was approved. (7) So decomposition sometimes hands over an output that lies beyond the overseer's evaluation, which contradicts (4). (8) Since (6) is an observation and (1) through (3) are what the scheme needs in order to work at all, the scheme is self-undermining: either it is redundant, or it silently transfers to the model a product nobody ever checked. Decomposition does not preserve alignment, and for the same reason it does not preserve competence — because the property in question was never being checked at the level where it actually lives.

Here is what I want from you. Tell me where this goes wrong, if it does: which step is the faulty one, why that step looked obviously true to me when I wrote it down, and what a defender of the decomposition strategy would say in reply — in particular whether there is a respectable sense in which (4) and (6) can both stand without the whole thing collapsing. If instead you think the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is cheapest to abandon, then price the options for me: say what giving up (2) costs, what giving up (3) costs, what giving up (5) costs, and which abandonment leaves the scheme with the most of its original promise intact. I should tell you where my own sympathies lie, so you can push against them: I would much rather keep (6), since it is the only step I have actually watched happen, than keep (5), which I wrote mostly to make the conclusion sting. I am also aware that (6) may be an artifact of my having decomposed badly rather than evidence about decomposition as such, and I would like you to say plainly if you think that is the whole answer. Finally, I am not asking for references or a reading list — I cannot chase those down and I would rather have the reasoning — but I do want you to tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you settle on, and to single out the point in your own reasoning about my argument, the place where you are most likely to be wrong.
