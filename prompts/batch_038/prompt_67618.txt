I am the risk analyst for a mid-sized mutual insurer. Each renewal season our capital committee chooses between the two excess-of-loss structures the actuarial team has modelled. The pack always presents them in the same form: the recovery each structure would produce under the modelled scenario set, together with the modelled probability of each recovery. The two structures are priced comparably in every year, so the committee has treated its task as a straight choice between two modelled recovery distributions. Where a structure is listed below as guaranteed, the actuaries model it as paying that amount in every scenario. The minutes record votes and nothing else, so the record has to be read behaviourally rather than from stated reasoning.

The record, in millions of dollars of recovery:

1. 2015 — (a) $5 guaranteed; (b) 10% chance of $30, 89% chance of $5, 1% chance of $0. Chose (a).
2. 2017 — (a) 11% chance of $5, 89% chance of $0; (b) 10% chance of $30, 90% chance of $0. Chose (b).
3. 2019 — (a) $12 guaranteed; (b) 25% chance of $60, 75% chance of $0. Chose (a).
4. 2019, second vote at the same meeting, on a proportionally scaled-down version of the same tower — (a) 20% chance of $12, 80% chance of $0; (b) 5% chance of $60, 95% chance of $0. Chose (b).
5. 2022 — (a) 50% chance of $26, 50% chance of $8; (b) 60% chance of $45, 40% chance of $0. Chose (b).

This renewal the pack offers: (a) 65% chance of $31, 35% chance of $0; (b) 55% chance of $34, 45% chance of $11. I have to brief the committee, and I want the brief to be honest about what our own record does and does not determine.

Please address the following.

1. Identify which of the five votes are incompatible with expected-utility maximisation, singly or in combination, and state precisely what has to give in each case. Say also whether the formal structure at issue is even present in this year's pair, or whether the new pair is a different kind of test altogether. Given that the record as a whole cannot be rationalised as expected-utility maximisation, explain what standard of self-consistency is still available to us, since being consistent with our precedents cannot mean being consistent with that axiom.

2. Reconstruct the candidate decision rules that accommodate all five votes. You may consider a premium attaching to probability one, non-linear probability weighting, a threshold or safety-first rule, a worst-case rule, anticipated regret, or rules not on this list. For each candidate, state whether it survives the whole record and which particular vote eliminates it if it does not. Where a surviving rule carries a free parameter — an implicit threshold, a weighting function, a curvature parameter — say exactly what interval or constraint the five votes pin it to, and show the arithmetic that pins it down.

3. Predict this year's choice, and identify which of the five precedents is the closest analogue to the new pair, explaining the respects in which it is close and the respects in which the analogy fails.

4. If the record genuinely underdetermines the new pair, set out what principle the committee would be adopting by deciding each way: not merely which option it would take, but which account of its own past behaviour it would be endorsing, and what it would be giving up. Then state your own best guess and the reasoning that tips it.

5. Design one further pair of structures, with explicit probabilities and amounts, that would separate whichever rules remain tied after this year's vote, and state what each surviving rule predicts for it.
