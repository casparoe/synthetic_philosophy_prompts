I work on grant evaluations for a small foundation, and this quarter I was asked to produce a single dashboard slide that would tell our board whether violent harm to humans is rising or falling over the long run. What should have been an afternoon of work turned into a two-week argument with a colleague who is by training a statistician, and I have come away convinced that the argument we were having is not the argument we thought we were having. My draft slide reported the per-capita figures: homicide rates across Europe running at a small fraction of their medieval levels, and battle-deaths per hundred thousand people at historical lows over the last several decades, on the strength of which I wrote that the world is markedly less violent than it was. My colleague's objection was not that any number on the slide was wrong. He said that the slide was, in his phrase, an instance of a category error committed in good faith: that the war-death series is heavy-tailed, so a run of quiet decades licenses almost nothing about the underlying distribution's mean; that dividing by a population that has multiplied several-fold quietly converts a fall in absolute harm into a fall in a ratio; that "homicide" in the deep past is reconstructed from court and clerical records whose very existence, coverage, and legal definitions shifted over the centuries, so the early data points measure record-keeping as much as killing; and that once you admit what the structural-violence tradition counts as violence — coercion, carceral confinement, preventable deprivation enforced by institutions — the trend line I drew may not even be a trend line in the same quantity at both ends.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that the public version of this debate — the optimist's long-run decline thesis, championed most famously in *The Better Angels of Our Nature*, against the anthropologists working from the ethnographic and osteological record of small-scale societies, the statisticians pressing the fat-tail objection, and the historians pressing the source-criticism objection — is not one disagreement but at least four that have been fused under a single question. Call them: a dispute about the extension of the term (is the bundle of phenomena grouped under "violence" homogeneous enough that a single time series exists in principle, or is it a cluster whose members diverge, so that "is violence declining?" is ill-posed rather than contested?); a dispute about the choice of estimator and aggregation function (mean versus tail quantile, rate versus count, and whose denominator is doing the work); a dispute about evidential standards and what the surviving records license; and a dispute about what the answer is *for* — a retrospective description of what happened versus a prospective risk assessment of what will happen, which I suspect are the two questions the optimists and the tail-risk critics are each really answering.

Diagnose the structure of this impasse rather than adjudicate it: which of these four loci is doing most of the work, which are genuinely substantive, which are close to verbal, and where, if anywhere, the two sides are simply answering different questions while appearing to contradict each other. I want you to engage the actual arguments each side puts forward, not summarize positions from a distance, and I want you to be explicit about the parts of my taxonomy that are wrong, redundant, or missing a category I have not thought of. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction that dissolves part of it, a new kind of data that could bear on it, a formal result about heavy-tailed series and what decades of observation can and cannot support, or a reformulation of the question into several that each have determinate answers — or argue, if that is your conclusion, that the impasse is permanent and explain what feature of the question guarantees it. Finally, and practically: if your diagnosis is right, what should my slide have said?
