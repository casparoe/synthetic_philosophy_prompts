I'm preparing the presentation for a graduate reading group on probability and evidence — eight or nine of us, a mix of philosophy and statistics students, meeting in someone's kitchen on Thursdays. We were supposed to be working through the confirmation paradoxes, but I keep getting pulled back to a more basic question: what should govern the way my beliefs ought to move when my senses deliver something less than certainty? I can state a tidy rule for the certain case — shift each hypothesis's probability to my conditional probability for it given what I've learned — but my own verdicts about everyday perception don't line up with that rule, and I can't articulate the principle I'm actually relying on. Here they are.

1. Under dim light I look at a card drawn from a deck and can't tell red from black; my credences settle at 0.7 red, 0.3 black. I then raise my confidence that a second, unseen card is red in proportion, weighting by my conditional probabilities for its color given each color of the first. That seems clearly right — the only sensible way to handle mushy perception.

2. Later the lights come up and I see the first card is black. I now think my earlier update was still rational even though it was not knowledge-producing; I'd been appropriately cautious, not confused. Also right.

3. But suppose that instead of the lights coming up, I simply became certain the card was black, with no further look at it. My final beliefs about the second card would then differ from what they'd have been had I conditioned on blackness straight away from the start. That seems like a defect: the order and manner in which certainty arrives shouldn't matter, yet here it does.

4. A variant of the same worry. On Monday my credences over a shuffled deck go to 0.6 that the top card is a face card; on Wednesday they're 0.4, for no reason I can identify — I hadn't looked again. Conditionalizing on my Monday state and then reversing the shift doesn't recover the Wednesday state, and I think it shouldn't: a bare drift in my observational probabilities, with nothing new impinging on me, is no way to acquire evidence.

5. Here is where I get stuck, because case 4 looks structurally identical to a case I'm happy to endorse. I glance at my socks and shift to 0.8 that they match, then to 0.95, with no second glance — the extra confidence just came over me. I think that's irrational, close to pulling a belief out of thin air. But now suppose I take a bottle of thirty pills, judge from a hasty first count that there are about 28 left, and a week later, feeling unwell, sit down and recount carefully and conclude there were only 21. My credence that I accidentally doubled a dose rises sharply, my credence that some unrelated hypothesis about my health is true falls, and I think all of that is plainly rational. In both cases the probabilities of the "data" moved without my acquiring anything describable as new evidence; only one of them feels like genuine learning.

Is there a principle — or a small, precisely stateable set of competing principles — that delivers these verdicts? For each candidate I'd like the clause that earns its keep tied to the case that motivates it, what it says about situations I haven't thought to raise (for instance: my perceptual probabilities shifting because of what I already believe about the scene, or because a friend tells me the lighting is deceptive), and an unvarnished account of which of my five verdicts it cannot save. If the honest conclusion is that cases 4 and 5 can't be pulled apart by anything a rule could state, tell me which of the two I should surrender, and what I gain by surrendering it.
