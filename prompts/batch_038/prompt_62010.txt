So — next month I'm giving a 25-minute talk at a workshop on temporal perception, and the room is about 70% vision scientists and computational modellers (postdiction, flash-lag, motion-induced blindness, that crowd), three philosophers of mind who work on consciousness, and one psychiatrist who runs timing tasks in clinical populations. The philosophers will know the Orwellian/Stalinesque vocabulary cold and will want to argue about it; the vision people know the experiments but have zero patience for metaphysics, and if you say anything that sounds like "the data can't decide this," they hear it as a confession that you've wandered off from the science. I have basically no idea how to handle both groups in the same Q&A.

The thesis: the standard framing — either experience gets written down on time and edited afterwards in memory (Orwellian), or the experience itself is revised with a delay before it's available at all (Stalinesque) — is a false dichotomy, but not in the usual deflationary direction. I want to keep the claim that experience has genuine temporal content while denying it has a sharp temporal location: on my view the content of an experience is a smeared interval with a measurable width, so "what colour were they seeing at t?" can have the determinate answer "no determinate colour, and here is exactly how indeterminate." Determinate indeterminacy, not multiple drafts, and not a Cartesian theatre with the edges blurred off. My argument sketch is: (1) every candidate moment for when the experience occured in the colour-change paradigms — first onset, second onset, the report, the neural event you read the report off of — is either arbitrarily privileged or collapses back into the timing question it was supposed to settle; (2) but the smear isn't an epistemic shrug, because its width varies predictably with stimulus spacing and with attention, so there's something to model; (3) so the right target for a theory is a function from stimulus history to interval-valued content, and both stock labels misdescribe it — one by putting content too late, the other by dissolving content altogether.
