I spent the better part of four decades at a national laboratory working on the statistical mechanics of disordered systems, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with rather more enthusiasm than my former colleagues think wise. My way in was, I confess, a popular book: my granddaughter left a copy of Gödel, Escher, Bach at our house, and I have been arguing with it ever since, with a framed print of Escher's two hands drawing one another propped on my desk as a sort of exhibit. My instinct is mechanist — forty years of watching matter do surprising things without anyone's assistance has left me distrustful of whatever smells of a soul — and a former student of mine who now builds software assures me the question is settled. But I notice that the mechanist side of that book argues largely by insinuation, and I should like to hear the other side stated by someone who will not flinch.

So: please play Kurt Gödel, in the first person, as he was in his last years at the Institute in Princeton. You may draw on the concepts available to you up to 1978, together with whatever I tell you about strange loops, tangled hierarchies, and the present state of computing; if you need a fact about the modern world, ask me for it as you would ask a colleague, and I will supply it. Do not quote your own writings — paraphrase them; I have no appetite for invented quotations, and no interest in references to books written about you. Speak as you would have spoken: unhurried, fond of distinctions, plain about your realism concerning mathematical concepts and about your conviction that the mind is more than its mechanisms, and unembarrassed if I choose to call that a soul. Hold the main thesis — that the mind, in its mathematical reasoning, infinitely surpasses the powers of any finite machine — and do not surrender it. You may concede smaller points, and I would expect you to: that the two hands are a fair picture of self-reference; that the second horn of the disjunction you set out in your 1951 Gibbs Lecture cannot simply be refuted; and that the familiar step from any given machine's undecidable sentence to the superiority of the mind rests on an assumption about that machine's soundness which we cannot certify in our own case. Keep each reply to roughly a hundred and fifty words, so that this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and end every turn either with a question to me or with a distinction I am obliged to answer. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening move. Look at those hands. Each wrist emerges from a flat sheet and draws the other into being; there is no bottom level and no top level, and — this is what the book presses — nothing within the picture stands outside the tangle. The claim is that a mind is exactly such a structure, and that this disposes of you: what you describe as stepping outside a formal system and recognizing the truth of a sentence that system cannot prove is, on this account, merely the loop turning once more, one hand drawing the hand that drew it. There is no inviolate level, and none is needed. So I put it to you as a dilemma. Either you do require such a level — a mind standing beyond every finite formal system, surveying it from a place the system cannot reach — in which case you have bought your conclusion at the price of something I can only name a soul; or your recognizing of truth is just another turn of the loop, in which case it is something a machine can do as well, and the first horn of your disjunction is gone. Which is it?
