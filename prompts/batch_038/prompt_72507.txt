The paper is by a very bright MA student in my seminar on the philosophy of discovery, and the target is the whole apparatus of scientific credit — priority, eponymy, prizes. Their argument, tidied up but I don't think distorted:

(1) If a result would have been produced by someone else at roughly the same time had its actual producer never lived, then the actual producer made no contribution that deserves credit. The result was coming anyway; they just happened to be standing there.
(2) For essentially every major result in the history of science, that counterfactual is satisfied. The record is full of multiples. Calculus turns up twice, with a priority war afterward; oxygen is isolated by more than one chemist in the same decade before anyone has the concept straight; natural selection is mailed to Darwin by a young man in the Malay Archipelago and read alongside his own notes at a learned society. And where there is no actual second discoverer, there is always a documented rival working the same problem with the same tools, which is all the counterfactual in (1) needs.
(3) So essentially nobody in the history of science made a credit-worthy contribution.
(4) Credit is deserved only in virtue of a credit-worthy contribution.
(5) So no allocation of scientific credit is ever deserved — prize committees arent tracking anything, they're just pulling a name out of a set of interchangeable candidates.
(6) But (5) is absurd, and the student half-sees this: they happily bite the bullet about prizes while insisting it is still literally true that Darwin discovered natural selection and that the rival who would have got there anyway did not.
(7) So something in (1)–(4) has to go.

The student's stated motivation for (1) is the collective-harm literature — Parfit's harmless torturers, the marginal polluter whose share of the damage is below any threshold — read as showing that "makes no difference" can't be the test for whether an act was wrong; they say the same must hold on the credit side, symmetrically. I'm quite attached to (2), which seems to me just solid history, and obviously attached to (6). I'd give up (1) if I had to, but I suspect that's a cheap fix the student will see through in a minute, and I'd rather not pay it without knowing the price. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look innocent when you first read it, and what does rejecting it cost? And if your diagnosis lands on (2) rather than (1), tell me how much of the actual history of science I have to surrender to escape the argument, because that's the version of the reply I'd find most intersting but least expect to work.
