I chair the Clinical Risk Committee of a regional nonprofit blood service: we collect, process and issue platelets to twelve hospitals, and I am the only member with any philosophy training, which is apparently why I have been asked to referee a dispute between our two staff analysts. I finished a doctorate twenty-odd years ago and have not kept up, but my colleagues now assume I hold a settled view because I once used the word "axiom" in a meeting. Each quarter we sign off on a class of precautionary spending decisions, and this quarter's item is whether to license a pathogen-reduction step in platelet processing. The committee has already agreed on two things that are not in dispute. First, a common welfare metric — harm-units, where one unit is one patient permanently injured or killed — and a linear utility over it, because we could not agree on how much worse a forty-patient incident is than a one-patient incident and so stipulated linearity to move on. Second, a single set of probability estimates drawn from fifteen years of haemovigilance data. The options, expressed per year, are: S (status quo), a 1% chance of an incident costing 40 harm-units and otherwise nothing; H (partial uptake, treating only the short-shelf-life components, roughly a third of supply), a fixed cost of 0.25 harm-units and a 0.3% chance of a 40-unit incident; and P (full uptake), a fixed cost of 0.75 harm-units and no residual pathogen risk. Analyst A ranks these by ordinary expected utility and gets H > S > P. Analyst B wants the committee to adopt risk-weighted expected utility with the convex risk function ρ(p) = p², on which the ranking is H > P > S: the two extremes trade places. A says B is either double-counting — catastrophe aversion belongs in the utility function, and had we settled on a concave one we would not need B's apparatus — or, worse, mislabelling an epistemic worry as a preference, since A suspects B does not really believe our 1% figure and is smuggling distrust of the data in through the risk function. B says A has it backwards: the issue is not how bad the worst outcome is but how much of the risk is concentrated in it, and no concave utility can express that, because it is the independence axiom that forbids it. B has also pointed out, not unreasonably, that if the risk function is doing normative work the committee explicitly declined to put into the utility scale, then adopting it would silently reverse a decision we took deliberately.

Two further things I cannot resolve on my own. Both analysts apply their rule to a single year and multiply by five, the licence term; but when I bundled the five years into one gamble and ran B's rule over the package, I got an emphatic recommendation for P rather than S, driven as far as I can tell by the state in which five incidents occur in five years, whose probability is on the order of one in ten billion. Either I have made a computational error, or the framework is far more sensitive to how decisions are bundled than anyone has said out loud, and I cannot tell which. Separately, a friend of mine who teaches decision theory glanced at my numbers and said our case was "just the Allais pattern again," which I do not think is quite right — I cannot see where the common consequence is supposed to come in — and I would like to know whether she was being precise or merely loose.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, roughly 1,200 words and no more than 1,500, addressed to a committee of which two members have no technical background at all. It should open with a bottom line on this quarter's item and on the standing decision rule, then give the reasoning in short headed sections. I need it to do five things. Establish whether the disagreement between A and B can be recaptured by a suitably concave utility function, what axiom is actually being surrendered if it cannot, and whether my friend's remark about Allais is accurate. Take seriously A's deflationary diagnosis that B's risk function is a crude proxy for distrust of the incidence estimates, and say whether the right response to that worry is a different decision rule or something else, such as sensitivity analysis over the parameters. Address the standard dynamic-choice objection that an agent using B's rule can be drained by a sequence of individually acceptable offers, together with the standard reply that the rule must be applied globally to whole packages of decisions rather than case by case — and then say what that reply costs a public body that must treat like cases alike, that changes membership between quarters, and whose "package" has no obvious boundaries. Say whose risk attitude is in play: an individual may be entitled to be cautious with her own life, but we are selecting a risk profile on behalf of people who are not in the room, where those who bear the fixed costs and those who benefit from the averted catastrophe are largely different people, and I want the memo to say whether that is legitimate and what would make it legitimate. Finally, state the strongest case for whichever side loses, in terms that side would recognize rather than as a straw man, and end with a rule or test the committee can actually apply to next quarter's item without reopening the argument from scratch. Do not send me references or a reading list; the committee has no appetite for the literature. But the technical vocabulary must be used correctly, and wherever a claim is mathematical it should be shown briefly on our numbers rather than asserted.
