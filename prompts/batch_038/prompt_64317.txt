Thirty years of reactor physics and safeguards work left me with a habit of asking what a claim would take to be wrong; only in retirement have I turned that habit on moral philosophy. Anscombe's pamphlet against Oxford's awarding Truman an honorary degree has persuaded me: choosing to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is always murder; double effect is unavailable here, since the deaths in the two cities were the instrument of the intended surrender rather than a side-effect of some other aim; and merely foreseen killing becomes murder where the agent is careless about alternatives. Nor is this pacifism — she allows just wars and the killing of combatants — which is part of what makes it hard to wave away. What would count against it?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Weigh the empirical ones: the target-selection record, Truman's own description of Hiroshima as an army base, Japan's position in the summer of 1945, the Soviet entry, the absence of an ultimatum before the second bomb — and say whether a non-consequentialist verdict should depend on any of them, given that Anscombe's framing leans on the war's being nearer its end than the bombing assumed. Then the conceptual ones: whether the combatant/non-combatant line survives total war and mass mobilization; whether the absolutism is available to anyone who rejects the natural-law and theological commitments behind it; whether 'the innocent' is doing hidden work. Which of these would a committed Anscombean herself concede as counting against the view, and which would she dismiss as beside the point? Finally, judge honestly whether the position has been qualified — by the means/foreseen distinction, by the carelessness clause, by the appeal to what the agent chose — beyond the reach of any evidence, and whether that is a defect in a moral claim of this kind or simply what such claims look like. Where the history, or the reading of Anscombe, is genuinely disputed among people who know the material, tell me so plainly rather than presenting one side as the settled view.
