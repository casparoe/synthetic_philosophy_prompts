I cover AI and adjacent ideas stuff for a general-interest magazine, which mostly means I get smart people to explain things to me until I understand them wrong. I'm working on a piece about goal misgeneralization — the CoinRun-style results where you train an agent on levels where the coin is always at the far right, then at test time you scatter the coin around the level and the agent beelines for the right wall and walks right past it. I get the setup. What I dont get is an argument one of my sources walked me through afterward, which he said shows the whole research program is confused, and which I couldn't answer at the time and still can't.

Best reconstruction I can manage:

(1) The only non-question-begging way to tell what goal a trained system actually has is to look at what it robustly pursues in environments outside its training distribution. Inside the training distribution, "collects coins" and "reaches the right edge" predict exactly the same behavior, so nothing in there can settle which one is the goal.

(2) In the shifted environments the agent robustly goes right. So going-right is its goal. The coin-goal was never in there, and researchers who say the agent "was trained to" collect coins are describing their own intention and calling it a property of the system.

(3) The same test applies to us. What we robustly pursue out-of-distribution fixes our goals too, and out-of-distribution — junk food, contraception, feeds, supernormal stimuli of every kind — we pursue sugar, fat, status and novelty, not nutrition, reproduction or truth.

(4) So either everybody's goals are proxies and there's no principled difference between an aligned system and a misaligned one, or:

(5) Goal ascription is probe-relative — which goal you find depends on which shifted enviornments you test in. His support for this: you can take the same task, change the training recipe (more varied levels, some regularization), and end up with an agent that does go grab the coin out-of-distribution; pick a different probe set and a different behavior looks like the "real" goal. If the answer moves with the test set, there's no fact about the goal independent of the test set.

(6) And if there's no such fact, "goal misgeneralization" isn't a discovery about agents. It's a report about our choice of test environments.

(7) So the alignment problem in its "the model ended up wanting something we didn't mean" form is empty. Not hard — empty.

I'm pretty sure somethings wrong here, because (7) would also have to make "the agent got worse at the task when we moved the coin" empty, and that's a thing you can just measure in these experiments. But I can't find the loose step. (1) seems obviously right to me, it's basically the whole point of doing distribution-shift work. And (3) seems right too, plus it's the part my editor would like.

So, three things. First: which step or steps actually fail, and why did each of them look fine going in? Second: what does giving it up cost — concretely, what would I have to stop claiming in the piece if I reject it? Third: is (3), the human analogy, doing any real argumentative work, or is it rhetoric that just makes (4) feel inevitable? And while you're in there — is he entitled to (5) from the training-recipe point, or does that point establish something weaker than he needs? If the honest verdict is that the argument is valid and (7) is a bullet worth biting, say that instead and tell me what biting it costs him.
