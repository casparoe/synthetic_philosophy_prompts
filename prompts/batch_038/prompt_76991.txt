I write novels, and last weekend I got seated at the dinner after a festival panel I hadn't been on, next to the two people who'd apparently spend the whole afternoon circling each other. They went at it for two hours and I was too fascinated to break it up. Neither knows I'm writing this down, so I'll call them A and B — A is a critic who writes about immigration, B is a translator who's spent thirty years commuting between Lisbon and Warsaw.

The subject was whether a novelist is ever entitled to write a sentence about a national character. Not about a character who happens to be Portuguese. About the Portuguese.

Here's roughly how it went, in their order.

(1) A: Sentences of the form "the X are Y" are either insulting or empty. Trait distributions of neighboring countries overlap almost completely, and even where the means differ the gap is dwarfed by variation inside any single country, so a generalization of that shape gives you essentially zero purchase on the person actually standing in front of you. A novelist who writes one is inviting the reader to do that inference anyway.

(2) B: You're measuring the wrong thing. "Germans keep appointments" isn't a claim about German neurochemistry, it's a claim about a standing expectation that a German can rely on and will be blamed for breaking. That's real, it varies wildly across borders, and it's as much a fact as a bridge is. A novelist who declines to notice it isn't being careful, she's being blind.

(3) A: Then you've quietly swapped character for institutions — and institutions you can describe without any "the Germans are…" sentence at all. Describe the railway, the school, the penalty for lateness. The second you put it in plural-plus-copula form you're back to traits, and that's where your argument stopped working.

(4) B: No, because the reason those rules hold is that people have swallowed them. Somebody feels shame. That's not a timetable, that's a disposition, and disposition is exactly what a novelist is in the business of. Also — you keep talking about the person standing in front of you, but that person is never "a German." She's a 54-year-old dental technician from Bremen with a bad knee and a dead brother. The generalization isn't competing with a base rate. It's competing with nothing at all.

(5) A: That is the most dangerous thing you've said. The reason it isn't competing with a base rate is that the reader supplies one silently and runs the inference backwards, from group to individual, which is the textbook definition of a stereotype. And there's a study, a big one, something like fifty countries, where they had people describe the typical member of their own culture and then compared those descriptions to the average personality scores of actual people in those countries — and the two barely lined up. People are confidently describing a thing that isn't in the data.

(6) B: Granted. But your rule has a consequence you haven't looked at. Nobody writes "the Swedes are honest" with a straight face; the form selects for the pejorative. So when you ban it you hand the whole territory to the writers who don't care, and you forbid the affectionate version — the Nigerian novelist's joke about Nigerians that only a Nigerian would get, and that a Nigerian reader would love. You've made the field less accurate and less generous and called that discipline.

(7) A: Affectionate generalization is still generalization, and the affection is doing cover work. And "only a Nigerian would get it" is a membership test, which is worse than the original sentence was.

(8) B: Then what about voicing? I'm not asserting it, a character is thinking it. If a narrator can't voice a prejudice you can't write a prejudiced person, which means you can't write people.

(9) A: Readers can't track that. Irony leaks. The sentence does its work on the reader whether or not you put it in a bigot's mouth, and you know perfectly well that's why the line in your own book —

She didn't finish, he changed the subject, and I've been thinking about it ever since. I'm not going to tell you which of them I wanted to win, because I keep switching, which is presumably its own problem.

Go through 1–9 and tell me who's right at each one: solid, half-right, or wrong, with a sentence or two of reasoning each. Where a move depends on a distinction nobody actually drew, name the distinction and say whether it rescues the move. Then step back — is there some piece of this phenomenon that neither of them brought up, something that would make the whole argument look different from the outside? I'd rather have that than a score.
