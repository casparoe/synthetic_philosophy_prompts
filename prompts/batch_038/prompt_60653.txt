Our regional health authority runs a small compensation scheme for patients who say they were aware during surgery, and I am one of the nine members of the panel that hears the disputed claims — the ones where the trust refuses and the claimant appeals. Almost every file looks like this: the claimant describes a stretch of lucid, frightened awareness, often with specific dialogue, during a window in which our records show an adequate depth of anaesthesia — stable agent concentration well above the usual threshold, no movement, no surge in heart rate or blood pressure, and a processed-EEG index sitting in the deep range. There is no recording of consciousness and never will be, so the panel has to decide what the claimant's own report can carry. Our policy requires "sufficient evidence that the claimant had conscious experience during the procedure" and then stops, which is why these hearings keep turning into arguments about the phrase rather than about the facts.

The panel is split, and not along lines of sympathy. One camp holds that a detailed, internally consistent, non-bizarre report from a claimant with no evident motive and no history that would predict confabulation is the best evidence obtainable, and that treating testimony as second-class here condemns an entire class of genuine injury to being unprovable by construction. The other camp thinks this is a category error. Their argument — which one of our members, a philosopher by training, insists is simply the old Malcolm-and-Dennett dispute about whether dreams are experiences at all, replayed inside an insurance procedure — is that a narrative produced on waking is not a recording of the interval it purports to describe. It could be manufactured at the moment of emergence by a brain that experienced nothing whatever during the preceding hour; and the emergence period is exactly when confident, vivid, retrospective narratives are most reliably produced. This camp points to two files in our own archive in which details the claimants reported as overheard in the operating theatre appear word for word in ward documentation generated after the procedure — details available at the time of reporting and not before. They also point to the mirror image: our records contain patients who, under the technique that leaves one arm unparalysed while the rest of the body is paralysed, obeyed verbal commands during the operation and afterwards recalled nothing of it at all. Report and experience appear to come apart in both directions.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate to the panel before Thursday's session, on the appeal now pending: a thirty-four-year-old claimant reporting roughly eleven minutes of awareness, including two distinct utterances from the theatre team. The first of those utterances appears in the anaesthetic chart at precisely the time she places it; the second appears nowhere in the documentation and is corroborated only by a scrub nurse who says she remembers it being said, though nobody asked her until eighteen months afterwards and by then the nurse had read the claimant's statement. The claimant says she did not know such claims existed until a physiotherapist mentioned them six weeks after her discharge, and she filed a month later than that. Give the panel something it can act on — uphold, dismiss, or uphold in part, with the reasoning stated so the dissenting camp could reply to it — and propose the evidentiary rule we should adopt for this class of claim going forward, since the undefined phrase will keep generating these disputes. Then take the losing side's strongest point seriously and answer it: if you recommend dismissal, say what you make of the fact that testimony is the only evidence a genuine claimant of this kind could ever possess, and what a rule that discounts it does to the people who really were aware; if you recommend upholding, say how your rule is supposed to handle the confabulation files in our own archive, and why it amounts to something other than paying people for having had a particular kind of memory. Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables — the way one would write a letter to colleagues — and put your recommendation in the first two or three sentences so the chair knows immediately where the opinion lands.
