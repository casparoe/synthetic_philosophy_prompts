I'm working through Aristotle on money and exchange and I've hit a knot I can't get untangled, and I think it runs through both texts at once. Can you walk me through the conceptual machinery?

Here's the two halves as I have them. In the Ethics, in the reciprocity-and-exchange part of the discussion of particular justice, money gets introduced as the middle term that measures everything so that a builder's product and a shoemaker's product can be equalized and a proportion set up. But then he says the real standard underneath is demand or need, and money is just its conventional stand-in — nomisma from nomos, something that exists by custom rather than nature and can be changed and made useless at will. He also admits money's purchasing power wobbles over time, though it holds up better than other things. And then the concession that stops me cold: "Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes."

Meanwhile in the Politics he's making what looks like an opposite move. Every possession has a proper use and an exchange use; money gets invented because people needed a portable stand-in for natural goods; and household acquisition is naturally bounded because the needs it serves are bounded. But once money exists, a branch of the acquisitive art starts treating money as both the medium and the goal, and since there's no limit on how much of it you can pile up, that branch has no internal limit at all. Then interest-lending comes out as the worst case, because there the profit comes from money itself rather than from what money was invented to do — money literally breeding money.

So what I want explained:

1. Where exactly does the limit live? Is the difference between the bounded and unbounded forms of acquisition about the object (goods vs. money), about the end (use/consumption vs. more of the same), or about quantity? Those seem like three different diagnoses and I can't tell which one is doing the work.

2. Is the conventionalism doing double duty in a way that's a tension, or is it the same insight showing up twice? I.e. the fact that money has no use except as a convention is precisely what lets it be a neutral measure in the Ethics and precisely what lets it become an unlimited end in the Politics. Is Aristotle aware of that, and does he think it's a problem or a diagnosis?

3. Given the strict-commensurability concession, what's left of the idea that exchange can be *exactly* just? Does proportionate reciprocity collapse into an approximation, and if so does he have a story for why approximations are good enough to hold a city together?

4. Is the usury objection just the no-limit argument applied to the worst case, or is it a separate argument about money's nature? The breeding-money point sounds like it's about what money is for, not about limits, and I can't see how those two hook up.

Gut-level intuition first, then the technical bits. No need to point me at books or hunting down references — I've got the texts, I just don't get how the pieces fit.
