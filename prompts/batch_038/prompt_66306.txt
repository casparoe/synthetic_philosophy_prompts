I spent thirty-eight years at a national measurement institute, most of them concerned with the difference between how a unit is defined and how it is realized, and I have come to legal philosophy late and from that direction. I have now worked through the 1958 exchange between Hart and Fuller, and I find that the usual summaries — positivism against natural law, separation against necessary connection — leave me unable to say what is actually being disputed. I should like to propose a reconstruction of the disagreement and to have you argue for it properly, rather than merely remark that it is suggestive.

The reconstruction runs as follows. In metrology there are two structurally different ways of fixing a quantity. The first is operational, in the sense that Bridgman gave the word when he said that a concept amounts to nothing more than the set of operations by which it is determined: on that view the kilogram simply is what the platinum-iridium cylinder in the vault at Sèvres says it is, there is no gap between standard and application, and consequently no sense in which a particular weighing could be a poor instance of weighing. The second is the modern arrangement, under which the definition is fixed by stipulating the numerical value of a constant — since May 2019 the kilogram is defined by taking the Planck constant to be exactly 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds — while every actual realization of it, whether a Kibble balance or an isotopically enriched silicon sphere, is an approximation carrying an uncertainty budget. On the second view the definition is never identical with any of its realizations, and that gap is not a regrettable accident but precisely what makes it intelligible to call one realization poor, and to compare two of them against each other.

My claim is that Hart treats law on the first model and Fuller on the second. Hart's rule of recognition is a procedure: it is a matter of social fact what officials accept and apply, and because criterion and application cannot come apart there is no room for asking whether a rule satisfying the criterion is law in a greater or a lesser degree. Fuller's eight demands on lawmaking — generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, absence of contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between declared rule and official action — are, on my reading, not moral constraints that a system must satisfy over and above being law. They are the conditions under which a body of directives counts as a successful realization of the thing defined at all: they are law's uncertainty budget, and a regime that fails badly enough on them has not become unjust law so much as it has ceased to realize law.

Argue for this. In particular I want the argument to do four things. First, say which of the eight carry real weight on the reconstruction and which fit it badly or not at all; I suspect congruence and promulgation are doing most of the work, and I do not know what to make of non-retroactivity. Second, settle whether Hart's separability thesis survives the reconstruction intact — my own suspicion is that it does, since no moral truth is required, only a constitutive standard — and whether that survival vindicates Hart or hollows him out. Third, meet the objection that the analogy presupposes law has a function or a telos in the way a measurement standard does, so that the reconstruction quietly assumes the very thing Fuller must establish. Fourth, show what the reconstruction implies about the postwar German handling of the woman who informed on her husband to the authorities under a statute that was on the books, and whether it lands where Hart landed or where Fuller landed.

Reason rather than cite. I keep no library, I have no use for a reference list, and I would far rather you paraphrase both authors than risk putting words in their mouths.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Say which of the four parts you regard as load-bearing for the case, identify the point at which you think my analogy is most likely to break, and state plainly whether it breaks because definition and realization are notions that make sense only where there is a correct value to be measured against — something law may well lack.
