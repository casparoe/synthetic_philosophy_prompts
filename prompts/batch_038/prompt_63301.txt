I am preparing the final problem set for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of physics on the interpretation of quantum theory, and I need one extended problem on the ψ-ontic / ψ-epistemic distinction, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The problem must be entirely self-contained: candidates are given every definition and every number they need, and they are not permitted to consult the literature, so nothing in the problem, the model answer, or the marking scheme may turn on a citation, a reading list, or recall of who proved what. Any reference to well-known no-go results should be conceptual and descriptive only.

The problem is to be built on the following apparatus, which I have already fixed. Please reproduce it exactly in the exam text — you may improve the notation and layout, but do not alter any of the numbers. Before you write anything else, check that each of the three models below in fact reproduces the stated quantum statistics for the measurement M, and that your answers to parts (c) and (d) are consistent with those numbers; if you believe something in the apparatus is inconsistent, say so explicitly at the end rather than silently substituting your own values.

Operational scenario. A single qubit. Two preparations, A and B, corresponding to the pure states |0⟩ and |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, whose quantum overlap is |⟨0|+⟩|² = 1/2. Two measurements: M, the projective measurement in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis with outcomes 0 and 1; and N, the projective measurement in the {|+⟩, |−⟩} basis with outcomes + and −. Quantum statistics: p(0|A) = 1, p(0|B) = 1/2, p(+|A) = 1/2, p(+|B) = 1.

Definitions to be stated stipulatively in the problem. An ontological model supplies an ontic space Λ, for each preparation P an epistemic state μ_P (a probability distribution over Λ), and for each measurement a response function ξ(o|λ) giving the probability of outcome o conditional on λ, such that Σ_λ μ_P(λ) ξ(o|λ) reproduces the quantum statistics. For two preparations A and B, define the directional overlap D(A,B) = Σ_{λ ∈ supp μ_B} μ_A(λ), and the symmetric overlap ω(A,B) = Σ_λ min(μ_A(λ), μ_B(λ)). A model is ψ-ontic if the epistemic states of every pair of distinct pure states have disjoint supports, and ψ-epistemic otherwise. A model is maximally ψ-epistemic (for a given pair) if both D(A,B) and D(B,A) equal the quantum overlap |⟨A|B⟩|².

Three ontological models, each initially specified only for the measurement M.

- T1: Λ = {λ1, λ2}; μ_A = (1, 0); μ_B = (1/2, 1/2); ξ(0|λ1) = 1, ξ(0|λ2) = 0.
- T2: Λ = {λ1, λ2, λ3}; μ_A = (1, 0, 0); μ_B = (0, 1/2, 1/2); ξ(0|λ1) = 1, ξ(0|λ2) = 1, ξ(0|λ3) = 0.
- T3: Λ = {λ1, λ2, λ3}; μ_A = (1/2, 1/2, 0); μ_B = (0, 1/2, 1/2); ξ(0|λ1) = 1, ξ(0|λ2) = 1, ξ(0|λ3) = 0.

A candidate's argument to be evaluated in the final part. After being shown the three models, a student in the seminar advances the following reasoning: (i) T1, T2, and T3 all reproduce the same operational statistics; (ii) they nonetheless fall on opposite sides of the ψ-ontic / ψ-epistemic divide, and only one of them is maximally ψ-epistemic; (iii) therefore no possible experiment on this system could decide whether the quantum state is a state of reality or a state of knowledge; (iv) therefore the question whether the wavefunction is real is not a factual question at all, but a verbal dispute about how to carve up an arbitrary Λ, and the whole technical apparatus of ψ-ontology theorems merely dresses up that verdict.

The problem is to have four parts, worth 5, 5, 8, and 7 marks respectively, for a total of 25, and is intended to occupy roughly ninety minutes of a take-home problem set.

Part (a) should require candidates to classify T1, T2, and T3 by computing D(A,B), D(B,A), and ω(A,B) in each case, and to explain why T1 is a case in which the two overlap measures come apart — ω matches the quantum overlap while neither directional condition is satisfied in both orders — identifying the structural feature of T1 responsible for the asymmetry.

Part (b) should require candidates to show that in T1 an agent who learns the ontic state can sometimes predict the outcome of M with certainty where an agent who knows only the preparation cannot, and to draw out what this implies about the relation between the contemporary ψ-ontology distinction and the older distinction between hidden-variable and no-hidden-variable accounts. The point to be reached is that ψ-epistemicity, as stipulated, does not entail that λ is informationally weaker than ψ.

Part (c) is the analytical core. Candidates must attempt to extend each of the three models so that it also reproduces the statistics of N, and must establish that T1 admits no extension whatsoever, that T2 admits an extension only at the cost of a stochastic response function, and that T3 admits an extension in which every response function for both M and N is deterministic. They must then explain why T1's failure is not an artefact of the particular numbers chosen: within the family of models with μ_A = (1 − q, q, 0) and μ_B = (0, q′, 1 − q′) over Λ = {λ1, λ2, λ3} and the M-responses fixed as in T3, an extension to N exists only when q ≤ 1/2, and is outcome-deterministic only when q = 1/2, which is exactly the value required by maximal ψ-epistemicity for this pair.

Part (d) should ask candidates to evaluate the student's argument: to state its strongest form, to identify the step at which it fails, and to say what would have to be true for it to succeed. The model answer should make the scope distinction explicit — the classification attaches to a model of the whole of quantum theory rather than to a fragment, and the fact that several mutually inequivalent models can be fitted to a restricted set of preparations and measurements does not by itself show that the realist question is empty — and should note, descriptively and without citation, that arguments of the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph type, which add an assumption about the independence of the ontic states of separately prepared systems, are what give the distinction empirical traction at the level of the full theory. It should also credit the observation that the results of part (c) cut against premise (iv) even within the fragment: the three models are empirically equivalent, yet the classification constrains what each of them can be made to do, so it is not a merely verbal carve-up of Λ.

These should include, at minimum: asserting that T1 and T2 could be distinguished by an experiment on this system; treating ω and the directional overlap as interchangeable; inferring from part (b) that ψ-epistemic models are hidden-variable models in the classical sense, or from part (c) that ψ-epistemic accounts have been refuted; and applying a preparation-independence argument to a single-system fragment in which no composite preparation ever occurs.
