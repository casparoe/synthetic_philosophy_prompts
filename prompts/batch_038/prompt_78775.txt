My reading group has just reached the point in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* where Hofstadter turns the incompleteness theorems toward minds — the chapter on jumping out of the system — and one of us, who writes proof assistants for a living, insists that the whole "Gödelian analogy" is nothing more than the Lucas–Penrose argument with better typography. His reconstruction goes like this: if a machine's reasoning is captured by some consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory T containing enough arithmetic, then there is a sentence G_T that T can neither prove nor refute (Gödel's original version needed ω-consistency, Rosser's needs only consistency); yet we, standing outside T, can see that G_T is true, since its truth follows from T's consistency. He adds that this is precisely the capacity no formal system has — recognizing truths that outrun any system that models the recognizer — and that when Hofstadter talks about self-referential patterns giving rise to an "I," he is just offering a poetic gloss on the claim that human minds are non-algorithmic.

If that is really what the analogy amounts to, I cannot see how Hofstadter escapes a dilemma, and this is what I would like your help with. Either our own reasoning is captured by some formal system, in which case we are in exactly the machine's predicament with respect to our own Gödel sentence — and the second incompleteness theorem would seem to rule out our establishing our own consistency, which is the very premise the "we can see G_T is true" move depends on — or our reasoning is not captured by any such system, in which case the strange-loop story does no explanatory work at all and could be replaced, without loss, by the bare assertion that humans are not machines. So which horn does he take? A third member of the group also threw in the objection that which sentence counts as "the Gödel sentence" of a theory depends on how the axioms are written down and numbered rather than on the theory's theorems alone, so that there is no determinate Gödel sentence of a person to be seen; the logician in our group dismissed this as pedantry, and I am not sure he is right to.

Where does my reading go wrong, assuming that it does? I would like an account of what the analogy is actually being asked to do in the book — in particular whether Hofstadter is committed to the anti-mechanist conclusion my friend attributes to him, or whether he uses the incompleteness theorems for a different purpose that my dilemma simply fails to capture. Please also tell me what force, if any, the second incompleteness theorem has against a person who claims to see the truth of a system's undecidable sentence, whether the relevant asymmetry between us and a machine could survive the observation that the recognizer must already be granted the system's consistency, and what I should make of the numbering objection: is it a genuine threat to arguments of this family, or does it just show that we were sloppy about which presentation of a theory we had in mind?
