I run a second-year seminar on social and political philosophy — 24 students, 75 minutes, one room with three long tables and no projector, since the AV in there is broken till after Easter, so please don't build anything that leans on slides. They've had a lecture on judgment aggregation and can parrot the discursive dilemma back at me perfectly well, but none of them have ever watched a group walk into one. I want an activity where they do, and I want them to notice it before anyone supplies the name.

Here's the shell I've got — tell me if it's workable or where you'd change it rather than just fleshing it out. The class acts as the grants committee of a student society. A real applicant (I'll get a colleague to put their name to it, so nobody can say it's hypothetical) has asked for £60 out of the pot the department gives me for student-run events. The society's funding rule, which I'll hand out printed at the start, says a grant is awarded if and only if three things hold: that the application came in inside the deadline, that the proposed event falls within the society's stated educational remit rather than being party-political campaigning, and that the award can be made without pushing the reserve below the floor set in the constitution. Each committee member gets a private role card fixing what they believe about each of those three, and the conclusion follows for them automatically — nobody believes all three and refuses the grant, or refuses one of them and awards it.

The distribution I want on the cards is: 7 accept all three; 6 accept the deadline and the remit but not the reserve; 6 accept the deadline and the reserve but not the remit; 5 accept the remit and the reserve but not the deadline. Before you write anything else, check that against both voting procedures and tell me plainly if it doesn't deliver the result I'm after.

Phase one is the voting. Ballots anonymous, counted by hand, no phones — and I want you to specify the order of questions, because I suspect the ordering is doing work I'm currently not controlling for. Phase two is the bit I care about most: I dont announce which tally counts. Instead the committee has to produce a short written decision, one paragraph giving the reasons, which I have genuinely promised to email to the applicant, and they get ten minutes to agree the wording. My guess is they'll discover that the record they're being asked to sign contradicts itself, and that some of them will start trying to talk each other out of premises they were perfectly happy with five minutes earlier. Arrange the rules and the deadline so that this happens without me saying anything, and give me a fallback for the case where they just shrug and split the difference by voting to award £30 and calling it a day.

What I need back: the contents of the role cards, with reasons on them that make each pattern of belief feel like a person rather than a row in a truth table (the five who accept the remit but not the deadline especially — they need a story); the full mechanics of both votes including how you tally them quickly with 24 people and no technology; the debrief, which should map particular moments from phase two onto the gap between conclusion-wise and premise-wise aggregation, and then onto the harder question of what a group is supposed to do once it has noticed it disagrees with itself, including whether they're allowed to reopen a premise; the ways this predictably goes sideways the first time you run it and what I should say or do in the moment; and an honest note on which feature of the problem the exercise simply can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking.

Two further things. I've noticed the three premises aren't the same kind of thing at all — one is a checkable matter of fact, one is an interpretive question about what the constitution means, one is a forecast about money — and I can't work out whether that difference is a gift for the debrief or a distraction that lets them explain the mess away as different sorts of question being mixed up. Take a view on it. And one of my sharpest students will definitely ask whether anyone is permitted to vote against their own card in order to get the outcome they actually want, and whether that's cheating or just politics; build that into the design somewhere rather than leaving it to come from the floor.

Write the whole thing back as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, not even for the role cards, which you can describe in sentences. British English throughout, please. And be straight with me about the shaky parts: say which piece of the design you're least confident will actually produce the paradox on the day, where you think my reasoning about the second phase is most likely to be wrong, and what would change your mind about the whole thing — what should make me scrap it and do something simpler instead.
