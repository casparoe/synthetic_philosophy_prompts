I chair a quarterly technical panel at a company that designs compute hardware for edge deployments and operates a modest estate of our own. Between six and ten proposals reach the panel each quarter, from internal teams and from vendors, and for each one we make a single decision: reject, fund a four-week engineering feasibility study, or escalate to a physicist for a proper thermodynamic review. The fund is small. A false positive costs about a month of two engineers' time and, when the vendor learns the outcome, some of our credibility; a false negative costs us an idea that might have been worth having. The panel is five software and systems people. None of us has studied statistical mechanics beyond a single undergraduate module decades ago, we cannot solve differential equations, but we are fluent with orders of magnitude and we will do arithmetic on a whiteboard. I have about forty-five minutes per proposal, and I have to leave the room with a written recommendation.

Increasingly, these proposals invoke the thermodynamics of information. I understand the rough outline: that erasing one bit at temperature T costs at least kT ln 2 of energy dissipated as heat; that Maxwell's demon is defeated not by the act of measurement but by the eventual resetting of the demon's memory; that logically reversible computing avoids erasure but accumulates garbage bits that must be uncomputed rather than thrown away; and that the bound is a quasi-static limit which no real device approaches. My recollection is that kT ln 2 at room temperature is of the order of three times ten to the minus twenty-one joules per bit, and that a switching event in a modern transistor dissipates something like a hundred thousand times that. Please correct me wherever I have this wrong or merely vague, and tell me which of my beliefs are load-bearing, because at present I cannot distinguish them from the decorative ones.

What I want is a screening procedure the panel can actually run, not an explanation of the physics. Specifically: the tests to apply in order, each phrased as a question we can put to a proposer; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a reject; where it stops with a fund; and where it hands the proposal to a physicist, together with what the handoff note must contain so that the physicist can pick it up in ten minutes rather than re-doing our work. It should name the two or three numbers we need in a form usable without derivation, say what to do when a proposal contains no numbers at all, and identify the one or two diagnostic questions that expose most of these confusions in a single exchange. It should state which distinctions must survive the simplification for a panel with no physics and which we are entitled to drop — in particular, I want to know whether adiabatic means the same thing to our vendors as it does to a physicist, and if it does not, what to ask instead. Finally, mark explicitly every step that is a judgement call wearing the clothes of a rule; I would rather know where the procedure is soft than have it pretend to be an algorithm.

Please run the finished procedure on three real proposals.

1. "Correlation-aware erase." From one of my own storage engineers, whom I rate highly and whom I would like to support. The observation is that most blocks written in our workloads are highly redundant with data we already retain. The claim is that, because a block's contents are predictable from what we keep, deleting it destroys less than one bit of information per bit of storage, so the thermodynamic cost of freeing it should be correspondingly below kT ln 2 per bit. The proposal is a controller that maintains a predictive model of the data, defers or skips physical erase where the model is confident, and reports a banked thermodynamic saving. If the procedure tells me this is a reject, or tells me it is sound physics attached to an unsound project, say so plainly and explain what we would actually be building if we funded it.

2. "Reversible-adiabatic accelerator." A vendor proposes a fixed-function accelerator for a signal-processing kernel, built from reversible gate logic and charge-recovery clocking, which they describe as adiabatic. Their headline claim is that energy per operation will fall below the Landauer limit for the equivalent irreversible implementation, that a logically reversible datapath generates no heat beyond leakage, and they include a slide placing their energy per operation next to kT ln 2. The design is three times larger in area and runs at a tenth of the clock frequency. Give me the verdict, and then tell me separately whether there is a narrower version of this proposal that the procedure would have funded and this one obscures.

3. "Demon-class controls." A vendor offers a control layer that samples rack temperatures and utilisation at high frequency and uses those measurements to route workloads, alongside a heat-recovery loop. Their material describes the system as a Maxwell's demon that converts information about the estate into free cooling, and claims a twelve per cent reduction in total site energy. I suspect the framing is incoherent and the twelve per cent may nonetheless be real. Tell me whether the procedure should reject a proposal because its stated mechanism is impossible, or evaluate the mechanism and the engineering separately, and if separately, how the two verdicts are combined into one recommendation.

Two further requirements. First, state what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire: the false positives it will let through, and — this worries me more — the false negatives. The largest energy savings we have ever measured came from moving data less and batching better, which has no thermodynamic story whatsoever, and I want to know whether a screen built for these claims would notice such a proposal at all, or whether I need a separate lane for it. Second, do not ask me or my panel to read anything to use it: whatever the procedure depends on must be contained in the procedure itself.
