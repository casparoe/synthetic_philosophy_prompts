You are a philosopher of mind who works on personal identity and on the metaphysics of abstract objects. Before drafting, do some brainstorming of your own about what a merely well-informed student would write, and design the question to defeat exactly that answer.

I am setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the self, and I need one essay question on Dennett's proposal that the self is to be understood on the model of a center of gravity — not a concrete particular in the head, but a posit belonging to a useful explanatory style. The question must not be answerable by competently restating the view, and it must not presuppose the secondary literature.

Here is the crux I want it built around. A center of gravity is an abstraction from a real mass distribution: derivative, but with fixed properties, and one can compute it wrongly. A character in a novel is likewise not a concrete particular, yet nothing underwrites the correctness of claims about her beyond the fiction itself. The seminar has not been given this distinction, so the question should hand it to the students and then put them where they must say which of the two the self is supposed to be on this view — and why the view may not simply answer: both.

To force the issue, use this case, presented as I describe it. G, at thirty, invented a past for himself — a childhood in another country, a brother who died, a career as a surveyor — and has lived in exact conformity to it ever since. The invented narrative is coherent, it organizes his decisions, and it makes him highly predictable to those who know it. He is under no illusion: he knows it is fabricated and maintains it deliberately. And because his actual history was uneventful, the fabricated narrative predicts and explains his conduct better than any accurate one would.

Please deliver three things. First, the question exactly as it will appear on the paper: 25 marks, 45 minutes. Second, a model answer of about 700 words that takes a position rather than surveying them. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a first-class answer must make — in particular, how it handles the two horns: either something determinate underwrites the narrative, in which case the view has quietly reinstated a concrete particular of the kind it denied, or nothing does, in which case G's narrative is not about G in any sense that could make it his self — and that lists the mistakes which should cost marks, among them quarantining G as pathological when he is stipulated not to be deluded, treating the mere usefulness of the narrative as an answer to the question of what makes it about him, and conflating the view with eliminativism about persons.

No references and no reading list; I want the argument, not the scholarship.
