Last autumn I was drawn by lot onto our municipality's standing review panel: fourteen residents, none of us trained in law, statistics, or economics, charged with reaching findings on contested local questions — a disputed school siting, a complaint against a contractor, an appeal of a planning refusal. Our findings carry real weight, and we decide by majority vote. At our second sitting, one member argued that since there are fourteen of us, the majority is "mathematically almost certain" to be right, and proposed that we adopt whatever a bare majority reports as our standing practice and dispense with the long argument sessions. Two others agreed enthusiastically. The chair has asked me, because I once took a course in political philosophy, to open next month's meeting with forty minutes on the Condorcet jury theorem: what it actually says, what it needs in order to say it, and why it does not settle the question my colleague thinks it settles.

I am not asking you to explain the theorem to me. I want a plan for how I should explain it to them, under these constraints: forty minutes, a whiteboard, no slides, and no formulas beyond arithmetic a person will accept while standing at a table. A short table of numbers is welcome; a proof is not.

Please give me the following.

First, the order of presentation, with the dependency reasons stated. In particular, tell me why the audience cannot be handed the headline result before they have understood that its force depends entirely on each member being individually better than a coin flip, and that the same reasoning runs backwards with a vengeance when they are worse. Tell me where the assumption of independence has to be introduced — before or after the numbers — and what breaks if I introduce it later.

Second, the single example that should carry the whole thing. I want you to justify the choice by saying what such an example must contain (a genuinely binary question with a determinate right answer, members who have some skill but not certainty) and what it must be carefully free of (an answer so obvious that the room short-circuits the argument, any setup in which members can see one another's votes before casting, and anything that smuggles in an averaging intuition). Say explicitly whether the familiar urn-and-ball or repeated-coin framing serves or misleads at this level, and if it misleads, propose a replacement drawn from the sort of ordinary factual questions a panel like mine might actually face.

Third, a misconception map. I expect at least these, and I want each one placed at the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, with a note on whether any of them should instead be allowed to surface on its own so that I can answer it in public:

- that the theorem is the same thing as the intuition that many independent guesses at a continuous quantity converge on the truth, with errors cancelling out, when in fact it concerns discrete right-or-wrong questions and nothing cancels;
- that "the majority is more likely correct" licenses each individual member to defer to the majority rather than exercise judgment of their own, and the difficulty that arises if everyone draws that inference, since the competence the theorem presupposes would then have no source;
- that independence means only an absence of collusion or private dealing, rather than something about how members' correctness stands to one another once the truth of the matter is fixed;
- that the theorem describes what happens in a deliberating body, when deliberation is very nearly the thing that destroys its central assumption;
- that it is a claim about fairness, legitimacy, or whose interests get represented.

Fourth, one question I can put to the room at the end that will separate those who have grasped the result from those who have merely adopted a slogan, together with a description of what a confident wrong answer to it would sound like.

Fifth, what I should openly decline to cover at this level, and how to say so honestly. I have in mind the mathematics behind the limit result and what happens to votes that are correlated rather than independent. Tell me which of these to disclaim, in what words, and which to gesture at anyway because leaving it wholly unmentioned would give my colleague's proposal an unfair advantage.

I am not looking for readings, sources, or anything to assign the panel afterwards. I need the architecture of the explanation.
