I spent thirty-odd years in a solid state lab — mostly X-ray diffraction, plus the occasional cryostat crisis at three in the morning — and since retiring in the spring I've been reading philosophy properly for the first time. A mate from my old department reads along with me and we argue about it in the pub most Thursdays, and I've also joined the little book group at the village library, nine of us, mostly retired like me. This month they've asked me to produce some sort of flowchart about infinity for the next meeting, because at the moment nine people say nine different things and nobody can tell whether they're actually disagreeing or just using the same word differently. Which is roughly where I am myself, so I thought I'd ask for help from the other side.

Here's the muddle, in my own words. (1) The hotel with infinitely many rooms, all of them occupied: when the new guest turns up and everyone shuffles up one room, is that a demonstration that such a hotel could exist, or is it a reductio showing that no hotel could ever really be completely full in that sense? Some of us think the second, but I can't say what "really full" means there. (2) Is "there is no end to counting" the same claim as "the counting has all been done"? I keep feeling those are being swapped about mid-argument, and that half the disagreement is just that. (3) Cantor seems to show that some infinities are bigger than others, which looks like the actual infinite arriving whether we like it or not — but then people say that's only true in the abstract and can never be true of the concrete world, and I don't know whether that escape is respectable or whether it's just a way of having your cake. (4) If the universe had no beginning, has an infinite number of events already been completed? My experimentalist's instinct says nothing ever completes, but I can't see what's actually wrong with a past that just goes back for ever. (5) The one that nags me most from the lab side: whatever is going on, we could only ever check finitely many rooms, or measure finitely many digits, so what evidence could anyone have that distinguishes an actually infinite collection from a finite one that is merely, absurdly, unimaginably large — and does that question even make sense, or does it quietly collapse (3) into an epistemology problem? If any two of these five turn out to be the same question wearing different clothes, please tell me which.

What I want, then, is a sorting instrument I could hand round the library, not an essay. Concretely: start from the underlying questions themselves and lay out the space of answer-combinations they permit, so we can see the logical geography before anyone gets given a label — that's the bit I suspect we're all skipping. Then give me eight or nine diagnostic questions, each one a concrete scenario or a plain claim (the hotel, the switch that's flipped ever faster until infinitely many flips have happened inside an hour, a shelf with one book for every whole number, a universe with no first moment, a number so large nobody could ever write it down or count to it), each phrased so a member can answer it without any idea where their answer leads. Nothing of the form "do you believe in actual infinities?", please — that just sorts people by which slogan they like. Then locate the positions in that space: I'd want leaves for a view on which infinite collections exist only potentially, never all at once; for one that says actual infinities are fine in mathematics but could never obtain concretely; for a full-blooded realist about infinite sets; for the view that denies the question is meaningful at all; and for the strict finitist who says only finite collections are legitimate. Tell me whether the more extreme position — the one that won't even grant that a number like ten to the ten to the ten picks out a proper collection, because no human being could ever survey it — deserves a leaf of its own or collapses into strict finitism. Mark any cell of the space where nobody stands, and say briefly why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, rather than pretending it can, and put one plain sentence under each leaf giving the commitment its holder finds most awkward to swallow. And don't bother with references or a reading list — I'll chase anything up myself at the county library, and the group would glaze over instantly.
