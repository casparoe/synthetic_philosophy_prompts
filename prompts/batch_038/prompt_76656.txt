Famine is now declared by a technical test. Under the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, an area counts as being in famine only when three conditions hold at once: at least 20 percent of households face extreme food shortages with limited ability to cope, acute malnutrition in children exceeds 30 percent, and deaths exceed two per 10,000 people per day. The declaration names no agent, imposes no binding obligation, and describes the condition of a population rather than the conduct of anyone — in sharp contrast to the adjacent category of genocide, where the name is a legal one that attaches to perpetrators and carries duties with it.

Reconstruct the theory of famine this machinery presupposes. Does a criterion set with no agent-slot commit us to famine being a threshold-crossing in a natural variable rather than something done to people by someone, and what account of causation and of responsibility does it require — including what it makes of the fact that a death rate of 1.9 per 10,000 per day is not a famine while 2.1 is? Consider also that the intellectual foundation of modern famine analysis cuts against any accusation: the entitlement approach explains how people starve amid adequate aggregate food supply precisely by declining to look for a shortfall, and its diagnostic vocabulary has no place in which to put a perpetrator. Say whether the practice is nonetheless neutral between a natural-disaster and a political-crime ontology, or whether it settles the question without ever arguing it.

Then test your reconstruction against Bengal in 1943, Ireland in 1845–52, and China in 1959–61 — in each of which requisition and denial measures, adherence to market orthodoxy, procurement quotas set against inflated harvest reports, and continued movement of food out of starving regions were entangled with the mortality, while the available diagnostic vocabulary identified no one who was starving anyone. Where the apportionment of cause is historically contested, say so rather than making your argument depend on it.

Finally: what would have to change — the criteria, the evidentiary standard, the identity of the declarer, the thing declared — if famine were classified the way genocide is, as a category attaching to agents rather than a state attaching to populations? And would that change cost the practice the cooperation, access, and data on which any declaration depends, so that the agentless threshold turns out to be a philosophical commitment or a political compromise — and tell me how one could distinguish the two.
