Right, so I run a monthly reading group — a mix of social policy DPhil students, two philosophers, and one economist who genuinely seems to enjoy ruining the vibe — and next month we're doing the gender-equality paradox, i.e. the finding that the more gender-egalitaran a country is on the usual indices, the more gendered its study and occupational choices tend to look. We tried to talk about it once already and spent ninety minutes shouting past each other, and afterwards it became clear that at least four completely different commitments had been collapsed into a single binary of "biology versus socialisation". Some people were making a claim about measurement, some about the origins of preferences, some about what justice requires, and none of them noticed. So I want to build a proper sorting instrument for the session that puts people somewhere by what they actually say about cases and data patterns, not by what they call themselves. My co-organiser Priya keeps insisting we just do a straw poll on the first night, which I think will reproduce exactly the problem.

You'll have to wear two hats at once here: a philosopher of social science who cares about measurement, comparability and reference-group effects, and a political philosopher who cares about when a distribution of jobs counts as unjust. Please think carefully and start with a bit of brainstorming before you draft anything — list the candidate commitments you think could do the sorting work, then tell me which ones you've thrown out as redundant or as just restating another one in different clothes. Then give me the deliverable: a decision tree, or if you think the space is genuinely two-dimensional and a grid sorts people better, say so plainly and give me the grid instead. Eleven or twelve nodes, six to eight leaves, answerable in about twenty minutes by people who have never looked at a regression in their lives. No node may be a question you can only answer correctly if you already know which leaf it leads to — so nothing of the form "do you think differences are innate?".

Here's the seed material, which you should use consistently and tell participants is stipulated so nobody wastes the evening disputing it. Vardland sits in the top decile of a standard national gender-egalitarianism index: individual non-transferable paid parental leave for each parent, 82% female labour-force participation, 46% of its MPs are women — but women are 11% of its engineering graduates and 79% of its nursing graduates. Kessaria sits in the bottom quartile of the same index: no statutory paid leave at all, 38% female labour-force participation, 19% of MPs women — and women are 33% of engineering graduates and 58% of nursing graduates. I'd like the early branches to be about the apparatus rather than the metaphysics: whether the same preference questionnaire means the same thing in both countries (someone rates nursing as "very satisfying" in a place where it's the best-paid work open to her and in a place where it isn't), whether a revealed choice made under real constraint counts as evidence of a preference, whether raw segregation is even the right metric as against a pay- or prestige-weighted one, and whether the two countries' graduate numbers are comparable given who gets to university at all in each. Only then should it move to the substantive stuff — whether aggregate differences in preferences could have a partly non-social origin, whether affluence and a welfare state remove the constraints that would otherwise suppress such differences, whether formal equality of opportunity delivers anything you'd call substantive equality — and it should end on a normative node where the participant has to pick between leaving a stated imbalance alone, subsidising entry into it, and reserving places, since I suspect that's where a lot of the hidden disagreement actually lives.

For leaves, at minimum I want: the scarcity/expressiveness reading of the paradox's own explanation (egalitarian conditions let innate differences finally show up), the structural-persistence or social-role view, the measurement-artefact deflationist who thinks the whole thing dissolves once you fix comparability, the adaptive-preference critic who denies that reported satisfaction can vindicate a distribution, an interactionist or pluralist, and the frame-rejecter who thinks the occupational distribution isn't the right locus of justice at all. Give each leaf a name of your own choosing, one sentence on the tell (what this person says at a party within five minutes), and one sentence on the empirical finding that would most embarass it. Then, and this is the bit people always skip: list the combinations of answers your instrument permits but that nobody actually occupies, and diagnose each one — is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? And flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate. My hunch is that the artefact view and the adaptive-preference view end up observationally equivalent on survey data alone, and I'd rather you told me whether I'm right or talking nonsense than papered over it. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations anywhere in the handout — it's going to be printed and handed out to people who've read nothing on this, and it should work on that basis.
