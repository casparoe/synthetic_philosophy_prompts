I am a philosophy graduate student spending this year on placement with a hospital ethics consultation service, where I attend case conferences as a non-voting observer and write up the arguments for the file. Last week's conference produced a disagreement I have been unable to resolve on my own, and I would like a careful referee's report on it. I will give you the case and the exchange as they unfolded, without indicating where my own sympathies lie, because I genuinely do not know.

The patient, whom the file calls Eleanor, is 81. At 68, three years after her husband died and while she was by everyone's account entirely clear-headed, she executed an advance directive. Its substantive terms refuse artificial nutrition and hydration, and refuse antibiotics for any infection arising once she has "irreversible dementia with no reasonable prospect of my conversing again as myself." Two of its clauses are unusual, and they are what the conference turned on. The first provides that any attempt she makes to revoke the document, and any appearance of contentment with her condition, "is to be treated as a symptom of my illness and not as an expression of my wishes," and that nothing she says after onset is to be taken to alter what she has written. The second names no healthcare proxy and expressly forbids her daughter from being treated as her surrogate, on the stated ground that her daughter's own feelings would distort any reading of the document. Eleanor now has moderately advanced dementia. She is not terminal. She is, by the nursing staff's consistent report, cheerful: she sings, she enjoys music, and on some days she recognizes her daughter and says her name. She has developed a mild aspiration pneumonia and is eating poorly, and the question before the committee was whether to treat with antibiotics and whether to place a feeding tube if the pattern repeats. Shown the directive twice by her daughter, she said on both occasions that she did not want to die and asked to be treated. Her daughter wants the directive honored; the attending wants to treat.

The argument was between Dr. Okonjo, who is in palliative care, and Professor Lindqvist, who sits on the committee as the outside philosopher. It went as follows, and I have numbered the moves.

(1) Okonjo: The first clause is void, and not merely because the law makes directives revocable. Authority over a person's body runs either to her own competent judgment at the time or, when that is gone, to the instructions of the earlier competent self. But no earlier self can foreclose the question whether its instructions are still being applied to the situation it actually contemplated. A directive that forbids its own reconsideration is not an exercise of autonomy; it is the suspension of one.

(2) Lindqvist: That reverses the direction of authority. A precedent directive is made at the moment of greatest information and reflective stability about what makes a life worth living; the later self is, by hypothesis, the one whose judgment is compromised. If a competent person may decide that her demented self's protests do not speak for her, then saying so explicitly in the document adds nothing objectionable — it is the same decision, spelled out. To strike the clause is to prefer the later self's utterances to the earlier self's considered judgment, which is exactly the substitution Dworkin warned against.

(3) Okonjo: The clause is self-undermining in a narrower sense than that. It does not state a substantive refusal; it declares inadmissible a particular kind of evidence — the patient's own sustained, apparently contented testimony about her life. A person may bind her future choices, but she cannot bind what counts as evidence about whether the factual and evaluative premises of those choices still hold. Ulysses asked the sailors to ignore his pleas; he did not instruct them to treat his pleas as carrying no information about whether the plan remained worth pursuing, and he fixed both a duration and a scope. This clause has neither.

(4) Lindqvist: The disanalogy fails, because asking to be ignored is itself an evidential filter — that is precisely what the request means — and nobody thinks Ulysses's instructions self-destruct. More importantly, your appeal to falsified premises misreads the document. Eleanor did not write about suffering. She wrote about a life stripped of the goods she cared about: recognition, conversation, agency. Her present cheerfulness is therefore not evidence against anything she asserted; it is beside the point of what she refused.

(5) Okonjo: Then the clause does no work at all, and is worse than useless, since on that reading nothing Eleanor could now do or say would count against the directive. Any protest is a symptom and any contentment is irrelevant. A refusal that no possible state of the patient could weaken is not a refusal she is still making. It is a decision that has stopped being hers.

(6) Lindqvist: That is an unreasonable test. Many decisions are not invalidated by the decider's later attitudes. A will executed at forty is not undone because the eighty-year-old has grown fond of the niece she disinherited; a promise binds although the promisor's feelings have changed. What confers authority is the conditions under which the decision was made, not its continuing endorsement.

(7) Okonjo: Wills dispose of property and promises concern the expectations of others. This concerns whether a living, protesting, apparently contented person is fed and given antibiotics. The interest in play is her experiential welfare now, and a being who cannot retain a commitment across even an hour can hardly be wronged by its release.

(8) Lindqvist: If "she seems happy today" is permitted to defeat the directive, you license every family to defeat every directive whenever the patient happens to be pleasant — and that is precisely the erosion the whole practice of precedent autonomy exists to prevent.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the eight, tell me whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and why; where a move is a reply, say specifically what it answers and what it leaves standing. Then tell me which single move is the crux — the one on which the rest of the dispute genuinely turns, as distinct from the one that was most heatedly argued. I also want to know what both of them missed: whether the two are even arguing about the same question throughout; whether some distinction they might have drawn — between the authority of a directive and its interpretation, say, or between what Eleanor actually refused and what she would have refused had she foreseen that she would be contented — would dissolve or relocate the disagreement; and whether the daughter's exclusion from surrogate status changes anything about how the document should be read in circumstances its author did not specifically contemplate. Finally, give me your verdict on who is more right overall, and state what you would advise the committee to do about the antibiotics.

Please write all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a considered email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italicized emphasis, and no tables. Take the moves in order and give each a paragraph or two, stating your rating of each within the sentence rather than displaying it in any layout.
