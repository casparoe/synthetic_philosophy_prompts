I'm the methods person on a serial-awakening study in a sleep lab, and I've been asked to write the short "theoretical framing" section of the protocol. I'm stuck, and it's not a writing problem. What the PI wants to end up claiming in the results is stuff like "the subject had a visual experience during N2," on the basis of a verbal report collected maybe forty seconds after we woke them up. My gut says that sentence isn't licensed by the evidence we actually have, but I can't explain why without smuggling in a whole position on whether dreams are experiences at all — and the two positions I know of (Malcolm's, roughly, that the question whether they really had those images while asleep never gets a grip; and Dennett's reply, roughly, that the whole thing might get stamped in at the moment of waking instead of being played out across the night) both look to me like they'd forbid the sentence too, for opposite reasons.

So before I read any more, I want the spec. What would a theory of dreaming have to deliver to count as adequate for *our* purpose — which is deciding what a lab is entitled to assert when all it has is polysomnography plus a report, and nothing else. Give me the requirements list, not a survey of the debate.

For each requirement I want the case or consideration behind it, not just the label. Roughly the shape I'm expecting, though fix the shape if you think it's wrong:

- the cases it has to handle. e.g. a report after an awakening from a stretch with no REM; the lucid-dreamers who signal with pre-agreed eye movements from inside the dream (does a theory have to count that as experience unfolding in real time, or can it explain the signaling away, and what would that cost it?); the subject who says there was nothing but insists the night "felt like" something; reports that visibly mutate as the subject narrates them; the subject who describes a vivid dream while the recording shows they were lying quietly awake; mentation under anesthesia; nonverbal subjects — infants, animals, someone with severe aphasia — where a theory that ties dreaming to reportability has to say what it says and own it.
- the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse. Having an experience versus being disposed to report one. Occurring during the interval versus occurring at the awakening. Imagistic versus merely thought-like mentation. Absence of experience versus an experience of absence. Content versus the sense of having had content. Add whatever others you think I've missed, since this is the part I'm least able to generate myself.
- what it has to stay consistent with, on both sides: the sleep-science findings it can't just talk around (REM/NREM differences in report rate, how unreliable recall is, the fact that how you wake someone changes what they give you) and the general philosophy-of-mind commitments it inherits from whatever account of experience the theorist already has — attention, memory, transparency, and so on.

Then mark each one as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, because I strongly suspect a chunk of what gets demanded in this literature is camp branding. And here's the part I most want: if some subset turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say which subset and argue it out informally. My own guess is that something like first-person authority over dream content, plus reports being reconstructive, plus the requirement that an experience has to be had by someone while it's happening, can't all stand together — if that's right, then our protocol is asking for something impossible and I'd rather find out now than from a reviewer. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work ruling out candidates.

Last thing, and keep it short: how do Malcolm, Dennett, and the plain view that dreams really are experiences and reports are just lossy evidence of them each do against the list. A few lines each, no scorecard table, and definitely no bibliography or reading list — I'm not writing a lit review and I can't chase references anyway.

Please be upfront about how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the one item on your list you'd be least willing to defend in front of the PI.
