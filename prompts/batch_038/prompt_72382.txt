I need help writing a commencement address, and the difficulty is that the address is expected to answer a question I am not permitted to answer with the only kind of evidence that could answer it. In my working life I supervise the evaluation of large machine-learning systems, and most of my time goes to a single dispute: whether to judge such a system by what it produces or by the steps it takes to produce them — outcome supervision against process supervision — and, since process supervision assumes someone who can recognize a good step when they see one, what becomes of the arrangement once the system has outgrown the person doing the recognizing. Six years ago the headmistress of the school I attended read something I had written about this and wrote to me. This June she steps down after twenty-two years, and she has asked me, a graduate of her first full cohort, to give the address at her final graduation. The faculty have separately asked me for something else: they want the address the school has always refused to give, one that says plainly what became of us afterwards.

The school is small — sixty-two pupils in the upper years — and for its entire existence it issued no grades, no ranks, no prizes, and no predictions. Three times a year a pupil received a single page in three parts. The first part, which the pupils called the record, listed what the pupil had actually done, in order, without assessment: the drafts, the abandoned lines of work, the weeks in which nothing was handed in. The second part, the reading, was the teacher's account of why the pupil had done those things; it was marked as conjecture, and it carried a standing right of rebuttal, so that a pupil could write back and oblige the teacher either to revise the reading or to withdraw it in full. The third part was a rule governing the first two, and the school treated it as the heart of the method: anything the teacher could not have witnessed — the private revisions, the reason a piece was destroyed, the hours spent on something never shown — was barred from the record, even in the pupil's favor. Legible process was the only process the school would supervise, and the headmistress held this to be a virtue, since it kept the record honest about the limits of its own reach. Hold onto the consequence, because it is the hinge of my problem: the school also kept no register of its leavers. Destinations were never tabulated, on the explicit ground that tracking outcomes would quietly convert the record into a ranking. So when anyone asks whether the experiment worked, nobody — not the headmistress, not the faculty, not I — possesses any data. The absence is not an oversight but a decision, taken in advance and defended for twenty-two years. I also have my own case, which the address should probably use. In my last year I dropped a project I had worked on for two terms, and my teacher's reading said that I had dropped it 'so as not to be seen failing at it.' I exercised the rebuttal; she withdrew the line; the record for that term shows the work, the withdrawal, and no reason at all. In the eighteen years since I have never been sure the withdrawal was correct, and I have never told anyone at the school that it might not have been.

Write the address: roughly a thousand words, seven or eight minutes, to be read aloud by me at the end of June. The room is four audiences at once — thirty-one leavers of seventeen and eighteen, who have never been given a grade and who know exactly how the world outside grades them; their parents, who paid for the experiment and would like to be told it worked; the faculty, who ran the practice and know better than anyone where it bent; and the headmistress in the front row, who is handing the school over and who has one request, that the address not become a results report, since the school's whole claim has been that a person is not the sum of what they produce. The address cannot cite outcome evidence about any cohort, because none exists, and it cannot substitute confident forecasts about these leavers for evidence, which is the same evasion in a better coat; nor may it pretend that results do not matter, which the leavers would not survive being told and which I would not survive saying. Somewhere in it, the address has to say the hard thing: that under a rule which supervises only what can be witnessed, the pupils who looked best supervised may simply have been the most legible, and that the school may have spent twenty-two years rewarding the appearance of good process. That admission must be survivable in the room — it cannot read as an accusation against anyone present, and it cannot be dissolved into a generality about human nature. The address must also take an actual position on the decision not to keep the register: was the headmistress right that measurement would have corrupted the record, or was the refusal a way of ensuring her claim could never be tested? Come down on one side and argue for it in a register a seventeen-year-old can follow. Finally, the leavers go on Friday and no one writes their record from Monday onward; give them something concrete to do with the removal of the supervising eye, including the particular hazard that they will go on producing legible process for an imagined audience that no longer exists. You may name the connection to my own work at most once, in a single sentence, and it must not explain itself — there is to be no vocabulary of reward models, training signals, or alignment in front of these pupils. Spare me the standard furniture of the genre: no injunction to go forth, no benediction built on the headmistress's name, and no ending assembled from a list of predictions.
