I am in the middle of a novel whose long central section is narrated, chapter by chapter, by the men of a German reserve police battalion in occupied Poland in 1942, and I have been sending those chapters, one man at a time, to a friend who is a historian of the period. She was trained in the aftermath of the Browning–Goldhagen argument and has never quite left it; she reads a character the way she would read a deposition, asking only what does the explaining. Her verdicts on my drafts have been consistent enough that I have begun to suspect a rule, and I would like it named before I finish the book.

The man who, before the first action, is told by his commanding officer that those who do not feel equal to the task may step out, and who does not step out, and who is sick afterwards: possible.

The convinced antisemite who volunteers for the firing line and keeps a tally in a notebook: possible, with the remark that he is supplying his own explanation rather than requiring one.

A man just as convinced of the propaganda who contrives, at real cost to his posting, never to be present at a shooting, and who is never disciplined for it: not a perpetrator at all, and she advises me to cut the chapter.

The man who can fire only when schnapps has been issued beforehand, and who is described by his comrades as incapable of it sober: possible.

The man who refuses once, is not punished, is not shunned, loses nothing whatever, and then joins the next action of his own accord: the falsest thing I have written, because nothing in him accounts for the return.

The man who fires without distress, without conviction, without drink, without attachment to his comrades and without career interest, and who, asked why, says only that it was his duty: she says I have described an absence and mistaken it for a person.

The man who informs on the neighbours hiding a fugitive family but never attends a shooting: perpetrator, emphatically.

The village bully who takes obvious pleasure in the humiliations of the assembly square but fires no more and no less than the man beside him: possible, and the only one of my men, she notes, whose explanation lies inside him rather than around him — though it explains the wrong act.

The chapter I must settle this week is my central one. He steps out when the offer is made at the first action and is assigned to the cordon; two comrades make him ridiculous about it for a month, and he is passed over for a promotion he had been promised. He drinks nothing, holds no opinions he could put into sentences, has no grievance against anyone, and is not cruel — his neighbours remember him as harmless and slightly absurd. Three weeks later, at the second action, he asks to be given a place in the line. He is refused, asks again, and is posted at the far end, where he shoots four people. He is not ill afterwards and not elated; he mentions it to his wife once, in the tone one uses for weather.

Predict her verdict, with your confidence in it. Then do the more useful thing: reconstruct the rule she has been applying. I can see at least two that fit every ruling above — one on which the killing must always be purchased from a man's circumstances and only the cruelty may come from his character, and one on which what she demands is simply that no unbridged gap be left between the man and the act — and my new chapter is exactly the case on which they part company, since he has already shown that refusal was open to him and that he survived it. Tell me which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog to him and why; whether a third rule fits the record better than either (I have wondered whether the operative variable is not cause at all but what a man is able to say afterwards, and to whom); and where the Browning–Goldhagen axis would place him — whether he is an ordinary man, or a case for which that debate has no slot. Finally, name the shortest further case, one drafted man in a paragraph, that I could send her to separate the candidate rules; and if her record genuinely underdetermines him, tell me what I would be asserting about perpetration by writing him as believable, and what by writing him as impossible.
