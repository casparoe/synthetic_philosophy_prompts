I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment threads for the better part of two decades, and in that time I have watched the quantum immortality argument get rehearsed, refuted, and revived on something close to an annual cycle. A thread I was moderating recently produced a version of the dispute that does not look like any of the older rounds to me, and the two participants, having argued themselves into a deadlock, have asked me to find a referee. I would much rather have your adjudication than another summary of the standard debate, so I will state the case and then ask for specific verdicts.

The disputed case is a modification of the familiar thought experiment. Instead of a device that either kills you or leaves you unharmed, suppose one that, on each trigger, yields branches of total Born weight 1/4 in which you continue unimpaired, 1/2 in which you survive with severe and permanent cognitive damage, and 1/4 in which you die. The procedure can be repeated.

The exchange ran as follows.

1. Nils: the observation-selection structure of the original argument is untouched by the change of weights. The only branches containing an observer are branches in which you are alive, so conditional on there being anyone to receive the outcome, you are in one of them. First-personally, the probability of surviving is 1, exactly as before; the weights are not available as evidence about whether anyone will be there to have experiences at all.

2. Kesh: that inference is available only if you have already decided that measure makes no difference to anticipation. Everettians who want to recover the Born rule say your self-locating credence should track branch weight among the branches containing your future continuants. Once you say that, weights are perfectly available: they distribute your anticipation across the surviving branches, and here two-thirds of the surviving weight sits in the damaged branch. So the correct first-person conclusion is not that you will survive but that you will survive, and probably badly. The thought experiment was meant to deliver reassurance; the same logic, applied honestly, delivers the opposite.

3. Nils: you have conceded the point that matters. If you are distributing credence over survival branches by weight, you agree that the death branch is not a candidate for your location, and that is precisely the step the original argument needs. What you have added is a claim about which survival to expect, which is an ordinary prudential calculation about a hazardous device, not an objection to the metaphysics. Quantum immortality never said such experiments were safe; it said that you will not observe your own death. Your conclusion is compatible with mine.

4. Kesh: then follow the arithmetic. After n repetitions the total weight of branches in which you remain unimpaired is (1/4)^n, and the weight of branches in which you are alive at all is (3/4)^n. If, with Parfit, what matters in survival is a relation that admits of degree and whose significance depends partly on how many continuants stand in it, the prudential reason to care about your own future shrinks geometrically as well. Your guarantee is a guarantee of indexical continuation, not of continuation worth caring about, and the reassurance people actually take from the argument is exactly what the weights remove.

5. Nils: now you are trading on an ambiguity. In Everett, weight is a probability, not a quantity of existence; a branch of weight 10^-100 is as real and as full-sized from inside as one of weight 0.99, so there is nothing there to diminish. If you insist that weight is a value to be maximized you owe an argument for it, and it has an absurd consequence: you would have reason to prefer a device that splits you into many high-weight copies over one that keeps you single, which no Parfitian should accept. If measure really were value, moreover, ordinary life would already be a story of dwindling measure and this whole argument would be beside the point.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each of the five, assign a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification that engages the argument as actually made rather than the position it most resembles. Then answer these questions directly: is Nils correct in move 3 that Kesh has conceded the decisive step, or does Kesh's weighting of self-location leave the concession merely apparent; is Kesh correct in move 5 that weight is being used in two incompatible roles, or does Nils's reductio about preferring high-weight fission genuinely dispose of measure-as-value; and is Kesh's move 4 entitled to treat branch weight as a proxy for the number of continuants? Say who is more right overall and about which specific claims. Then tell me what both of them are missing, including anything about the difference between first-person and third-person stakes, about whether a realistic device of this kind could even deliver the tidy branch structure they are assuming, and about whether the argument has any bearing at all on ordinary, non-quantum mortality. Finally, identify the single assumption whose resolution would settle what remains between them, and say whether it is a question about physics, about personal identity, or about something else entirely. No citations, please; I know the usual sources and want your own reasoning.
