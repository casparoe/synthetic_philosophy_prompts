I retired four years ago from experimental nuclear physics, most of that time spent counting events and quoting cross-sections with error bars attached, and I have taken up the philosophy of probability with the seriousness other retirees bring to gardening. The doctrine I have been least able to digest is the Principal Principle — Lewis's requirement, as I understand it, that when I know the objective chance of an outcome and my remaining evidence is of the permitted sort, my degree of belief should simply be that number. The slogan I grasp perfectly. What I cannot do is apply it to my own records. Over the years I kept notes on situations in which a chance was on the table and I had to state a belief. Below are seven entries, with the verdicts I actually reached. I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A source I take to be unbiased tells me a coin is fair; it will be tossed once. Asked beforehand, with nothing else to go on, I say 0.5. No hesitation.

2. The same toss has already occurred and the coin is under a cup. A technician who has reported the contents of such cups to me correctly four times out of five murmurs: heads. I say 0.8 — not 0.5, and not 1.

3. For eleven years I logged the outputs of a randomizing device whose manufacturer certifies it fair at 0.5. It gave heads in nine of ten runs. Asked about the eleventh, I said 0.75. I record, with some embarrassment, that I would at the same moment have said that if I knew the true chance, that number would settle my belief. So I cannot tell you whether 0.75 was my estimate of the outcome or my estimate of the chance.

4. A procedure has a published survival rate of 0.9 for all comers and 0.4 for patients over ninety. The patient is ninety-four. I say 0.4. When a colleague protested that 0.9 was the real figure, I answered that of the two chances on offer, only one of them is about this patient.

5. A theory I accept assigns chance 0.5 to outcome H in an experiment run tomorrow. I also take seriously a rival theory on which H has chance 0.9, and I accept that if H occurs, tomorrow's data will decisively favour that rival. I know all of this today. I say 0.5. I have read that this is the correct disposition and that the urge to shade upward is a confusion, so I follow the advice; but I notice that I am committing myself, in advance, to a state of mind that my present self is confident is wrong.

6. A national hazard assessment publishes 0.3 as the chance of a great earthquake here within fifty years. I happen to know the panel inflates its published figures by roughly a factor of two to keep building codes strict. I say 0.15. Here, unlike in entry 5, I do not hesitate to average over my doubts about the number itself.

7. An apparatus is constructed so that the chance it assigns to H is set equal to whatever credence I announce beforehand. My announcement is self-fulfilling: say 0.7 and the chance becomes 0.7. I announce 0.5 and hold 0.5. I cannot tell you why not 0.7, since both are perfectly self-consistent; I know only that announcing 0.7 would feel like a trick I was playing on myself.

My own tentative rule, offered for you to break: defer to the most specific chance you know; discount the number when your doubts concern its provenance rather than the world; set it aside altogether when your evidence bears on how this particular run turns out.

What I am asking for is the following.

(a) State the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, precisely — I am entirely comfortable with conditional notation, and I would rather see the conditional spelled out than described.

(b) For each clause, identify the entry that forces it. If a clause is doing no work in my seven cases, say so.

(c) Apply the result to three entries I have left unresolved. First: the coin is fair at 0.5 and my evidence about the toss is unchanged, but I have taken a drug that I know will shortly make me feel subjectively certain of heads — what should my credence be now, and what should it be once the drug takes hold? Second: two well-tested theories assign 0.5 and 0.9 to the same outcome and I have no basis whatever for weighting them, no symmetry and no simplicity argument — is a credence required of me, and if so, is it 0.7? Third: an atom with a known half-life has chance 0.5 of decaying within the hour; it belongs to a million identically prepared atoms, and I learn that seventy per cent of them decayed in the first minute, far beyond what the statistics permit — independence says nothing has changed for my atom, and yet I cannot believe the 0.5 survives such a violation.

(d) Tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot keep. If all seven can be kept only by adding clauses, tell me whether those clauses are principled or merely in the business of saving the appearances, and what the simplest principle that overturns exactly one verdict would look like.

(e) Above all, adjudicate the contrast between entries 5 and 6. In both I am less than certain about what the chance is; in one I average and in the other I refuse to. Is there a distinction that makes both defensible, or must one of those two verdicts go — and if one must go, which one should a working scientist surrender?
