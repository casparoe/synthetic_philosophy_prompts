I run the consent side of a research office at an academic medical center. I'm the person who has to say whether a study team's consent process actually holds up before we let them enroll anyone — about 30 reviews a week, and increasingly they aren't about the main study at all. They're about the add-ons: biobank storage, permission for future unspecified research on the samples, sharing with an industry partner, recontact in five years, sequencing that might turn up something we'd be obliged to tell the participant about.

The thing I can't get a handle on is what I've started calling consent by accretion. The main consent is fine — clean, well-written, reviewed. Then the team bolts on four or five secondary asks, and collects them all at once, at minute 90 of a visit, often after something uncomfortable has happened, from someone who is tired and grateful and wants to be a good patient. Everything is disclosed. Nothing is hidden. And I genuinely don't know whether what we end up with is a valid authorization or a piece of paper.

I'd like a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run, not a framework. Concretely:

- The questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each.
- Where the procedure stops at "no" outright, and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is about values rather than facts.
- The escalation triggers that send it to me or to counsel. Right now escalation is gut feel and it's wildly inconsistent between reviewers; I need it named.

It has to survive these four patterns, which are 90% of my workload:

1. Disclosed but buried — the commercial sharing is on page 14 in a paragraph headed "How We Handle Your Data."
2. Timing — the ask lands at the worst moment in the visit. Is that a defect in the consent, or just bad hospitality? I need the procedure to take a position, because my committee splits on this every single time.
3. Bundling — participants seem to think declining the biobank means dropping out of the study. Our own exit interviews suggest roughly one in four believe this, and six rounds of rewording hasn't moved it. At what point does a persistent misunderstanding that disclosure can't cure become the site's problem rather than the participant's?
4. The "sure, whatever you need" participant, and the $75 for a follow-up visit. When is a payment a problem rather than just fair reimbursement for their time? I want a test, not a dollar figure.

Then run it on three cases, one of which I expect will make me unhappy — if your procedure signs off on everything I currently do, it isn't worth my time, and if it kills everything, I can't use it either. Say plainly which one you think has to change and what the change costs the study.

Two more things. It has to be runnable in twenty minutes by someone with a science background and no legal training, and it has to work when the reviewer doesn't have the full protocol in front of them — half the time they have a consent form and an email. And I don't want a tour of the theories of consent. Pick the account of what makes consent valid that you think is actually right, build the procedure on it, and defend that choice briefly; then in one paragraph tell me how the procedure would come out differently if you'd built it on the main rival. That's the only place I want alternatives.

Be committed. Where the literature genuinely doesn't settle something — and the deception cases are like that — make the call, say why, and flag it as a call. Also tell me which steps of your procedure are judgment calls wearing a labcoat, because I'd rather know that going in than discover it in an audit. No bibliography needed, I care about the reasoning.
