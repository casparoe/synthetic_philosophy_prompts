My dad had a stroke in April and I've been semi-useless at work and semi-useless at home ever since. The one thing keeping me sane is a Thursday-night Groundwork group my neighbour runs — Margit, retired after thirty-odd years teaching Kant, about eight of us around her dining table, mostly not philosophers (I do ops analytics at a freight company). The format is weird and I've come to love it: you write a maxim on an index card, first person, present tense, and you have to include every condition you'd honestly admit to; then Margit rules it in or out and says *how* it fails. I've been keeping the cards. Record so far:

- "I'll promise to pay the money back when I know I won't." Out. "The promise eats itself."
- "I'll never do anything for anyone and never ask anyone for anything." Out — but she was insistent this one fails differently from the promise: "a world like that is perfectly thinkable, people would manage, nothing collapses. The problem is you can't will to live in it."
- "I won't practice; I'll let the thing I'm good at go and be comfortable." Out, though reluctantly: "this one only bites if you think you owe it to yourself to make something of your capacities."
- "I'll take the textbook off a pirate site, since there's no chance I'd have bought it." In. Somebody said what-if-everyone and she said that's a question about a different maxim: "your card has conditions on it and the conditions are part of what you're willing. A world where everyone only takes what they'd never have paid for still has publishers."
- "I'll run the red light at 3am when the intersection's empty." Out. "You're counting on everyone else stopping. And you'd want them to keep stopping."
- "I'll keep the $200 he accidently wired me; he'll never notice." Out. "The only thing making your need count more than his is that it's yours."
- "I won't mention the test results until after her sister's funeral, and if she asks I'll say I don't want to talk about it tonight." In. "You've asserted nothing false. You've decided when to speak."

Tuesday it's my turn and I know exactly what's on my card, because I've been living it since April. My company went "results only, no tracked hours" after the reorg — genuinely unlimited time off, nobody counts anything. I've been doing maybe three days a week, my output is fine on paper, my manager's happy, and two people on my team quietly cover the gaps. What I'd write is something like: "When nobody's counting hours and the rules aren't enforced, I'll take as much time as I want, as long as my work still gets done and as long as I can see that enough of my colleagues are still keeping something like the old hours — and if it starts looking like the policy's about to be pulled, I pull back first." I'd also say it isn't an exception carved out for me: I'd tell anyone in my shoes to do the same.

So — what's she going to say? Walk me through which readings of her rule are consistent with all seven cards (I know some of these map onto named distinctions in the literature; use whatever labels you like, I'm not after a bibliography), and then what each of those readings gives for my card. I keep flip-flopping on whether my conditions are like the pirate-textbook conditions, which she let through, or like the empty intersection, which she didn't, because the emptiness depends on other people — and I can't tell which one she'd see. Give me your best guess and how confident you'd be, plus an honest read on how much of the record is just Kant's own examples in costume versus Margit actually having a view of her own. Then give me one more card I could put on the table next month that would settle it between your top two readings, and say why that one rather than some other. Last thing: if she rules my card in, does that actually mean I'm off the hook? I have a feeling the verdict and the ethics come apart here, and I'd rather hear it now than in her dining room.
