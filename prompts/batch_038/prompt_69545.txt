The undergraduates cross-listed into my decision theory seminar have just worked through the St. Petersburg paradox and the bounded-utility reply to it, and the professor has asked me to run their supplementary session: fifty minutes next Thursday on the Pasadena game, and on gambles whose expected value does not merely grow without bound but fails to exist at all. Roughly half of them have never taken a rigorous analysis course, so conditional convergence is not something I may assume -- and neither is the further fact that a conditionally convergent series can be reordered so as to sum to anything whatsoever, which strikes me as the whole reason the case is philosophically interesting rather than a curiosity about infinite sums. What I need from you is a plan for the session, not the content itself. Set out the order in which the components must be introduced and what each one depends on; tell me which single gamble should carry the fifty minutes, and whether that ought to be the Pasadena game as it is standardly stated or a leaner case constructed for this room, together with what that case must contain and what it must leave out; say where the Altadena variant belongs in the sequence, since it is what converts a puzzle about series into a puzzle about ranking options; identify the misconceptions the students will bring or form -- I predict they will hear this as the St. Petersburg paradox with negative payoffs attached, that they will treat the difficulty as computational rather than definitional, and that some will infer that once no value exists anything is permissible -- and pin down the exact point at which each should be headed off; give me one closing question whose answers would reveal whether they have genuinely separated an infinite expectation from an absent one; and tell me what I should announce at the outset that I am not going to cover.
