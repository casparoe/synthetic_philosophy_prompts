I am lecturing in a first-year philosophy of science module and have been given the fifty-minute slot in week seven on al-Ghazālī's attack on causal necessity in the Incoherence of the Philosophers — the seventeenth of his twenty charges, the one directed at the philosophers' claim that the disruption of causal sequences is impossible. The complication is that week six was Hume. The students arrive with a ready-made slot labelled "skeptic about necessary connection" and will file Ghazālī into it before I have said anything. What I want from you is not an exposition of the argument but a plan for how to deliver it: the order the pieces must go in and what each one depends on, which single case should carry the session, and the precise points at which the Hume frame will do damage rather than work — I have in mind especially the difference between an epistemic complaint about our warrant for inferring effects and a claim about who is really doing the acting.

There are components I already know I want included, and I would like you to place them in the sequence and justify the placement: the distinction between observing that two events are conjoined and observing that one brings about the other; the claim that what Ghazālī denies is necessity in the connection rather than the existence or reliability of the sequence itself; the step from that denial to God as the agent in each case, acting in a settled customary way; and the payoff that the regularity we depend on is sustained but not logically forced, which is what simultaneously leaves room for miracles and leaves prediction intact. My suspicion is that the burning-cotton case is a trap as the main carrier: it invites "so God burns the cotton" as a punchline before the students have felt the weight of the step that precedes it, and it makes the doctrine look like a pious restatement rather than a substantive metaphysical claim. Tell me whether you agree, and if you do, propose a replacement and state what that example has to have and has to lack — I want something the class can interrogate rather than merely picture.

Please also give me a misconception map ordered by likelihood in this particular room, which contains both students for whom this material is a matter of religious identity and students who expect medieval theology to be irrelevant to science: that Ghazālī denied causes or science altogether; that he is simply Hume six centuries early; that occasionalism makes prediction, and therefore physics, impossible; that the position destroys human agency and with it responsibility; and the popular claim that he personally ended scientific activity in the Islamic world. For each, say whether I should head it off preemptively at a specific point in the sequence or let it surface and answer it, and how. Then give me the single closing question that would reveal whether they grasped the distinction between denying necessity and denying the connection, rather than only remembering a vivid medieval example. Finally, tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and announce as much to the class — Ash'arite atomism, the doctrine of acquisition, Ibn Rushd's rebuttal, and the Arabic technical vocabulary are my guesses — and close by stating how confident you are in the ordering you propose and which one point in your reading of Ghazālī you are least sure of, flagging anywhere you are relying on an interpretation that specialists actually dispute.
