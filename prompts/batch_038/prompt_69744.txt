ok so I've been down a rabbit hole on the betting argument for probability — the one where if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability rules, someone can sell you a bundle of bets that loses you money no matter how things turn out. I follow the math. But when I try it on people I actually know my gut goes all over the place, and I want to know if there's a real principle under my verdicts or if I have to throw some of them out.

Here's what I think, case by case:

1. My little brother says there's a 70% chance his team wins Saturday and a 65% chance they lose. I offered him two tickets at once, $6 each, each paying $10 depending on which way it goes. He took both, so he's out $2 guaranteed. Obviously irrational, no debate, and the irrationality is in his numbers.

2. Same brother, same numbers, except he just says them at dinner and nobody offers him anything. I say he's exactly as irrational as in case 1. The bets didn't create the problem, they just exposed it.

3. My cousin knows perfectly well it's 50/50 whether it rains tomorrow. She also loves gambling, and she'd happily pay $5.50 for a $10-if-rain ticket and then $5.50 for a $10-if-no-rain ticket if you sold them to her one after the other — guaranteed $1 loss. My gut says she hasn't made any error about the odds whatsoever, you could quiz her and she'd ace it, so she's not irrational in the way my brother is. But every time I try to defend that out loud it turns into "it's fine to walk into a wall as long as you like the feeling," which I definitly don't believe. This is the one I keep flip flopping on.

4. My friend Sam won't commit to anything. Chance the final is curved? "somewhere between like 30 and 70 percent." And he refuses every bet in that range — I offered him a ticket paying $10 if it's curved for $2, which is a gift even at his low end, and he said he doesn't do bets. No book can be made against him because there's no single number to attack. But I don't think he's fine, and "you can't be tricked out of your money" feels like a really strange compliment to give someone.

5. My chem partner hears a rumor the test covers acids and jumps from 0.5 to 0.9. The next day he decides 0.9 was arrogant and talks himself down to 0.1, even though he hasn't learned anything new at all — he just wants to be humble. If I bet with him on both days I can take his money whichever way it goes, and yet he isn't wrong at any single moment: his numbers are fine Monday and fine Tuesday. I'm still confident he's irrational. What bothers me is that I can't point to the moment where he goes wrong.

6. My grandma is, depending on the day, 90% sure there's an afterlife and 90% sure there isn't. If you could bet her on it you'd clean her out. But nobody ever can, not even in principle, because nothing settles the question while she's alive. I think she's irrational anyway. Except by now I've admitted the bets aren't doing any work in my reasoning and I'm not sure why I brought them up in the first place.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? I'd want it precise enough that I could actually apply it — if I handed you someone's numbers plus their betting behavior, it should say irrational or not without me squinting. Tell me which case each part of the principle is there to handle, and tell me what it says about cases I didn't think of. One I'm already worried about: someone whose credences are totally coherent but wildly wrong, like 99% the earth is flat — every version of this I've tried comes out saying that person is fine, and I'm not sure I can live with that.

And be honest about which of my verdicts it has to overrule. If the answer is that I can save 3 and 4 or I can save 1 and 2 but not both, say so and argue for one side. No reading list please, I'm doing this in study hall and can't look anything up, just explain it straight to me.
