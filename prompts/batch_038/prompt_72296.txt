seminar paper due friday on whether the 1945–48 tribunals were law or just force with good stationery, and my supervisor wants my actual position stated on one page so she can attack it properly. here it is, please tell me if it holds together:

1. the legality principle is substantive, not procedural. what makes punishment legitimate is that the person could have known, while acting, that *they personally* were exposed to criminal punishment — not merely that their conduct was condemned somewhere by somebody.
2. so the ex post facto objection to crimes against peace fails anyway, because aggressive war had already been renounced as an instrument of national policy by treaty in 1928. the wrong was legally marked before 1939. what the Allies supplied in 1945 was the missing forum, not the missing law.
3. my distinction, which i think does the real work: call it *substantive* retroactivity (a new norm, or a new personal duty, or a new penalty attached to conduct) versus *jurisdictional* retroactivity (an old norm, a new court, maybe a new procedure). only the first is a defect. setting up a court after the fact is no different in kind from a state finally creating a specialist tribunal to hear old cases that are still inside the limitation period.
4. who the judge is doesn't bear on legitimacy at all. validity of the norms plus fairness of the procedure is the whole question. victor-run or neutral-run, same verdict, same legitimacy.
5. and yet tokyo sits much worse with me than nuremberg, and specifically for a constitutive reason: at nuremberg four sovereigns signed a charter among themselves; at tokyo one commanding general proclaimed the court and then approved the charter for a tribunal sitting on the population of a country he was administering.
6. a tribunal is legitimate only if the norms it applies are actually applied evenly to everyone who violates them.
7. but the absence of any Allied defendant is no objection, because nobody ever claimed to be trying the Allies and selective prosecution is just ordinary prosecutorial discretion. in fact i count the submarine warfare episode as a point in the tribunal's favor: as i recall it declined to weight unrestricted submarine warfare against Dönitz's sentence precisely because the US Navy had been doing the same thing from day one of the Pacific war, which shows it cared about even-handedness.
8. Pal was right that the majority never answered the retroactivity point on crimes against peace — it mostly just treated the legality maxim as a principle of justice rather than as a limit on what sovereigns can do, which isn't an answer. but his conclusion doesn't follow, since the conventional war-crimes counts rested on law that was already there.
9. i accept the crimes-against-humanity count's requirement of a connection to the war as a sound jurisdictional choice, even though it put the pre-1939 persecution of German Jews outside the court's reach, and even though i think that persecution is the central moral reason the trials had to happen at all.

one test i want you to run rather than just audit: suppose that in 1943 an Axis-convened tribunal had tried Allied aircrew for the bombing of cities under the same substantive norms, with defense counsel, evidentiary rules, and some form of review. on (3) jurisdictional retroactivity is harmless, so i think i'm committed to calling that trial legitimate. i don't want to say that. can (3) be patched so it condemns the 1943 tribunal and acquits the 1945 one without special pleading, or does the whole distinction collapse under that case?
