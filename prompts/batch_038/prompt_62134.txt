Thirty-four years doing X-ray crystallography, retired two years back, and now I read philosophy the way I used to read methods papers — slowly and suspiciously. A friend whos writing a trade book on personal autonomy sent me a paragraph from his consent chapter to sanity-check, and I'd be gratful for a second opinion, because I still can't tell a good gloss from confident nonsense.

Here's the paragraph: "Consent is nothing more mysterious than a yes that is free and informed, and it can be withdrawn at any point. Anything that bends your yes counts as coercion, so a factory owner who offers a starving man a wage he can't live on has coerced him, and that agreement is worth nothing. Competence means being rational enough to tell right from wrong, which is why we discount children's consent and treat a heroin addict's consent as void. Deception only voids consent when it concerns the act itself, so keeping quiet about the risks isn't a problem: silence isn't deception. And once a competent adult has said yes without pressure, the act is permitted and there is no further question of whether it was wrong."

For what it's worth I found the second sentence pretty convincing — an offer that leaves a man unable to eat looks like pressure to me — and I have doubts about the last one without being able to say why.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one per sentence of his paragraph, each a sentence or two, saying what holds up, what's a sloppy-but-forgivable shortcut, and what a specialist would call flat wrong. In the fifth point, also say whether his closing sentence survives once the earlier ones are cleaned up. No introduction and no wrap-up.
