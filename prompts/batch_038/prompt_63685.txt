Ok so I've been co-teaching a seminar that puts classical Indian epistemology next to contemporary stuff, and the pramana-counting question eats three weeks of it every single year with nobody budging an inch. The setup: everyone agrees on the same stock cases — the guy who's told a gavaya is an animal like a cow and later picks one out in the forest; fat Devadatta who never eats during the day; knowing there's no pot on the floor because you look and there isn't one. And everyone seems to agree about what the person in the case ends up knowing. What they can't agree on is how many distinct sources of knowledge those episodes reveal. Buddhists say two, perception and inference, and everything else collapses into those. Naiyayikas say four, and handle absence by just treating it as something you perceive (the floor, qualified by the pot's not being there). Mīmāṃsakas and Advaitins say six, and insist postulation isn't inference because there's no invariable connection to run an inference on — only a clash between two things you already know that has to get resolved.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to attack rather than flatter: the impasse isn't about the cases at all. Its that the sides individuate pramanas by different criteria and never notice they're doing it — by causal mechanism, by object-type known, by the distinctive character of the resulting cognition, by what confers warrant. Each criterion sorts the same episodes differently, so every reduction argument and every irreducibility argument is aimed at a different target and they just pass through one another.

Here's a test I've started using on my students; tell me whether it's any good or whether it quietly begs the question. Take a marginal case and ask whether the two descriptions — one pramana versus two — ever diverge in their verdict about whether the person knows, or is entitled, or would be blameless in going on believing. If they never diverge anywhere, the dispute is taxonomic. Candidate marginals: a small child who trusts a reliable speaker while having no inkling why she's reliable; someone who postulates night-eating without ever having observed the day-fasting in a way that could ground a general connection.

So — right, wrong, or too tidy? Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one about the architecture of cognition, two questions wearing one label (a causal-taxonomic one and a normative one about warrant), a hidden empirical question cognitive science could in principle speak to, or an ill-posed question on the grounds that 'how many' already assumes these are the sort of thing that comes in determinate numbers? Argue from what each position actually commits to, not from a tour of the schools. And finish by telling me what would count as progress: a distinction, a reformulation, a case that forces the descriptions to diverge, or a reason to expect none of those.
