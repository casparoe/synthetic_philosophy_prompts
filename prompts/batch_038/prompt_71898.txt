I am a junior, and for my government class I am writing the script for a mock constitutional convention. The country is fictional: Valtoria, which has just emerged from twenty-two years of one-party rule. Two advisers are addressing the drafting assembly, each recommending a different charter.

**Charter One (presidential):** a president elected by nationwide two-round majority vote for a single five-year term, with veto power over legislation and the authority to issue decrees in areas the assembly has delegated to the executive. Removal is possible only by impeachment — a two-thirds vote of the assembly after a finding of a defined legal violation, a process that in practice takes eight or nine months. The assembly cannot be dissolved early, and it also has a five-year term.

**Charter Two (parliamentary):** a ceremonial president chosen by the assembly, and a prime minister and cabinet that remain in office only while they retain the assembly's confidence. A motion of no confidence does not take effect unless it simultaneously names a successor who can command a majority. The prime minister may ask the ceremonial president to dissolve the assembly and call elections within sixty days.

Both advisers are evaluating their charter against the same test case, which I want stated in the first exchange and then worked through for the rest: in the third year, Valtoria's chief executive sells a large tract of public forest to a consortium partly owned by a brother-in-law, at a price that independent auditors call fair. Nothing illegal has occurred. The public is furious, and half the country suspects the auditors were pressured.

Please write this dialogue subject to the following conditions:

1. It should consist of exactly five numbered exchanges. In each exchange, Adviser One speaks and then Adviser Two replies to what was just said. Please name them and keep the names consistent.

2. Neither adviser may use words like gridlock, chaos, stability, or accountability until they have identified the particular institutional feature — the veto point, the fixed term, the succession requirement, the dissolution power — that produces the outcome they have in mind. I want mechanisms, not slogans.

3. Each adviser must concede at least one point that genuinely cuts against their own charter, not a concession that is really an argument in disguise. In particular, I would like them to address the fact that Charter Two's succession requirement, combined with a legislature split among three parties, may make it just as hard to remove a prime minister as impeachment makes it to remove a president; and the fact that Charter One's single nationwide constituency can give the executive a claim to speak for the people that no assembly majority can match.

4. By the fifth exchange they should have moved from abstract principle to a prediction about what would actually happen in Valtoria after the forest sale, given its fresh democratic habits, its three-party legislature, and its tense border. They should end still disagreeing, each committed to a different prediction, rather than reaching a polite compromise.

5. Do not include an introduction, a conclusion, stage directions, or any note about what the drafting assembly decides. Numbered exchanges only. Please keep the whole piece under about 900 words, and write it so that a reader who knows the standard case for and against presidentialism, but has never studied comparative politics formally, can follow every step. Do not cite real constitutions, real court cases, or invented political scientists.
