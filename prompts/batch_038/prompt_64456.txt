I'm putting together a reading-group session on how far consciousness extends across the animal kingdom, and I pulled a passage out of a Huxley lecture from 1874 (the one on whether animals are automata — it seems to have been printed under more than one title). I'm stuck on it and cant get a clean read. Here's the chunk:

> But Descartes went further, and he stated that in the normal and ordinary condition of things these changes in the form of muscle in the living body only occur under certain conditions; and the essential condition of the change was, says Descartes, the motion of the matter contained within the nerves, which go from the central apparatus to the muscle. Descartes gave this moving material a particular name [...] The first proposition that you find definitely and clearly stated by Descartes is the view that the brain is the organ of sensation, of thought, and of emotion—using the word "organ" in this sense, that certain changes which take place in the matter of the brain are the essential antecedents of those states of consciousness which we term sensation, thought, and emotion. [...] In the second place, Descartes lays down the proposition that all the movements of the animal bodies are effected by the change of form of a certain part of the matter of their bodies, to which he applies the general term of muscle. That is a proposition which is now placed beyond all doubt whatever. [...] Nowadays we should not say that the animal spirits existed, but we should say that a molecular change takes place in the nerve, and that that molecular change is propagated at a certain velocity which has been measured from the central apparatus to the muscle.

Three things I can't work out. First, what is Huxley asserting here versus merely reporting that Descartes asserted? He grades the items differently — the muscle proposition is "now placed beyond all doubt whatever," the nerve-motion one gets a shrug and a modern redescription — and I can't tell whether the grading is meant to carry over to the brain proposition, which is the one that actually concerns consciousness.

Second, and this is the one that matters for the session: does "essential antecedents" do any work about whether consciousness causes anything? My instinct is that it only makes brain change necessary for the conscious state and leaves wide open whether the state then feeds back into behaviour — in which case this passage is nowhere near the machine-with-a-spectator-inside picture people usually pin on Huxley, and it licenses no conclusion at all about which animals are conscious. Note also that he's careful to talk about "one of the higher animals" having a brain-plus-cord apparatus; is that just anatomy, or is it quietly doing the work of sorting creatures into conscious and not?

Third, the animal spirits redescription. When he says we wouldn't now say the animal spirits existed but would say a molecular change is propagated along the nerve, is he claiming the old talk and the new talk pick out the same thing under different names, or that the old theory was discarded and replaced? It matters because whether you can translate the machine vocabulary into the physiological vocabulary without loss looks like exactly the hinge the whole automaton question turns on.
