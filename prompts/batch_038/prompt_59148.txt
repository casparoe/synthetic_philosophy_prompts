I'm a PhD student sitting on the working group that's reviewing my university's "institutional statement" policy, and I keep failing to say clearly what bugs me about it, so maybe you can help me pin down what the thing assumes.

The policy says the university as such takes no position on political or moral controversies unless the matter bears directly on its own core activity — so no statements about foreign wars, court rulings, elections. The preamble contains the line the drafters seem proudest of: the university is "a venue, not a voice". Exceptions do get made for things reframed as conditions of inquiry: it spoke out about a law regulating campus protest, and about a funding cut to the humanities, on the grounds that those touch whether the work is possible at all.

Here's the bit I can't resolve. The same university gives money to political student societies, hosts partisan speakers on purpose, and lets departments issue statements whenever they like — only the centre is gagged. Meanwhile it makes every first-year sign a community covenant committing them to named values and to bystander intervention, runs residential colleges with compulsory hall dinners, and it's mission statement says the place exists to form people of character. Last year it closed for a day and flew flags at half mast after one atrocity, then declined to do the same after another two months later, and the vice-chancellor's explanation was that the first one "concerned the university directly", which nobody in the room could get to the bottom of.

So: what picture of a university does that venue-not-a-voice line presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed rather than the policy defended or attacked — what it commits us to about what kind of thing the institution is, about whether a corporate body can hold views at all, about whether members arrive with convictions already formed or get them from the place, and about what staying silent costs and who pays. Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, because I suspect a purely prudential story about donors and litigation would licence the whole thing with no moral theory in sight at all. Then tell me which rival understandings of a university it quietly forecloses, and whether the covenant-plus-gag combination is actually inconsistent or can be squared by keeping the two at different levels. And finally, if you swapped in the view it excludes — a university as a community with a shared conception of the good, entitled to speak in its own name — what would concretely have to change about the half-mast decisions, the funded societies, and who gets to authorise a statement?

British English please, and no bibliography or reading list, I've got stacks of those; I want the reconstruction.
