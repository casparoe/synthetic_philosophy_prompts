For thirty-eight years I earned my living at a national metrology institute, where most of my time went not into taking measurements but into establishing what would have to be shown before a measurement could be said to have been justified at all: which assumptions the operator was licensed to make, which errors the certificate actually covered, and over what range the instrument's stated accuracy held. I retired three years ago, have read philosophy with more appetite than prudence, and have lately become absorbed by the two-envelope problem. What I find unsatisfying is not that many resolutions have been proposed. It is that they are argued over as though the only question were whether a given one is correct, when the question I want answered first is what would have to be true of an account for it to count as a resolution at all. I should like that standard written down before I read any further candidates; I intend to bring the candidates to a small reading group I have started and judge them myself.

The deliverable is a numbered list of desiderata for an adequate resolution of the two-envelope paradox, of roughly 1,200 words. Please observe the following in constructing it.

1. For each desideratum, give the consideration or the concrete case that motivates it, and state whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a requirement imposed only by a particular camp — Bayesian, frequentist, decision-theoretic, or otherwise — naming the camp and explaining why it insists.

2. At least one desideratum should concern the localization of the error. An account that tells me the conclusion is false without identifying which step of the familiar calculation fails — the assignment of one half to each possibility, the treatment of the amount in my envelope as a fixed number while simultaneously treating it as a random variable, the appeal to expected value itself, or something else — ought in my view to be graded incomplete. Argue for or against including this requirement; if you include it, say what counts as an adequate localization and what does not.

3. At least one should concern what the agent may be assumed to know. In my old work the difference between a sound and a spurious explanation of an anomalous reading almost always turned on which facts the operator was entitled to use. Here: a resolution that depends on the agent's knowing how the envelopes were filled must say what he is to do when he does not know, and must not silently grant him information the statement of the problem withholds.

4. At least one should concern the passage from diagnosis to decision rule. Showing that an argument is invalid is not the same as telling me whether to switch. I should like the list to require an account to yield an operative policy, and to specify what form that policy must take and against what it is to be evaluated.

5. State which variants the list must handle, with a line each on what goes wrong if a candidate mishandles it. I have in mind at least: the version in which no probabilities are mentioned and only the two possible gains and losses are compared; the version in which the second envelope is filled by a coin toss after the first has been fixed; the version in which the two amounts are independent draws, so that a randomized threshold rule can select the larger better than even; the two-player version in which each of two men reasons that the exchange favours him; the version in which the stuffer's funds are bounded, as all real funds are; and the version in which the agent has already opened his envelope and seen a definite sum.

6. Most important: if some subset of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so and sketch the obstruction. I have noticed that the standard history of this problem is a resolution proposed and then defeated by a slight change in the setup, and I suspect this is not bad luck but evidence that the desiderata conflict. If they do, that conflict is the result I most want, and it should be stated as a finding rather than left implicit in the ordering of the list.

7. Finally, name the single requirement that you think eliminates the largest number of otherwise plausible candidates, and explain why it has that effect rather than some other.

For a closing section you may assess three to five families of resolution against the non-negotiable subset, one or two lines each, in a table if you prefer. Describe each family by what it claims rather than by who claims it: I do not want names, dates, or a reading list, and I would much rather you left a gap than filled one by guesswork.
