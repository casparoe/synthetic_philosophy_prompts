I am a novelist, and I need a disambiguation rather than an argument.

My third book is narrated in the first person by a woman who has come out of eleven days in an intensive care unit into a flat, watchful state that her doctors decline to name. Her narration develops a register I have not seen handled honestly in fiction: she reports her perceptions accurately and at length, and in the same breath denies that there was anything present to be reported. The passage at issue has her at the kitchen window at four in the afternoon, able to say where the light falls, to correct herself about its hue, to notice that it has thinned since Tuesday — and then to tell the reader that it was a grey with no grey in it. I have been calling sentences of this kind "hollow avowals" in my notes, and the book is built out of them.

I paid a philosophy doctoral student to check the manuscript for exactly this kind of thing. She came back with a single sentence — "consciousness is an illusion" — and used it to recommend that I cut the passage and rewrite the chapter so that the narrator describes only her own judgments, expectations, and dispositions. Her reasoning, as I reconstructed it from her email, was that illusionism (she gestured at Keith Frankish and at Dennett's treatment of consciousness as an evolved user-illusion) removes the phenomenal character from the picture entirely, so a character cannot coherently avow experience while denying that her experience has any feel to it: either she is had by something or she is not, and on this view nothing is being had.

My strong suspicion is that what she handed me is not a thesis but a piece of shorthand covering several quite different theses, and that whether my sentence survives depends entirely on which one she had in mind. I do not want to cut the best thing in the book on the strength of an ambiguity, and I do not want to keep it if it is simply incoherent — a character cannot be deep by being confused about her own confusion.

So: lay out for me the distinct precise claims that "consciousness is an illusion" can be taken to be making, from the ones that are merely true by stipulation through the ones that are substantive and defensible to the ones that are contentious or self-undermining. For each reading, tell me which camp of people who repeat the slogan actually means it, and — what matters most to me — what that reading implies for a hollow avowal: whether under it my narrator's sentence is incoherent, coherent but revisionist about the ordinary concept of experience, or a report the theory simply does not speak to.

I also want you to engage with the objection I have ready and she did not answer. An illusion, one would think, must appear to somebody as something; if the seeming itself has no phenomenal character, in what sense is there an illusion rather than a mere computational misregistration? Tell me which readings can absorb that objection and which cannot, and whether any reading leaves a residue of phenomenality — some leftover "what it is like" for the misrepresentation to be like something — that my narrator could plausibly be pointing at when she says the grey was not there.

Please write the reasoning as prose, and then give me a machine-readable summary at the end as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "what_my_reader_needed": one sentence describing the work the slogan had to do in order to justify the cut
- "readings": an array, ordered from least to most radical, in which each element is an object with:
  - "name": a short label for the reading
  - "claim": the thesis stated precisely, in one or two sentences, with the terms pinned down (what is being called illusory, illusory to whom, at what level of description)
  - "status": one of "platitude", "substantive_and_defensible", "substantive_and_contested", "false_or_self_undermining"
  - "meant_by": who typically intends this reading when they say the sentence
  - "hollow_avowal_under_it": one of "incoherent", "coherent_but_revisionist", "untouched_by_the_theory"
  - "absorbs_the_seeming_objection": boolean
  - "phenomenal_residue": what, if anything, is left that feels like something on this reading; use null if nothing is left
  - "notes": anything else bearing on whether I keep the passage
- "the_reading_she_probably_had": the label from the array, plus one sentence on why
- "verdict": one sentence stating whether any reading actually licenses the cut she recommended
- "instruction_to_the_manuscript": one of "cut", "keep", "keep_with_revision", followed by a single sentence saying what the revision should do if that option was chosen
