Ok, bit of an odd request. I'm an engineer, no philosophy background at all, and I'm building the membership/governance layer for a platform co-op — roughly 8k members spread over 30 countries, plus contractors, plus a bunch of people who use the service but aren't members. At some point we have to decide, in code, who actually gets a vote when the co-op changes its own rules. Every time I try to write that rule I hit the same wall: whatever criterion I pick (affected by the decision? subject to the rules? resident? paying member?) seems to need someone whos already decided who counts, and I can't find a non-circular way to bootstrap it.

Turns out this is apparently a whole debate in political theory. So: I'd like you to act as a political theorist who's spent their career on democratic inclusion and institutional design, and build me a decision tree / taxonomy of the positions on this problem. Not a survey of the literature and definitely no reading list or citations — I want something I could paste into a design doc as a flowchart and actually use to sort my team's intuitions.

How I'd like you to work: think carefully and start with some brainstorming before you produce the tree. List maybe 12–15 candidate splitting questions, say for each one which positions it actually seperates and which it doesn't, then prune down to the ones that earn their place. Only then write the tree.

Constraints on the tree itself:
- every branch node is one concrete yes/no question about a case, phrased so someone could answer it without knowing which position their answer leads toward. Use real cases, not abstractions: the tourist arrested under a law they had no say in, the contractor in another country who works entirely under our rules but never sets foot here, the 12-year-old, the long-term resident who never naturalized, somebody affected only fifty years from now, the member who wants to leave and take their stake with them.
- 8 to 12 nodes, 5 to 8 leaves.
- each leaf gets the name of the position if it has one (all-affected, all-subjected / all-coerced, pre-political membership or self-determination views, territorial sovereignty, the cosmopolitan line that no bounded demos can be legitimate, anything else that belongs), plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. If a combination has no defenders, label it "unnamed" and say why nobody's there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
- build it out of the logical space first (the questions and the answer-combinations they permit), then locate the actual positions in that space. I want the empty cells as much as the occupied ones.
- flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot tell apart.

Two things I specifically want in there, because they're where my team keeps tripping:
1. The circularity problem — that a people can't vote on who the people are without already assuming an answer — should show up as an actual node in the tree, not as a caveat at the bottom.
2. "Just let people exit instead of voting" should be evaluated as a candidate leaf. Tell me straight whether that's a real answer to the boundary problem or a category error, and if it's a genuine position somewhere, where it sits relative to the others.

Also, at the end: tell me how confident you are in this mapping — which leaf placements you'd put money on, and the single claim in your answer you're least sure about, the one most likely to be wrong. I have no way to check any of this, so I'd much rather know where the soft spots are than get a confident-sounding tree that quietly mislabels things.
