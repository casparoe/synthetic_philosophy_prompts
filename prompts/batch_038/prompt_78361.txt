I'm retooling the causation seminar I run for grad students, and the week on omission is where it always falls apart — partly my fault, because I keep handing them the same two or three stock cases everyone's seen since undergrad and then asking them to sort the theories, and they just end up defending whatever they walked in believing. So this year I want a nastier battery, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on it before I commit. Here's what I've sketched so far: (1) The pool — a babysitter is absorbed in her phone, doesn't notice the toddler wander out to the deep end, toddler drowns; the baseline case where almost everybody wants to say the omission caused the death. (2) The crowd — same drowning, except four neighbors also saw nothing and did nothing, one of whom was on a ladder call and couldn't have gotten there in time, plus (why not) a stranger in another hemisphere who was equally not rescuing the child. Now we have a pile of candidates with the same counterfactual profile and only one of them feels like a cause. (3) The greenhouse — a tech fails to top off the coolant and a sample overheats, but the line was scheduled to be drained by a maintenance crew at three anyway, and that crew was going to be stood down at 2:55 by a phone call that also never got made. (4) The rosebush — I don't water my rosebush all August and it dies, except there was a drought, and even a conscientious waterer would have been drawing on a well that went dry in July; so whether my omission matters seems to turn on facts about a well I'd have sworn were irrelevant, and if you swap the drought out, the very same inaction is suddenly causal. Also relevant here: the Queen of England never waters my rosebush either, and her counterfactual dependence looks identical to mine, but nobody's tempted to list her. (5) The two gatekeepers — one is drugged and physically can't move, the other is perfectly able and just stays in his chair; same prisoner escapes through the same gate, and the causal verdicts come out wildly different, which makes me suspect "could have done otherwise" is doing non-causal work. (6) The forgetful gardener — my not watering seems to have been caused by my forgetting, and my forgetting by a bad night's sleep, so we apparently need causation running *into* an absence and between two absences, not just out of them.

Here's the ask. Take the main families of accounts — plain counterfactual dependence views and their probabilistic cousins, conserved-quantity or causal-process views, interventionist views, contrastivist views, the camp that says absences just aren't causes and we should reach for some weaker relation instead, plus any family you think I've left out (add one or two if there's one that would actually bite on this material) — and rank them by how they do across *this battery*, not on causation in general. Give me a tier list and a score out of 10 for each, and for each one name the single case that hurts it most and say in a sentence or two why. Where a theory saves a case by appealing to a pragmatic or contextual filter, tell me whether that filter is principled or whether it looks like it's been reverse-engineered from the verdict we wanted — I want to be able to press students on exactly that. Then tell me which of my six you'd cut as not diagnostic, since a case every theory handles the same way is just padding and I have three hours to fill, not four. And put a number on how much of your ranking you'd expect to survive contact with people who work on this full time — say, the fraction of your orderings that a room of specialists would agree with, and which single ranking decision is most likely to be the one they'd overturn.

One thing that matters for how I use this in class: please keep a hard line between what's actually settled here and what's still genuinely live. My impression is that there are real disagreements among people who know this material cold — including about whether there's any principled difference at all between an omission and a mere non-event, and about how much of causal selection is metaphysics versus bookkeeping — and I don't want a crisp-looking writeup that quietly promotes one camp's conclusion to the consensus. So flag which of your judgments are you extrapolating from a theory's internal commitments rather than reporting something people have actually argued for, and if your own ranking rests on a contested premise (say, that absences have to be causes for the pool case to come out right), tell me that's the premise it rests on.
