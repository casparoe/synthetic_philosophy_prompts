I've got an argument that I think is bad news for Dutch book arguments, and I'd like someone to tell me it's wrong, because if it isn't I have to give up something I care about.

(1) If a bookie who knows your betting prices can assemble a package of bets you'd accept that costs you money no matter how the world turns out, then your credences are irrational — that's the entire force of the Dutch book argument.

(2) That's what makes probabilism compelling: for each of the probability axioms, violating it means such a package exists, and respecting it means no such package exists.

(3) "Credence 1 in every logical truth" and "if p entails q, your credence in q is at least your credence in p" are just consequences of the probability axioms, not extra assumptions bolted on.

(4) So by (2), somebody who puts 0.9 on a theorem of arithmetic is Dutch bookable: they'll happily pay a few cents now for a dollar if the theorem turns out false, and it never does.

(5) So coherence demands logical omniscience — your credences have to be defined over a set of propositions closed under entailment, with 1 on every logical truth.

(6) No finite agent can do that. Working out whether something is a theorem of arithmetic isn't decidable in general, and the collection of things you'd have to be certain of is infinite.

(7) So no finite agent's credences are rational, and "irrational" stops meaning anything once it applies to everybody, me included.

(8) Therefore (1) is false: being exposed to a guaranteed loss isn't a mark of irrationality after all, and probabilism has lost its best argument.

I want to keep (1). I've never seen a better case for the probability axioms than the one that says an incoherent agent pays good money for literally nothing, and (7) bothers me too — I don't want a standard of rational belief that nobody can meet. I can't find a step that's simply false, but I also can't bring myself to accept (8), so either there's a break I'm not seeing or one of these is less painful to abandon than I think.

Where does it go wrong, if it does? Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no wrapping up at the end — just the diagnosis. Whichever steps you take on, say why the step looked innocent when I wrote it down and what I actually pay for dropping it. British English, please.
