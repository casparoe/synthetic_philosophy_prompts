I am presenting a paper at a workshop on the temporal structure of perception, and I want to be ready for the question period. My thesis is a qualified Stalinesque one. In the color-phi demonstrations — a red spot and then a green spot flashed at nearby locations, reported as a single spot moving and changing color partway along its path — the mid-flight change is genuinely experienced, not a later rewriting of the record; the way to absorb the timing puzzle is to hold that the content of experience is not fixed until the second spot has been processed, so that experience lags its stimulus and nothing need be retroactively revised. My positive argument leans on a pilot study of my own: when subjects are pushed to keypress the color of the second spot as fast as possible, the responses still show interference of the kind expected from a single changing object rather than two discrete flashes, at latencies I claim are too short for an Orwellian rewrite to have finished.

The room is almost entirely experimental psychologists of vision and attention. Several run temporal-order-judgment and apparent-motion paradigms and care about psychometric functions rather than metaphysics; one is a computational modeler who thinks latency differences and motion extrapolation already explain every postdiction effect on the table; one is a global workspace theorist who will suspect I have relabeled broadcast; and one is a committed Dennettian for whom any determinate moment of availability is a Cartesian theater in disguise.

Give me the seven or eight objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them. For each: a reply I can deliver in under a minute, a note on where that reply is softest, and what I should concede in its first sentence. Close with a line beginning "Verdict:" naming the single objection I am hoping nobody asks.
