I spent the summer co-teaching a short interdisciplinary workshop on institutional fragility — half the room from a law school, half from a political science department — and in the final session I put up a chain of reasoning I had drafted after our morning on Thucydides and Xenophon, mostly to give the constitutionalists something to attack. It worked too well. The historians said my steps were sloppy; the political theorists said the conclusion was obvious and my colleagues on the right of the room were in denial. Nobody would say precisely where the thing breaks, and I have been unable to settle it myself. Here it is, numbered so you can point at the joint.

(1) If an institution produces the same kind of failure repeatedly, across different crises, different officeholders, and different factions, the failure belongs to the institution rather than to the people occupying it. (2) Inside roughly a quarter of a century, the Athenian assembly voted to put every adult male of a revolted allied city to death and enslave the women and children, then reversed that vote the following day and got a second trireme to Lesbos only just in time; committed the largest force it ever fielded to Sicily on the strength of assurances about a city's treasury that turned out to be staged for the inspectors, and then recalled its ablest commander mid-campaign to answer a religious charge, driving him to defect; tried six of its own victorious generals collectively in a single vote for failing to recover shipwrecked men when a storm had made recovery impossible, and executed them, over the objection of the one member of the presiding committee who refused to put the motion — the same man who would later tell a jury he had risked prison and death for that refusal; and, a few years after the surrender, condemned an elderly citizen on a capital charge brought largely to settle accounts over the war. (3) Each of these decisions was afterwards regretted, and after the trial of the generals the city turned on the men who had engineered the illegality and prosecuted them in their turn. (4) So the Athenians came to see the trouble as procedural rather than personal, and the procedures they built in response — the distinction between standing law and assembly decree, the indictment against an illegal proposal, the panels convened to legislate, the amnesty of the restored democracy with its ban on recalling grievances — were aimed at the decision procedure itself. (5) Any body that is simultaneously the source of law, the judge of its own acts, and free to reverse itself at the next meeting, with nothing outside it competent to say that a considered judgment and a passing mood are different things, will show this pattern. (6) A modern legislature and its electorate are the same kind of thing: sovereign, self-validating, able to undo last session's work this session, and answerable to no authority that can deny that its decision was a decision. (7) Therefore modern democracies carry the same structural liability, and the two and a half centuries of comparative stability they have accumulated is a run of luck rather than a dividend of design. (8) It follows that the machinery supposed to buy that stability — bicameralism, fixed terms, entrenched rights, judicial review — does no real work, and at most retards a reversal that any sufficiently intense mood will eventually complete.

I cannot accept (8), and (7) sits badly with the comparative record, yet I have no clean diagnosis: every step looked like a plain description of something, and I suspect I am losing the argument in one transition rather than in any premise. So tell me where it goes wrong. Name the step or steps you reject, explain why each of them looked unobjectionable on first pass — I want the mechanism of the illusion, not just a label — and price the alternatives: if I give up (1), what am I giving up about institutional explanation generally, and does the historical evidence in (2) still license anything; if I give up (5) or (6), do I owe an account of why Athens's own review machinery, which (4) concedes existed, failed at Arginusae while ours supposedly does not; and if the chain is in fact valid, which premise is cheapest to abandon. I should say where my sympathies lie, without pretending it settles anything: I would rather keep (1) and (3) than any later step, and I have a vague sense that (6) is where the wheels come off, though I cannot explain why, since (6) reads like nothing more controversial than a definition of sovereignty. Please write in American English.
