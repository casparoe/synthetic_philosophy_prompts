I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and our editor has commissioned a special issue on counterfactual history — an unfashionable genre, and I have been handed the assignment everyone assumed would be its weakest link: the old question of whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. In reporting it I have come to think the two camps do not so much disagree as talk past each other. The structuralists I interviewed insist that Weimar's collapse was overdetermined — defeat, the peace settlement, hyperinflation, the agrarian depression, an officer corps and judiciary that had never been democratized — and that some far-right government would have taken power with or without one Austrian corporal. The intentionalists reply that what took power would then have been a different thing: an authoritarian nationalist regime of a recognizably European type rather than a movement party organized around a racial-biological program, and that this difference was causally load-bearing rather than merely descriptive. Both sides, notably, treat themselves as answering the same question, and neither will say what would settle it.

One source offered a concept I have not seen in print and which I think does real work. She is an economic historian, and she called it the bench depth of an office: the number of people positioned, credentialed, and disposed to occupy a given role and pursue roughly the same program if the actual occupant were removed. Bench depth, she said, is very high for a modern central-bank governorship and close to zero for a movement entrepreneur working in an institutional vacuum, where there is no succession procedure, no credential to inherit the role by, and no machinery to constrain what the occupant is permitted to want. On her account the great-man-versus-structure quarrel is really a quarrel about which offices have shallow benches, and the structuralist owes a specification she called the understudy clause: how long she is prepared to wait for a substitute, and what would count as a good-enough one. Her own view, which I have come to share, is that the Hitler question is not one question but at least three, separated by which explanandum is held fixed — that some far-right authoritarian government takes power in Germany in the 1930s; that the government has the ideological content National Socialism had; and that it then undertakes the particular sequence of decisions it did undertake — and that the structuralist wins the first outright while the great-man theorist wins the second and third decisively. Hence her sharper claim, and the one I want you to defend: that this is not a peace treaty but a verdict, because the explanandum the structuralist wins is the one nobody actually means by the question, while the two that carry the historical and moral weight go the other way, leaving structuralism true about what it can handle and idle about what matters.

Please make the strongest argument you can for that position, at around a thousand words, with the premises laid out and the work each is doing identified. Then meet three objections I can already hear. The first is the conservation objection: structural pressure, it is said, fixes outcomes irrespective of the ideology nominally in charge, so a non-Nazi nationalist regime facing the same pressures arrives in roughly the same place, which would collapse the second and third questions back into the first and leave your thesis with nothing to distribute. The second is the unfalsifiability objection: that bench depth is a post-hoc redescription of whatever happened — deep benches when a substitute appears, shallow when he does not — and that the understudy clause is an ad hoc rescue rather than a commitment. The third is mine, and I think it is the hardest: that any counterfactual in which a person is absent requires a similarity metric over worlds, and that the metric is chosen by whichever of the three explananda you have already decided to hold fixed, so the disaggregation may be doing its work by fiat, deciding the debate in the setup rather than in the argument. Alongside the replies, tell me what evidence could actually bear on the bench depth of an office — what an historian or a social scientist would look for, in Germany in the 1920s or anywhere else — and be candid about what my position concedes to the structuralist, since this piece will be read by historians who will assume I am smuggling in hero worship through the back door. I am not after references or a reading list; I want the reasoning in your own words, precise enough that I can paraphrase it for a general audience without flattening it.
