So I've been reading around the philosophy of pain stuff and asymbolia keeps getting wheeled out as the killer datum. Rough story as I understand it: patients with lesions around the insula will tell you they feel the pain fine — right location, right intensity, they'll even use the word "pain" — but it doesnt bother them, they don't pull away, sometimes they'll joke about it while you're doing something genuinely noxious to them. And then people say: see, that shows the unpleasantness comes apart from the pain sensation iteself, so badness can't be built into how pain feels; it has to be a matter of the subject's attitude toward the feeling — wanting it to stop, or the experience carrying some kind of "stop this!" content.

Can you unpack what's actually at stake? Two things I want. First, why would anyone think asymbolia settles anything about the badness of pain, and what do the competing pictures of unpleasantness look like that it's supposed to adjudicate between? Second — the part I care about more — the obvious pushback, which is that the patient's own testimony can't do the work being asked of it: if the affective machinery is what's damaged, maybe what's left isn't a pain experience at all but a stripped-down tactile one, and the person is just borrowing our word "pain" to describe it. On that reading asymbolia isn't a discovery about pain, it's a change of subject. So what would it even take to settle whether these people are having pains? Give me your own verdict on which side is right and argue for it — I'd much rather you commit and defend something than hedge and hand me a balanced menu of readings.
