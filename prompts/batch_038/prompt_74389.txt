Ok so I've been going in circles on a dilemma I built for a functions seminar. It's supposed to land on "nothing has a function," which I obviously don't believe, but I cant find the joint where it snaps, and my tutor keeps saying I'm just being cavalier with the word "selection."

Setup so the steps mean something. Two accounts of function. Etiological: F-ing is a function of x only if x's F-ing is (part of) why x is there — some history of retention or reproduction did the keeping. Causal-role: F-ing is a function of x in containing system s iff x's F-ing contributes to some capacity of s.

The dilemma:

(1) Either the etiological account is right about functions or the causal-role account is.

(2) Etiological horn: our group built a synthetic minimal cell from scratch last spring, call it S-1. It has a membrane pump that keeps the interior osmotically stable. But S-1 has no history — nothing retained that pump, people just assembled it. So on the etiological account S-1's pump has no function whatsoever, even though keeping the interior stable is exactly what the pump is there for.

(3) Unless: the team did run a selection-ish process. They built a hundred variants, kept the ones that stayed intact in the medium, scrapped the ones that lysed. So the pump's contribution to stability does explain why this pump design ended up in S-1 — it explains the retention of the design across the team's series.

(4) But if retention by human designers counts as a selecting process, then differential retention by anything counts. The reason that particular mug is in my cupboard is that it holds coffee; the reason that rock is still on the slope is that friction keeps it there. So the etiological account spreads to everything that stays put, and what stays put is just whatever actually contributes to something — which is the causal-role account.

(5) And that account spreads as well, because for any item and anything it happens to do you can name a containing system whose capacity it contributes to. The function of the mug's handle is to make the cupboard's centre of mass very slightly lopsided.

(6) So on either account, function attributions come out true of everything, or — in the S-1 case — false of the very thing we were most confident about.

(7) A predicate that's true of everything, and one that's false of the paradigm cases, both fail to say anything.

(8) So nothing has a function.

I'd like to keep both accounts and loose (8), and I'm pretty convinced (8) can't be right. But I don't want to just grab whichever step I find least offensive, which is what I did last time. So: which step actually goes? Is there an equivocation or a scope slide I'm not seeing, or is the thing valid and I have to eat one of the premises? And whichever it is, tell me what rejecting that step costs — what do I give up elsewhere in the theory of function — plus why the step looked so innocent when I first wrote it down.
