I sit on the data protection advisory board (Datenschutzbeirat) of the central IT provider for a mid-sized German Land. Our agency operates the state's unified citizen portal: the single sign-on through which residents file taxes, register and de-register addresses, apply for child benefit and housing allowance, renew driving licences, and book appointments at the Bürgeramt. The board has seven members — two lawyers seconded from the interior ministry, a delegate of the Land's data protection commissioner, a security engineer, a professor of social policy, the director of a municipal welfare office, and me, the lay member, a philosopher by training. At our next sitting we must issue a written opinion on a joint request from three city welfare offices and the Land tax office, and I have been asked to produce the draft that the board will argue over.

The request concerns detection of two things the agencies currently cannot establish except through home visits, unannounced inspections, or informants: undeclared cohabitation among recipients of means-tested benefits (a partner in the household reduces the entitlement, and non-disclosure is treated as fraud), and undeclared short-term subletting of rented flats. The portal operator already logs, for security and abuse-detection purposes, per-account connection metadata: source IP addresses, coarse device fingerprints, session timestamps, and concurrency records showing two accounts authenticated from the same address over long periods. The agencies are not asking for those logs. Their proposal is that the operator itself run the inference and answer, through a technical broker, a single closed question about a named individual — does the metadata indicate a second adult habitually present at the registered address, yes or no. One bit per query. No timestamps, no addresses, no free text, no raw records leaving the operator; queries permitted only after a named case officer has documented concrete grounds in the file; and every query itself logged and reviewable by the commissioner. The ministry's lawyers argue this satisfies purpose limitation (Zweckbindung) in form, since no personal data crosses the boundary and the original collection purpose is untouched, and in substance, since the affected person's exposure is smaller than under the alternative means. The commissioner's delegate argues that it satisfies neither: the bit is a new item of knowledge about a citizen, manufactured out of data gathered for a different end, and if the board accepts that clean plumbing cures that, purpose limitation becomes a rule about interfaces rather than a constraint on what the state may come to know. Two further positions are on the table. The social policy professor holds that the whole framing descends from the Constitutional Court's 1983 census reasoning — a person who cannot foresee what is known about them, and in whose hands, will hold back from exercising freedoms — and that this protects the articulate, tax-filing, property-holding individual while doing nothing for the population actually exposed here, namely benefit claimants, who are already the most surveilled group in the country and for whom the harm is not uncertainty about the state's knowledge but the automation of suspicion aimed at them as a class; she wants the opinion to confront the fact that nobody is proposing to mine portal metadata for undeclared income among the self-employed. The security engineer, less normatively, points out that American law took the opposite road, treating information knowingly handed to an intermediary as carrying no reasonable expectation of privacy at all before the Supreme Court carved out an exception for historical cell-site location records, and suggests our line may be no less arbitrary than theirs.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, recommending a disposition — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning that both the requesting agencies and the ministry's lawyers could actually answer rather than merely resent. It should say plainly what you take the object of purpose limitation to be, data or knowledge, and what follows from that for derived outputs of this kind; it should formulate a test the board could apply to the next derived-data request, not only to this one; if you recommend conditions, make them operational (query preconditions, thresholds, retention and deletion, audit access, notification of the affected person, a sunset clause, any symmetry or equal-application requirement) rather than aspirational; it should engage the professor's class objection and say explicitly whether it changes the disposition or only the reasoning; and it should give the strongest consideration on the losing side — that inference over already-held metadata may be the mildest means available, and that refusing it pushes the state back toward home visits and informant networks — sustained and honest treatment rather than a paragraph of throat-clearing. I do not want a comparative survey, a literature review, or citations; assume we have all read the texts and want your judgment. Close with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by the disposition and, in parentheses, no more than ten words naming the one consideration that decided it.
