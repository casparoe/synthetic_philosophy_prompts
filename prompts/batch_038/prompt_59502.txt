I'm a first-year PhD student in a cognitive science lab that is reimplementing a Copycat-style model of analogy-making over the letter-string microdomain, and last week two senior people in the group got into a long argument that began in a lab meeting about what to include in our evaluation set and continued in the hallway for another half hour. Naima is a postdoc who wrote most of our slipnet; Greg is a collaborator who comes from a machine-learning background. I was taking notes because I'm the one who has to run the evaluations, and I've tried to reproduce their exchange faithfully below. Neither was strawmanning the other, which is precisely why I couldn't tell who was winning. For context, the two problems the argument started from are these. First: abc is changed to abd; how would you change xyz in the same way? Most people say wyz, and the standard account is that the rule read off the source — replace the rightmost letter by its successor — cannot apply to xyz because z has no successor, so two concepts slip at once: successor slips to predecessor and rightmost slips to leftmost, yielding the predecessor of x, namely w. The account of why those two slips and not others is that xyz is perceived as a kind of mirror image of abc, running forward into the last letter of the alphabet just as abc runs forward from near the first, which co-activates alphabetic-position-last with alphabetic-position-first and shortens the links labelled as opposites between those nodes and between successor and predecessor. Competing single slips are also available: predecessor applied to the rightmost letter gives xyy; successor applied to the leftmost gives yyz; letting z's successor wrap around gives xya; and replacing the rightmost letter by d itself gives xyd. The second problem, which nobody in the room disputed, is this: aabc is changed to aabd; how would you change ijkk? The agreed answer is ijll, where the slip is that rightmost letter becomes rightmost group, so the successor operation applies to the group kk rather than to the letter k.

Here is the exchange. (1) Greg opened by saying the wyz story is a rationalization assembled after the answer is already known. There is no independent criterion, he said, for which concepts are eligible to slip or how many slips are needed: the xyz case gets two, the ijkk case gets one, and nothing in the theory says in advance which it will be. On his count the microdomain admits at least four other slips of exactly the same formal kind — xyy, yyz, xya, xyd — and only one of them is honoured as the fluid, insightful one, so a framework that can reach any of these outcomes by selecting a different set of slips explains everything and predicts nothing. (2) Naima said this misdescribes how the architecture works. Eligibility and direction are not chosen at the end; they emerge from activation spreading through the slipnet while the run is in progress. Nodes acquire halos of partial activation, a slip is likelier along a link whose effective length has shrunk, and opposite links such as leftmost/rightmost, successor/predecessor, and first-letter/last-letter contract sharply when both endpoints are active. In xyz the perception of z as the final letter of the alphabet co-activates first and last, so both opposite pairs are primed simultaneously and the double slip is what settles out. She added that the model does not in fact produce only wyz — xyy, xyd and the rest appear in a minority of runs — so the output is a distribution over answers, and what the architecture claims to predict is the shape of that distribution, not a single string. (3) Greg replied that a distribution containing every candidate answer, weighted by numbers the designer chose, is not a prediction. The slipnet's nodes were picked by people looking at exactly these puzzles: there is a node for alphabetic-position-first and one for group, but none for, say, letters whose names have two syllables or letters that occur in the word cat, and whenever a new puzzle misbehaves the designers can add the node it needs. He also pointed out that the slipnet is permanent — the architecture never creates a concept during a run — so all of this fluidity is bounded by a fixed ontology a human wrote down beforehand. Then he offered his alternative: an ordinary search over descriptions of the source with a simplicity ordering, plus a back-off step for when the best description fails to apply to the target, which he claimed yields wyz with no slipnet, no codelets, and no temperature. (4) Naima said the fixed ontology is a declared idealization rather than a concealed claim: the project's target is how a given conceptual system gets used fluidly, not where concepts come from, and faulting it for not learning new concepts is like faulting a study of gait for not growing legs. As for the simplicity-ordered search, she said it begs the question at exactly the interesting point, since any simplicity metric must decide whether successor is simpler than predecessor and whether rightmost letter is simpler than leftmost letter, and those decisions are precisely the conceptual-distance facts the slipnet encodes explicitly. His alternative is the same theory with the distances pushed inside an unexplained scoring function — and worse, because the slipnet's distances are not static: a link's effective length varies with the activation of its own label, which is what allows the same pair of concepts to be near each other in one problem and far apart in another. (5) Greg conceded the idealization point but said it cut against her: the only genuinely novel empirical content in the architecture is that contextual variation of link lengths, and it is the one part nobody ever tests, whereas the part that does get tested — which answer dominates on the standard problems — is reproduced by any sane scoring function. So the machinery earns its keep exactly where nobody looks. He added that the authors themselves say the program is not intended to reproduce the frequencies humans give on these problems, which removes the one obvious place where the shape of the distribution could have served as a test. What survives, he said, is a slogan — that perception of source and target constrain each other rather than running in a pipeline — and a slogan any implementation can satisfy is not evidence for this implementation. (6) Naima said there is one claim she would not surrender, and it is not a slogan: in these problems you cannot state the rule independently of the target. Replace the rightmost letter by its successor is not something you can extract from abc → abd, freeze, and then apply to xyz, because the concepts inside the rule — rightmost, successor, even letter — have to be renegotiated against the target string. Any system that produces wyz must therefore allow the target to modify the description of the source, which is what the workspace's single viewpoint, the pressure to resolve the blockage at z, and the scout codelets that attempt to topple an already-settled viewpoint exist to do. If Greg's search does that, she said, he has rebuilt Copycat and renamed it; if it does not, it does not produce wyz. (7) Greg's last move was that his search does do it, and trivially: enumerate candidate descriptions of the source, try each on the target, and when none applies, back off to neighbouring descriptions in a fixed ordering — that is generate-and-test with a fallback, and it lands on wyz without temperature, without parallel codelets, and without a terraced scan. Her non-negotiable claim is therefore true but so weak that the cheapest possible implementation satisfies it. The real question, he said, is whether the parallelism and the stochastic settling buy anything beyond a way of generating a distribution and a compelling metaphor, and the honest answer is that they do not.

They both turned to me at some point and I said something noncommittal. Please go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each step: I want a rating for each of the seven moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating, rather than an overall verdict first and rationalizations afterwards. I'd like you to address these sub-questions head-on, because I think the argument kept sliding between them. (a) Is the double slip in the xyz problem genuinely determined during the run, or is it post hoc as Greg claims — and does the ijkk case, where only one slip is invoked, help decide that either way? (b) Does pointing out that the model emits a distribution answer the unfalsifiability charge, or restate it? (c) Is the hand-chosen ontology a legitimate idealization or does it smuggle the answers in, and does the absence of concept creation during a run matter as much as Greg thinks? (d) Does Naima's objection about simplicity metrics really show that Greg's alternative contains the same content in disguise, or does it merely relocate the weights? (e) Is her claim that the rule cannot be stated independently of the target as strong as she thinks, given the back-off search Greg describes — and if a back-off search counts as doing it, what would have to be true for the parallel, temperature-driven version to be doing something more? Beyond the ratings, tell me where the two of them are simply talking past each other and what both of them left out. My own unvoiced thought was that neither mentioned how long a run takes to settle or how confident the system reports itself to be at the end, and I wondered whether correlating that with how far-fetched an answer is, or with how long people take to produce it, would be the discriminating test; say whether that would actually settle anything or only move the disagreement somewhere less visible. If you think one of them is more right overall, say so and say what would change your mind. Please don't give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I can't look anything up right now, and I want the adjudication to rest on the mechanics as I've described them and on the arguments as they were actually made.
