Some years ago I ran into the puzzle of the two envelopes — one contains twice as much money as the other, you take one, and you are then offered the chance to switch — together with the argument that switching must be worthwhile, since the other envelope is equally likely to hold double what you have as half, which makes it worth a quarter more. I never found the usual ways of dissolving that argument satisfying, so I have been constructing versions of my own and writing down what I actually think in each. I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my answers, and if there is not, which of the answers I should abandon.

Here they are, case by case.

1. Neither envelope opened, nothing known about the amounts, and the choice must be made blind and then stands. Switching seems to me a matter of perfect indifference. The situation is symmetrical, and I cannot see how an argument is entitled to break the symmetry.

2. The same, except that I may open my envelope first, and I find $60. Still indifferent. A number on its own does not appear to tell me which of the two envelopes I am holding.

3. The organizer announces beforehand that the smaller amount is a whole number of dollars between $1 and $1,000, and I open my envelope to find $1,600. Do not switch. Were $1,600 the smaller amount the organizer would have broken their own rule, so I must be holding the larger, and the other contains $800.

4. The same announcement, and I open my envelope to find $500. Switch. Either $500 is the smaller and the other holds $1,000, or $500 is the larger and the other holds $250, and I can see no reason to rate one of those above the other; gaining $500 outweighs losing $250. What unsettles me is that the same reasoning appears to work for every amount up to $1,000 — $6, $60, $500, $998 — and to fail only above that, where doubling has already been ruled out by the announcement. So the famous argument is not simply invalid. It is correct across the whole range of amounts I would expect to encounter, and it misfires only in a region the organizer's promise has already fenced off.

5. A machine fills the envelopes. It tosses a fair coin until it lands tails, counts the heads, and puts two raised to that number of dollars in the smaller envelope: $1 half the time, $2 a quarter of the time, $4 an eighth, and so on, with no upper limit. I open mine and find $128. Indifferent. I worked this through and the gain and the loss cancel exactly, which surprised me, because the possibility that I hold the smaller envelope is the less likely of the two and yet switching still neither helps nor hurts.

6. The same machine, but the coin lands heads two times out of three, so the smaller amount is $1 a third of the time, $2 two-ninths of the time, $4 four-twenty-sevenths, and so on. I open mine and find $128. Switch — and I would switch whatever amount I found, however large. This is the case I cannot shake, because it means that "always switch" is not a confusion but a true description of at least one perfectly definite arrangement. It also sits badly with case 1: if I would switch whatever I found, I cannot see why I should not commit to switching before either envelope is opened, and yet in case 1 I am confident that switching does nothing.

7. In the arrangement of case 6, a friend and I each hold an envelope and each is offered a switch. I do not accept that we should both switch. Only one of us can finish better off, and if the reasoning applies to me it applies identically to her, so it cannot be tracking anything real. One of case 6 and case 7 is wrong and I do not know which.

8. In the arrangement of case 6, I open my envelope and find $4 billion. Do not switch — but not for a numerical reason. At that point I have stopped believing the organizer, or stopped believing that anyone seriously means that $8 billion is sitting in an envelope on a table. I am uneasy about this answer, since it suggests case 6 was only ever accepted on sufferance.

My own tentative rule, which I cannot get into good shape, is this: switch when the other envelope is at least as likely to be the bigger one as the smaller one. That handles case 3, and it seems to handle 1 and 2, but it gives what I take to be the wrong answer in case 4, and it flatly contradicts case 6, where I want to switch even though holding the smaller envelope is the less likely thing. So the rule needs repair or replacement.

The case I keep going back and forth on, and the one I most want your view on: the organizer says only that the smaller amount was fixed by some perfectly definite procedure that they decline to describe. I open my envelope and find $60. Half of me says indifferent, exactly as in case 2. The other half says that my ignorance is a fact about me and not about the envelopes; that there is some answer to whether $60 is likelier to be the smaller or the larger; and that professing indifference is not avoiding an assumption but smuggling one in. I do not know whether a defensible verdict exists here at all, or whether the case is malformed.

So: is there a principle, or a small number of competing principles, that accounts for these answers? Please state it exactly rather than as a slogan, and say which of my cases each part of it is there to handle. Then tell me what it implies for two or three arrangements I have not thought of, chosen because they test the principle rather than because they are strange. And tell me plainly which of my answers it cannot accommodate. If cases 6 and 7 cannot both stand, say which goes, and what giving it up costs me elsewhere.
