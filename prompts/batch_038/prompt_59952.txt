So I got talked into serving on our university's research integrity panel — I'm apparently the faculty member who "knows about arguments," which in practice means people expect me to notice when a report's conclusions don't follow from its findings. We've just closed the fact-finding stage of a case and the panel is split in a way I've never seen before, and since I'm the one who keeps asking questions, I've been handed the job of writing the memo that goes to the Research Integrity Officer and to counsel before Friday's meeting. Here's the situation. Our policy requires three things for a finding of misconduct against a researcher: (a) that fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism actually occurred; (b) that it was done intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly rather than being honest error; and (c) that it was a significant departure from accepted practices in the relevant research community. All three are necessary, and the burden is preponderance of the evidence. The allegation is that two panels in a figure are the same microscopy field duplicated and relabeled as two separate experiments. There were supposed to be seven voting members, but one recused herself because she's a coauthor with the respondent on an unrelated paper, so six of us voted — and on counsel's advice we voted seperately on each of the three elements and then separately on the bottom-line question "did research misconduct occur."

The element votes: on (a), five yes, one no (F thinks the adjustments fall inside the journal's stated image policy). On (b), four yes, two no (D is convinced this was a file-management disaster, not recklessness). On (c), four yes, two no (E is a working cell biologist and says re-using a representative field with a disclosure in the legend is common enough in her subfield that it isn't a significant departure). Then the bottom-line vote came out three to three, because D, E, and F each voted no — each of them accepts two of the three elements but thinks one fails, and since all three are required, each of them thinks the charge isn't made. A tie means no finding under our charter, i.e. the charge is dismissed. So the same six people, looking at the same evidence in the same room, produce "misconduct established, with a two-thirds-or-better majority on every element" when you tally element by element, and "case dismissed" when you tally the conclusion. I know roughly what this is called in the social choice literature and I'd rather you not lead with the label — my readers are an administrator and a lawyer, and if the memo is dense with jargon it'll come straight back to me.

What I need is a memo, call it 800–1100 words, that does three things. First, tell the panel what to actually report in this case — a finding, no finding, or some third option (remand, a finding on some elements only, an express statement that we can't reach a determination) — with reasons spelled out well enough that the respondent, who sees the report, can tell she wasn't railroaded or let off by an accident of arithmetic. Second, recommend which tallying method the panel should adopt as a standing rule for future cases, and defend it against the strongest argument for the other one; in our meeting that argument came from F, who said something like: under the element-by-element method the panel can end up issuing a finding that not one of its members actually holds, since each of the three of us who voted no on the bottom line rejects the finding as a whole — so in what sense is that the panel's judgment rather than an artifact of how the votes were counted? I think there's something to that and I don't want it waved away. Third, handle two loose ends: one member said out loud, after seeing the tallies, that he might switch his vote on (c) if that's what it takes to make the two counts line up, which struck me as bad for reasons I couldn't articulate on the spot — should the memo say anything about voting on elements with the resulting outcome in view? And counsel wants the final report to read as a single unified finding with reasons, not a vote breakdown; is that compatible with what you recommend, and if not, what do we tell her? Also, briefly: should the respondent be told which method we used and why, and does anything change if you consider that the recused seventh member would almost certainly have voted yes on all three elements and yes on the bottom line, in which case the divergence between the two methods would have vanished? One of my colleagues thinks that last point matters a lot; I don't; I'd like the memo to say which of us is right.
