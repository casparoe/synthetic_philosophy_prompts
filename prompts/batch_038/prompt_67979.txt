So I fell down a rabbit hole on the whole "revolutions devour their children" thing and now I've got this itch to work out what the phrase actually picks out, because I use it constantly and I can't say when it applies. Here's my gut on a bunch of cases, roughly in the order I thought of them:

- Danton and Camille Desmoulins, April 1794: counts, obviously. Desmoulins was Robespierre's schoolfriend, Danton more or less willed the Revolutionary Tribunal into existence the year before, and then the Tribunal ate them.
- Robespierre himself, 9 Thermidor, guillotined alongside Saint-Just with no trial by the Convention using the emergency machinery he'd built: counts, and I think it's the paradigm.
- Fouquier-Tinville, the Tribunal's own public prosecutor, guillotined in 1795: counts, though he feels more like the teeth of the machine than one of its children.
- The Girondins — Brissot, Vergniaud, the rest — October 1793: counts. Vergniaud voted for the king's death and spent a year legitimating the exact thing that killed him.
- Marie Antoinette two weeks earlier: doesn't count at all. She's the meal the revolution was built to eat, not its child.
- Louis Capet: doesn't count, same reason.
- Condorcet: declared an outlaw in '93, went to ground, arrested in a village outside Paris, dead in his cell overnight, probably by his own hand. Counts, and it's the one that gets me most, even though no law formally killed him and he'd spent his last year attacking the Montagnards.
- Olympe de Gouges, guillotined November 1793: counts, though I hesitate, since she was a pamphleteer and never held power.
- The Hébertistes: counts, though they were the ones screaming for more terror, so maybe that's just a trim rather than a devouring.
- Carrier: the Nantes drownings dont count for me (the victims were counter-revolutionaries and locals), but his own guillotining by the Thermidorians does.
- The September Massacres: doesn't count. Popular slaughter of prisoners, no institutional shape to it.
- Mirabeau: dying of illness in 1791 obviously doesn't count, but the Panthéon threw his body out once it came out he'd been taking money from the court, and later put Marat in the tomb instead — and I think THAT counts as a kind of devouring, which worries me, because if posthumous erasure counts then Marat counts too (pantheonized, then dug back up months later) and basically everyone counts.
- The Thermidorian White Terror of 1795, Jacobins murdered in the south: doesn't count, that's the reaction eating the revolution, which is a different animal.
- Kronstadt 1921: counts, barely — the sailors were the showcase troops of 1917 and in open rebellion by '21, so I can't tell whether they're children being eaten or enemies being fought.
- Trotsky, ice pick in Mexico, 1940: counts, even though no revolutionary court laid a hand on him.
- Bukharin and the Old Bolsheviks: counts.
- Liu Shaoqi, dead in a Kaifeng infirmary in 1969 after two years of beatings and self-criticism: counts, and I'd say the teenage Red Guards who got shipped to the countryside not long after count too, even though they'd been the perpetrators.
- A rank-and-file sans-culotte denounced by his own section and guillotined in 1794: I want to say doesn't count, and I hate that I want to say it, because it makes the metaphor a story about famous men.

My tentative rule is: the victim has to be (i) one of the movement's own, (ii) destroyed by an instrument the movement built in its own name, and (iii) — the part I'm unsure about — a co-author of that instrument, at least by endorsement. But it fails my own verdicts in both directions. Condorcet and Trotsky fail (ii) hard and I still say they count. The sans-culotte passes (i) and (ii) easily and I said he doesn't. And (iii) can't be about guilt — I'm fine with a victim who genuinely conspired — but I'd say a real royalist spy executed by the Tribunal doesn't count, so it's about standing rather than innocence.

Can you build me the actual principle? I want the clauses stated precisely with which of my cases motivates each one, and if there's a genuine fork I want both candidates rather than a fudge. Then tell me what your best one says about cases I didn't list: the princesse de Lamballe murdered in September '92, Charlotte Corday, a Jacobin who really did plot against the Committee and was caught at it, and someone purged and scrubbed from the official record who then dies of old age abroad. Be honest about which of my verdicts you have to overturn — I'd rather lose one than end up with a rule that just says purge and dresses it up as devouring.

Last thing, and it's why I care: does the principle you land on make the eating structural or accidental? If it has to include co-authorship and self-built instruments, it starts to look like the Terror was always going to circle back on its makers, which is a big claim about 1789 and not just about 1794. If it doesn't, the metaphor is just a label for the fact that winning coalitions fall out. Tell me which of those I'm buying if I keep my verdicts.
