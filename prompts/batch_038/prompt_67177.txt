I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy; I intend to write my dissertation on the epistemic foundations of solution concepts in non-cooperative game theory. After a reading group last Thursday, two fellow students — I will call them Nadia and Omar — argued for the better part of an hour about a three-by-three game that had been put on the board. I was present, I followed nearly all of it, and at no point could I tell who was ahead. Below I have tried to reproduce the exchange faithfully and without signaling my own sympathies, including the moves that struck me as weak on both sides.

The game (row player's payoff listed first):

```
                 L            C            R
 U           (2, 0)       (2, 5)       (2, 0)
 M           (5, 4)       (3, 1)       (0, 0)
 D           (0, 0)       (3, 1)       (5, 4)
```

The exchange:

1. Nadia: U is strictly dominated and goes first. Not to M, which is worse at R, nor to D, which is worse at L, but to the fifty-fifty mixture of M and D, which returns 2.5, 3, and 2.5 against L, C, and R respectively and so beats U's 2, 2, 2 at every column. She adds that under strict dominance the order of deletion never matters, so the reduction is well defined.

2. Omar: That is not dominance; it is a trick played on the definition. The whole force of the argument is supposed to be that something else is better whatever the opponent does, and that is a claim about things an agent might actually do. A mixture returns 2.5 only if the agent really randomizes; if she ends up playing M she can receive 0, and if she ends up playing D she can receive 0, both worse than U's guaranteed 2. Admitting mixtures as dominators therefore smuggles a behavioral assumption — that agents randomize — into an argument that is supposed to be prior to every such assumption. Restrict the test to domination by pure strategies and it is clean and assumption-free; and on that test nothing whatever is deletable in this game, so all six strategies stand.

3. Nadia: You have conflated a payoff guarantee with an epistemic property. In a two-player game a pure strategy is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy if and only if it is never a best response to any belief about the opponent's play. U fails that test outright: were U a best response to some belief (qL, qC, qR), then 2 ≥ 5qL + 3qC and 2 ≥ 3qC + 5qR, and adding these yields 4 ≥ 5(qL + qR) + 6qC = 5 + qC ≥ 5, which is absurd. There is no conceivable belief that makes U worth playing, so the mixture introduces no assumption about behavior — it is only the convexity of the payoff space doing work that was already there. And nothing about that is special to this example: the equivalence holds in every finite game.

4. Omar: That last sentence is false, and it is the heart of the matter. The equivalence is a two-player artifact. With three or more players, "not strictly dominated by any mixed strategy" amounts to "a best response to some belief" only if beliefs are permitted to be correlated, that is, only if they are joint distributions over the opponents' pure strategy profiles. If conjectures must be independent across opponents, as they are in one of the two original formulations of rationalizability, the two notions come apart: a strategy can fail to be strictly dominated and yet never be a best response to any product belief. So "survives iterated strict dominance" and "is rationalizable" do not pick out the same set in general, and you cannot present your procedure as the neutral canonical one against which mine is a mere restriction.

5. Nadia: I grant that for three or more players, and it is beside the point on a board with two. Note instead how far your pure-only rule falls short even here. Once U is gone, C is strictly dominated for the column player — and again only by a mixture: half L and half R returns 2 whether the row player chooses M or D, against C's 1 and 1, while L alone returns 0 at D and R alone returns 0 at M. Your rule therefore cannot complete even a second round; mine terminates at the two-by-two game on {M, D} × {L, R}. That is what dominance-solvability is: C is eliminated despite being the column player's unique best response to U, and what is left is a pure coordination problem in which we know the players will coordinate.

6. Omar: Two replies. First, it is not dominance-solvable, because nothing in that two-by-two game is dominated by anything; the procedure halts with four profiles and two strict equilibria and says nothing about which will be played. A set is not a prediction. Second, and worse, letting mixtures delete costs you equilibria. (U, C) is a Nash equilibrium of the game as written: C is the column player's unique best response to U, and U is at least as good for the row player as either alternative against C. Your procedure deleted both U and C, hence deleted an equilibrium, hence is not equilibrium-preserving — and the order-sensitivity that everyone agrees infects weak dominance infects yours as well.

Neither would yield, and I left no better informed than I had arrived.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? In particular:

- Check the technical claims rather than accepting them as reported: the alleged dominating mixtures in moves 1 and 5; the inequality argument in move 3; whether Omar is right in move 2 that nothing is deletable when domination is restricted to pure strategies; whether anything further is deletable in the two-by-two game that remains after moves 1 and 5; and whether (U, C) is in fact a Nash equilibrium of the original game. Where a move's claim can be settled without any computation at all, say so and give the general reason.
- Rate each of the six moves on a five-point scale (1 = clearly mistaken, 3 = substantially right but containing a significant error, 5 = clearly right), and justify each rating by saying precisely which claim within the move holds and which fails.
- Say who was more right overall, and whether the margin is owed to the technical slips or to the methodological quarrel of move 2.
- Take up that methodological quarrel on its merits rather than as a matter of convention: is Omar's pure-strategy-only procedure a legitimate rival that genuinely yields a different surviving set here, and if two coherent procedures disagree, what — if anything — could decide between them?
- Identify what both of them missed or mischaracterized. I suspect there is at least one substantial point neither made, possibly concerning the status of Omar's slide from mixed strict dominance to weak dominance in move 6, or the fact that the entire cascade is hostage to a single first-round deletion.

I am not after a survey of solution concepts, a taxonomy of refinements, or pointers to the literature. I want a verdict on these six moves and on the argument underneath them.
