I have no academic training in philosophy — I left school at sixteen, work in logistics, and have spent the last few years reading about minds on my own, mostly to settle two private questions: whether my dog has beliefs in anything like the sense that I do, and where the large language models I now use daily at work fall on that spectrum. A friend lent me a chapter from a popular book on animal intelligence that uses Daniel Dennett's fourfold classification of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — and the chapter's treatment is the clearest I have found. I want to know how much of it I can trust before I build my own thinking on it.

I am paraphrasing rather than quoting, so please judge the ideas and not my wording. The chapter's account runs, in brief, roughly as follows:

Dennett sorts minds into four kinds, each named for a thinker whose insight it embodies: Darwin for selection, Skinner for conditioning, Popper for conjecture, and the psychologist Richard Gregory for the way tools extend intelligence. Darwinian creatures are pure instinct — evolution has hard-wired their responses, so a moth flying to a flame is one. Skinnerian creatures can learn: their behaviour is reshaped by reward and punishment, which lets a pigeon or a dog cope with situations its ancestors never met. Popperian creatures go further, carrying an internal model of their surroundings, so they can try out actions in imagination instead of in the world and thereby let their guesses perish in place of themselves; a chimpanzee stacking crates to reach a fruit is Popperian. Gregorian creatures are the only ones that can think about their own thinking, because they possess language. Dennett treats these as four distinct kinds of mind arranged on a ladder of increasing sophistication, and any animal can be assigned to exactly one rung. Two consequences follow for machines: a system trained purely by rewards and penalties can be an excellent Skinnerian creature but will never rise above that rung, however well it performs, since nothing in reinforcement supplies an internal model; and a chatbot, whose outputs were shaped by approval and disapproval from trainers, is Gregorian in appearance but Skinnerian in substance — it handles symbols without ever having acquired the model of the world that Popperian and Gregorian creatures require.

Please work through this claim by claim. Which statements would Dennett, or a careful reader of him, endorse without qualification? Which are legitimate simplifications for a general audience? And which are actually wrong, in ways that matter rather than in ways that merely lose nuance? I would especially like to know whether the four rungs really are exclusive categories, what the Gregorian upgrade actually consists in on Dennett's account, and what Popperian competence requires — whether it demands anything like conscious imagining.

Then tell me whether the two conclusions about machines survive the corrections. I confess I find the pigeon verdict about reward-trained systems persuasive and the chatbot verdict suspiciously tidy, but I cannot say why. Finish by stating plainly what you think follows for my two original questions, and flag anywhere the issue cannot be settled without the book itself. I have no library access, so please do not send me a reading list.
