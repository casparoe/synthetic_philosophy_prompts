I serve on the founding board of a newly incorporated settlement of roughly two hundred households, built on a decommissioned airfield and populated almost entirely by people who arrived within the same eighteen months. We therefore have no customs whatsoever. My job has turned out to consist of arbitrating questions that should not be difficult: which side of the shared path we pass on, whether the laundry house runs a first-come queue or a sign-up sheet, at what hour the communal generator is switched off. What unsettles me is that residents keep demanding to see the original agreement, and keep proposing that we simply put the matter to a vote and enact a rule. I have been rereading David Lewis's Convention (1969) in the evenings, and I have become convinced that both demands rest on the same confusion. I would like a piece that dramatizes that confusion rather than explaining it.

Please write the owner's manual for the NormForm™ Convention Installation Kit — a product our board has, in this fiction, purchased from a consultancy promising turnkey social norms to new communities. Around 900 words, in the deadpan register of appliance documentation: numbered sections, imperative instructions, a troubleshooting FAQ, a list of known limitations, and warranty terms.

The humor must come from getting the theory right rather than from generic corporate satire. Specifically, the manual should:

- contain a diagnostic checklist distinguishing a genuine convention from a mere regularity of behavior, tracking the interlocking expectations and the conditional structure of the preferences at issue — each resident prefers conformity only on condition that the others conform, so that the lone deviant is a coordination failure rather than a wrongdoer;
- explain, in installation-step form, that a decree cannot by itself produce a convention, and that the included herald module (an announcement delivered in a place where everyone is present and everyone can see that everyone else is present) exists precisely because a private letter to each household cannot generate common knowledge — a limitation the manual should note is not curable by sending follow-up letters;
- treat the selection among rival equilibria as made by salience and precedent rather than by reasons, and present as a feature rather than a defect the fact that the installed regularity is arbitrary in the sense that an alternative would have served equally well;
- decline, in the known-limitations section, to install any norm that is not a coordination convention, referring the distributive question of who receives the larger plot to a separate Conflict Module that the manual reports as indefinitely back-ordered because the analysis on which the kit is built does not extend to it;
- state plainly in the warranty section that no agreement was ever made, that none is required, and that claims will be rejected from customers who produce a signed founding compact, on the grounds that they have mistaken the product for something else;
- describe the obligation the kit yields as defeasible — a reason to conform that holds only while general conformity is expected, and one that the manual cheerfully notes may be overridden, without ever quite saying by what.

The target is the practice of designing norms from above, together with the assumption that a binding norm must be traceable to an act of consent. The reader should finish uneasy about how little work consent is doing here, without the manual ever arguing the point or winking at us; the irony should stay entirely in the register of a vendor proud of its engineering. No real person or company should be named, and the vendor is invented. It should read comfortably to a resident who has never opened the book, and land considerably harder for someone who has.
