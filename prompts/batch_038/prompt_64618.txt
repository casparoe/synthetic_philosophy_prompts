Write me a short story, maybe 1200–1800 words, about someone who wakes up in a sealed room with no memory of how they got there and has to work out which direction time is flowing in it purely from the stuff in the room: a beaker of water with ice in it, a drop of ink, dust along a shelf, a wound on their own forearm, a clock they can see but can't tell which of its faces is the front. The catch is that they were told — before whatever wiped them, by someone they only half trust — that in this facility they can invert the local entropy gradient and then set it right again, so the room might genuinely be running backwards relative to everything outside, and there's no independent way to check. No heartbeat they trust, no memory to lean on, no way to see out. I want the reasoning to happen as noticing and inference rather than a lecture paragraph about thermodynamics, and I want the narrator to reach a verdict and then act on it — open the door, drink something, let some process finish — and be wrong in a way that's clear to the reader but not to them. Slip in one detail early on that only makes sense once you know which way time was actually going, so the thing reads differently the second time through. And dont resolve it by revealing it was a simulation or a dream; the point I'm after is that the arrow is supposed to be a fact about this particular region of the world rather than a law you can count on, and the character has to make a decision anyway while that's still up in the air.
