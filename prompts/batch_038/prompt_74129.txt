I am a junior, and for the past two years I have run an unofficial reading group with four classmates and our history teacher; we meet on Thursdays in the library annex and work through whatever one of us nominates. This month I nominated a unit on Kant's claim that the moral worth of an action lies not in what it accomplishes but in the principle on which it is done, largely because I wanted to argue against it, and I have instead ended up unable to state clearly what I think. Our teacher suggested we each turn our notes into a short position paper for a regional essay competition, and mine is supposed to say what moral worth consists in. Rather than start from a theory, I want to start from the verdicts I actually have, because I suspect that when they are laid out together they will either reveal a principle I already believe or reveal that some of them have to go.

Here are my verdicts, in roughly the order I came to them. The shopkeeper who charges every customer the same honest price because his business would suffer if he were known to cheat: no moral worth, even though the price is right. The same shopkeeper, keeping the same policy after his competitors have all fled and cheating would now pay handsomely, because he judges that cheating would simply be wrong: full moral worth. A classmate who logs her soup-kitchen hours because her application requires them, and who is nonetheless warm and attentive to every person she serves: I want to say very little worth, though noticeably more than a person who does the same hours while being cruel. My grandmother, who has written a birthday card to every relative for fifty years because making people happy delights her and she gives the matter no further thought: I want to say full moral worth, which seems to put her on the same footing as the second shopkeeper. A volunteer who has been depressed for a year, feels nothing, and goes anyway because she judges that the people depending on her matter: full moral worth, and if worth comes in degrees, perhaps more than my grandmother's. Huckleberry Finn, who cannot bring himself to betray his friend and does the right thing while sincerely believing it is wrong and that he will be damned for it: real moral worth, which is the verdict I am least willing to abandon. A man who helps a stranger because he knows a camera crew is filming and wants to be seen helping, and who would also say, truthfully, that helping is the right thing to do: no moral worth, or close to none. Someone who donates because his horoscope promised a fortunate week, and who also holds, unconnectedly, that donating is right: no worth at all, even though his belief about rightness is correct. A person who does exactly the right thing for exactly the right reason but only because a drug given to her without her knowledge has rearranged her motives: no worth, since nothing in it is hers. And a friend of mine who reports a classmate's cheating because he says fairness demands it, and fairness does demand it, but who I am fairly sure would not have noticed the unfairness at all had he not wanted revenge: I keep flipping on this one, and it is the case I most want an answer about.

My tentative principle is that an action has moral worth just when the agent does it because she recognizes that it is right. It seems to handle the two shopkeepers, the filming man, the horoscope, and the drug. But it clearly gives the wrong answer for my grandmother and for Huck Finn, and I do not know how to amend it without also giving worth to the camera-pleaser or to my revenge-seeking friend. So: state as precisely as you can the principle, or the small number of competing principles, that best captures these verdicts, and for each clause tell me which of my cases it was written to accommodate. Say explicitly which of my verdicts your account cannot save and which one you would tell me to drop. Tell me whether worth on your account is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees, and why, and what that implies for the soup-kitchen classmate who is kind while padding her application. Then give me your account's verdicts on three cases I have not listed and would not have thought to construct, and finally apply it to the one I left unresolved: whether a mother who pulls her child out of a burning car, purely out of love and without a trace of reasoning, acts with moral worth, with mere instinct, or with something in between.
