I'm teaching a session on archival silences and I want a case that's harder than the usual 'the record was never created' or 'the box got weeded' story. Build me one to these specs.

Realistic local records office or hospital archive, present day, no sci-fi, one paragraph for the scenario. Nothing is destroyed or withheld: every file survives, retention is followed, there's no censor and no bad actor, and the archivist is competent and well meaning. Yet a researcher looking for the history of one group of people who passed through a particular institution between two dates finds effectively nothing, and can honestly report the archive as silent about them. The cause has to be arrangement — it falls out of respecting des fonds, so the group's traces are split across several creators' series, interacting with at least one other perfectly defensible practice (a finding aid that just mirrors the creator's own series structure, say).

Then show me it works:

1. exactly what the researcher does find, and why the group is invisible in it;
2. which stage of Trouillot's account of how silences get produced this sits at — making of sources, assembling of archives, retrieval, or the later write-up — and whether a second stage is doing work too;
3. what the neutral custodian-of-evidence view says (I suspect: thats just a poor finding aid, a technical problem, not power);
4. what a post-custodial or community-description critic says about the same case, and what they'd repair.

Finish by saying which feature of the case is doing the work, then break it: if you re-arrange by subject, does the silence vanish, and does that vindicate the neutral view or the critical one? Argue it, don't split the difference. And tell me whether it still works if the archivist is fully reflective and does everything right — I need to know I haven't smuggled in a villian. No bibliography or citations, please.
