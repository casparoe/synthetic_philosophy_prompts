Write a pantoum of four quatrains in which a risk-averse agent defends her refusal of a long-odds bet that has a positive expected value. Because the form carries lines 2 and 4 of each stanza forward as lines 1 and 3 of the next, let the recycled material be exactly the theoretical crux: the same sentence about the odds and the payoff should return each time bearing a different weight, so that the reader feels how much turns not on the probability attached to any single branch but on where that outcome sits within the whole distribution. The poem should make plain, without naming any theorist or any paradox, that her reluctance is an attitude toward risk in its own right rather than a byproduct of diminishing marginal utility for money, and the closing stanza should fold the opening lines back in while conceding what the position costs her: that she cannot price her options branch by branch, and that a sequence of such choices can be arranged against her. Someone who knows the literature should be able to identify the theory from the poem alone; someone who does not should simply find a good poem about refusing a gamble.
