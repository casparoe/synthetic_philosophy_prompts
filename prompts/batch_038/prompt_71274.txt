So I've been keeping a spreadsheet for about a year now, 230-ish people I actually know, and for each one I've got birth year plus a pile of markers: age they got a phone that was theirs, whether they grew up with a shared household landline, first social network they joined, whether they learned to drive before or after everybody had a camera in their pocket, whether their parents split, whether the house had cable. Started as a party trick, now I can't stop thinking about what it means for whether generations are real kinds at all. Here are my verdicts. I want to know what principle they add up to, if any.

The 1996/1997 line. I think the specific year is arbitrary, nobody born in '96 is meaningfully different from someone born eight months later. But I also think that for the overwhelming majority of people, which generation they're in is a fact about the world and not a bookkeeping choice. I want to hold both.

Two kids in the sheet: born 2000 in Seoul, born 2005 in a ranch town in eastern Montana. The Seoul kid had a smartphone at eight and lived in group chats through middle school; the Montana kid didn't get a phone until 16 and had dial-up-era parents the whole way. I think they're in the same generation, way more alike to each other than either is to there birth-year peers in my data. And yet I also think the Montana kid's older brother (1998) is a Millennial and his younger brother (2002) is Gen Z, and neither label is a mistake about them.

A friend who moved from Lagos to Houston at ten. On slang, music, phone habits, she's in the American cohort, no hesitation. On attitudes toward authority, money, what you owe your parents, she's in neither cohort — she's in some third thing I can't name. I'm willing to accept that as a real answer but I don't know what question it's an answer to.

"Boomer" as an insult online. I want to say it's a homonym, a word about a temperament that happens to be spelled like the demographic one. But I also think calling a 46-year-old a boomer is a stretch rather than a category error, which suggests the temperament and the cohort aren't independent. Does my principle have to predict one from the other, or is the overlap allowed to be a coincidence?

Gen Alpha. I don't think it's real yet, and I'm tempted by the idea that a generation isn't finished being made until its youngest member is an adult. But I also think Millennials were a real kind in 2004, before most of them were adults. Those two don't sit together and I can't tell which one I'd surrender.

Lost Generation versus Gen X. The first feels real in a stronger sense — the war and the flu and the Depression landed on a whole society at once in a way nothing has since. Then I remember 9/11 and 2008 and the pandemic and I lose my nerve, and latchkey kid starts to look like a marketing story told backwards about people who were just unsupervised. I can't say what would settle it.

The Xennial / Oregon Trail band, roughly 1977 to 1983. I think most people who claim it are coping. But I have four friends in that band who are more alike to each other than to anyone else in my sheet, and I can't explain that away.

Here's my tentative principle, and I want you to attack it rather than flatter it: a generation is the maximal set of people whose formative window — say ages six to sixteen — overlapped with the same shift in the ordinary technology of daily life, and who were in the same society when it happened. Two clauses, both doing heavy lifting. Does it deliver my verdicts? Go clause by clause and say which case each clause exists to handle. Then tell me what it says about cases I didn't list: twins born on either side of a New Year boundary; a kid raised in a plain Anabaptist community with no screens whatsoever; someone homeschooled offline until college; a 2014-born whose parents banned phones until fourteen — is that person Gen Alpha, and does the answer depend on whether they ever actually get one? And be blunt about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. If saving all seven costs me a principle with five clauses and two hidden parameters, say so and tell me which verdict is cheapest to drop. Last thing: I keep sliding between cohort, meaning people born in a window, and generation, meaning people who share something, as though they were the same sort of thing. I suspect a chunk of my confusion is that they aren't. If that's right, tell me how many kinds I'm actually juggling and which of my verdicts belong to which.
