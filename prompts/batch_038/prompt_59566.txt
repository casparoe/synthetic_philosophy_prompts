**Working title: Conventions That Only Work in the Dark**

**§1. The target**

Lewis's analysis is built out of coordination problems: recurring situations in which each of us does better by matching, and in which what matters about the match is that it is a match rather than that it lands on any particular option. The picture underneath is one of common interest — everyone's preferences point the same way, because everyone wants us not to collide — and Lewis formalises this with games of pure coordination. His definition of a convention then requires, roughly, a regularity R in the behaviour of members of a population P in a recurrent situation S, such that it is true and common knowledge in P that everyone conforms to R, that everyone expects everyone else to conform, that preferences over the combinations of actions are broadly aligned, and that each member prefers that others conform given that the rest do. (I think the last clause is stated with an "almost all" rider rather than a strict "everyone," and whether that rider is doing quiet work matters for what follows — flag it if you know.)

Two features are going to be my problem. First, the unanimity clause: the analysandum is a single regularity that each member of the population follows. Second, the common knowledge clause: not only does everyone conform, everyone knows everyone conforms, knows they know, and so on. Where the coordination problem has several equilibria, Lewis brings in salience or prominence to explain which one gets hit, and I take it the natural extension is that salience can also sort people into roles when the equilibrium is asymmetric.

**§2. The cases**

Some regularities that look, smell and behave exactly like conventions have the property that they are good only up to a point. The commuter's back road is faster than the highway only while few people know about it. The norm of applying to the most selective programme rather than the safest one pays off only while most people don't follow it. The etiquette of not being first to leave a gathering, the practice of sending one's paper to the top journal first, the habit of queuing at the stall with the shortest line — in each case the payoff to conforming falls as the rate of conformity rises, and the stable pattern is a mix: some conform, some don't, in roughly the right proportions.

Note what these are not. They are not cases of conflicting interests over which equilibrium to hit, and they are not cases where salience can sort us into roles. There is no feature of me that makes me the person who takes the back road; nobody is the designated deviant. The mix has to be anonymous and statistical, and which role any individual ends up in is settled by noise, timing and private hunches. So the Lewisian apparatus has nothing to grip: there is no single R for everyone to conform to, and no labels for salience to fasten onto.

**§3. The twist: common knowledge isn't merely absent, it's fatal**

Here's the part I think is interesting, and the part I'm least sure of. In these cases the regularity isn't just failing to be common knowledge as a contingent matter of fact. Common knowledge would destroy it. Announce the back road in the local paper and it stops being the back road. Publish the list of which restaurants the locals eat at and the locals stop eating there. Tell every applicant, as a public information campaign, that they should apply to the most selective programme, and the advice becomes self-defeating in a way that advice about driving on the right never could.

So there's a class of self-sustaining, arbitrary, sanction-backed, teachable regularities — conventions on every intuitive test — that require the population to be kept in a state of calibrated ignorance about the frequency of conformity. Call them dark conventions. If common knowledge is genuinely a necessary condition of conventionhood, then dark conventions aren't conventions, which seems like a bad result because they are the paradigm case of an informal norm that does real social work. And if common knowledge isn't necessary, then it isn't doing the sustaining work Lewis assigns to it, and we owe an account of what is.

That's why I want to say — and this is the sentence I'm most worried about — that Lewis hasn't given a theory of convention but a theory of one species of it: unanimity norms, where the good is non-rivalrous and the only question is which of several identical outcomes we land on.

**§4. What sustains them instead**

My proposal is that the sustaining knowledge in a dark convention is aggregate rather than interpersonal. What each agent needs is a reasonably accurate estimate of the compliance rate, not a map of who is complying. And the estimate comes from public signals that are one step removed from the behaviour itself: journey times, waiting lists, acceptance rates, how full the place looks on a Tuesday. The signal is public; the regularity it indicates stays private. Mutual expectation is replaced by a shared reading of an indicator plus a private decision rule conditioned on it.

Two predictions fall out, and I think they're testable. First, dark conventions can't be repaired by exhortation, since the failure was never a failure of anyone's intentions. Second, they fail silently and then overshoot: there is no moment you can point to at which the convention broke, because conformity was never individually observable, and by the time the indicator has moved decisively the population has already swung past the equilibrium to the other side. Convention death by oscillation rather than by erosion.

**§5. Consequence for anyone trying to manage one**

If §4 is right, then a public body that depends on a dark convention — a funder whose selectivity does the signalling work, a city that wants its secondary routes to stay secondary, an institution whose prestige depends on people being uncertain about who gets in — has a standing incentive to keep its own success opaque. Transparency about the regularity is precisely the intervention that dissolves it. That puts such bodies in the ugly position of having to obscure, from the people they serve, the fact that the arrangement is working.

I'm not sure §5 belongs in this paper at all. It might be the seed of a separate one.
