I help run a small working group that's part pest-management people, part animal ethics people, and after two years we've made literally zero progress on one question. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right.

Setup, so you can engage the actual positions: everybody in the room agrees on the empirical stuff. Insect biomass is down a lot. A contact spray kills fast, minutes to hours. A systemic insecticide (neonicotinoid, say) gets taken up by the plant and kills slowly, over days, and probably leaves insects disoriented and unable to feed for a while before they die. The numbers affected are astronomically larger than in any vertebrate case we'd ever litigate. Nobody disputes any of this. Nobody even disputes that if insects are sentient at all, this is where the moral mass is.

Where it jams: we tried to build a shared metric for comparing treatments — call it nociceptive exposure, roughly number of insects affected times how bad and how long the dying process is. One faction says this is the only honest way to compare, because treating a death as a death ignores everything that actually matters morally. The other faction says the metric is garbage, and they keep repeating three reasons: it assumes badness adds up across individuals in the first place, it assumes we can rank ways of dying with no evidence about what insect experience is like, and — the one I find hardest to answer — it books the poisoned larva's slow death as fresh disvalue when that larva was overwhelmingly likely to be eaten or desiccate anyway, so the marginal harm might be near zero or even negative.

And then there's a third thing that keeps blowing up meetings: some people quietly switch from individual deaths to population trajectories and argue we should just prefer whatever reduces insect numbers overall, which horrifies the conservationists in the room, who point out they're supposedly on the same side.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this isn't one disagreement but at least three. One is genuinely empirical and answerable — what happens to total insect deaths when you swap a fast spray for a slow systemic in a real rotation, including what gets substituted in and how much land use changes. One is about whether disvalue aggregates across very small minds. One is verbal, about what we even mean by suffering in an animal with a distributed nervous system and no obvious central整合. And I suspect the metric fight is mostly people arguing past each other about which unit of analysis counts: the insect, the death event, or the population.

So diagnose it. Which sub-disagreement is doing the real work in the stalemate? Where are the two factions using the same words for different things, and where is it a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for and shouldn't pretend otherwise? Use what the sides actually say as I've described them, be concrete, and don't hedge into "it's complicated." Then tell me what would count as progress for a group like ours — a distinction, a specific piece of data, a reformulation of the metric, scrapping the metric entirely — or make the case that we should expect none, and why, given who's in the room.
