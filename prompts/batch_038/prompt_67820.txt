Help me build a nonidentity case to spec. I want it for a very particular argument I keep losing.

The pitch I keep hearing, roughly: the nonidentity problem only bites if you cling to the person-affecting restriction; drop it, go impersonal, run the same-number quality-of-life comparison, and the whole puzzle dissolves. I want a case where it doesnt dissolve. So build me one where:

- the choice changes which particular people come to exist (standard nonidentity structure), and the two outcomes contain *different numbers* of people, so the same-number comparison never gets off the ground;
- every life in play is clearly worth living, and nobody who exists in either outcome is worse off in the actual outcome than they would have been in the alternative — do this check person by person and don't fudge it;
- the choice is still wrong, and obviously so. A first-year student reading it should feel the pull of the verdict without me telling them what to conclude;
- the wrongness isn't coming through any of the usual side doors: no harm, broken promise, or rights violation affecting anyone alive at the time of the choice; no "the kid would rationally prefer never to have been born"; no souls or possible people waiting in limbo;
- and the agent isn't wrong because she's reckless or vicious about something else. Her reason for acting is trivial and self-regarding but not independently bad, and she knows exactly what she's doing.

Realism constraints: no sci-fi, no time travel, no embryo selection, fertility clinics, or genetic screening — an ordinary person making an ordinary decision with ordinary information. And don't recycle Parfit's stock cases (the teenager who has the baby now instead of waiting a couple of years, the energy policy whose buried waste leaks centuries later). I want a new one.

Scenario first, one paragraph, about 150 words max. Then, separately:

(a) the two outcomes side by side — who exists in each, roughly how good each life is, and the person-by-person worse-off check;
(b) why the impersonal same-number principle is genuinely silent here rather than just inconvenient;
(c) the structural feature that's actually generating the wrongness, named precisely;
(d) what happens to the verdict if you delete that feature and hold everything else fixed;
(e) the best counterattack from the person I'm arguing with — they'll probably say I've smuggled the impersonal principle back in wearing a person-affecting hat — and whether that attack lands.

Be straight with me in (c) through (e). If some constraint makes the case impossible to build, say so and tell me which one has to go; a scenario that just asserts the verdict instead of producing it from the structure is worthless to me.

Last line, on its own, beginning "Verdict:" followed by the load-bearing feature from (c) in six words or fewer.
