And here's the student's answer, verbatim except that I've fixed a couple of formatting things:

> **(a)** Aumann's theorem, from his 1976 "Agreeing to Disagree," runs as follows. Two agents 1 and 2 share a state space Ω with a common prior P. Each agent's private information is represented by a partition of Ω — agent i's atom at ω is the finest thing he can pin down given what he observes. Fix an event A ⊆ Ω and define agent i's posterior at ω as the probability of A conditional on i's atom at ω. Then: if it is common knowledge between 1 and 2 that agent 1's posterior is x and agent 2's posterior is y, it follows that x = y. Two such agents cannot agree to disagree.
>
> The assumptions are: (i) a common prior P; (ii) Bayesian conditioning — each agent's posterior is just P(A | his atom); (iii) the information structure, i.e. both partitions, is common knowledge between them; (iv) the two posteriors on A are common knowledge at the state in question; (v) they are estimating the same event, and this last one matters more than people admit — A itself has to be common knowledge, otherwise the two numbers are not estimates of a shared proposition and comparing them is meaningless. The conclusion is a claim about the equality of two numbers at a state. It is not a claim about the long run, not a claim that they agree about anything else, and not a claim that either of them is right about A.
>
> **(b)** Let C be the event that 1's posterior is x and 2's posterior is y, and suppose C is common knowledge. Then any common component K of C — an atom of the meet of the two partitions, the finest common coarsening — is a union of 1's atoms and also a union of 2's atoms. Since 1's posterior is x throughout, P(A ∩ K) = x · P(K). Symmetrically P(A ∩ K) = y · P(K). As long as P(K) > 0 we get x = y. That double-counting of P(A ∩ K) is the whole engine, and it needs K to be a meet-atom, because only a meet-atom is simultaneously a union of 1's atoms and of 2's atoms.
>
> That said, I think the hypothesis is stronger than the argument really requires. You don't literally need all infinitely many levels. Mutual knowledge three or four levels down already hands you an event that is, for practical purposes, a union of atoms of both partitions, so the same double-counting goes through with a slightly larger K. Common knowledge is the limiting case of deep mutual knowledge — it's a notational idealization, not a separate ingredient. So the honest reading of the theorem is that it's about mutual knowledge sufficiently far down. Which means that when two forecasters publish their numbers in the same week and everyone reads the same outlets, the hypothesis is satisfied for all intents and purposes, and we should be surprised when they don't converge.
>
> **(c)** The columnist is wrong, but for a less dramatic reason than he imagines. The theorem is not a claim that rational agents never hold different beliefs; it's a claim about what follows when the *difference itself* is common knowledge. Two perfectly Bayesian economists with different information partitions will routinely land on different posteriors about a recession and violate nothing.
>
> Smuggled assumption 1: same published data = same total information. Their information sets include unshared stuff — a call with a logistics manager, an in-house nowcast the other hasn't seen, years of prior exposure to different datasets. Differing posteriors are exactly what the setup predicts when the partitions differ. The theorem doesn't say disagreement implies dishonesty; it says *commonly known* disagreement implies equal posteriors.
>
> Smuggled assumption 2: that the two posteriors are common knowledge. In the columnist's scene they are reading each other's published point forecasts, with a lag, rounded, and neither is certain the other has seen the latest revision. That's loose mutual knowledge of an approximation, not common knowledge of exact numbers, and the theorem simply doesn't engage.
>
> Smuggled assumption 3, half-hidden: the common prior. Two people trained in different traditions may not share one, and then the hypothesis fails at step (i) and no conclusion about either person's honesty is available.
>
> What survives: the intuition that *acknowledged* disagreement is unstable. If two Bayesians genuinely and truthfully exchange precise posteriors in public, repeatedly, they cannot stay apart, because at some point the fact of the gap becomes common knowledge and the double-counting in (b) bites. So the defensible slogan is weaker but still useful — sustained, mutually acknowledged, precisely specified disagreement is a diagnostic that something in the setup is failing: the common prior, the identity of the event, or the truthfulness of the announcements. What doesn't survive is the leap from "the diagnosis fails" to "one of them is lying." And note that once you've established both are Bayesian and honest, the size of the remaining gap tells you how much information they haven't yet pooled — a 45-point gap in the columnist's example means roughly 45 points' worth of unshared evidence, which is why disagreement spreads between markets are so informative.
