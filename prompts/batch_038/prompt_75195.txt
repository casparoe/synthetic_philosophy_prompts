I'm organizing a two-day workshop and I've kind of been volunteered to produce the document that two people in my department should have written a year ago instead of sniping at each other in seminars. One is a diplomatic and military historian who works in counterfactuals and holds something like the for-want-of-a-nail view: small events, individual decisions, near misses, and timing genuinely bend the long-run trajectory, and historians who deny that are just confusing narrative convenience with causation. The other is a cliometric economic historian, institutions-and-demography person, who thinks outcomes are massively overdetermined — geography, disease environment, agrarian productivity, fiscal-military capacity, elite coalitions — and that talk of contingency is mostly an artifact of hindsight plus the fact that we only ever observe one run of history. They've agreed to co-sign whatever we hand them, which is the only reason I'm asking.

So: draft the adversarial-collaboration statement they could both actually put their names to. I want the claims both can sign (about what the sources license, about what a counterfactual claim is even asserting, about where each side thinks the other has a point); then the residual disagreements, each one worded so that its own side would accept the wording as fair — that's the hard part, please don't collapse them into a mush both sides find blandly true; then the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one of them. Two things I'd like built in. First, I want them to try stating their disagreement in a shared dynamical vocabulary — size of the perturbation relative to the basin, recovery time after a shock, whether a given event pushes the system out of its basin or just re-routes it inside — and then to report honestly where that vocabulary breaks down or smuggles in one side's assumptions: is the state space even well defined for a society, and does the structuralist's willingness to speak this language concede half the argument? Second, please seperate out level-relativity explicitly: I suspect a big chunk of the fight is that one of them is talking about contingency in who, when, and by what route, and the other about contingency in the macro outcome decades later, and those aren't the same claim. Where you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say so and say which part.

For the cruxes, seed them with the three cases these two have been fighting about all year, and for each one get both sides' predictions logged in advance rather than argued after the fact: (a) the Mongol withdrawal from Hungary in the spring of 1242, where one side says it's the news of Ögedei's death and the succession politics that pulled Batu east, and the other says the army had already hit its logistical ceiling — depleted pasture, stone fortifications it couldn't take, siege specialists lost — and the khan's death just gave a retreat that was coming anyway a convenient date; (b) the July Crisis of 1914, and whether the sequence of decisions in those weeks was load-bearing or whether the alliance system and mobilization timetables made a great war a near-certainty within a few years regardless; (c) the shots fired at the Miami rally in February 1933 that missed FDR-elect and killed Chicago's mayor Anton Cermak instead — the one case where the contingentist thinks he has a clean individual-level hinge and the structuralist thinks the New Deal coalition was going to happen with or without that particular man in the White House. For each case I want what evidence or argument both would accept as bearing on the crux, what each predicts, and what each would count as disconfirming their own general view. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with a column for each side's own preferred wording, then the cruxes with the cases and predictions underneath. And if you think one of the two positions is unfalsifiable as stated, put that in the document as a finding rather than hiding it.
