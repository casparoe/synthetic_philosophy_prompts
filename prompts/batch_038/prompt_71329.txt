For thirty-five years I worked on the statistical mechanics of phase transitions and, latterly, on the kinetics of reacting systems. Since retiring I have read philosophy with increasing seriousness but no formal training, and for the past four years I have belonged to a reading group of perhaps a dozen people — two philosophers, an economist, three sociologists, a historian, and several amateurs like myself. Twice monthly we meet; for the last two of those years we have been circling the same problem: whether social explanation must ultimately be grounded in claims about individual agents, or whether wholes can carry explanatory weight that no distribution of individual properties can carry. We have made no progress whatever, and I have come to suspect that the reason is structural rather than a matter of stubbornness.

I should like a diagnosis of the impasse, not a survey of the two camps. To make the diagnosis concrete, here are the three claims I have distilled from my colleagues; please treat them as the primary material and test your conclusions against them.

First, a sociologist, whom I shall call A, maintains that certain macro-level regularities — the stubborn persistence of residential segregation, the intergenerational reproduction of educational inequality — are neither the sum nor the intention of anyone's choices, and that an account told exclusively in the vocabulary of individual beliefs and incentives systematically obscures the causal structure it purports to explain. Second, an economist, B, holds that an explanation which never descends to agents, their information, and their incentives is a promissory note rather than an explanation: it may be cashed out eventually, but until it is, it licenses no prediction and no intervention, and it protects itself from refutation by vagueness. Third, a philosopher, C, argues that A and B are not in fact contradicting one another, because B's demand is a semantic or methodological requirement disguised as an ontological thesis, and that the only genuine question is whether collectives are indispensable posits of our best social theories.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and entirely open to rejection, is that C is half right: the group has been running together three separable questions — what exists, whether macro-level generalisations can be autonomous yet correct, and whether a good social explanation must be translatable into individual-level statements — and has been winning arguments on one question by importing its answer from another. I have further proposed to the group a criterion of my own devising, which I call the derivability-with-limits test: a macro-level claim is explanatorily autonomous just when, for agents with our computational and evidential limitations, eliminating the macro vocabulary destroys either the generalisability of the explanation or our capacity to act on it, even where the derivation from micro-level facts exists in principle. I would like you to say bluntly whether such a test would settle the social case or merely relocate the dispute, and if the latter, to say where it relocates to.

Please also assess the analogy I have been pressing on the group from my own field. Thermodynamic descriptions are in principle derivable from microscopic dynamics, yet nobody supposes that temperature or critical exponents are eliminable from practice; the derivation is often unavailable, and where available it is not illuminating. Tell me whether this parallel genuinely carries over to societies, or whether it misleads — societies lack fixed micro-laws, their constituents read and react to the theories formulated about them, and their boundaries are not given by nature. I want to know precisely where, if anywhere, the analogy breaks, and whether its breakdown favours A or B.

Finally, consider two cases and tell me whether either could adjudicate. In the first, a macro pattern of segregation emerges from individually mild preferences, with no collective agent and no collective intent. In the second, a banking panic or a currency run, the macro-level belief alters the individual incentives that generated it, so that the whole appears to feed back on its parts. Does either case dissolve the disagreement, or does each get claimed by both sides — and if both, what does that tell us about the nature of the stalemate?

Deliverables, in this order: a verdict on what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, a clash of evidential standards, a divergence about which cases count as data, bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or two questions answered under one heading — supported by what A, B, and C actually say; an assessment of my derivability-with-limits test; a statement of what each of A, B, and C would have to surrender in order to be wrong, which I regard as the sharpest available probe of whether their claims are genuine claims; and an account of what would count as progress, whether a distinction, a case study, a formal result of some kind, or a reframing of the question, together with an honest judgement if you think no progress should be expected and why. Close with a short paragraph advising how a group of this composition might restructure its agenda over its next six meetings.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, no attributions of doctrines to named authors: I want argument, and I have no means of checking references. Second, do not soften your conclusions out of courtesy to my hypothesis; if you think my tripartite diagnosis is itself a way of evading a real substantive disagreement, say so and argue for it. Roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, in clear prose rather than bullet points, though you may use numbered sections.
