I'm a backend engineer (mostly Go, no philosophy coursework past a required freshman thing I barely remember) and I've somehow ended up as one of seven voting members on my company's data governance board. It sounds like a compliance rubber stamp but we actually have teeth — we can block a release. Here's the case we're stuck on, and I want you to draft the memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting.

Our internal standard has a clause, section 4.3, that reads: "When a customer closes their account, all personal data associated with that account must be deleted from our systems within 30 days." That clause has been applied maybe 40 times over four years and every single one of those cases was about data you could point at: rows in the production DB, log lines, nightly backups aging out on rotation, CSV exports sitting at a subprocessor. Nobody ever argued about it, it just got done. Now a team has been training a churn model on historical customer records, including free-text transaction notes, and that model is in production. When accounts close we delete the rows like always, but the closed accounts' data is still in the weights in some sense — security ran a membership-inference probe against the deployed model and got a real signal on a sample of closed accounts, so its not just a thought experiment. There's also a derived feature store with aggregates that include closed accounts' activity.

Two members have dug in and I genuinely can't tell who's right. Dana (legal) says 4.3 obviously reaches this: the point of deletion is that a person's data stops shaping how we treat them, and here it demonstrably does, so a reading that lets weights and feature stores off the hook turns the clause into a joke and wouldn't survive a regulator keeping a straight face. Raj (staff engineer, was adjacent to the drafting team) says the opposite: what the rule means is fixed by how it's been used, and every past application plus everything the drafters would say is equally consistent with "applies to stores you can query" and with "applies to anything carrying information about the person," so there is no fact of the matter about which one 4.3 has meant all along. On his view we aren't interpreting 4.3 on Tuesday, we're writing a new rule and then pretending we found it inside the old one, and he'd rather we admit that and just vote. He also has a move I can't answer: the original author left the company, we emailed her, she said "of course trained models count," and Raj says that's just her making one more application of the rule today, not evidence about what it already meant — her intention has to be interpreted too, same as the text. My roommate did a philosophy minor and says this is the Kripkenstein rule-following problem, that Raj is technically right, and that this somehow isn't a problem in practice, which I find both plausible and completely useless at 9am on a Tuesday.

So: can you write the memo? I need a bottom line the board can actually vote on — either "4.3 covers model weights and derived features, here's the compliance path and the deadline," or "4.3 doesn't reach this and we should adopt a new 4.3bis," or something better that I haven't thought of. Give a concrete timeline, given that a full retrain excluding closed accounts is about six weeks and $200k in compute and our audit is five months out. Give us criteria we can apply to the next version of this fight — caches, embeddings we get back from a third-party vendor's model, a fine-tune we ship to customers. And engage Raj for real: I don't want the memo to swat him with a "spirit of the rule" flourish, because if he's right that there's nothing there to discover, then the memo should say what we're actually doing when we decide, and why that's still legitimate rather than just the majority flexing. Finally, flag where you're most likely to be wrong: which argument would flip you from Dana's side to Raj's or back, and what in this reasoning you'd want a real philosopher or a real regulator to check before we vote on it.
