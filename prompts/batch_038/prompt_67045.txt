I'm drafting a paper for an applied ethics seminar arguing that the lying/misleading distinction does not track moral severity, and my supervisor's objection to every case I've produced so far is that they smuggle in an extra wrong-making feature: my misleader is always a doctor or a trustee, or is trading on a hearer's inattention. So I'd like you to build me a case to specification. In it, one speaker tells a straightforward lie and another speaker, in a comparable position, brings a hearer to the very same false belief using only sentences he believes to be true — and the second speaker's conduct is the more serious wrong.

Constraints: (1) both speakers have identical information, identical motive, and the same live alternative of simply saying nothing; (2) both hearers are equally attentive, competent, and disposed to check what they're told; (3) the misleader occupies no special role — no clinician, lawyer, fiduciary, or official — and exploits no vulnerability the liar doesn't equally exploit; (4) the lie is a standard assertion of something the speaker believes false, and both hearers accept what they're told; (5) ordinary realistic setting — a property sale, a job reference, a newsroom, a used-car lot — one paragraph, no exotic machinery; (6) the additional seriousness must come from something structural about getting a false belief across by means of truths, not from any extra harm, extra victim, or nastier character.

Then show the case works rather than asserting it. Tell me what a view on which lying is the paradigmatic violation of a duty to communicate one's actual mind says about each speaker, and whether it has any resources for ranking them; what an assurance- or expectation-based account says, and in particular whether the liar's having explicitly offered an assurance counts against him or whether the misleader's conscription of the hearer's own inferential work counts against him instead; and what a purely harm-and-outcome account says, including whether it can see any difference at all given that the beliefs and consequences are matched. Finish with an honest audit: which single feature of your scenario is doing the work in reversing the ordinary ranking, whether the case collapses if I remove that feature, and how a defender of the position that lying is always at least as wrong as misleading would attack it — including whether their best move is to deny that one of the speakers lied, to deny the symmetry I've stipulated, or to accept the verdict and revise the theory's claims. Note that the case is meant to generate the ranking from the theories themselves, so if the intuition comes mainly from how you've described the two speakers as people, the exercise has failed.
