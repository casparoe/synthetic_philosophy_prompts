You are an expert in formal decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice, with serious training in the axiomatic foundations of expected utility and in the design and interpretation of laboratory choice experiments. Think carefully and do the arithmetic yourself before you write a word of dialogue.

Below is a fully specified study. Your job is to write a dialogue between two researchers who disagree about what it shows and who are arguing over the discussion section of their joint paper.

**THE STUDY**

All amounts are real money, paid at the end of the session. Subjects are graduate students in statistics, economics, and mathematics; every subject has previously passed a comprehension check on compound lotteries. All choices are made on a screen that displays each act as a fully reduced probability distribution over final payoffs, so nobody is choosing between a description and a description.

*Problem 1.* Choose one:
- **Act A:** $9,000 for certain.
- **Act B:** a 50% chance of $20,000 and a 50% chance of $4,000.

Observed: 68% chose A.

*Problem 2.* A separate group faces the following. In both acts a fair coin is flipped first. On tails, the subject is entered into **Lottery Z**, which pays $2,000 with probability 1/2 and $0 with probability 1/2 (a second, independent flip). On heads, the subject receives Act A or Act B respectively. Reduced to final distributions:
- **Act A′:** 50% chance of $9,000; 25% chance of $2,000; 25% chance of $0.
- **Act B′:** 25% chance of $20,000; 25% chance of $4,000; 25% chance of $2,000; 25% chance of $0.

Observed: 61% chose B′.

Note that A′ and B′ differ only in the heads branch: the tails branch, Lottery Z, is literally the same in both.

*Follow-up condition.* A third group faces the sequential version: the coin is flipped first and, if it lands tails, Z is resolved and paid and displayed on screen; only on heads is the subject then asked to choose between A and B. Among the subjects in the second group who chose B′ and who, in Problem 1, chose A:
- 72% of them choose A again when they reach the heads node.
- They are then shown, on screen, the algebra establishing that choosing A at that node means they have, in effect, selected A′ rather than B′, and that this costs them $1,500 in expectation.
- After seeing this, 44% revise their Problem 2 answer to A′; 56% keep B′. Several of those who keep it say that the sequential situation and the compound situation are not the same choice.

*Debriefing condition.* In a fourth group, subjects are shown both problems together with the demonstration that A′ is a 50/50 mixture of A with Z and B′ is the same mixture of B with Z, before choosing. The 68%/61% pattern falls to 55%/58% but does not disappear.

**THE TWO CHARACTERS**

**Dr. Miriam Haldane** holds that the independence axiom is constitutive of rational choice under risk, not a mere descriptive generalization, and that the observed pattern is therefore evidence of error somewhere — in how the choice situations are individuated, in how the acts are described, or in the subjects' reasoning — rather than evidence against the axiom. She has the resources of expected utility theory, of statewise dominance reasoning, and of dynamic-consistency arguments at her disposal, and she is willing to use them aggressively.

**Dr. Emeka Nwosu** holds that the pattern is not an artifact but a real normative finding: that expected utility theory systematically misdescribes rational risk-taking because it cannot represent the value of a guaranteed outcome, and that the subjects who choose A and B′ are not confused. He is not a psychologist reporting a bias; he thinks independence is false as a principle of rationality, and he has answers to the dynamic-consistency objection.

**THE TASK**

*Part One — preparation (no more than 300 words, written before the dialogue, in your own voice).* Confirm, with explicit calculations, whether the observed pattern A together with B′ is representable by any expected-utility preference with any utility function over the payoffs $0, $2,000, $4,000, $9,000, and $20,000. Show the cancellation. Note in particular whether the conclusion depends on normalizing u(0) to zero. Then list three distinct lines of attack Haldane could press and three Nwosu could press, and mark which ones you judge weakest and why. Use only the strongest material in the dialogue.

*Part Two — the dialogue (1,200 to 1,800 words, at least sixteen turns).* Write the argument between Haldane and Nwosu as they draft the discussion section.

**REQUIREMENTS**

1. Neither character is a strawman. Each must at least once make a move that genuinely embarrasses the other, and each must concede at least one point, stating explicitly what is being conceded and why it does not settle the matter.
2. Real back-and-forth: every turn should respond to something the previous speaker actually said. No character may deliver a prepared lecture that the other simply ignores. If a character raises an objection, the other must engage with that objection rather than with a nearby one.
3. At least one extended exchange must consist of the two of them working through the mixture arithmetic out loud and agreeing on what it proves, and then disagreeing sharply about what follows from it.
4. The follow-up condition must be fought over in detail. Haldane should press the dynamic-consistency and money-pump considerations; Nwosu should press the claim that the sequential and the compound presentations are not the same object of choice, and should explain what makes that claim principled rather than an ad hoc escape. Neither may simply assert their position. The 44%/56% split after the algebra is shown must be interpreted by both, and they must interpret it differently.
5. Both characters must at some point address the debriefing condition, since it removes the simplest explanations. Neither may treat it as decisive without saying why.
6. Do not have either character cite books, papers, authors, or a reading list, and do not produce a bibliography. They may name well-known positions and tools — the independence axiom, the sure-thing principle, the certainty effect, rank-dependent or non-expected utility models, dynamic consistency, sophisticated choice, preference for commitment — but every argument must be carried by reasoning from the stipulated data and from first principles, never by appeal to authority.
7. If either character introduces a new act, lottery, or variation on the study to make a point, state it completely: all outcomes and all probabilities. Do not gesture at a hypothetical experiment you have not specified.
8. Keep the prose plain and argumentative. Stage directions, if any, should be minimal and should not carry content. Do not insert narration between turns summarizing what has just been established.
9. The dialogue must not end in agreement, must not convert either side, and must not dissolve into a vague mutual tolerance. It should end with the two of them identifying the single further experiment or the single premise whose resolution would decide the dispute, and disagreeing about whether that experiment or that premise is available even in principle. Make the final exchange precise enough that a reader could say exactly what would count as settling it.
