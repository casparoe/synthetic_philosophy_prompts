I spent 35 years running a hydrology lab, so I have a professional habit of attaching an error bar to every number I write down. Since retiring I've been volunteering on our county conservation commission's appeals panel, and I've taken up reading philosophy in the evenings (my wife says I've just swapped one hobby involving graphs for another) mostly because of something that bugs me about this volunteer work. The ordinance we enforce says a landowner can't cause "significant hydrological alteration" to a protected seasonal wetland, and there is no error bar on the word significant. Last month we heard a guy who filled in about a tenth of an acre of a vernal pool on his parcel, and two of us spent an hour going back and forth with nothing new to say on either side. It's worse because three years ago we let a nearly identical fill through on a different parcel, and nobody on the panel can say whether that earlier decision was right, or just ours. That dragged me into the vagueness literature, specifically the fight between epistemicism, where there really is a sharp cutoff but nobody can know where it falls, and supervaluationism, where in a genuinely borderline case there's no fact of the matter either way even though it's still true that the alteration either is significant or isn't.

What I want to know is whether any of that abstract business should actually change how a five-person volunteer panel does its work. So give me the five most important implications of the epistemicism-versus-supervaluationism debate for a panel like ours deciding cases under a vague standard, ordered from most important to least. For each item, say which of the two views drives it (or whether both do) and what we would concretely do differently on a Tuesday night in the county annex. One bit of seeding you can use or throw out: I've invented my own homegrown distinction, because I couldn't find a term for it. I call a ruling "finding the line" when the panel is trying to report where the line already sits, and "pouring the line" when the ruling itself is what fixes where the line sits from then on. Also, if for one of the five the honest answer is that the two views give us exactly the same advice, say that flat out inside the item rather than dressing up a non-difference as a difference; I've read enough philosophy by now to know that's the standard dodge, and I definately don't want a survey.

Two ground rules. Don't point me at anything to read, since I lost my university library card and won't chase down references, so the reasoning has to stand entirely on its own. And please write in American English, because alot of what I've been reading is British and my own spelling is starting to drift. Format: exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, tight, with no introduction and no conclusion, and nothing at all before item 1 or after item 5.
