Thirty-seven years I spent doing process control on refineries — cascade loops, PID tuning, the whole business of making a plant track a setpoint it never actually reaches — and since I retired I've been working through philosophy of mind, mostly because I can't stop thinking about the animal welfare question and someone pointed me at the machine version of it. My son writes software and he has two favourite ways of explaining a reinforcement learning agent to me. One is from my own trade: the reward signal is just the error signal, the policy is the controller, and reward hacking is what happens when you tune hard against a proxy variable instead of the real process measurement, which he says is basically Goodhart with a graphics card. The other is the old rat-and-lever story from the fifties, the animal with an electrode in a so-called pleasure centre pressing a bar tens of thousands of times an hour, ignoring the food next to it until it drops. It's that second one I want picked apart.

Here's the thing that's bugging me. The same rat gets waved around in print to support two opposite conclusions about whether a reward-chasing system could have anything going on inside worth caring about. One camp says look at the rat: it is plainly in the grip of something, it will trade away everything else for it, and if you built a machine whose whole behavioural economy ran on one signal like that you'd be building something capable of being trapped — so reward signals are at least candidates for valence. The other camp says the exact opposite from the exact same animal: the "pleasure centre" picture was picked apart decades ago, the stimulation was driving the wanting machinery and not necessarily any liking, and that's why the rat starves with the bar under its paw — so the analogy shows a reward signal can be a pure behavioural driver with nothing felt on the inside, and the welfare worry is sentimentality. A guy in my reading group, retired psychopharmacologist, swears the analogy was already broken before anyone applied it to software. I half suspect both of them are leaning on the rat to smuggle something in, and I have my own list of candidate disanalogies that I can't rank: the rat could stop pressing and doesn't, whereas the agent's policy was built by training so it has no more option to stop than my controller had an option to stop integrating error; the rat's electrode is parasitic on an evolved system that already had valence in it, whereas for the agent the reward signal isn't parasitic on anything, it's the whole show; and the rat loses something visible — food, sleep, its body — while I can't point to any seperate good the agent is forgoing, which makes me worry the "reward isn't welfare" move has nothing to bite on in the machine case.

So: audit the rat for me. Give the correspondence explicitly (lever, electrode, the bundle itself, the food it ignores, the experimenter, the collapse — what plays each role in the RL agent), then seperate the disanalogies that actually bear on the suffering question from the ones that are just decoration, and tell me whether either conclusion survives once they're on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying both of them all along and can't carry either. If there's a better analogy, propose one — feel free to raid control engineering, or don't, I'm not attached to my own field. No references or reading lists please, I can't chase those down and I don't need them. Answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, nothing before or after the list: (1) the correspondence, (2) the disanalogy that does the real damage, (3) a disanalogy that looks fatal but isn't, (4) what survives of each camp's conclusion, (5) your better analogy plus, in the same breath, how confident you are in this whole audit and which of the five you'd trust least.
