I teach a cross-listed graduate seminar this term on minds, machines, and infinity, and we open with the first chapter of Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, where the reader is handed the MIU-system: a single axiom MI, and four purely typographic rules — append a U to any theorem already ending in I; duplicate the whole string following the M; replace any occurrence of three consecutive I's with a U; delete any occurrence of two consecutive U's. The assignment is to derive MU. It cannot be done, and the reason is not exhaustion of tries but an invariant the system itself never names: the number of I's in any theorem is never divisible by three, so MU, with zero I's, lies outside the reachable set. Hofstadter's moral is that seeing this requires stepping back from symbol-shuffling to a property defined on the strings rather than inside them — the maneuver he calls jumping out of the system, and the one thing, he suggests, that a merely mechanical reasoner seems constitutionally unable to do; though he is candid that the jump is itself just another mechanical procedure performed at a higher level, which cannot see its own limits either.

For the second week I have assigned Borges's "The Library of Babel" (1941, later folded into Ficciones), and I want the two read against each other with some rigor. Consider what the story supplies: an orthography of exactly twenty-five symbols, books of uniform format running to 410 pages, hexagonal galleries repeating indefinitely, and the axiom the inhabitants hold — that the Library is total, that it contains every book those symbols and pages permit, which is to say almost nothing but gibberish alongside a handful of lucid volumes. Around that axiom the story generates its sects and its hopes: the Purifiers wandering in search of the Crimson Hexagon, the belief that some single volume is the compendium and cipher of all the rest, and that some librarian has read it and become the Man of the Book; the report that certain librarians abhor the superstitious habit of hunting for meaning in books at all; and the closing image of the Library as unlimited and cyclical, outlasting the species that populates it. A colleague in Spanish warns me that Borges is not writing about logic and that this pairing will embarrass us. I want the reading to be good enough to survive that objection.

So please write the reading, and organize it as exactly five numbered items with the following roles, two or three sentences each, with no introduction, no conclusion, no quotation from either text (paraphrase only), and no references or further reading: (1) the strongest version of the case that the Library functions as a formal system and its inhabitants are trapped inside it in precisely Hofstadter's sense; (2) the moment in the story where that reading does its sharpest work, saying what it explains that a purely thematic reading of the same passage does not; (3) the point at which the story resists or embarrasses the analogy — where the honest critic has to concede that Borges's impossibility is of a different kind from the MU-puzzle's, since a total catalogue is merely unfound rather than formally underivable, and saying how much of the reading survives that concession; (4) what the story shows about the regress Hofstadter only gestures at — whether the narrator's own totalizing vision of the Library is itself a jump out of the system, and whether jumping out of Babel simply lands one in another gallery; (5) your verdict on what Borges contributes to the debate about mechanical reason and levels that argument alone cannot supply, and whether you would assign the pairing to my students knowing they will blur the two notions of impossibility unless the reading keeps them apart.
