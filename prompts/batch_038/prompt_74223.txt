**The question as set:**

> In *I Am a Strange Loop*, Hofstadter holds that a single human self is not confined to the skull in which it arose, but is distributed, in varying degrees, across the brains of those who have known it. Explain this claim. Your answer should address: (i) what kind of thing a self is on Hofstadter's account, and why he thinks talk of selves should be taken literally rather than as poetic; (ii) in what sense distribution is a matter of degree, and what fixes where a given case falls on that scale; (iii) what, if anything, the claim implies about whether death is a misfortune for the person who dies, and about the rationality of grief.

**The student's answer:**

> (1) Hofstadter's starting point is that an "I" is not a thing but a pattern. In *Gödel, Escher, Bach* he argued that a mind stands to a brain as a colony's mentality stands to its ants: no individual neuron has anything resembling a self, yet at a higher level of description a stable, self-referential structure appears, which he calls a strange loop — a loop that crosses levels, so that the high-level description feeds back into and governs the lower-level activity that realises it. The self is that loop: the brain's model of itself, which, because it sits inside what it models, ends up modelling itself modelling. Because such a pattern is real in the same way a whirlpool or a colony's foraging strategy is real — it persists, it makes a difference to what happens next, and it can be described without constant reference to the particulars carrying it at a given moment — talk of selves is not polite fiction. Hofstadter is no eliminativist; on his account the illusion is one that is genuinely there.
>
> (2) This is also what makes distribution across brains possible, and the ant colony does the real work here. Once we accept that what matters for the existence of a mentality is organisation rather than the stuff carrying it, there is nothing special about the boundary of a skull. The very same higher-level pattern realised by billions of neurons in one head can be realised, less completely, by the neural activity of another head that has spent years tracking the first person's reactions, imitating their turns of phrase and predicting what they will say next. Hofstadter's own case is his wife Carol, who died in 1993: he describes finding her habits and her ways of seeing things still running in him afterwards, and insists this is literally so — a matter of her pattern being instantiated in his brain — rather than a bereaved man's way of speaking. A self, then, is a pattern capable of several partial instantiations at once, in different substrates, none of them complete.
>
> (3) Distribution comes in degrees because instantiations have fidelity. What fixes the scale is how much and how long one person has been exposed to another, how finely they can predict and reproduce the other's responses, and how much of the other's internal structure, as opposed to their outward behaviour, has been absorbed. A long-married partner sits high, a casual acquaintance low, and a reader who never met the author lower still — though Hofstadter thinks a book can carry an attenuated version of its author's pattern into heads the author will never enter. Nothing is either wholly present or wholly absent, so "is X's self in my head?" is the wrong shape of question; the right question is how much of it, and at what resolution.
>
> (4) This should be separated from the extended mind thesis. Clark and Chalmers argue that when someone relies on a notebook in the right way, the notebook becomes part of the machinery of that person's own ongoing cognition: the boundary of the self moves outward, but there is still one process, in one life. Hofstadter's claim runs on a different axis. It is not that my cognition leaks into your skull and your artefacts, but that one pattern admits of multiple simultaneous low-resolution instances, most of which will outlive the highest-resolution one. Extended mind relocates the vehicle of a process; Hofstadter multiplies the instances of a type.
>
> (5) The natural way to state the resulting metaphysics is Parfit's. If a self is a pattern that can be instantiated to greater or lesser degree in more than one place, then identity in the strict sense cannot be what matters, and cases of distribution are really fission cases: the pattern branches, and since a branching relation cannot be one-to-one, whether the person in the second brain is numerically identical with the person in the first has no determinate answer. Parfit concluded that identity is not what matters in survival; Hofstadter's Carol material makes the same point from the other direction, namely that what matters is the degree to which the pattern continues, wherever it continues. On this reading, whether Carol is still around is indeterminate rather than tragic or not.
>
> (6) It follows that death is a lesser misfortune than we suppose, and in proportion: the more widely and richly a person's pattern is instantiated in others, the less is lost when the original instance stops. Grief, on this account, rests on a false premise, since the bereaved mourn the disappearance of something that has not disappeared but been redistributed at lower resolution. Hofstadter's own grief is then best read as his intellect failing to catch up with his metaphysics.
