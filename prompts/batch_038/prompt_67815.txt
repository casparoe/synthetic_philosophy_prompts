I write novels, which is to say I make my living inventing people who never existed, and I suspect that is precisely why the chair of the grants committee at the small private foundation where I serve thought I would be useful. There are seven of us: two neuroscientists, two machine-learning researchers, a large-animal veterinarian who does welfare consulting, a bioethicist, the chair, and me. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before our spring meeting, and I want it good enough that the two members who will disagree with me can answer it rather than dismiss it.

Here is the case. A research institute has applied for a three-year grant of roughly $1.4 million for what its proposal calls an affective dynamics program. In outline: some five thousand separate reinforcement-learning agents will be trained in a rich, partially observable environment. The detail that split our committee is that most of the signal these agents chase is not handed to them by the environment at all. Each agent carries a learned model of what to expect, and the quantity it optimizes is largely the discrepancy between what happened and what it had predicted. A second module penalizes the agent for arriving at situations its own model finds surprising, and a third gives a small positive increment for reducing that surprise over time. The consequence is that a great many of these agents will spend their entire training lives being pushed around by numbers generated internally: the world itself is close to neutral, and what the agent has, if that phrase is even admissible here, is a long unbroken stream of things turning out worse than it expected, punctuated by relief. The principal investigator is candid that he does not believe any of it is felt, and he has nonetheless proposed a welfare protocol — a pause threshold that fires if the share of episodes ending in an extreme negative discrepancy exceeds a set value, a ceiling on the duration of any single run, and an annual public accounting of how often the thresholds were triggered. Our veterinarian says that protocol is either meaningless theater or a quiet admission of the very thing its author denies. Our two machine-learning members say that suspending a training run because a scalar got large is a category error, and that paying for it dignifies a confusion they would rather see named and buried. There is also an opportunity cost: the same $1.4 million is the second-ranked proposal in this cycle, behind a two-year study of stunning methods in two cephalopod fisheries, which our veterinarian calls urgent, unglamorous, and long overdue. One further wrinkle that nobody has yet handled: the five thousand agents are near-copies of one another, and a subset are repeatedly resumed from saved states, so an identical bad condition will recur in hundreds of nearly indistinguishable systems, some of them continuations of the same earlier run.

Please draft the memo I will put in front of the committee. It should take a definite position — fund, fund with conditions, or decline — and it should be explicit about what would have to be true for an internal, expectation-relative discrepancy signal to count as valence rather than as bookkeeping, and about what evidence would count against that reading; I do not want the question dissolved by appealing to the fact that the two systems are organized in similar ways. It should say whether the proposed protocol changes anything at all: does a laboratory behaving as if there might be suffering generate information, or only manage reputational risk? It should take a position on the duplication problem, even a provisional one, without pretending to settle the metaphysics of identity. And it should address the cephalopod money head-on, since a decision under uncertainty about moral status is exactly what forces that comparison. Treat the strongest case against your recommendation honestly and in its best form, not as a straw figure. Finally, because the chair has asked whether we can reuse this reasoning, give the committee two or three criteria a reviewer could apply to the next proposal of this kind, and attach a concrete bottom line — conditions, milestones, and a trigger for revisiting the decision. Aim for something in the neighborhood of a thousand words, in plain prose that a reader with no philosophy behind them can follow; I have spent fifteen years learning that a sentence nobody understands has not, in fact, said anything. No footnotes, bibliography, or list of sources, please — I want the argument, not the apparatus.
