Been chewing on Dennett's free-floating rationale idea for a while now — the claim that a reason can belong to no reasoner, so that when we say a woodpecker's skull is built the way it is because that was good for something, no beliefs or representations are doing any of the work. I find it attractive, but I've got a case I can't get a handle on, and what I want is your credence on the claim below plus the decomposition, not a survey of who's said what.

The case. A tiny engineering firm builds a device in the 1970s for a customer who goes under a year later. The thing gets installed in a basement and is still running, still doing precisely what it was made to do. Everyone who designed it is dead. The one document recording why they made the choices they made sits on an encrypted drive whose key was destroyed — not misplaced, provably unrecoverable, so nobody ever will represent that reasoning. And it can't be reverse-engineered from the outside either: the industrial process it served is extinct, and to any observer it's just a box that hums and rocks a lever back and forth. So all three routes to the rationale are closed — no surviving ascribers who knew, no records, nothing legible in the behavior.

The claim, call it STRANDED: there is still a determinate fact of the matter about why this device has the specific features it has rather than others — a rationale that holds even though nothing now, and nothing ever again, could represent it, and that isn't merely a fact about what some actual or hypothetical ascriber would be disposed to say.

My own credence is around .65 and I suspect thats mostly inherited from finding Dennett stylistically persuasive rather than from any argument, so part of what I want is a check on that.

Give me:

- the sub-questions STRANDED actually breaks into. My guesses: whether function ascriptions are observer-relative or grounded in historical etiology; whether an etiological because is a real explanatory relation or just tidy bookkeeping about causal history; whether determinacy with no possible access is even coherent — a fact nobody could ever get right, and what distinguishes it from there being no fact; whether "reason" at this level is the same notion as personal-level reasons or a convenient homonym; whether artifacts and organisms should get the same treatment. Add, cut, or reorder as you see fit — I'd rather have your list than mine.
- a rough probability and one line of justification for each term
- how they combine into the headline number — multiply, take the minimum, weight them, whatever you think is actually right, and say why that rule
- which single term the number is most sensitive to, and roughly how far the headline moves if you flip it
- which of the sub-questions, if any, you think is secretly verbal, and what you'd have to stipulate to make it substantive
- the one argument or piece of evidence that would move you most, in either direction

And at the end: do you land in the same place on the biological case — the woodpecker's skull — as you do on this device? If your two answers differ, tell me what's doing the work of separating them.
