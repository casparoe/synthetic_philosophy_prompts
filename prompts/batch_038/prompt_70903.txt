Here is the argument. It is my own reconstruction of the case against judicial review, assembled for a seminar, and I have come to suspect that it proves far too much.

(1) An exercise of political power is legitimate only if it can be traced to the judgment of those over whom it is exercised.
(2) In a large modern state, the only collective judgment to which power can be traced in that way is the judgment of a present majority of the electorate, expressed through officials that majority can remove.
(3) When a court invalidates a statute enacted by such a legislature, the resulting settlement of the question is traceable to officials the electorate cannot remove, and not to the present majority.
(4) Therefore judicial review of legislation is illegitimate. (This is Bickel's counter-majoritarian difficulty, stated as a validity condition rather than as a regrettable feature.)
(5) Nothing in (1) through (3) mentions courts except incidentially: what does the work is that the settling judgment is not the present majority's.
(6) But entrenched constraints of every kind displace the present majority's judgment — bicameralism and the supermajorities required to amend, the federal division of powers, fixed terms and staggered elections that let a defeated faction keep governing for a while, an independent central bank, the formal requirements an enactment must satisfy to be a statute, and, decisively, the rule that identifies which enactments count as valid law at all.
(7) So by (1) through (5) every one of these is illegitimate, and the only legitimate arrangement is one in which a bare present majority may do anything at once, including abolishing the offices and procedures by which its own enactments become law.
(8) Yet the legislature whose judgment (3) says the court displaces is itself a creature of those procedures: it can make law at all only because the entrenched rules confer that capacity on it. The argument's target cannot be described except by presupposing the legitimacy of the entrenchment that (6) and (7) condemn.
(9) Therefore at least one of (1) through (3) is false, and (4) cannot be supported by this route.

Two things about my own stance, so you know what I am trying to protect. I want to keep (1); a legitimacy principle with no connection whatever to the governed strikes me as indefensible, and giving it up feels like conceding the entire question. I would also like to keep something like (4) at least as a presumption — a reason to want review to be narrow, or to be justified specially — which is precisely why (8) unsettles me, since it appears to show the argument is not merely unsound but self-defeating.

(b) I suspect that two distinct slides are being run together under one label. The first is temporal: (1) speaks of the judgment of the governed, which might be the judgment of a people extended over time, while (2) silently narrows this to a present majority. The second is about agency: (3) says the court's judgment displaces the majority's, but a court applying an entrenched standard may be transmitting someone else's judgment rather than substituting its own. Tell me whether these are two independent ways of blocking the chain, whether each is available to someone who wants to preserve (4) as a presumption, and whether either collapses when pressed.

(c) Price the exits. For each of the following, state exactly what must be given up and how expensive the giving-up is, and say whether it rescues (4) in weakened form or merely relocates the problem: the constitutive-versus-limiting distinction, on which some entrenched rules create the legislature rather than restrict its output, so that only the second kind generates the difficulty; the precommitment reading, on which a constitution is an earlier self binding a later one, together with the objection that the Ulysses analogy fails because Ulysses arranged for his own instructions to be obeyed against his later wishes, whereas entrenchment binds a later majority that never issued those instructions; the representation-reinforcement move associated with Ely, on which review is legitimate where it repairs the channels through which the majority forms its judgment rather than pre-empting its substantive choices; Waldron's strategy of restricting the case against review to well-functioning democracies in which the courts, the legislature, and the public are broadly in good working order; and the departmentalist reply, on which (2) is simply false because judicial interpretation is one of several coordinate expressions of the same constitutional judgment rather than a rival to it.
