Question 4 (short essay, ~600 words, 25 marks). Neurath described Isotype not as a language but as a "language-like technique," and insisted it was never meant to replace verbal exposition. Explain what work that hedge does inside his larger programme — physicalism, the unity of science, his account of social statistics — and then say whether the hedge is stable: does a rule-governed pictorial sign of that kind commit him to something more language-like than he allows, or is his self-limitation simply the accurate description of what pictures can do?

And here's the answer, transcribed as submitted:

Neurath's picture work — the Vienna Method, later Isotype — is best read as the practical wing of the same programme the Circle pursued theoretically: strip out metaphysics, rebuild knowledge on a basis anyone can check. Where Carnap wanted a unified scientific language made of physicalist statements, Neurath wanted a unified visual one, so that a factory hand in Red Vienna and a schoolchild in Holland could look at the same unemployment chart and read off the same content with no translation step in between. In the protocol-sentence controversy his contribution was in effect to push the issue one stage further back: if protocol sentences were where language touches observation, pictures were to be where the touching is made visible, and Neurath argued that observation reports should ultimately be given in pictures rather than words.

Two features of the practice support this. First, the pictograms are deliberately conventional rather than naturalistic. The repeated little human figure standing for a fixed number of workers isn't meant to resemble anyone, and the first rule of the method is that you never enlarge a figure to show a bigger quantity, you repeat it — because a scaled-up figure smuggles in an impression of intensity the data doesn't license, and impression is evaluation. Second, the chart isn't the raw data: it's the output of a rule-governed transformation performed by a trained person, the transformer, who decides what to leave out and how to group what's left. That's why the museum's line was that remembering simplified pictures beats forgetting accurate figures — the simplification is a disclosed act, not a distortion.

But I think the hedge in calling all this "language-like" is unstable, and unstable against Neurath rather than for him. His caution is the right one: the pictures can't stand alone. Yet once you concede that nothing in the chart is legible until you also have the transformation rules and the accompanying words, you've conceded that the pictorial signs aren't doing any semantic work at all. The rules do it, and the rules are verbal. This follows directly from the picture theory Neurath was working with: a proposition pictures a state of affairs by sharing its logical form, and what separates picturing from mere symbolising is that the pictorial relation is available in the thing itself. Isotype has no such relation, since the same figure can be assigned any value you like by convention. So the honest description of Isotype isn't a language-like technique but a mnemonic — illustrations pinned to a text that could be written without them — and the internationalist claim collapses for the same reason, because two readers who share no language share no transformation rules either. What survives is an excellent design discipline rather than a contribution to the unity of science.
