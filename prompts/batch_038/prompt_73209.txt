My family-policy seminar is weighing two ways to spend the same sum of money: a universal cash allowance paid to parents, or a substantial expansion of publicly subsidised formal childcare. The proverb 'it takes a village to raise a child' was invoked on both sides — first to argue that institutional daycare crowds out the informal networks that do the actual raising, and then to argue that childrearing has never been a self-contained two-parent affair, so the state should fund the wider circle rather than assume a private household can absorb the work. I left the session unable to say why both uses felt unearned.

Could you set out the distinct precise claims that proverb might be making, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical? I would especially like the readings separated along three lines: how many caregivers a child needs versus who those caregivers may be; whether 'village' picks out a number of people or a kind of relationship — reciprocal, ongoing, unmediated by wages or contract; and whether the proverb is describing how children are in fact raised or prescribing how they ought to be. For each reading, say whether it is a conceptual claim or an empirical one, whether it is plausible, and which of the two seminar arguments actually requires it. Then tell me whether any single reading can serve both arguments, or whether one of them is simply borrowing the proverb's warmth.
