I'm a first-year archives MA student, and my program put me on the university's special collections access committee because they like having a student vote. Next week we decide what to do with an accession that's already sitting in our building: the surviving administrative records of a state residential institution for people labelled "feeble-minded" or "mentally defective," which ran from the 1920s until it was closed down in the early 90s. Roughly 900 case files, plus admission registers, disciplinary logs, staff correspondence, and a few hundred photographs of residents, almost none of them identified.

Here's the mess. For a lot of these people, this is the only documentation of there life that exists anywhere — their families have nothing, and the state's copies were pulped years ago. But everything in the files was written by the people confining them. Residents show up as numbers, the diagnoses are straight eugenics vocabulary, and the only actual voices are the superintendent's. So the same box is both the sole evidence these people existed and a product of the machinery that erased them.

Two descendants' groups have written to us wanting opposite things. One says open everything, we need to find our relatives, and sealing it lets the institution win by keeping its subjects invisible. The other says these are surveillance records created without consent, and putting them online with full-text search turns a superintendent's judgment into the permanent googleable identity of someone who can't answer back.

Our access policy is no help: it just says records containing personal information are restricted for 75 years from creation. That treats a disciplinary log entry and a letter a resident wrote herself identically, and it doesn't distinguish at all between who made a record and who it's about. About half the files are past 75 years and half aren't.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? I need a real recommendation with a workable access scheme — what's open, what's restricted, under what conditions, and who decides when a descendant's request overrides the default — not a paragraph about balancing competing interests. It should take seriously the best argument on whichever side you end up rejecting, and I'd like it to say something about description, since our finding aid is going to reproduce the institution's own categories whether we mean to or not. Something a committee can read in ten minutes.
