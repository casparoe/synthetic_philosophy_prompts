so I've been trying to pin down what I actually mean by a "hinge of history" moment — the idea that some times are pivotal in a way that most times just aren't, and the stronger version, that we might be in one right now. I don't want to wave at "big change happened." Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of moments and I want to know whether there's one principle underneath them or whether I'm just pattern-matching.

- Farming. Hinge. Once a society started farming it couldn't go back, and basically every later possibility runs through it.
- The printing press. Not a hinge. Huge, but it was coming — movable type already existed in East Asia long before, and what the press did depended on stuff that was already in place. It sped things up, it didn't fork the road.
- The industrial revolution, steam and all that. Hinge.
- 1945, first bombs used in anger. Hinge. You can't un-know it, and it added a way the whole species can end that you can't take back off the table.
- The Soviet early-warning officer in 1983 who decided the missile alerts were a false alarm and didn't escalate. Not a hinge. Which embarasses me, because that might be the single most consequential decision of the last hundred years.
- The Cuban missile crisis. Same verdict, same reason.
- The fall of Rome. Not a hinge. Bad, but the rest of the world kept going and Europe came back.
- Writing. Hinge, probably. Except notice it was invented independently at least twice and maybe more, which is exactly the kind of redundancy that made me say Rome doesn't matter.
- IBM in the early '80s putting the PC together out of off-the-shelf parts and signing a non-exclusive deal for the operating system. I keep saying hinge and I hate that I keep saying hinge. Nothing about it feels civilization-scale.
- The double helix. Not a hinge. A very good tool, not a fork.
- Hypothetical: five labs in five countries all release roughly the same capability within a month of each other, and nothing anyone could have done would have changed that. I want to say not a hinge, because nobody's decisions mattered, even though the world obviously changes.
- Hypothetical: a single durable world government that fixes its values in place for a thousand years. Hinge, obviously, and nothing was even invented.

My tentative principle is something like: a moment is a hinge when the choices available then permanently close doors that would otherwise have stayed open for a very long time. But I already know that's not right, because the bomb didn't close a door so much as open one we can't shut, and Petrov closed the biggest door there was and I don't want to call it a hinge.

There's also a worry I can't shake. If you can only tell from afterward, then every moment is a hinge, since every moment is an ancestor of now. I want the notion to be usable from the inside, at least in principle, or I don't see what work it's doing.

So — what principle, if any, fits all of this? Give me your best candidate, or two if the honest answer is that it forks, and state each clause precisely with the case that motivates it. Then tell me straight out which of my verdicts it can't save and what it costs me to hold onto them anyway; if the only way to keep everything is a five-clause monster, say that and tell me which verdict you'd rather I drop. After that, tell me what your account predicts for cases I left out: the transistor, the Black Death, and some future treaty that permanently bans a line of research. And finally, what it says about whether now is one. If the real answer is that my concept can't support that claim at all, I'd rather hear that than a pep talk. No need for references, I'm not writing anything up, I just want the reasoning.
