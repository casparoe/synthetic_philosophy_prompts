You're a political theorist who's spent a lot of time on democratic legitimacy and jurisdiction — the question of who actually gets to be in the electorate, not just how votes are counted. I need you to referee an argument I sat through and I want you to think hard about it rather than just splitting the difference down the middle.

Context: my town's utility wants to build a wastewater plant just outside the city line, in an unincorporated pocket whose residents pay nothing into the utility and vote in nothing the city does, but who'd get the trucks and the smell. The council wants to put it to a binding referendum of city voters only. I was at one of the public meetings and afterwards two people who'd been at each other in the comments section kept going in the parking lot. I'll call them Amir and Bea. Neither of them is a philosopher, but they were both sharper than me and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Here's the exchange as close as I can reproduce it:

1. Amir: the referendum is illegitimate as designed. Democracy's own standard is that people who are subject to a decision should have had a say in it. The pocket residents are going to be bound by this — road closures, permits, the whole thing — with zero input. Excluding them isn't a technical detail, it's just rule by force with nicer paperwork.

2. Bea: agree with the standard, but it can't be applied democratically, and that's the whole problem. To decide who votes you already need a body that's entitled to decide, and that body is exactly what's in dispute. So the boundary has to be fixed before the vote, by something that isn't a vote. Any procedure that "includes the outsiders" needs a rule for including them, and the rule is the thing we're arguing about. You can't bootstrap.

3. Amir: then run it in two steps. First a franchise vote, open as wide as possible — city residents plus everyone in the affected area — that decides who gets to vote on the actual plant. Then hold the real vote under whatever rule wins. That's not bootstrapping, it's just starting wide at the top and narrowing by decision instead of by fiat.

4. Bea: that's the regress again wearing a hat. Who votes in the franchise vote? "Everyone affected" — smell carries with the wind, the truck route feeds a corridor two counties over, the ratepayers are the whole district, and honestly the people downstream of the outfall are affected too. You've moved the boundary problem up one level and now the top boundary is set by nobody, i.e. by you, the guy who wrote "affected" on the agenda. And you could add a third level, and a fourth.

5. Amir: you're running two different things together. Some boundaries are already set by force and history and can't be un-set, so the only live question is what default to use under indeterminacy — and the defensible default is the widest circle you can describe, not the narrowest one that's convenient for the people holding the pen. And it does terminate in practice: past some point the affected people have a stake so thin they know it, and you can write the outer limit into the rule openly instead of pretending it isn't there.

6. Bea: "widest circle" isn't neutral and it isn't costless. Widening the electorate changes who wins. That corridor two counties over doesn't care about design or mitigation, they want the plant dead wherever it goes, and they don't pay into the rate base. So your default rule doesn't protect the pocket, it hands control of a city utility to people with no skin in it. If the real complaint is about being coerced, the fix is a veto for the immediate neighbors, a compensation package, or an appeal to the county or state — not more voters. You've smuggled a claim about being subjected to power into a claim about controlling other people's money.

7. Amir: and your fixes are boundary decisions too. Who gets the veto — the first half mile? the whole pocket? Who gets compensated, out of whose fund? Those are the same question in smaller clothes, except now they get settled in a back room by staff and nobody votes on them at all. At least my version is out in the open about who's deciding.

They basically stopped there because it started raining.

Finally: what are both of them missing? I have a feeling there's at least one thing neither of them touched that changes the shape of the argument.
