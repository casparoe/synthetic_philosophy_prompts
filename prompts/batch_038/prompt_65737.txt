I am a third-year philosophy student on a philosophy of biology module, and my end-of-term essay requires me to defend the selected-effects account of biological function against the causal-role account. Our seminar went round in circles on this for the better part of two hours last week and nobody managed to state either position cleanly, so I would like to rehearse the exchange properly before I commit anything to paper. Please play my opponent: a committed causal-role theorist, holding that to say a trait has a function is only to say that its present capacity to do something contributes in a specifiable way to some containing system's capacity to do whatever we are currently trying to explain, with no reference at all to the trait's evolutionary past. Make this a serious position rather than a caricature. You are entitled to its real resources: that function ascriptions are always relative to a chosen description of the containing system's characteristic activity, and that this relativity is a virtue rather than an embarrassment; that the normativity people claim to find in function talk is an artefact of comparing one instance of a system with other instances of the same kind; and that an account which makes a bench biologist's function claims hostage to facts about deep phylogenetic history that nobody can verify is an account that cannot do the work biologists actually need done. Concede points that are genuinely small — that selective history is often a useful heuristic for settling which of a trait's many causal contributions we happen to care about, for instance — but never the main one.

Stay in character and in the first person throughout, until I write 'stop': no stage directions, no meta-commentary about the exercise, and no citations, references, or named literature, since what I want is the argument rather than a bibliography. Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so that it reads as a conversation and not as an essay. If you need a detail about the case that I have not supplied, ask me for it as the character would, and then proceed on my answer.

Here is my opening move. Consider a conditional knockout mouse in which one gene is deleted in hepatocytes only, by a liver-specific recombinase, and is left perfectly intact everywhere else. The protein is still made in this animal's kidney and muscle, where it plainly does what it has always done; in the liver it is simply absent, and the liver consequently fails at a metabolic task the protein normally underwrites — it cannot clear the compound we challenge it with. Every biologist in the room says without hesitation that the gene's function is that hepatic contribution and that this mouse's liver lacks it: a defect, a failure of function, in an animal that is otherwise healthy and breeding normally. Yet your account ties function to present causal contribution to a containing capacity, and in this animal's liver, right now, there is no contribution to find — there is only an absence, and absences do not cause anything. So either you say that the gene has no function in that tissue, which flatly contradicts the practice you claim to be elucidating, or you say that the relevant containing system is the species-typical organism rather than this damaged one, in which case you have imported a standard of normalcy that this individual fails to meet — and normalcy of that sort looks like exactly the historical notion you insist you do not need. Tell me which horn you take, and why what you end up saying is not the etiological account arriving by the back door. Please write your reply in British English.
