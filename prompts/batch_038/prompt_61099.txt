I am a retired limnologist — forty years of lake survey work, chiefly phosphorus loading and winter oxygen depletion — and I have come to philosophy late and from the outside, mainly through jurisprudence, which turns out to be closer to my old habits of mind than I expected. Since retiring I have chaired the Standing Committee on Constitutional Review of a national federation of naturalist societies and field stations: some two hundred member societies, a Council of delegates elected for three-year terms, a small permanent Secretariat, and an annual General Assembly. The Committee must report next spring on whether the Charter's amendment machinery can be repaired, and I have drafted my own position for circulation to the other members. Before I circulate it I would like it audited, in the spirit of a referee's report on a methods section: not a survey of the literature, not a tutorial on the concepts I have used, but an assessment of whether the set below is jointly tenable.

The provisions that matter. Article 12 governs amendment: a two-thirds majority of the Council, followed by ratification by a simple majority of member societies voting within ninety days. Its final clause provides that Article 12 itself may be neither amended nor repealed. Article 5 empowers the Council to make standing orders for the regulation of its own proceedings and provides that standing orders lapse at the end of each term unless renewed. In 2019 the Council carried, by two-thirds and with ratification duly obtained, an Amendment 3 which left Article 12 untouched on the page but added a new Article 12A empowering the Council to amend the Charter by simple majority, without ratification. Some forty measures have since been enacted under 12A, among them the subscription scale, the field ethics code, and the terms of a merger with a neighbouring federation. Nothing has been challenged; the Secretariat publishes 12A among the operative provisions; member societies vote under it.

My commitments are these.

1. What makes a provision of the Charter valid is a matter of social fact and nothing further: provisions bind because, and so far as, the Federation's officials — Council delegates, the Secretariat, the officers of member societies — actually accept and apply them as criteria of validity, in something like Hart's sense of a rule of recognition. There is no additional question whether that acceptance is justified. Accordingly I settle such questions by getting the account of validity right and then applying it, and I do not treat the disruptiveness or unpopularity of a conclusion as an objection to it.

2. This does not reduce the Charter to a description of regular behaviour. Delegates are under an obligation to apply it; a delegate who votes contrary to a standing order is in breach of a rule, not merely out of step with a habit. I find 1 and 2 harder to hold together than I can articulate, and I half-expect that this is where the trouble lies.

3. No Council can bind its successors. Each Council holds the Federation's full powers for its own term. What survives a Council is only the Charter, and the Charter can always be changed by whatever procedure it currently prescribes. I took this up from Article 5 and from sixty years of unbroken Council practice, and I have found no counterexample.

4. Entrenchment is nonetheless real and does work. The final clause of Article 12 is not decoration: it is the only thing standing between the member societies' right of ratification and a Council that wishes to abolish it. Some provisions lie beyond the reach of every amendment procedure the Charter contains.

5. Amendment 3 was validly adopted. It complied with the manner and form that Article 12 prescribes, and compliance with the prescribed manner and form suffices for validity whatever the content of what is enacted.

6. Everything enacted under 12A since is valid. Officials have treated 12A as operative throughout, and by 1 that settled acceptance is what makes it so.

7. Were the Council to use 12A next spring to repeal Article 12 outright — extinguishing the ratification requirement and with it the member societies' only check on the Council — that measure would be invalid, notwithstanding that 12A on its face authorises it.

8. The paradox of self-amendment — that a rule empowering its own amendment must either empower its own destruction, and so cease to empower anything, or be unamendable, and so empower nothing — is a logician's artefact. Real systems revise their amendment rules constantly and nothing of jurisprudential interest happens when they do.

Please audit the set. Identify the genuine tensions and rank them by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad; for each, state exactly which commitments are involved, what would have to be surrendered or reinterpreted to remove it, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the list. Say also which pairs only look inconsistent and why they survive inspection — I would rather be told that an item I suspect is innocent than have every item flagged. I have no attachment to any particular number; tell me which cannot survive. Then name the single revision that would restore the most coherence at the least cost, and state its consequence for the forty measures already enacted.

Close with one line in exactly this form: Verdict: N — where N is the number of the single commitment whose revision does the most work.
