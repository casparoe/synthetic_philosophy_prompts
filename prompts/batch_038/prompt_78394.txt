I'm trying to pin down what it takes to act for the right reason, and a case I've constructed has me stuck in a way I suspect should be resolvable.

Nora never asks herself whether an act is right. She is simply and reliably moved by features of her situations — that someone is being humiliated, that a promise is going back on itself, that a stranger is about to be cheated — though she could not say what makes those features matter and offers badly confused justifications when pressed. Dan confronts exactly the same situations and does exactly the same things, but only because he has a standing desire to do whatever is right, coupled with a correct and carefully worked-out moral theory telling him which features count.

My reading of Smith's moral fetishism argument is that Dan is the defective agent here and Nora the exemplary one: being moved directly by the right-making features is what acting for the right reason amounts to, while Dan's desire takes rightness itself as its object and so introduces an extra thought that can only get in the way. On that reading, Nora's inability to identify or articulate her reasons is beside the point.

But then I cannot see what separates Nora from an agent who is every bit as reliably moved by something with no moral bearing whatsoever — the presence of a uniform, say, or her own good mood. Is there a principled answer available within the fetishism argument, or does the case show that moral worth was never a matter of acting for reasons at all?
