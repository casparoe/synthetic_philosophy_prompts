You are a philosopher of mind whose work sits at the seam between the conceptual debate over introspection and the empirical literature on self-report, and I would like you to treat what follows as a colleague's draft rather than as a student's essay. Please reason carefully, and before you reach any verdict, sketch out for yourself which of my commitments are first-order claims about the mind, which are claims about how self-knowledge is acquired, and which are methodological claims about what counts as evidence — much of the work in auditing a package like this lies in noticing that two statements are not even about the same thing. Where a commitment is one you associate with a particular author, take my description of it as authoritative rather than my name-dropping; several of them are my own formulations, and I am not asking you to check them against the literature. Above all, I want an audit and not a lecture: no survey of the reliability debate, no exposition of standard positions, no advice about which books to consult.

The context is this. I am a fourth-year graduate student, and our self-knowledge reading group has adopted a format for its final session in which each participant circulates their own commitments in advance and the room spends two hours trying to break them. I have written mine down below, and although I am fairly confident that the individual items are each defensible, a friend who read the list said flatly that it "wants to have it both ways," without telling me where. I would therefore like to know, in order of severity, which of these genuinely pull against one another; for each such tension, the argument for it in a few sentences, the least expensive revision that resolves it, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package. I also want you to identify the pairs that only look contradictory and to say exactly which distinction dissolves them, since I would much rather be told that I have two problems than that I have seven. If the set is more coherent than I fear, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a tension in order to seem thorough. Please end by naming the single revision that would repair the most damage, and by telling me whether the package is salvageable at all without abandoning the ambition that motivates it. Write in American English, and keep the whole thing to roughly 1,100 words.

Here is the list as it will appear on the handout.

1. There is no single faculty of introspection. Judgments about what is presently in my visual field, about my imagery, about my emotional reactions, and about the causes of my own judgments are produced by different mechanisms, so the question of whether introspection is reliable is answerable only relative to a domain. I accept the general skeptical thrust of Schwitzgebel's work: naive confidence about one's own current mental life is misplaced in a number of these domains.
2. Within the domain of present sensory phenomenology — whether there is an afterimage on the wall, whether the hum is in my left ear, whether the taste is sweet and then bitter — reliability is high. Errors there are rare, and when they happen they are explained by inattention or by a poor choice of words for a state that was perfectly well detected, not by a defective channel.
3. Within the domain of dispositional states — my standing beliefs, my values, my temperamental tendencies — I hold the opposite. People are frequently quite wrong about these, and what a partner says about me, or a month of my own behavior, is often better evidence about my values than anything I can find by looking.
4. I accept the standard lesson of the confabulation literature in the Nisbett and Wilson sense: subjects confidently report causes of their judgments that demonstrably were not the causes, and I read this as showing a failure of kind rather than of degree. There is no introspective access to those processes at all; it is not that the channel is noisy.
5. About belief in particular I want the transparency line associated with Moran together with the constitutivist line that Shoemaker's objections presuppose. When I ask whether I believe that p, I do not turn an inner eye on anything; I consider p and the reasons bearing on it. And for a rational agent there is no gap between judging that p and believing that p: belief is partly constituted by endorsement, not a further condition to be discovered by an inner sense.
6. I maintain that 5 is consistent with 1 through 4, because transparency is not a claim about the fidelity of a detection mechanism but a claim about how a question is answered, and so reliability talk simply does not apply to it.
7. A methodological commitment drawn from my own work. I have been running a beeper-based experience-sampling pilot in which participants characterize the few seconds preceding a tone, and when I code their responses I treat agreement with a trained outside observer as the criterion for calling a report wrong. If a participant says she was not irritated and the coder scoring the video says she was, I count the participant as having erred.
8. I have been using that practice to motivate a general distinction, which I call checkability. A fact is introspectively checkable just in case there is, in principle, some route to it other than the subject's own report — behavior, a partner's testimony, a recording, a physiological measure — that could confirm or disconfirm that report. I hold that reliability rankings are meaningful only over checkable domains, because a domain admitting no independent check has no determinate accuracy to rank.
9. I resist any deflationary upshot. I do not want to conclude that there is no such thing as phenomenal character, or that we are systematically deluded about our own experience, and I believe my pluralism leaves the phenomena intact.
10. Finally, I hold that some self-ascriptions partly constitute what they report. Sustainedly taking myself to be the sort of person who values candor makes it partly true that I value candor, rather than merely tracking a fact already settled independently of my self-conception.
