I'm trying to pin down what Szasz would have made of psychiatric advance directives and I keep flip flopping. Here are the calls he actually made, as I read him: involuntary commitment is imprisonment dressed up as treatment; the insanity defense should be abolished, since the accused should be tried as an agent and punished if guilty; psychoanalysis is defensible not because it works but because it's a contract between two adults that either one can walk away from; addiction isn't a disease and the drugs should be legal; a person has the right to kill himself and shouldn't be seized and "rescued" against his will; and no pattern of behavior counts as an illness, because illness is something bodies do.

New case: a woman carrying a bipolar diagnosis, while she's well, drafts and gets witnessed a directive asking to be admitted and medicated if she goes manic — and she adds a clause saying that she wont be competent to revoke it once the episode starts, so the directive should be enforced over her protests at the time.

Predict his verdict on it, and tell me which of the listed precedents is the closest analog and where that analogy breaks down. I'm more interested in the rule he's running than in the answer: is it "all coercion is illegitimate, whoever authorized it", is it "whatever free adults put in writing binds them", is it "the only question is whether psychiatry is doing medicine or law", or something else that fits everything above just as well. The no-revocation clause seems to be the sticking point — it looks like it cuts against the contractarian reading, but maybe not if you think of the well self as the one who signs. And then: name the further case that would actually separate the rules you're choosing between, and say what each rule predicts about it.
