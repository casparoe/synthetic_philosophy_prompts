I cover science and ideas for a weekly — mostly physics and AI, but my editor put me on a series about places that want out of the countries they're in, and six months of that has left me with a stack of gut calls I can't reconcile. So: help me find the principle underneath them, or tell me there isn't one and I'm just collecting prejudices.

Here's what I actually think, case by case.

1. **Scotland, 2014.** Completely legitimate. London agreed to let the vote happen, the question was settled in advance, 45% voted to leave, and the result genuinely settled the matter for a while. But when the Scottish parliament later tried to legislate for a second referendum without London's sign-off, the UK's top court said it couldn't, and I think the court was right — which unsettles me, because it means whoever happens to hold the legal keys also gets to decide whether the question is ever asked again.

2. **Quebec, 1995.** The vote itself was fine. If "yes" had scraped through by a point on that famously convoluted question, I think Ottawa would have been obliged to sit down and negotiate — but not obliged to accept separation on whatever terms Quebec alone came up with. And a 50.5% yes on a question that long wouldn't have felt like enough to me regardless.

3. **Catalonia, October 2017.** Not a legitimate basis for independence. Turnout somewhere in the 40s, the constitutional court had already killed the law the vote was called under, and the declaration that followed lasted about as long as a sneeze. I also think Madrid's conduct on the day, and the prosecutions afterwards, was indefensible. Both of those are true for me simultaneously and I can't explain how.

4. **Kosovo, 2008.** Legitimate. It had no permission from Belgrade either, and the international court everyone went to about it pointedly answered a narrower question than the one anyone wanted answered. Still: legitimate.

5. **Crimea, 2014.** Not legitimate. And I don't think the difference from Kosovo is *only* that Russia's troops were already on the ground — though I'll admit that's most of what I've got.

6. **Bougainville, 2019.** Legitimate in a way I can't cash out. Something like 98% voted for independence in a poll everyone agreed beforehand was advisory, and years later the national parliament still hasn't ratified anything. I feel they've earned the thing by the vote alone. I also feel Port Moresby isn't obviously wrong to insist the final say is its.

7. **Somaliland.** Ran itself as a functioning state for three decades with elections and a currency and not one UN member recognizing it, until one finally did recently for reasons that had little to do with the merits of the case. I lean toward "it should be recognized," but my reason looks nothing like my reason for Kosovo, and I can't say what it is.

8. **Puerto Rico.** Its voters have chosen statehood more than once, Congress is free to ignore them forever, and there's no unilateral exit. I think that arrangement is illegitimate — even though every single part of it is legal and nobody is being oppressed. Compare with #3, where I said an illegal vote gives you nothing.

9. **A rich region that just wants to stop writing checks to poorer compatriots.** No claim, in my view, even with a 65% majority and clean polls and zero oppression. Same verdict for the American South in 1861, which I reach without needing to relitigate anything.

The phrase everyone in these stories reaches for is self-determination, and I've realized it does no work at all in my head: it can't tell me why Kosovo gets a pass and Catalonia doesn't, or why Bougainville's advisory poll seems to matter more than Catalonia's actual one.

So: is there a principle — or a small set of rival ones — that delivers most of these verdicts? If these clusters have names in the literature, give me the names and the shape of each, but skip the citations and reading lists, I just need to know which of my nine each one would sign off on and which it would call confused. For each clause of whatever you land on, tell me which case forced it in.

Then run it on cases I haven't thought about. Specifically: (a) a region that's been self-governing for forty years, distinct language, no oppression, flat refusal by the parent state to allow any vote at all, and a durable two-thirds majority for leaving — does my principle license going unilateral, and if not, what exactly is the region supposed to do with its two-thirds; (b) a compact district *inside* a region that votes to leave, where the district votes to stay — who's the "self" there, and does your principle let the district peel off, which starts to feel like it eats everything; (c) does the same reasoning that makes me comfortable with Kosovo also license a breakaway entity inside a state I'd otherwise defend, and if it does, is that a bug I should live with or the sign that #4 was wrong.

Finally, be straight with me about which of the nine you have to sacrifice. I'd rather lose one verdict than get a principle with nine clauses. If two rival principles each save a different seven, I want both, side by side, with the trade spelled out.

End with a line beginning `Verdict:` giving the principle you'd defend in one sentence, followed by the numbers of my cases it overturns.
