I am a second-year graduate student working on the foundations of probability, and I would like you to take on the role of an expert in both the metaphysics of chance and formal epistemology — someone who can move fluently between the question of what chances would have to be for them to exist at all and the question of what deference to them requires of a rational credence function. Please think carefully rather than answering quickly, and begin with a short brainstorming stage: enumerate the distinct ways the thesis below could fail, and the distinct routes by which it could be true, before you commit to any numbers. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for any references; I want your own credence and the arithmetic of your own thinking laid bare.

Here is the proposition. Call it C: there are objective single-case chances in the actual world — facts of the form *the chance that this particular nucleus decays within the next hour is x*, obtaining independently of anyone's doxastic attitudes and independent of what would be revealed by an ideal census of frequencies — and these chances are precisely the quantities to which rational credence defers in the manner the Principal Principle describes: an agent whose total evidence includes the chance of some future-tense proposition, together with only admissible additional information, ought to have credence in that proposition equal to the chance. I intend C to be substantive rather than stipulative. That is, the deference requirement must be doing genuine normative work: an agent who knew the chance and formed a wildly discrepant credence would be making a real mistake about how to proportion belief to the world, not merely misusing a technical term. If the requirement could only be made true by building the credence-constraint into the meaning of "chance," I would count that as C's failure, not its vindication.

To fix ideas, here is a case a classmate constructed that our seminar could not resolve, and which I suspect is diagnostic. Consider a machine that emits heads or tails once per day for exactly a million days, and suppose the manufacturer hands you the full specification: a known function from the machine's *entire* output sequence, including the part that has not yet happened, to the sequence of daily chance values. You have watched every toss so far. Because the chances depend on the total history, you cannot compute today's chance from what you have seen; you can only enumerate candidate values, each of which is compatible with some continuation of the run. But the converse also holds, and this is what unsettles me: if someone were to tell you today's exact chance, that information would itself constrain which futures are open, ruling out some of them outright. So the very evidence the Principal Principle tells you to defer to appears to carry inadmissible content about the outcome you are deferring about. Does this show that no agent embedded inside a world whose chances supervene on the whole history can ever possess chance information admissible in the required sense, or is it a technicality that a sufficiently careful formulation can absorb? Please give your own probability that the case is fatal, and treat it as one term in the decomposition if you think it belongs there.

So: state your credence in C as a single number, and then decompose it. For each sub-question, give a probability and one sentence of justification, and show me how the terms combine — multiplicatively where the dependence really is conditional, and with an explicit note wherever two terms are not independent, since I expect several of them to be correlated. The terms I would expect to see, though I want you to tell me if I have left out an important one or included a pseudo-question: the probability that chances are features of the world at all rather than a useful way of describing our own ignorance; conditional on their existing, the probability that they are Humean summaries of the total pattern of particular events rather than irreducible dispositions or single-case propensities; the probability that a Humean account can support both the deference requirement and its substantivity clause without collapsing into the undermining difficulty described above, which even its most famous defender eventually conceded needed repair; the probability that a non-Humean account escapes that difficulty at an acceptable cost; the probability that the deference requirement is knowable a priori, and your view on whether C's substantivity clause can survive if it is; and the probability that our best current physics leaves room for single-case chances at all, given that the leading interpretations differ so sharply — deterministic unitary evolution with branching, a hidden-variable picture in which nothing is genuinely chancy, and collapse theories in which chances are irreducible. Finally, tell me which single term your overall number is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move that term furthest.
