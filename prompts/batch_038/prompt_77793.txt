I am preparing a workshop submission on proof-based agency — the family of designs in which a system adopts a plan only when it can derive, inside its own reasoning system T, that adopting the plan serves its goal — and I have written down what I take to be a fatal objection to the whole family. My collaborators say the objection proves too much to be believed, but neither of us can locate the failure. T is a consistent, recursively axiomatisable theory, strong enough to represent its own proofs, so that Löb's theorem applies to it.

1. The agent adopts a plan only if T proves that adopting it serves the agent's goal; otherwise the agent is relying on unverified reasoning.
2. Reliance on T amounts to reliance on verified reasoning only if T is sound, that is, only if every instance of Prov_T(⌜S⌝) → S holds.
3. If the agent cannot prove in T that T is sound, then the agent cannot exclude the possibility that T proves falsehoods.
4. By Löb's theorem, whenever T proves Prov_T(⌜S⌝) → S it already proves S; so a consistent T proves that schema for no sentence it fails to prove, and in particular proves neither its own consistency nor any global soundness principle about itself.
5. Therefore the agent cannot exclude the possibility that T proves falsehoods, and so, by (1) to (3), may adopt no plan whatsoever.
6. Nothing in (1) to (5) turned on the agent's being a machine: any reasoner who demands verified warrant for a conclusion satisfies (1) to (3) with her own reasoning in place of T, and any such reasoning, if consistent, recursively describable and able to represent its own warrants, is subject to (4).
7. Therefore no consistent reasoner may accept any conclusion on her own authority: self-trust of the kind the objection describes is available to nobody.
8. Yet (6) and (7) are themselves conclusions I have accepted on my own authority, so by (7) I may not accept them.
9. Hence the argument either establishes that nothing can be established — in which case it has not been established — or it fails, and it is not in a position to say which.

I would rather keep (1) and (4), since (1) is the design I mean to be objecting to and (4) is a theorem, and I would much rather not end up defending (7), whose sceptical reach is plainly absurd. What I cannot tell is whether the argument contains a local defect I have missed, or whether it is valid and I simply have to choose the least damaging premise to abandon.

Please answer in British English, as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (i) the first step at which the argument becomes untenable, together with the precise equivocation or scope shift it contains; (ii) why that step looked compelling in context; (iii) the best repair — which premise to weaken, and the conclusion the argument then legitimately supports; (iv) what survives of the Löbian obstacle for proof-based agents once that repair is made; (v) what the repair costs the objection, and what would persuade me that a different premise was the cheaper sacrifice.
