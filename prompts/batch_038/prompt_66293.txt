I've been arguing with a friend for about three weeks now about how much of safety engineering actually transfers to AI alignment, and I've gotten to the point where I care more about what my own gut says than about what the sequences say. He's a formal methods guy — wants proofs, thinks anything without a worst-case guarantee is just vibes with extra steps. I lean the other way and think worst-case guarantees in this domain always smuggle the hard part into the threat model. But when I sat down and wrote out concrete cases with my verdicts on them, the pattern didn't come out the way either of us predicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, fits all of it. Please don't hand me a survey of maximin versus expected utility or a paragraph about Knightian uncertainty as though that settles something — I've read all of it. I just want the principle that reproduces my verdicts, or the honest statement that there isn't one.

Here's the list. One: a lab proves its shutdown mechanism works against any system whose goals fall in a specified class of goals, where the class was picked by the lab. Not fine — the proof is airtight and worth close to nothing, because the class is doing all the work; its a lock under warranty against the burglars you already imagined. Two: exactly the same mechanism and the same proof, but the class is chosen by an outside group whose only incentive is to make the lab look bad. I want to say that's better, but when I check, what I'm actually endorsing is "safe against people who are trying to embarrass you," which isn't safety. Three: no proof at all, just an empirical estimate — across a large ensemble of trained models in simulated deployment, catastrophic failure happens about once in ten to the twelfth model-years, with error bars. I want to say this one is fine, and it's also the one that makes me queasiest, since the ensemble is generated by the same people and I dont see why the measuring apparatus stays stable under the thing being measured. Notice that three has no proof and I trust it, and one has a proof and I don't. That's the pair I most want you to attack. Four: aviation, where the certification target for the worst failure category is on the order of one in a billion per flight hour and I get on planes without a second thought. Fine, obviously — but I can't tell you whether that's a worst-case guarantee or an average-case one, because it looks like a probability bound on the worst outcome, which seems to be neither, and it's the only entry on my list that has actually worked at scale. Five: a scheme with a genuine worst-case guarantee that costs ten percent of capability — deploy it. The same guarantee at a sixty percent capability cost — don't, because somebody else ships the unguaranteed system and you've bought nothing and lost. My verdict flips and the thing that flips it has nothing to do with the guarantee. Six: a recovery scheme with no bound on how often things go wrong but a proof that whatever goes wrong gets caught and rolled back to a known-safe state. My favorite, fine. Same scheme except the monitor keeps interrupting the operators, who therefore have a standing incentive to disable it: not fine. I want to insist that's a difference in kind, and I also suspect it's just case two wearing a different hat.

My tentative principle, offered for you to break: what I want is neither worst-case nor average-case but the smoothed-analysis move — I think it's Spielman and Teng, where you ask not about adversarial inputs or typical inputs but about adversarial inputs plus a small random perturbation, which is roughly how the simplex method stopped looking exponential — translated as: a guarantee should hold across a whole neighborhood of systems around the one being shipped, where the neighborhood isn't drawn by whoever ships it. I can't say what the perturbation is supposed to be here, though. Noise in the weights? In the deployment environment? In my own priors about what the system is? And I suspect that vagueness is the whole ballgame. So: is there a principle behind one through six, does the smoothed version state it, and if you need an extra clause to save some case, tell me which case forced the clause. Then tell me what it says about two I haven't worked out — a guarantee that holds only so long as the input distribution doesn't shift, where the shifting is caused by the system itself; and a guarantee purchased by narrowing the goal until the thing is provably harmless and also useless, which I can't decide is worst-case safety or a category error. And say plainly which of my verdicts you can't accommodate. My guess is five, and that the flip in five is what forces you to admit I'm running two principles with no priority order between them; if that's right I want to know whether it's fatal or whether it just means the principle has to be about incentives rather than about guarantees.
