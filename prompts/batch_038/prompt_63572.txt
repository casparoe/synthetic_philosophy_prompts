I've been developing a Frankfurt-case argument in a reading group, and it has outgrown the conclusion I wanted: the last step is one I can't accept, and the premises I borrowed from actual-sequence theorists are ones they'd also refuse to endorse in that form. So I suspect a step has slipped, but I can't find it.

(1) Nothing about how an agent would have acted in circumstances that do not obtain can bear on whether she is morally responsible for what she actually did.

(2) In a standard Frankfurt case, the intervener's readiness bears on the action only in this counterfactual way: he monitors, stays out of it, and the agent decides and acts through her own reasoning. The situation in which he takes over never obtains.

(3) So by (1), the fact that the agent could not have done otherwise is irrelevant to her responsibility: she is responsible, and the principle of alternative possibilities is false.

(4) Now take a deterministic world. The claim that its agent could not have done otherwise is likewise a claim about what would have happened under circumstances that do not obtain, and the actual course of her deliberation contains no event that the Frankfurt case lacks.

(5) So by the same reasoning determinism is irrelevant to responsibility: determinism and moral responsibility are compatible, and any incompatibilist argument that runs through alternative possibilities is dead.

(6) Finally, take the manipulation cases: an agent designed and covertly programmed so that he forms precisely the desire he acts on. What the designers did leaves his own reasoning-and-choosing process exactly as it is; the case differs from the Frankfurt case only in that the programming was carried out rather than held in reserve.

(7) So by (1) once more, manipulation is irrelevant as well, and the programmed agent is exactly as responsible as anyone else.

(7) is where I have to stop, and I do not want to bite that bullet. Which step fails, and why did it look acceptable? I should say that I'd most like to keep (1), because abandoning it appears to hand the flicker-of-freedom objectors everything they want: if facts about non-actual circumstances can matter after all, then an untriggered backup mechanism in a Frankfurt case looks like just such a fact. So please also price the alternatives — what rejecting (1), or (4), or (6) would cost the rest of the argument, and what an actual-sequence theorist can say that distinguishes a manipulator's programming from a deterministic past, if there is anything to say.
