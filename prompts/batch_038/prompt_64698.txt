I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and I am reporting a long read about what became of the concept of utility once economics stopped treating it as a quantity that could be measured and started treating it as something legible from what people actually do — and about how that vocabulary has since been taken up, almost unexamined, by people who train machine-learning systems. My difficulty is a reporting one rather than an intellectual one. The economists, the philosophers of economics and the alignment researchers I interview all say "preference", and they mean at least three different things by it: a description of behaviour, the name of a mental state, and a target to be optimised. Interviews go nowhere until that has been settled, and I have stopped being able to settle it by asking people what they mean, because everyone answers "the standard thing".

What I want is a decision tree I can carry into an interview and use to sort a respondent by what they say about cases, not by which label they claim. Please build it as follows.

**Starting point.** The respondent is taken to accept only this: that we can observe what people choose; that the standard theory represents a person as having a complete and transitive ordering over their options; and that we cannot observe the ordering itself. Everything else is up for grabs.

**Axes first.** Before the tree proper, give me the four to six dimensions along which the positions genuinely differ — for instance, whether an attribution of preference reports an inner state or merely summarises a pattern of behaviour; whether choice is evidence of preference or constitutive of it; whether a violation of transitivity shows that someone lacks a coherent ordering, holds conflicting ones, or has simply been measured badly; whether an ordering exists before the moment of elicitation or is produced by it; and whether satisfying a preference necessarily makes someone's life go better. State each axis as a plain question about the world, and note for each one which leaves it is doing the work of separating.

**Nodes.** Twelve to sixteen, each a single question with a yes or no answer that a respondent could give in fifteen seconds without having read anything on the subject. No node may contain the vocabulary of the positions (nothing like "behaviourism", "welfarism", "ordinal", "axiom", "dispositional") and none may name a person or a school. Every node must be answerable by someone who has no idea which leaf it leads to.

**Cases.** Anchor the nodes in concrete situations rather than abstractions. Please use at least the following, and feel free to add more of the same grain: a person who takes the soup when the alternative is the salad and the salad when the alternative is the soup; a smoker who buys cigarettes every day and says, sincerely, that they would rather not; a shopper who pays more for a product because of how it was made, and reports no enjoyment and no expected personal benefit from doing so; someone raised with very few options who reports themselves entirely satisfied; a decade-long ledger of every purchase, tap, dwell time and declined offer that an app compiles into a complete ordering over everything the person has ever faced — and the question whether that ordering was found or manufactured, and what could possibly check it; and a model trained on millions of pairwise human comparisons, whose designers describe it as having learned preferences.

**Leaves.** Eight to ten, each with a short code. Where a position has a standard name in the literature, give it; where it does not, write "unnamed" and describe it instead. I want the tree to be able to reach, among others: the view that talk of preference is nothing over and above talk of choice; the view that preferences are real mental states for which choice is fallible and partial evidence; the view that preferences are settled by a person's whole pattern of dispositions rather than by any felt episode; the view that the ordering is a formal device carrying no interpretation until further assumptions are added; the view that choice and welfare come apart whenever someone acts from a commitment they do not rank above the alternative in their own wellbeing; the view that welfare just is preference satisfaction, with qualifications about information, impulsiveness and preferences formed under deprivation; the view that preferences are constructed in the act of elicitation, so that inconsistency is the norm rather than a defect; the view that utility means felt experience, so that a preference can be satisfied without anyone feeling any different; and the view that preference talk is a useful fiction answering to nothing at all.

**For each leaf, supply four things:** its name or "unnamed"; a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment, ideally the one an interviewer can press on; which of my three jobs for the word "preference" — describing behaviour, denoting a mental state, or serving as an optimisation target — the position assigns to it, since that is the spine of my piece; and one follow-up question I could ask off-script to confirm that the respondent really is at that leaf rather than an adjacent one.

**Then be honest about the instrument's limits.** Flag every pair of leaves that the tree cannot reliably separate, and say what extra question would separate them if any exists. List at least three combinations of answers that the nodes permit but that no leaf occupies, and for each diagnose whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored — and whether, in the last case, it is one that a machine-learning practitioner rather than an economist would be likeliest to hold.

Please set it out as a plain numbered branching outline (Node 1 — question; if yes, go to Node 5; if no, go to Node 2), followed by the leaf descriptions and then the two lists of limitations. No bibliography, no citations and no reading list: I will do my own checking afterwards. British spelling throughout.
