I have been asked to deliver the address at the memorial service for a colleague who died last month at eighty-two, and I have found that I cannot write it. I am a moral philosopher; I should like you to draft the speech, and I want to set out with some care what it may and may not do, because the occasion has a shape that none of the standard resources for a eulogy fits.

The facts first, since everyone in the room knows them and nothing is served by allusion. In 1998 he published an article that reproduced, with modest rewording and without acknowledgment, an unpublished seminar paper written by a graduate student he was directing. The university investigated, the finding was sustained, and he was barred permanently from supervising doctoral students and from holding any administrative office. He was permitted to go on teaching, and he did, for another twenty years. He never apologized — not once, in print or in person — and when people pressed him he said that he regarded the matter as closed by the university's letter and that he did not discuss it. The student is now an established professor elsewhere; she wrote to me after his death to say that she would attend, and that her only request was that I not tell the room a comfortable story. She asked for nothing further. She did not ask to be mentioned, and I have resolved not to mention her, since I do not intend to make her the object of a hundred people's attention at a service she is attending as a mourner.

The room will be about a hundred people: his wife, his children, his grandchildren; colleagues of my own generation, three of whom sat on the committee in 1998; perhaps fifteen former students who knew only the generous teacher of the last two decades, who was genuinely generous, who ran a reading group in his front room on Thursday evenings for twenty years and who paid for several of those students' books out of his own pocket; and a scattering of others who have come to see how it is done.

Now the constraint that governs everything else. I have spent my career on Strawson's account of moral responsibility, and I accept his division between remaining a participant in a relationship with someone — resenting, being grateful, forgiving, loving, feeling guilty, holding one another to demands — and stepping back from that relationship to regard the person instead as a case: something to be explained, managed, treated, trained, or avoided. I accept too his further point, which is the one people forget, that the second posture is no refuge. It is available to us only in bursts, and a life conducted entirely within it would be a life stripped of every relationship worth having.

I do not want the address to lapse into that posture, and this rules out a whole genre of things that are customarily said at services of this kind. It cannot say that he was a product of his upbringing; that he belonged to a generation that had not learned to think about these things; that his ambition was a wound; that the pressures of a difficult career broke him; that in his last years his judgment was going; that we are all frail; that time softens; that no one is only the worst thing they have done. Each of those sentences would be a way of leaving the relationship and observing him from outside it, and the room will recognize the maneuver, his children included.

The mirror image is equally forbidden, and this is the part I have not been able to solve. A memorial is not a tribunal, and the emotions of the participant stance addressed to a man who is dead and cannot answer are cruelty wearing the costume of seriousness. I may not stand up and resent him. I may not voice indignation on behalf of people who did not ask me to. I may not make the room complicit in a verdict delivered to a man who is not there to receive it. And I may of course not offer the consolation that is the usual business of the occasion — that he is at peace, that we shall carry him with us, that he has been forgiven by someone with the standing to forgive him, which is not me.

So the address must occupy a third position, and I want you to find it rather than to describe the difficulty of finding it. My own thought, for what it is worth: gratitude belongs to the participant stance as much as resentment does; it is not a neutral report about the past. To be grateful to him for the Thursdays, for the books, for the fact that he went on showing up to teach people who were nineteen, is not to have decided that 1998 stopped mattering. It is to stay inside the relationship and to keep one part of it while refusing to pretend about the rest. If you can build the speech out of that, without ever claiming to have squared the two, I believe you will have it.

As to form and manner: five minutes, roughly eight hundred words, to be read aloud slowly. Plain sentences; he despised ornate ones. No philosopher's name and no term of art — the words reactive, objective, participant, resentment, indignation, and moral luck must not appear, because the framework should be bearing weight underneath and not announcing itself from the platform. The wrong should be stated once, in a single plain clause, early, and not returned to; the room does not need me to dwell, and the family has heard it all before. Include the Thursday reading group and his rule for it — he did not permit anyone at that table to apologize to anyone else, on the ground that an accepted apology ended an argument before it had finished — and let the speech do something with the fact that I am about to break his rule in public. Address the room, not him; do not speak to the dead. Do not close on consolation and do not close on an exhortation. It should be possible for the hundred people present to hear one speech in which the three who sat on the committee, the woman sitting in the fourth row, and his children each find that it has not lied to them.
