I'm a cog sci PhD student and our reading group last Thursday turned into a two-hour arguement that I still haven't been able to settle in my own head. It was about Searle's original/derived intentionality distinction and Dennett's two-bitser style reply to it. The two people going at it were Elena, who builds embodied/robotic systems, and Sam, who's a straight-up philosophy-of-mind person. I was mostly taking notes and refilling the coffee, so I never took a side and I honestly can't tell who won. Here's the exchange as close to how it actually went as I can manage.

The case Elena put on the table is a real one: our lab has a little controller board in the greenhouse that opens a watering valve. Four states, wired to a humidity sensor. She said it's physically just a scaled-up version of a two-bitser — nothing in the electronics says "humidity" rather than anything else.

(1) Elena: the board is about humidity only because of its wiring history plus the fact that describing it that way is useful to us. Brains are the same kind of thing, only bigger. So there's no real line between original and derived content, and the distinction doesn't carve anything.

(2) Sam: that's picking the easy case. The board is an artifact — someone designed it and someone rewired it — so its content plainly depends on users and their purposes. A brain isn't anybody's artifact and doesn't depend on anyone's purposes. Generalizing from artifacts to brains is the question-begging move, since both sides already agree the artifact is derived.

(3) Elena: fine, delete the designer. Suppose nobody ever intended anything by that board: it was pulled out of a scrap bin, rewired in an afternoon by an undergrad who has since graduated and left, and nobody has ever described it as measuring humidity. Nothing about its causal role changes, but your dependence-on-a-user condition is now unsatisfied. If the states are still about humidity, then aboutness isn't coming from anybody's intentions, so it has to come from the causal/functional facts — and those are on offer for brains too. If they're not about humidity, then your view says a working control system has no content at all, which is a cost you should own rather than hide.

(4) Sam: no cost, because content goes with consciousness, not with function. A scavenged board is just silicon behaving. And your move makes the problem worse: if causal reliability fixes content, then those same states are about the greenhouse door being open (opening it changes the humidity), about the season, about the weather three towns over. You've traded one indeterminacy for a bigger one, and the usual fix — that the describer's interests select humidity over door-openness — is just the derivation you were trying to eliminate.

(5) Elena: that's the point, not a bug. Content is always fixed relative to some interests, brains included. What you call original intentionality is just the case where the interests doing the fixing happen to belong to the system itself. So original is a special case of derived, and the distinction is one of degree, not kind.

(6) Sam: that's verbal. You've redefined original as self-derived and then announced a refutation. And it costs you misrepresentation: if content is fixed by the describer's interests, nothing can go wrong from the system's own side — when the door gets left open and the board floods the bed, you can't call that a false belief about humidity, only a description you've stopped liking.

Can you go through these six moves one at a time and rate each of them — clearly mistaken, partially right, clearly right — with a sentence or two justifying each rating? I want to know who's right about which specific claim, not just who wins overall, though please give me that too at the end. Two extra things I'd like answered: is Sam's charge in (6) that Elena is merely changing the subject actually fair, or is she saying something substantive? And what are both of them leaving out — for instance, does a greenhouse board with no learning, no adaptation, and no history of use even qualify as a case of intentionality, and if not does that sink Elena's example before either of them gets going? American English, please.
