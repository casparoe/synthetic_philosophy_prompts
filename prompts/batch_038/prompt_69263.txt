I'm in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD, and the chapter I owe my committee has to be built around a real episode rather than a free-standing argument. I've come around to liking the constraint, though my first three attempts were all science-fiction cases that my adviser bounced as not episodes at all, only thought experiments with special effects. The one I want to use is the wormhole sequence at Caltech in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Take the following as given rather than reconstructing or fact-checking it, since I have already done that: in the mid-1980s Carl Sagan, drafting Contact, asked Kip Thorne for a way to move a character between two distant places quickly that would not embarrass a physicist; Thorne, working with Michael Morris, developed the traversable wormhole as the answer, and wrote it up partly as a pedagogical device for general relativity; Morris, Thorne, and Ulvi Yurtsever then showed in 1988 that such a wormhole could be converted into a time machine by taking one mouth on a relativistic round trip, that the resulting closed timelike curves required matter violating the weak energy condition, and that the arrangement did not appear to generate any unresolvable paradox; around 1990, in a paper led by John Friedman and including Igor Novikov, the same group argued that only globally self-consistent histories can occur in such a spacetime. Joseph Polchinski answered by letter with a version of the grandfather paradox deliberately engineered to contain no agents: a billiard ball fired into one mouth so that it emerges in the past on a trajectory that strikes its younger self and prevents it from ever entering the wormhole. Two of Thorne's students, Fernando Echeverria and Gunnar Klinkhammer, analyzed the case and found that self-consistent solutions exist — the returning ball delivers a glancing blow rather than a disabling one — and that there is typically more than one such solution, with later work by Thorne and Robert Forward indicating that certain initial trajectories admit infinitely many. They were unable to find any initial conditions admitting no self-consistent extension whatsoever. Hawking's response a couple of years later was the chronology protection conjecture: that physics beyond classical general relativity, in the semiclassical calculation the pile-up of vacuum fluctuations at the Cauchy horizon, prevents the time machine from ever forming.

What I want is a case analysis of that episode through the philosophical treatment of the grandfather paradox, and I want it run in both directions. The lens is the Lewis-style analysis, on which the paradox trades on an equivocation between two senses of "can": the traveler can kill his grandfather in the sense that he has the ability and the local facts permit it, but cannot in the sense that the act is not compossible with the whole settled past. Here is a piece of scaffolding I have been building for myself and would like you to use and then pressure-test rather than merely adopt. My claim is that a consistency paradox can play three distinct roles, and that the interest of this episode lies in the migration between them: as refutation, where the paradox is supposed to show the scenario impossible; as constraint, where the paradox is conceded to be resolvable but only at the cost of restricting which histories are admissible, so that consistency becomes a fixed-point condition on the physics; and as selection, where the constraint is satisfied many times over and the paradox reappears in a new guise, since nothing in the laws picks one admissible history over the others. If that is right, the philosophical toolbox was built almost entirely for the first role and goes quiet in the second and third.

So: map the episode onto the analysis with some discipline. Which features of Lewis's apparatus actually get purchase on Polchinski's billiard ball, given that the ball was designed to have no abilities, no intentions, and no access to the distinction between what it can do locally and what is compossible with the past as a whole — is the compossibility reply a solution to the same paradox the physicists were dissolving, or an answer to a question they had stopped asking? Then take up the selection role directly: when several self-consistent extensions of one and the same past exist, does the response that the past is fixed still do any work, and is the residual problem a paradox about time travel at all or an ordinary problem of indeterminism? I have been wondering whether John Norton's dome is the right comparison for the multiplicity result, or whether invoking it distorts the case, since there the non-uniqueness is a live argument against determinism and here it may be nothing of the kind; tell me which. I also want Hawking's move assessed — is chronology protection best read as a philosophical concession, the paradox never refuted but relocated into quantum gravity where it can no longer be pinned down, or as a sign that the two communities were arguing about different objects entirely? And say something about the Sagan origin: does the fact that the research program began as the solution to a novelist's plot problem matter epistemically to the status of what came out of it, or is that only good color? Throughout, be explicit where the theory is silent rather than making it speak; I would rather have three honest gaps than a seamless reading. Finish with a verdict on the reverse direction — whether this episode confirms the philosophical treatment, is an anomaly for it, or is a case it never anticipated, and what that implies for how much work the compossibility strategy can do in general. Roughly fifteen hundred words. No bibliography, no citation list, no suggested readings; I have the literature and only want the analysis, and I don't want the physics re-derived or the paradox itself summarized back to me.
