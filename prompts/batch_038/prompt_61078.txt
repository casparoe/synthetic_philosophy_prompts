In three weeks our museum holds its annual benefactors' reception, and as curator of invertebrate zoology I have been given five minutes — call it seven hundred words — before the unveiling of a new case. The case holds a small, pale, eyeless amphipod that we took from three karst springs in one valley across four field seasons; I described it two years ago, and it is being named after Adela Marchetti, the museum's late chair of the board, whose family will be in the front row. About half the room will be members and donors who have never heard the word holotype. The other half will include most of the regional systematics community, several of whom have read the preprint that appeared in February.

The preprint — by a former graduate student of mine, and in my judgment largely right — reports that the mitochondrial gene trees for our three spring populations do not form a group of their own but sit inside a widespread and utterly ordinary surface species whose name has priority, so that on the treatment they favor our animal is not a separate lineage at all but a handful of cave-dwelling offshoots of something you can find in any creek in the county, and the new name goes into the databases as a junior synonym. A second group has since argued the reverse: that each of the three springs holds a lineage reproductively and morphologically its own, so that if anything there are three of these animals, the name attaches to only one of them, and that one is the smallest and most imperiled. My own view, formed over twenty years of this work, is that a species is not a class with a definition waiting to be gotten right but a particular historical thing — a lineage that began somewhere and can end, closer to a river or a person than to a triangle — and that its boundaries mark where our sampling and our concepts give out rather than where the world has drawn a line. That view is why the February preprint does not keep me awake. It is also why I cannot do what the occasion seems to invite, which is to assure the Marchetti family that their mother's name is now attached to something permanent.

Please write the remarks for me. They have to be sayable aloud to people with no biology whatsoever, so none of the machinery may be named: no 'species concept,' no 'biological' or 'phylogenetic' or 'nominalist,' no explicit talk of individuals versus classes — the position must be carried entirely by concrete things said about this animal, this valley, and what we did to it in the course of naming it. They must contain nothing I believe false, and in particular must not assert that the name will still be in use in a hundred years, or that these springs hold something no other water holds, since both of those are precisely what is in dispute; but neither may they collapse into a shrug, and they must not comfort the family by demoting the animal to an arbitrary slice of a continuum, which would misrepresent what I actually think and would be heard by the biologists as a concession to the February argument. Somewhere in the middle there should be a passage that lands twice: to the family, simply a moving thing said about Adela Marchetti; to the systematists, a recognizable and defensible position on what a name of this kind is doing, which they will hear as answering both the preprint and the three-lineages reply without my ever appearing to argue with colleagues from a podium. End at the unveiling — the closing lines should carry me to the moment I pull the cord and read the binomial off the plaque; write [NAME] where it goes. After the speech, add one line beginning "Commitment:" stating in a single sentence, in the plainest language available, what claim about the world the speech has actually made — the thing I would be on record as believing if everyone in that room understood it the same way.
