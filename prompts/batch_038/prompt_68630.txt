Write a dialogue between two epistemologists arguing in a lab — both sharp, neither one a strawman. One is a plain reliabilist: knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable process, full stop, and she thinks the whole "bootstrapping makes knowledge too easy" complaint is overblown — if the instrument really is reliable, then reading it and noting that it lines up with what you take to be going on really does give you knowledge that it's reliable, even though an unreliable instrument used the exact same way wouldnt. The other thinks that's a reductio: it lets anyone manufacture warrant for a gauge out of nothing but the gauge's own readings, and it makes it impossible to explain why we ever feel the need for independent checks.

Set it concretely: the lab has two spectrometers from different factories, no calibration standard to appeal to, and so far they've agreed on every sample. Have them fight over whether that mutual agreement rescues bootstrapping or just doubles it, and over what the anti-bootstrapper is supposed to put in place of the reliabilists story (antecedent warrant for reliability? denying closure? restricting which processes count as basic?). At some point each should land a real hit the other can't just wave off. End with each of them naming the specific thing their own view has to give up — no tie-breaking narrator, they should still be answering each other in the last exchange.
