My friend and I have been going in circles for two days on a point I now think can only be settled by a case built to order, so I'd like you to build it.

He directs analytics for a public agency and holds what he states as a general principle of evidence: whenever an aggregate association conflicts with the association found within every stratum of a third variable, the stratified tables are the real evidence and the aggregate is an artifact of mixing unlike groups. His exhibit is the Berkeley graduate admissions data, stratified by department. My position is that his principle is not a principle of evidence at all but a heuristic carrying an unstated causal premise — namely that the stratifying variable is a common cause of exposure and outcome — and that when the premise fails, the aggregate is the table to act on and the strata are the artifact. He thinks I'm playing with words; I think he has never had to state his assumption aloud.

I want a thought experiment that forces the issue, to the following specifications.

The setting must be an ordinary program evaluation in a real kind of institution — a health system, a workforce agency, a school district, your choice — with a decision of ordinary stakes attached to it, and no fictional devices, no omniscient narrator, and no statistical machinery beyond two-way tables, counts, and averages. The scenario proper should run to about two hundred words.

The design must be genuinely randomized, so that the two arms are exchangeable at baseline as a matter of fact and not by stipulation. The stratifying variable must be measured after assignment and must be affected by the program — a post-randomization variable — and this must be something the analyst in the story could know from the design documents rather than from a diagram handed to him.

The numbers must actually work. Give me at least two strata with cell counts and rates, and check the arithmetic out loud: the direction of the association must reverse between every stratum and the aggregate. No cell may be so small that a reader could dismiss the rates as noise — nothing under a couple of hundred people — and no stratum may be degenerate.

There must be a live decision such that acting on the stratified tables and acting on the aggregate point in opposite directions, and the decision reached from the stratified tables is the worse one. Show me why it is worse by reasoning from the design, not by citing anyone's authority. Then explain what makes the within-stratum comparison something other than a causal contrast, and say precisely what quantity the aggregate comparison is an estimate of.

Then the honest part, which matters as much as the construction:

Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, and tell me what happens to the verdicts if it is removed — in particular, if the same numbers arrived with the stratifying variable measured before assignment instead of after, would my friend's rule become correct? Walk through it rather than asserting it.

State what distinguishes this case from Berkeley in terms specific enough that someone who had never seen either dataset could apply the distinction.

Give him his best reply. He has at least two available: that a program whose aggregate benefit consists largely of moving people between strata may not reproduce that benefit when scaled to a different population, and that there is a legitimate way to pose the within-stratum question which my framing simply ignores. Take both seriously and tell me whether my case survives them, needs qualification, or has to be rebuilt.

One constraint on what counts as success. I'll be showing this to analysts who have internalized his slogan, so the case must not be solvable by a slogan in either direction, and it must not tip its hand. If the reversal comes out of a stipulation — if the numbers are arranged so that only a fool would trust the strata, or if the story tells us the aggregate is authoritative — the case fails. Looking at the two tables and nothing else, a competent analyst should find my friend's rule compelling.
