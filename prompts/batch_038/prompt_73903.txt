So — Greywater is shutting down. Officially "Greywater: The Long Coast," fourteen years old, servers off for good on the 30th. My guild has been going since 2013 and we've rented the function room of an airport hotel in Leeds for the Saturday after: about 180 people, a livestream, and then everybody logs in together for the last hour. I've been asked to do the closing speech before the log-in, five minutes. This isn't a hobby thing for me. I met my wife in Greywater in 2014. We got married in 2019 in a registry office with eleven people in the room, and at the same time about fourty guildmates stood in a ring out in the Drowned Fens holding a ceremony of their own that was, by all accounts, longer and better. She died in January 2023. For most of the year after, I played her character — same class, same name, same habit of pulling one extra pack of mobs and then blaming lag. A few people did it with me. We call it keeping the chair: when someone's gone, nobody takes their slot, and at the start of a run somebody reads out the names of the empty chairs. I stopped in the spring. I don't regret the year and I have never found a way to describe it to anyone who wasn't in it.

Four things the speech has to carry and I can't get any of them right on my own. 1) The studio is shipping an offline client on shutdown day — single-player, whole map intact, every character record frozen where it stands — and their community manager asked me as a personal favour to mention it, because the line they want out in the world is "the coast stays." I don't want to say it. What I actually believe is that a world with no other people in it isn't a world that survived, it's a very expensive photograph, and that saying otherwise is the sort of comfort that turns out to have been a lie about six weeks later. But I also don't want to spend five minutes telling 180 grieving people that the thing they're being handed is worthless. 2) There's a joke in it somewhere: in 2016 a dupe bug made roughly twenty-four hundred copies of one sword, the studio refused to roll anything back, and our guild historian has spent eight years maintaining she can identify the original by which shard it was first seen on. She has asked me to name the true blade in the eulogy. I think this is exactly as sensible as insisting that one photocopy is the original photograph — but she has to laugh too, and the room needs a real laugh inside the first ninety seconds that isn't about how much of our lives we spent. 3) In 2017 the studio deleted a player-built district overnight, no warning, no compensation, and the people who had three years of work in it still get wet-eyed about it, and several of them are ashamed of that, and I want to say something that lets them stop being ashamed. There will be partners and parents in the room who never played, who came along to be supportive, one of whom said to me very kindly last week, well, it was only a game. Those are the same people who drove us to hospitals and cooked for us. If I go hard on the district I lose them, and losing them makes a worse speech, not a braver one. 4) Her character. I will not say she's still in there — she isn't, and pretending otherwise feels like a small theft from her — and I will not say the year I spent playing her was avoidance, because three of the people I love most did the same thing and it was probably the most useful and most courageous thing they did that year. Both of those have to be in the speech and neither one gets to be the banner I wave.

Audience, concretely: maybe 40 people with a decade-plus in the game who will catch every reference, two who have been in each other's voices for nine years and are meeting face to face for the first time at this event, a scattering of spouses and one adult daughter who are politely bewildered, the studio's community manager who is 26 and has had a genuinely miserable fortnight, and a journalist from a games site who will clip whatever sentence is easiest to headline. Tone is dry, warm, self-mocking; the room will not tolerate sentiment it hasn't earned. Don't use the word avatar (we say char, or toon), don't use journey, don't say virtual anything, don't say community more than once, and don't end on "the coast stays" or a synonym of it. Write it the way I'd actually say it, standing up in a carpeted room with bad acoustics and a mic that feeds back: short lines, obvious places to breathe, no clause longer than I can say in one breath, and a couple of spots marked where I should just stop and let it sit. And if you think one of the four things above can't be done alongside the others, tell me which one you'd cut and why before you hand me the draft, rather than quietly cutting it — I'll be able to tell.
