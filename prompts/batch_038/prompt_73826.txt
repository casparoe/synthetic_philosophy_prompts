I am a novelist, and for three years I have sat on the standards board of a literary magazine that publishes a weekly reader-advice column. As a novelist I spend my working life inducing readers to believe things about people who never existed, under an arrangement both sides understand; what has unsettled me about the matter now before us is that in a nonfiction column the arrangement runs the other way.

Last autumn the column's editor began drafting replies with a commercial language model, revising them substantially and signing them under her own name, as she has since the column began. Our policy clause, adopted in haste, reads in substance as follows: a system used in producing published text must not assert anything it does not believe, and must not be presented in a way that deceives readers about who is speaking.

The case. A reader described the year after her father's death. The published reply contained a first-person passage in which the columnist's voice recounts sitting with her own father through his last night — particular, tender, and so far as the board can establish accurate. The columnist confirms it happened roughly as written, though she had never published it, never told anyone, and never supplied the details to the system. She says the model produced them from the register of the letter, and that she kept the passage because it was true and because it was what that reader needed to receive.

Half the board wants the passage retracted and the editor censured, on the ground that a reader was caused to believe she was receiving testimony. The other half sees nothing to retract: no falsehood was asserted, and a rule under which an editor may not let stand a true sentence she did not originate would close the column.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate before our March meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition of this case — retract, correct, disclose, or let stand — and propose replacement language for the clause, specific enough that an editor could apply it to tomorrow's manuscript without convening us. I want it to distinguish what our clause is actually doing at each of its two limbs, to confront the possibility that the first limb is either vacuous or unsatisfiable where the system is concerned (including what weight, if any, to give the vendor's assurance that the model has internal states playing the role beliefs play in us), and to say where any wrong in this case is located: in the sentence, in the signature, or in the absence of a label, and whether any label we could print would genuinely repair it. Give the strongest available case for the disposition you reject.
