Colleague's been poking holes in a package of views I've been defending around the lottery and preface paradoxes, and I want an outside audit before I revise the paper. Here's the whole set:

1. Belief isn't just very high credence — it's a state in its own right; you can sit at .99 credence in p and still withhold outright belief.
2. The norm on outright belief is still probabilistic though: believing p is permissible just in case your evidence makes p at least t likely, for one fixed t that doesn't move from case to case.
3. Justification is closed under known entailment.
4. I don't know, and shouldn't believe, that my ticket will lose — even in a ten-million-ticket lottery.
5. The preface author is entirely rational: she's right to believe each claim in her book and right to believe that at least one is false, and she needn't retract either. And I mean the strong version — I'm happy to stipulate a book where each individual claim is *much* better supported than my lottery ticket's losing, say .999999 apiece, in a book long enough that the odds something in it is wrong are overwhelming.
6. I don't hold with the idea that purely statistical evidence is second-class, or that it can't ground knowledge. A probability's a probability; how it got generated isn't the norm's business.
7. I reject pragmatic encroachment: stakes and practical interests don't shift what you know or what you may believe.

Go through these and tell me which of them genuinely collide, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad. My colleague thinks 3 wrecks 5, and I've also been worrying whether 1 is even compatible with 2 — so say explicitly which of those dissolve on inspection and why, and don't pad the list with tensions that aren't there. For the real collisions: what's the cheapest revision, and what does it cost me downstream? I'm not attached to any particular item, so don't assume I'll fight to protect one.
