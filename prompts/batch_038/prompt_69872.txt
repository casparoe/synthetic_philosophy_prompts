So there's this argument I keep running into in the immigration literature — call it the welfare-magnet or impossible-trilemma thesis. Roughly: you can't have generous universal welfare provision, free movement across a border, and a democratic polity that will tollerating both at the same time; one of the three has to give, because redistribution supposedly only survives inside a bounded community of people who see each other as owing reciprocal obligations. Miller presses a version of it, Carens pushes back with the point that the arbitrariness of where you're born cuts against walling off a welfare state to protect it, and a lot of the open-borders crowd just denies the empirical premise outright.

I don't want it argued in the abstract. I want it run against a real episode, and I think the UK after the 2004 EU enlargement is close to a designed experiment. Here are the facts I'll supply so you don't have to guess at them:

- Eight central and eastern European states joined the EU on 1 May 2004. Most existing members applied transitional controls on their labour markets; the UK, Ireland and Sweden didn't.
- The official pre-accession estimate that everyone quoted afterwards was around 13,000 net migrants a year (I think the stated range was something like 5,000–13,000, but the exact number doesn't matter). Actual net arrivals over the next decade ran somewhere near a million, heavily Polish.
- A8 nationals were put on the Worker Registration Scheme, which ran until April 2011. They had to register when they took a new job. While registered they were shut out of out-of-work income-replacement benefits — income-based Jobseeker's Allowance and the like — until they'd completed 12 months of continuous employment. But they could claim in-work support (housing benefit, council tax benefit, tax credits) as long as they were actually employed.
- The fiscal studies done afterwards mostly found post-2004 A8 migrants were net contributors, not net claimants — low benefit take-up, young, working, relatively few dependants.
- Controls on Romanian and Bulgarian workers expired on 1 January 2014 and triggered a fresh political panic, even though actual flows were smaller than feared.
- Cameron renegotiated UK membership terms and got the "emergency brake" agreed in February 2016: permission to phase in in-work benefits over four years for newly arriving EU workers, usable once, for up to seven years. Four months later the country voted 51.9–48.1 to leave, with immigration the single biggest issue of the campaign.

What I want from you:

1. Map the thesis onto this properly before you evaluate it. Who are the relevant parties, what is the causal mechanism the trilemma claims is operating, and which of the above counts as evidence for or against it? The UK deliberately built a partial firewall around the welfare state while leaving the border open — so what does the outcome tell you?

2. I suspect the thesis blurs two very different things: drawing money out of a transfer system, and crowding locally rationed rival goods — school places, GP appointments, social housing, wages at the bottom of the labour market. The A8 case looks like almost pure congestion with very little transfer-draw. Does splitting those apart rescue the trilemma by identifying the real mechanism, or does it show the thesis was never actually about the welfare state?

3. Now the other direction. If the migrants were fiscally positive, does the episode support Carens — that a welfare state is compatible with, maybe even strengthened by, open movement? Or is that too quick, and what does the backlash itself say about the democratic-tolerance leg of the trilemma, which is the leg that actually seems to have snapped?

4. Be disciplined about where the theory is silent rather than making it speak. It doesn't look like it predicts the scale of the flows at all, and it certainly doesn't predict that a firewall secured by treaty in February 2016 would be worth nothing politically four months later. Say what it can't handle.

5. Then give me the verdict running both ways: does the case confirm, refine, or undercut the trilemma — and does it suggest the thesis was built for a different kind of case entirely, like humanitarian flows with high dependency ratios, and only got retrofitted onto this one?

No reading lists or citations, I can't look anything up and neither can you — I just want the reasoning. Do be explicit about your epistemic state though: put rough confidence on your main conclusions, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure of and say why. And if you think any of the facts I gave you above are wrong, flag that up front instead of quietly working around it.
