I am a senior in high school, and this Saturday I am giving the student toast at the retirement dinner of a teacher I will call Mr. Devereaux, who taught history here for thirty-one years and, for the last twelve of them, an elective senior seminar in informal logic. About eighty people will be in the room: his wife and three children, colleagues from four decades, the headmaster, and a large contingent of former students. Two of those former students are now graduate students in philosophy, one of them working in epistemic logic. My own logic teacher — the one who taught me the Church–Fitch knowability paradox last spring — will be seated at my table.

Mr. Devereaux had a habit that everyone who took his seminar remembers. On the first day of term he drew a narrow column down the left side of the chalkboard headed UNKNOWN, and he filled it over the year with true sentences nobody in the room knew: who actually wrote a disputed tract, what the second clause of a lost treaty said, whether a certain student's grandmother had been right about a certain summer. Every September he said the same thing to the new class, and he meant it entirely: that everything true can be found out, and that the only real question is whether anyone bothers to look. That sentence was on the card we gave him in June.

Two years ago he sealed an index card in an envelope, gave it to his daughter Claire, and told her to open it at his retirement dinner. He also told me — I was the student who stayed behind to argue with him that day, and he liked that — what was on the card in general terms: one true sentence, and he was confident that no human being would ever know it. I have not been told what the sentence is. Nobody has. Claire will read it aloud immediately after I sit down.

This is what makes the toast difficult, and I would like your help writing it. It has to honour a man's life's creed on his last night in the room, in front of his family, without asserting something false in front of two people who would know better. I have read enough to know that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is already known; that the derivation depends on knowledge being factive and on its distributing over conjunction; and that one common reply restricts the knowability principle to propositions whose being known is consistent. I do not want that material on the podium except in whatever single sentence a sixteen-year-old in the second row can follow. The rest of it belongs in your notes to me.

There is one moment I want the toast to notice, though I cannot find the phrasing. For the minute before Claire opens the envelope, if Mr. Devereaux is right about what he wrote, the room contains a true sentence that nobody knows — which is precisely the thing his motto says cannot happen. Opening the envelope makes the counterexample vanish, and it is not obvious whose side that proves to be on. I want the toast to hold that tension and to be affectionate rather than clever about it, and I want it to survive whatever the card turns out to say, including the possibility that the sentence is about its own never being known. It should sound like a seventeen-year-old who has thought too hard about this, not like a professor.

Please structure your reply in exactly these four sections, in this order:

1. **The toast** — 800 to 900 words, ready to read aloud, with one bracketed stage direction marking where Claire stands up.
2. **What the paradox is doing** — about 200 words, mapping where the knowability problem is genuinely load-bearing in the speech and where it is only local colour, and naming the one paragraph I should cut if the room is going flat.
3. **The card** — about 150 words on what the toast commits me to before the card is opened, why that commitment is safe no matter what it says, and how the last thirty seconds should be delivered if the sentence turns out to undermine itself.
4. **Objections from my table** — about 150 words giving the three sharpest things the epistemic-logic graduate student could say to me afterwards, and my answer to each.

Please do not include a bibliography, further reading, or any citations; I want the speech and the notes, and nothing I would have to look up.
