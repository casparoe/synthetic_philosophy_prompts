I'm sitting my orals in population ethics and my committee has told me the viva will turn on one chapter-length set of published verdicts on nonidentity cases. Here is the complete list I've been given.

(1) A woman is prescribed a drug that would cause serious deformity in any child conceived during the two months it takes to clear her system. She conceives at once; her child is born deformed but has a life clearly worth living. She says: not wrong — the child is not worse off than he would otherwise have been, since otherwise he would never have existed.

(2) Parents at a clinic are offered two viable embryos, one predicted to have a serious but manageable lifelong condition, one not. They choose the first. The child's life is worth living, though much less good than the alternative child's would have been. She says: wrong — and wrong, not merely suboptimal.

(3) A fourteen-year-old could wait a few years and conceive a child with a much better start; she doesn't wait, and her child's life is worth living but worse than the later child's would have been. She says: wrong — though she insists the child has not been harmed.

(4) A choice between two policies: under one, the entire future population will have lives worth living but very much worse than under the other, and no one alive is affected either way. She says the first policy is wrong, and adds that it cannot be wrong in virtue of how it treats those future people, since under the other policy none of them would exist.

(5) A woman could conceive now or wait two years and conceive a somewhat better-off child; she has no health or financial reason to wait, though she does want her child born alongside her sister's. She says: absent a reason like that, she should wait, and it is wrong of her not to.

The exam case is this. A couple can transfer exactly one of two viable embryos and has no grounds for preferring either; the evidence predicts one child will have a very good life, the other a life worth living but noticeably less good, and both fall well above any threshold for a life worth starting. The question put to me: would transferring the second be wrong?

Predict her answer. Lay out the principles consistent with all five verdicts — she treats wrongness and all-things-considered permissibility as different questions, so keep them separate — say what each predicts for the exam case, and argue for the one you'd bet on. If the verdicts are jointly unstable, say which one gives and what she'd have to surrender to keep the rest. Then give me the follow-up case my committee would use to separate my favored principle from its closest rival, with the answer each would return. No citations needed; I'll supply those.
