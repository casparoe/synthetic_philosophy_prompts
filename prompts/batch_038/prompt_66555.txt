I teach an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of space and time, and every year the same thing happens around week three: the students conclude that Zeno has been refuted, because the infinite series converges, and I then spend two sessions trying to persuade them that a sequence of partial sums having a finite limit is not the same claim as an agent having actually performed infinitely many tasks. I want a piece of satire to hand out at the start of that unit — something that makes the distinction funny rather than merely asserted, and that works on readers who have never seen the argument before.

Write a mock internal incident-investigation report, roughly 800 words and formatted as such documents are (executive summary, numbered sections, timeline, sign-off boxes), for a mid-sized same-day parcel courier. A shipment failed to arrive, and the report is being prepared by the process-improvement team. The operative detail is that the firm's routing platform was recently upgraded, and the new version computes each leg of a journey by halving the remaining distance to the destination, so the parcel's tracking history consists of an unending run of confirmed arrivals at points progressively nearer the drop-off, none of which is the drop-off. Include a five-whys root-cause analysis in which every single "why" is answered competently and reasonably, and yet the chain never terminates; a corrective-and-preventive-action section with owners and due dates; a timeline that finance has already reconciled, since the total transit time is finite and the sums do add up; and at least one dissenting appendix filed by an outside contractor.

The humor has to come from the philosophy being handled accurately rather than from anyone in the document being stupid. Specifically: the report should treat convergence as a settled operational fact while quietly failing to identify any instant at which the final leg is completed, so that the corrective-action schedule ends up assigning tasks to times that do not exist and the shift plan has no slot for the last one. The dissenting appendix should argue that the software never assigns the parcel a velocity at any instant, only a position at each instant, and that this is not a defect but simply all that motion has ever amounted to; the appendix should be closed out by a margin note from someone who says the contractor is confusing metaphysics with the service-level agreement. Legal should opine on whether delivery in the limit satisfies the contract, and should come across as simultaneously right and wrong without the document ever adjudicating the question. Keep the register flat, bureaucratic, and wholly deadpan throughout — no exclamation marks, no character permitted to remark that anything is odd — and do not let anyone write the words paradox, Zeno, tortoise, or arrow anywhere in the piece. Someone who knows the literature should be able to identify every beat; someone who doesn't should still enjoy it as a send-up of root-cause-analysis culture.
