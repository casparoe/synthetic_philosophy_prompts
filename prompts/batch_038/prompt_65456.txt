I am the certification lead for a farm-animal welfare scheme: my auditors inspect poultry, swine and dairy operations against a written standard, grade what they find, and decide whether a site keeps or loses its certificate. I helped draft the current standard and have spent eleven years working inside its vocabulary, which is fixed and which I know completely. Its moving parts are these. Welfare is assessed across five domains — nutrition, environment, health, behaviour, and the mental state the first four produce — each scored from evidence, with a standing distinction between animal-based measures (lameness scoring, feather or tail loss, body condition, fear-of-human testing) and resource-based measures (stocking density, enrichment provision, ventilation rates). Those scores feed an overall determination of whether a production system gives animals a life not worth living, a life worth living, or a good life. Findings are graded as critical, major or minor non-conformities: criticals are non-compensable, so a single failure of a critical control voids certification no matter how well everything else went, while majors and minors are compensable, meaning a shortfall can be offset by demonstrated performance elsewhere or discharged through a corrective action plan with a deadline. Slaughter enters the standard only as a process measure — stunning efficacy, time to loss of consciousness, handling at the point of kill.

Two structural features of the standard trouble me. Nothing in it registers death as a harm to the animal that dies: we score how an animal is killed, never that it is killed, and we have no line item for the fact that these animals were bred for the purpose in the first place. And our critical controls are justified in the standard's own preamble entirely by the intensity and duration of suffering, never by the fact that an animal is being used as a means to someone else's ends.

I would like you to restate Nozick's position on animals — the argument in the animal section of Anarchy, State, and Utopia, together with the tiered moral structures he sketches in the same stretch of the book: the maximization-only structure, the structure that adds stringent side constraints for persons alone, and the structure that extends a weaker, boundary-respecting constraint to animals that stops short of full inviolability — in the vocabulary of my standard, as faithfully as you can manage. Do the translation claim by claim rather than as a summary of Nozick. For each of his substantive commitments, say what audit object it would have to become: a new domain, a new measure inside an existing domain, a compensable deficit, a non-compensable critical control, a question about the scope of which animals the standard covers, or something with no home in the scheme at all. Work through his finger-snapping case and his baseball-bat case explicitly, showing what my scheme would actually record in each and whether that record tracks his verdict.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I want most. Which of his claims survive translation into compensable and non-compensable findings? Which survive only under distortion — and name the distortion? Which cannot be expressed at all, given that my scheme is indifferent to whether an animal exists, silent on being used as a means, and treats killing as a process parameter rather than a harm? Do not smuggle my standard's presuppositions into the translation and present them as Nozick's: where he would reject a premise my scheme takes for granted, flag the conflict instead of absorbing it. I am particularly interested in one asymmetry. My scheme has an obvious home for the brutalization argument that Nozick raises and dismisses — stockperson attitude, handling competence, staff training and rotation — while the ground he actually relies on seems to have none. Tell me whether that asymmetry is real, and what it means if it is.

Finish by taking a position rather than balancing the books. Does the residue show that my vocabulary is missing something — and if so, what would a measure capable of registering it have to look like, given that it must be observable by an auditor standing in a barn with a clipboard — or does it show that his boundary notion, whatever work it does inside a philosophical argument, is not the kind of thing that can guide a decision at the level where decisions get made? If the honest answer is both, say which parts belong to which.

No bibliography, reading list or citations. Where you are unsure of Nozick's position on some point, say so plainly rather than filling the gap.
