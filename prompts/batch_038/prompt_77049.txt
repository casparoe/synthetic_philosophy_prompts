I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences and have been asked to contribute a chapter to an edited volume on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment. Every other contributor is an engineer. My own formation is in continental philosophy, and my competence in the target idiom is bookish rather than practical: I can follow an argument about objective specification, scalable oversight, distribution shift, and the inner/outer alignment distinction, and I know in outline what reinforcement learning from human feedback does to a model, but I would not reliably recognize a false claim about current practice dressed in the right jargon. Please write as though your reader is that person — fluent enough to check your work, not fluent enough to correct it silently.

The task is a translation, and I want it done thesis by thesis rather than by loose analogy. Take Foucault's account of disciplinary power, as developed in Discipline and Punish (1975) and in the first volume of The History of Sexuality (1976), and restate it in the vocabulary that volume's other contributors actually use: reward models and their training data, specification gaming, monitoring and the limits of monitoring, generalization from the training distribution, evaluation suites, benchmarks and leaderboards, corrigibility, sandboxing, the behavior of a trained policy when the supervision that shaped it is absent. For each of the following claims, say what it would be if it were stated in that idiom, and say what the translation buys — what the engineering vocabulary makes precise that Foucault left gestural.

The claims to be carried over, in paraphrase:

1. The panoptic arrangement, in which an inmate cannot verify at any given moment whether he is being observed but knows that he may be, and who therefore eventually takes over the work of observation himself, so that the apparatus continues to function whether or not anyone is watching.
2. The claim that discipline is productive rather than repressive: that it manufactures docility and usefulness in a single operation, instead of suppressing a spontaneity that was already there.
3. The associated rejection of the repressive hypothesis about sexuality — that the nineteenth century did not silence sex but multiplied discourse about it, and that power works here by inciting confession and generating knowledge rather than by prohibition.
4. The examination as the procedure that fuses surveillance with judgment, and in the course of which the individual is constituted as a describable, recordable, comparable case; and the further claim that the human sciences emerged from this documentation of individuals rather than the documentation arising from a prior science.
5. Normalization: the fixing of a mean, the assignment of every individual a position in a distribution, and the penalty attached to deviation, where the standard is immanent to the practice rather than borrowed from law.
6. The claim that power and knowledge presuppose one another, so that there is no neutral standpoint from which to describe the arrangement one is inside.

Now the constraints, which matter more to me than the list. Avoid two failure modes. The first is domestication: rendering Foucault into propositions an incentive designer would already accept, and then reporting the translation as successful because it went smoothly — the smoothness may itself be evidence that the difficult content was dropped somewhere. The second is the reverse error: loading the technical terms with moral weight they do not carry, so that a reward model is quietly made to mean domination, and the residue then looks like a discovery about machine learning when it is only a stipulation about words. Wherever you press a technical term into service, state what you are relying on it to carry.

Then give me the residue report, in three registers: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in the target vocabulary. For every item in the second and third registers, deliver a verdict on the source of the failure — whether it exposes a structural assumption the engineering idiom cannot give up, or whether it shows that the Foucauldian thesis was less determinate than it sounded, doing rhetorical work where it appeared to do descriptive work.

Two residues I want addressed explicitly, because a colleague and I disagree about them. First, the thesis that the disciplinary apparatus functions without anyone at its center. In the technical idiom every reward presupposes a principal whose preferences it proxies. Is power without a subject genuinely inexpressible there, or is it only a way of saying that the aggregate behavior of many partial optimizers need not express anyone's intention — which that idiom says routinely, in the language of emergent objectives, multi-principal aggregation, and specification gaming at scale? If it is the latter, does the thesis lose its critical force once it becomes a claim about aggregation rather than about domination? Second, Foucault's refusal to rank normalizations as better or worse. Alignment work is defined by a standard of success; his claim is that the standard is produced by the practice that applies it. Is that a claim the technical vocabulary cannot state, or one it can state and dismiss? What is lost and what gained on each side?

Close with a single judgment, and commit to it rather than distributing the credit. Does the residue, taken as a whole, indict the target framework for presupposing a principal and a fixed standard it cannot interrogate, or indict the source for substituting a vocabulary of power for the ordinary observation that behavior is shaped by incentives whose standards are socially set? I need to be able to tell my engineering colleagues whether they should read Foucault as making a claim they have missed or as making, more obscurely, a claim they have already made more precisely.
