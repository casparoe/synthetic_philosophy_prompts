Second-year law at Manchester, jurisprudence seminar, and our tutor has said we can do the long essay on a work of fiction provided we actually do the jurisprudence and not just use the novel as wallpaper. I've picked The Picture of Dorian Gray against the Hart–Devlin argument about legal moralism, partly because the timing is so convenient — Devlin was answering the Wolfenden Report in his British Academy lecture in 1959, and Wilde himself ended up convicted under the gross indecency provision bolted onto the Criminal Law Amendment Act in 1885, the one that reached acts in private between consenting adults — and partly because I have a hunch I can't quite argue yet and I want you to prressure-test it before I commit to it in an outline.

My hunch is that everyone who pairs these two makes Lord Henry a Hartian, because he preaches yielding to every temptation, sneers at conventional sexual morals and treats living experimentally as the whole point, and then reads the novel as a refutation of Hart on the grounds that this brand of liberalism ruins a life. I think that's too quick. The more interesting figure is Basil Hallward, who is essentially Devlin's reasonable man walking around: the person who looks at Dorian's corruption, feels horror and disgust, and takes that feeling to be a report about the world rather than about himself. Devlin's whole test — find the morality of a society by asking what the ordinary decent person feels intolerance, indignation and disgust about — gets an awkward trial in Basil, because Basil's disgust is inseperable from his love and his aesthetic worship of the sitter, and the novel punishes him for voicing it by having him killed in the very room where the thing he's disgusted by is hanging. So does Wilde show the disgust test working, failing, or working only at a cost Devlin never mentions, namely that the people best placed to feel the disgust are the ones most compromised by it? And what do we do with the fact that the society in the book carries on perfectly well — it gossips, it goes pale when he comes into a room, it eventually closes ranks, and it never disintegrates? Is that a counterexample to Devlin's disintegration thesis, or is the gossip-and-exclusion machine just his informal enforcement getting on with the job without the criminal law, which would make it a point for Hart? Please get the thesis and Hart's objection to it right, including the slide between a society as an organised structure and a society as a shared culture, and say whether the novel gives Devlin any evidential support whatever.

Then three harder bits I'd like folded in. First, the portrait is a wrong that leaves no mark on anybody else: Dorian's face stays clean right up to the eve of his thirty-eighth birthday, when Basil finally forces the issue, so which side does that help? It looks like a case where the harm principle has nothing to bite on and yet something appalling is plainly happening — but is what's happening damage to a shared morality in Devlin's sense, or only damage to Dorian's relation to himself, and does that difference matter for whether you'd want a law about it? Second, Devlin thinks conscience and reputation aren't enough and that's why you need enforcement; but the punishments in the novel all come from somewhere else — Sibyl's death, James Vane, Alan Campbell destroying the body and then killing himself, and finally Dorian going at the canvas with the same knife and being found as a withered old man identified by his rings. Does that ending actually help Hart by making legal enforcement look redundant? Third, the French novel Lord Henry sends him: the book treats a text as a cause of corruption, which is precisely the premise behind the obscenity half of legal moralism, while Wilde's own preface denies that books have anything to do with morals at all. Does the novel contradict itself there, and does it matter for whether culture-corrupts is the kind of claim Devlin's position needs? Tell me plainly where you think the Basil hunch is over-clever, and where the novel refuses to be either man's illustration — Lord Henry is the obvious place, since he's got the liberal vocabulary and none of Hart's commitments about consent and harm. Finish by saying what the novel adds that a straight argument can't get: my feeling is it's something about what enforcement looks like when no state is doing it, just faces and dinner tables, but I can't defend that yet. Describe the scenes you'd hang each claim on rather than quoting them, I've got the text in front of me and I'd rather you didn't guess at wording. No reading lists or bibliographies please, my tutor hates padding.
