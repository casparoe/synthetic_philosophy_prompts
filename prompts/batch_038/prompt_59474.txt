So here's something that's been bugging me about how clinical genetics actually works, and I want the assumptions laid out rather than an opinion on whether the system is good. A sequencing lab looks at a change in someone's DNA and sorts it into five bins — benign, likely benign, uncertain significance, likely pathogenic, pathogenic — and the guideline defines the two likely- categories as something like 90% certainty, a cutoff the people who wrote it openly describe as arbitrary. Anything that lands in the uncertain bin is treated as carrying no usable information: counselors are told not to let it change a medical decision, and plenty of labs don't even report it to the ordering doctor. But the bins arent stable — different labs put the same variant in different ones, and a lab will sometimes move a variant years later once new data turns up, at which point somebody has to decide whether to track the original patient down and tell them the answer changed. Seperately, when a clinical test turns up findings nobody was looking for, most programs only hand back results in genes where something can actually be done about the risk, and stay quiet about everything else, including things that are serious but untreatable.

What picture of genetic information does this whole setup presuppose? Is it committed to DNA being a code carrying a determinate message that we're simply reading badly, or does the disagreement-and-reclassification part point somewhere else entirely? Reconstruct the implicit theory, then separate what the practice strictly can't do without from what it merely sits comfortably with — and tell me whether the 90% cutoff and the actionability filter rest on one view or on two different views bolted together. Finally, say what would have to change in the reporting pipeline if we adopted the rival view that a variant's meaning isn't a property of the sequence alone but of the sequence plus a population plus a current state of knowledge, including whether the uncertain bin would still count as empty of information under that view.
