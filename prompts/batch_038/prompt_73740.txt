I do copy-editing for a series of new translations of ancient Greek and Latin authors, and the longer I spend with the critical editions underlying them, the more convinced I am that the whole craft rests on an epistemology nobody in the field ever states explicitly. I would like you to state it, and then to test it.

The practice, as I understand it, is this. Almost no work of antiquity survives in the author's own hand; what survives is copies of copies, made over centuries, disagreeing among themselves. The editor does not simply follow the oldest manuscript, and she does not take a vote among the copies. She sorts the copies into a family tree on the basis of errors they share that are unlikely to have arisen twice independently — two witnesses containing the same piece of nonsense are taken to descend from an ancestor that already contained it — and she then reconstructs that ancestor, which is itself a lost manuscript nobody has ever seen. Individual readings are chosen by asking which candidate best explains all the others as corruptions of it. The direction of error is assumed to be predictable: scribes smooth out awkward phrasing, expand what has been abbreviated, harmonize a quoted line toward the version they remember, and gloss difficulties; they do not, as a rule, make an easy text harder. Hence a standing preference for the rougher, more difficult reading, subject to the qualification that it must still be a reading the author could plausibly have written. Hence also the rule that the sheer number of copies supporting a reading counts for nothing in itself, since a hundred may descend from a single bad ancestor. And when no surviving copy preserves what the editor judges necessary for sense or metre, she may print her own conjecture, flagged as such in the apparatus.

Meanwhile the historians who use these editions insist that our access to the past runs entirely through surviving traces — documents and material remains — and treat claims unsupported by any trace as speculation. There is also a persistent minority objection, pressed by readers attached to the text as a whole community actually used it rather than as specialists reconstruct it, that the reading carried by the great majority of copies deserves the weight the method denies it.

So: what theory of knowledge, and of testimony in particular, does the editorial practice presuppose, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Please separate what the method strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it comfortably — does the family-tree procedure need error to be biased in a knowable direction, or only to be non-random? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible accounts of justification would equally license printing a conjecture? Is there a genuine inconsistency between conjectural emendation and the documentary principle, or is the posited archetype already the same kind of move, so that emendation adds nothing new in principle? What happens to the method when the tradition turns out not to be a tree, because scribes copied from two exemplars at once? What does the whole enterprise presuppose about what a work is — that there was ever one determinate text rather than a fluid tradition that hardened only gradually — and is that presupposition carrying more weight than the editors admit? Does the majority objection retain any force once the genealogical picture is granted? And if we replaced the implicit theory with an openly probabilistic one, in which editions reported degrees of confidence instead of a clean reading text with all the doubt relegated to the apparatus, what in the practice would have to change, and what would we lose?

Please answer in continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bolding, no tables — as if you were writing me a letter. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want the argument.
