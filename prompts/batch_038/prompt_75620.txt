I write literary fiction — three novels out, twenty-odd years at it, I teach a craft class in the fall — so my native language is point of view, psychic distance, free indirect style, round versus flat characters, what a character wants in a given scene, how much backstory you can load on before the thing stops moving. Its the vocabulary I actually think in. I have no philosophy training beyond what I've read on my own.

The book I'm stuck on is narrated entirely from the outside of one woman. Eleven chapters, each a different person's account of her — her brother, the man she left, her cleaner, a debt collector, the daughter she barely raised — and she herself never gets a chapter, never gets interiority, never speaks except as reported by someone else. I built it that way after reading around in the African communitarian writing on personhood: the idea that a person isn't a finished self-contained thing that then enters into relationships, that you become a person through others, that it comes in degrees rather than flipping on at birth, that somebody can be fully human biologically and still be a very poor sort of person, and that the community stretches past death so the dead are still participants. There's also a fight inside the tradition about whether the individual has anything standing left over — one side says the community is straightforwardly prior, the other says that's too strong, that people have some autonomy and some claims that don't derive from the group.

So: translate that whole package into my craft vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. What is the novel-writing counterpart of personhood-comes-in-degrees? What corresponds to the claim that relationships constitute the self rather than merely reveal it — and please don't just answer "characterization through other characters"; tell me whether those two claims actually come apart in craft terms or whether the second one collapses into the first. Where does the quarrel between the strong and the moderate communitarian show up as a structural decision about how to build a book — whose chapters get interiority, whose don't, who's allowed to be unreliable? Then give me the residue report: what translated cleanly, what you had to bend or fudge to make it fit, and what has no expression at all in the language of POV and psychic distance. And take a side on that residue — is the craft vocabulary missing something real, because the novel as a form is individualist all the way down to its plumbing, or is the relational claim less radical than it sounds once you put it in a language where every working novelist already knows a character only exists in relation to other characters?

I'm asking partly because I need to know whether my book is actually built on an idea or whether it just has a hole in the middle wearing a philosophical epigraph. So dont be diplomatic about it. But also tell me where you're least sure of yourself: which of your residue verdicts would you bet the smallest amount on, and what would change your mind — what argument, or what way of handling a narration, would make you call it the other way? Point at where you think your own reasoning is most likely to break.
