You are a philosopher working at the intersection of normative ethics, social ontology, and the formal study of social conventions. I am drafting the conceptual groundwork for a book chapter that will argue that some everyday conventions generate genuine obligations while others generate only etiquette, and I want the specification fixed before I engage any candidate theory. I have found that most treatments of this material argue for or against a theory by pointing at a single case, so I would like the full list of requirements first. Please think carefully, and proceed in the order below.

**Step 1 — Brainstorm (unpolished is fine).** Before writing anything you intend to stand behind, list every candidate requirement you can think of that a theory in this area might plausibly be asked to meet. Do not yet judge which are legitimate. Then prune, merge, and rank, and say in a sentence or two what you discarded and why.

**Step 2 — The desiderata.** This is the main deliverable: a numbered list of requirements on an adequate theory of the normativity of everyday social conventions. For each requirement, give:

1. a one-sentence statement, precise enough that a theory could be shown to satisfy or fail it;
2. the case or consideration that motivates it, described in ordinary language;
3. why it is a requirement rather than a preference;
4. a classification — non-negotiable for any theory in this area, or demanded only by a particular camp (name the camp, and say whether you think the demand is question-begging);
5. what failure looks like: the thing a theory missing this requirement would be committed to saying that is clearly false.

Your list must actually resolve the following cases, which I have collected and which I do not think the standard treatments handle well:

- **The dead rule.** In my building everyone writes their name on a clipboard by the door when they take the shared conference-room key. The locks were replaced two years ago, the key opens nothing, and the room is on camera. The rule survives intact. Is complying with it still an obligation, a courtesy, or nothing at all — and what, if anything, distinguishes it from waiting your turn in a queue?
- **The announced opt-out.** A colleague emails the floor saying that he will no longer sign the clipboard, and explaining that the rule serves no purpose. The email itself feels like a violation. Can a convention-generated obligation be dissolved unilaterally by announcement? If so, why doesn't the same hold for the person at the front of a queue who announces she isn't waiting? If not, what is the difference?
- **The long-stay guest.** A researcher spends three months with a household that expects every guest to accept a second helping of meat at each meal, and where refusal is read as an insult. She is a vegetarian, and she has been eating their food and using their generator the whole time. Which community's conventions bind her? Does three months of participation change the answer relative to a three-day visit, and if so, at what point and why?
- **The convention worth breaking.** At a firm it is enforced by quiet ridicule that women dress more formally than men for the same client meetings. Compliance is cheap, refusal is costly and self-indulgent, and the norm is unjust. Is there an obligation to comply, to resist, both, or neither? Does your account have to converge with the ordinary moral assessment of the norm's content, or can a convention generate a real reason to do what is, all things considered, wrong?
- **The empty street.** Jaywalking at three in the morning on a wide, deserted avenue, in a city where the norm is otherwise strictly observed and where a few people on the sidewalk can see you cross. No one is harmed and no coordination fails; the only cost is to the norm itself. Is any obligation left, and of what kind?
- **The queue that resists money.** At a public clinic there is a strong local prohibition on buying and selling places in line, even where such a trade would help both parties and hurt no one else. A theory should explain why some conventions resist market substitution (queues, triage, jury service, gift-giving) while others invite it (hailing a cab, subletting a parking spot) — or should say why that contrast is not a datum at all.
- **The two equilibria.** Driving on the right. Nothing whatever can be said for either side, yet the theory must explain why the mere existence of the regularity gives me a reason to follow it rather than a reason to agitate for a switch.
- **The locally sane, globally ruinous convention.** In a fishing village the norm is to land as much as you can before your neighbor does; everyone knows the stock is collapsing; the norm is stable and universally observed.

**Step 3 — Separate requirements from prejudices.** Several demands are routinely pressed on theories here: that the obligation must be *content-independent* (the reason to comply is that it is the rule, not what the rule says); that a theory must show violators are blameworthy and not merely imprudent; that conventions must not be reducible to mutual advantage; that the obligation must be explained by the convention's function or purpose. For each, say whether it is a genuine desideratum or a camp prejudice imported from somewhere else (legal philosophy, the practice-based defense of rule-utilitarianism, the anti-reductionist side of social ontology), and argue briefly rather than asserting.

**Step 4 — The impossibility result.** Identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. This is the part I most want to see. Do not merely assert the tension: give the argument, state what has to go, and say which requirement a reasonable theorist should abandon and what the surviving theory would then have to concede.

**Step 5 — Exclusionary power.** Tell me which single desideratum does the most work in knocking out candidate theories, and which one is most often treated as central despite excluding almost nothing.

**Step 6 — Brief scoring.** Then, and only then, a short scorecard. My own tentative view, which I want tested rather than flattered, is what I have been calling the *burden-transfer account*: a convention obligates S just in case identifiable others have already borne costs by complying in a way that S's compliance would complete or reciprocate; the strength of the obligation tracks how specific and how recent the transferred burden is; it lapses once the burden is discharged or could be discharged by other means; and it requires no consent, only that S stand to receive the completed benefit. Say which desiderata it passes and which it fails, naming the case that shows each failure; say whether the "identifiability" clause is doing illegitimate work (does a burden borne by a million anonymous drivers count?); and say whether this is a genuine rival to fair-play accounts or a fair-play account with the politics hidden. Then score it in a table against four familiar alternatives: a coordination-plus-common-knowledge account of convention augmented with moral machinery to get from prudence to obligation; a practice-based or rule-utilitarian account; a joint-commitment account; and a straightforward fair-play account. Rows are the desiderata from Step 2, cells are pass/partial/fail with one clause of justification.

**Constraints.** No bibliography, no reading lists, no citations — I am working offline and cannot check any of them. You may use a theorist's or a school's name as a label for a camp, but then state the view's content yourself in a sentence or two rather than deferring to authority. Define any term of art on first use. American English. Roughly 1,600 to 2,400 words, with at least seventy percent of the length on Steps 2 through 4. Do not write the chapter, and do not defend a theory of your own beyond the scorecard. Finally: if my specification is itself misconceived somewhere — if one of my cases is a poor test of a theory, if two of them are really the same case, or if there is a requirement that matters more than anything I have listed — say so plainly rather than working around it.
