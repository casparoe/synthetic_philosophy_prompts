I am applying for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship at an interdisciplinary centre for Holocaust studies and social theory, and I need a proposal of roughly 1,300 words, set out under clear headings and written throughout in British English.

My half-formed idea is this. The quarrel over whether the Holocaust is singular or comparable, and Bauman's sociological claim that the Holocaust was made possible by ordinary features of modern rational-bureaucratic order rather than by a rupture in civilisation, are almost always conducted separately — the first as a normative and historiographical dispute, the second as social theory. Yet both make the same formal move: they place the event inside a wider class. My suspicion is that generalisation runs in two distinct directions here, and that the difference between them has been left implicit by everyone. Nolte's appeal to the Gulag generalised laterally, setting the Holocaust alongside the crimes of another regime so that the burden of guilt was distributed; Habermas's objection amounted to the claim that this redistributed rather than discharged responsibility, and so normalised. Bauman generalises upward and inward, treating the Holocaust as an instance of a condition the analyst and the reader still inhabit, which implicates rather than distributes. If that asymmetry can be stated rigorously, it may explain why comparability is objectionable in one form and indispensable in another, and it may also show what the sociological thesis has to give up in order not to lapse into the apologetic pattern it was never meant to serve.

Please turn this into a proposal containing: (a) a precise statement of the research question, formulated so that it admits of a determinate answer rather than a balanced survey; (b) an account of why the existing positions leave the question open — defenders of singularity, defenders of generic-genocide comparison, functionalist historiography, and the sociological modernity thesis each fail to address it, and I would like the proposal to say how each fails in its own way; (c) the method: what I would actually do, month by month, across the twelve months, together with a defence of why a conceptual project rather than an archival one is the right instrument; (d) what a successful result would look like — I imagine a test, or a small set of conditions, for distinguishing legitimate from illegitimate generalisation, applied to three or four live controversies — and what would count as failure, including the genuine possibility that the distinction collapses, for instance because every upward generalisation can be redescribed as a lateral one, or because the difference turns out to rest on the speaker's intentions and audience rather than on the form of the claim; (e) a paragraph taking seriously the most obvious objection, namely that the asymmetry is rhetorical or political rather than conceptual, and saying how the project would meet it; (f) a candid assessment of scope, identifying what I should cut if twelve months proves too little.

Please do not include a bibliography, a footnote apparatus, or a literature review organised by citation; I will supply references myself. You may name positions, thinkers and well-known arguments, but do not invent attributions or paraphrase-as-quote: where you are unsure who holds a view, describe the view instead of naming its author. Keep the register formal and the argument visible on the page.
