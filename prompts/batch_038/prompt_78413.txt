I am auditing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics this term, and our last two sessions have been on intuitionism: Brouwer's refusal to treat the principle of the excluded middle as a general licence, Heyting's account of what it is to possess a proof of each kind of statement, and the Kripke-model picture on which truth grows monotonically as one moves up a tree of possible stages, so that a claim can be neither affirmed nor ruled out at one node and affirmed at a later one. For the final session each of us has been asked to bring something non-expository — a diagram, a recording, a poem — that makes one of these ideas available intuitively to a room of people with quite uneven backgrounds, and mine will be read aloud. I have chosen a poem, and I would like your help drafting it.

The form I want is a pantoum of five quatrains, twenty lines, following the interlocking rule exactly: the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear verbatim as the first and third lines of the next, and the last stanza closes the circuit in the canonical way, taking the second and fourth lines of the penultimate stanza as its own first and third, the poem's third line as its second, and the poem's first line as its last. Rhyme is optional, and lines of roughly ten to fourteen syllables would suit; please do not contort the sense to reach a rhyme. The subject is double negation — specifically, the fact that from *it is not the case that there is no such thing* one may not conclude that there is such a thing — and I want the form to carry that argument rather than decorate it. The pantoum is ideal for the purpose because its returns are word-for-word identical yet differently placed, and because the form can never end on a fresh assertion: it must come back to what it has already said. That is precisely the intuitionist's complaint about a proof of a double negation — the words return, the entitlement does not, and nothing has been put in your hands. Please exploit that, letting each returning line mean something slightly less, or something other, than it meant the first time, and letting the two genuinely new lines in each stanza be where the poem does its work, the way a construction is the only thing that can introduce new mathematical content. Note also that the closing convention makes the poem's last line its first: I would like that recurrence to be the moment the reader feels the argument shut without ever delivering the object it was about.

Three requirements of accuracy and one of tact. First, the poem must not suggest that intuitionists reject the excluded middle across the board: for a decidable question about some particular object — whether this given number is prime, say — the disjunction is perfectly assertible, and it would be a real error to portray the view as a general refusal to choose. What is refused is the blanket licence for claims whose decidability has not been established. Second, get the directions right: an assertion does yield its own double negation, and a triple negation does collapse back to a single one; it is only the one step backward, from two negations to none, that is blocked. Third, if you can find a place for the fact that some constructivists are willing to take exactly that step when the predicate in question is decidable by a finite computation — a principle accepted by one school of constructivist mathematicians and rejected by another, and a live fault line rather than a settled matter — the penultimate stanza would be a natural home for it; include it only if it survives as poetry and not as a footnote in verse. As for tact: keep proper names and terms of art out of the poem entirely, so that no line resorts to words like constructive, intuitionistic, or excluded middle, or names a logician; the logic has to be recognizable from the movement of the lines alone. For the concrete register, something in the neighborhood of a search you can prove will never fail without ever being handed what it finds, or a room shown not to be empty without anyone located in it, would serve — though I would rather you chose the image than have me dictate it. Please give me the poem by itself, followed by no more than three sentences telling me where it locates the one step it refuses to take.
