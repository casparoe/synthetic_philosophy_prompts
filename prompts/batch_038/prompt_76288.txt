So, six weeks into a new job as assistant registrar at a mid-size sculpture museum, and I keep running into what feels like a philosophy problem dressed up as paperwork. I want you to take the metaphysics hiding in our forms seriously, not as a thought experiment but as an account of what we actually do.

Four things I've personally handled this month:

1. Our insurance schedule lists an artist's small clay maquette and the finished marble statue it was made for as two seperate items — two accession numbers, two valuations. The maquette is the one the artist apparently threw out and a dealer rescued years later; it's now worth a lot on its own, and we insure it for more per kilo than the marble by a wide margin.

2. A bronze in the collection lost a hand in a move in the 1980s. Conservation took a mold off an intact sister cast, poured a new hand, welded it on. Nobody here thinks we now have a different artwork, and the wall label still gives the original date with no mention of it.

3. This museum's founder licensed the estate of one of our artists to pour new bronzes from the original plasters after the artist died. Those posthumous casts get accessioned as works by the artist, dated "cast 1994," and they hang in the same gallery as the lifetime ones. Curators will absolutely go to the mat if you call them copies.

4. A donor has offered us a high-resolution 3D scan plus a CNC-carved marble reproduction of a statue we already own — carved from our own scan data, same stone type, with the estate's written permission. The acquisitions committee got visibly nervous about calling that an artwork at all, and it's currently sitting in a kind of limbo.

It's the gap between 2 and 4 that's bugging me. Replacing matter with matter, routine; realizing the same form in fresh matter, nearly fraudulent. On paper those look like the same move in opposite directions.

What view about what an artwork IS does this whole package presuppose? Reconstruct it as precisely as you can — is the work the shaped matter, the form, the artist's act or achievement, the historical lineage running back to the artist's hand, some four-dimensional object spread across the career of the thing? Which rival views does the practice actively rule out, as opposed to just sitting awkwardly beside them? And where is it underdetermined — where would two incompatible metaphysics generate exactly the same paperwork, meaning my forms aren't evidence for anything?

Then tell me whether 2 and 4 can be separated on principled grounds, or whether the museum is just squeamish about the market. And finally: if we flipped to the opposite view — say, that the work just is the shaped matter standing in the gallery — what would concretely have to change? Accession practice, labels, valuations, what counts as a loss when something breaks?

You're a philosopher who works on material constitution and artifact ontology and who has actually spent time in a collections department. Think in the register of people who argue about statues and clay — sortals, coincidence, temporal parts, relational definitions of art — but use the vocabulary only where it earns its place; don't lecture me. Before you commit to a reconstruction, brainstorm out loud: list the candidate theories that could license all four cases, and say which ones fail on which case. Then pick one and argue for it.

Be committed rather than diplomatic. If one reconstruction is clearly the best fit, say so, give me a rough confidence level, and say what would move you. No on-the-one-hand survey that ends with "it's complicated." Also flag the places where you're reconstructing charitably — where the museum probably has no view at all and you're supplying one to make the practice look principled.

And push back on me if the premise is off. If you think the exercise itself is confused — if insurance and accession practice is driven by money, tax treatment and donor relations and carries no metaphysical commitments whatsoever, so there's nothing there to reconstruct — say that up front plainly instead of humoring me. Same if you think I've misdescribed one of the four cases, or that 2 and 4 aren't really comparable in the way I'm assuming.
