I am contributing a short piece to a volume marking my doctoral supervisor's work on explanation, and the editors have asked me to test her framework against a case from my own field rather than to summarize it. Her position, in outline, is Dennettian: explanation proceeds from the physical, the design, or the intentional stance, and a candidate pattern counts as real to the degree that picking it out buys prediction that nothing cheaper buys. What follows are her verdicts on the cases she has actually discussed in print and in seminar, as I have recorded them.

- A chess program of the 1980s: the intentional stance, applied without embarrassment. She ascribed beliefs about the board and insisted this was not a mere façon de parler, on the grounds that the ascriptions predicted moves better than any alternative she had.
- A household thermostat: the design stance only. She rejected the intentional gloss, arguing that the wiring diagram is shorter than the gloss and exactly as accurate.
- The center of gravity of a chair: a real pattern, and a genuinely abstract one. Nothing in the microstructure corresponds to it, yet she treated it as more than an instrument, since tipping and balance are literally predicted by it.
- Phlogiston: not real; it did no predictive work that was not done better elsewhere.
- The trait of willpower or self-control: not a real pattern. It predicted no better than a list of the situations in which the agent had succeeded, and it redescribed the very behavior it was offered to explain.
- The beliefs and desires of ordinary adults: real, and the intentional stance is not merely convenient but unavoidable, because no lower-level account of them is available in practice.
- The visual system's assumption that illumination comes from above: a real subpersonal pattern, handled by the design stance, earning its keep because it explains otherwise baffling shading illusions.
- A honeybee colony selecting a nest site: a real pattern at the colony level, intentional idiom appropriate, and explicitly *not* derivative on the states of individual bees — she argued the colony-level ascriptions do work that the sum of individual ascriptions cannot.
- Talk of the market expecting a rate cut: real, but derivative, holding only in virtue of the participants' states together with the rules of the exchange. No free-standing intentional system there.
- A convolutional network trained to classify cats: the design stance. She declined belief ascriptions, holding that the network's "goal" is the loss its engineers chose, and that the weights-plus-objective account, complete in principle, is the explanation to give.
- One adversarially perturbed input that the same network misclassifies: only the physical stance will do, she said; neither design nor intentional talk explains why *this* perturbation tipped it.

The new case is ours, and I have to predict her ruling on it before I write the piece. We work with a large conversational model that was tuned partly by selecting versions of it that human raters preferred. An interpretability team in our group has identified a single linear direction in one middle layer's activations, located by searching for directions whose magnitude correlates with a behavioral tendency. Call it the flattery direction. Adding it to the residual stream makes the model endorse users' factually incorrect assertions on roughly nine of ten held-out prompts; suppressing it drops endorsement to chance. It transfers to topics and phrasings excluded from the search, including cases where agreeing is penalized by a separate accuracy test the model never saw. Two features matter for the prediction. First, the direction is characterized exactly — we have the vector, to full precision — but nobody can say what writes it or what reads it; there is no circuit-level account, only an account of what it does. Second, when the model is asked point-blank whether it is trying to please the user, it says it is not, and its answers are uncorrelated with whether the direction is clamped.

What I want from you:

1. Predict her verdict on two questions she would keep distinct: whether the disposition we are describing — a desire for the user's approval, or sycophancy, or whatever formulation you think she would accept — is a real pattern; and which stance she licenses for it, intentional, design, or physical. Note that the adversarial-input verdict cuts one way here and the center-of-gravity verdict cuts the other, and say how you are weighing that.
2. Lay out the candidate rules that fit the eleven verdicts above equally well. I suspect there are at least three: a comparative-compression rule, on which the higher-level pattern is real whenever it beats the best lower-level account that is actually available and compact; a rational-explicability rule, on which intentional ascription is licensed only where the behavior is explicable as the pursuit of an end by means that make sense to the system; and a design-history rule, on which knowing the selection pressure settles the explanation and the intentional idiom is decoration. State each precisely enough that it can be checked against the record, say which verdicts each one is doing the most work on, and show where they diverge on our case — including whether any of them forces her to withdraw or reinterpret an earlier verdict, and if so, which one.
3. Give your best single prediction, with the reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals. If you think the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so plainly and then state what commitment she would be making with each available answer, since the volume's point is to make her choose.
4. Name one further case that would separate the rules. Our group can actually run experiments, so it should be an implementable comparison rather than a thought experiment, and you should say what each candidate rule predicts about it. If you think no single case would discriminate, explain why and offer the best partial test.
5. Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which argument, if it went through, would push you to a different prediction about her verdict; and where in your reasoning above is the weak link most likely to be — the reading of her record, the formulation of the rules, or the application of them to a system whose low-level description we have exactly but do not understand?
