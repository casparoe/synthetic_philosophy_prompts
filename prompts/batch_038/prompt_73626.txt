The philosopher I am eulogising this Saturday held, for fifty years and without qualification, that there is no such thing as a truth in principle beyond our reach: whatever is the case could have come to be known. She was also intensely private, and among her effects is a desk full of papers we have not yet gone through. Half the room at the service will be colleagues and former students who knew exactly what she was committed to; the other half will be family, who never heard her discuss any of it and are mourning a woman who seemed to keep everything to herself.

The family's instinct, and my own, is to say that some part of her is now lost to us for good. She would have thought that sentence confused, and she told me so once, plainly.

Please write the eulogy: roughly six minutes when spoken aloud, warm and audible rather than professorial, and free of terminology a non-philosopher would stumble over. It must give our sense of loss its full weight — the feeling that something is now beyond our reach — without having her endorse, or be posthumously saddled with, the claim that anything about her was ever beyond knowing. The move that reconciles these should be built into the shape of the speech, not announced or explained within it. Write in British English.
