Background first. I'm a retired physiologist — thirty-odd years on comparative respiration, mostly fish, mostly in tanks — and since I gave up the lab I've been reading philosophy and bits of social science properly for the first time in my life. I've also, rather foolishly, let myself be made chair of the trustees of a small charity that runs six field stations round the UK: two coastal, one upland, one on a river, one on an island, and one on the edge of a city that mostly does schools visits. Six of us at the table, meeting four times a year — two accountants, a solicitor, a retired GP, a farmer and me. Nobody has any social science training whatsoever, and nobody has time to read a paper before a meeting.

Here's my problem. Almost every meeting has one or two items about how prescriptive our rules ought to be, and we handle them entirely by vibes. A station manager wants to relax something; the insurers want everything uniform; one trustee wants sanctions for people who file forms late; another thinks any rule we can't enforce is worse than no rule at all. I've been reading about the tight-versus-loose cultures idea — societies that demand a lot of rule-following and punish deviation, versus societies that permit a wide range of behaviour — and I want to know whether anything in that line of thinking survives contact with an actual organisation of forty-odd people and a shoestring. So far what I've read looks correlational and survey-based to me, which makes me suspicious, but I may be being unfair.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my board could actually run on any proposal to tighten or loosen a rule, and I mean run — not admire. Specifically:

- The questions to ask, in order, with a note on what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, and what happens when nobody knows the answer.
- A clear default: if the procedure runs out of information, does it default to tightening, to loosening, or to leaving things alone? Say why.
- Where the procedure stops dead and hands the decision to a vote, because the remaining question is about what we value rather than about the world. I've got a lot of sympathy for the thought that you can't derive an ought from an is, and I don't want a checklist that quietly sneaks values in and then presents the result as a finding. Be explicit about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.
- Where you expect it to misfire in our setting specifically.

One thing I need you to handle, because it comes up constantly: I suspect "tightness" is not one dial that a practitioner can turn. There's tightening the written rule, tightening how hard you enforce an existing rule, and tightening how closely you watch whether it's being followed, and those three come apart all the time. If the underlying idea treats tightness as a single quantity, tell me so and tell me what I'm meant to do about it in the procedure anyway.

I also have a home-grown notion I'd like you to test rather than flatter. Coming from dose-response work, I keep wanting to say that the right target isn't a level of strictness but a slope — that you should tighten only where a small deviation produces a large harm, and leave things loose where the response curve is flat. Related to that I've been trying to invent something I call a deviation budget: how much local variation a given rule can absorb before outcomes start to degrade. Tell me honestly whether either of these can be made coherent, or whether tightness just isn't the sort of thing that can be dosed rule by rule, in which case say what I should use instead.

Then run the procedure on three real ones. I'll tell you my priors so you can see whether it bites:

1. The island station wants to keep its own boat-booking arrangements instead of adopting the common system the other five use. There was a near-miss two years ago — engine failure, everyone got back fine — and our insurer has now written to us asking for a single written procedure across all sites. I'm inclined to side with the insurer.
2. The GP trustee proposes a mandatory annual incident return from every station, with part of a station's maintenance budget withheld if it's filed late. Nothing has gone badly wrong anywhere this year, and two managers say the paperwork is already suffocating them. I think this is overreach.
3. We've just found out that the upland station manager has been quietly letting experienced volunteers go out on their own in conditions the written rule forbids, for about five years. Nothing happened. She told us herself because she wants the rule changed, and her argument is that her judgement is better than the rule is. I like her and I'm inclined to let her carry on.

Please make sure at least one of the three comes out against me. A procedure that agrees with everything I already think is worthless to me, and I'd rather find out now which of my instincts is wrong.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do — the class of decision it will get wrong or refuse to touch — and keep the whole thing to something I could hand to the other five trustees as a couple of pages. No bibliography, no reading list, no named studies with years attached: I can't chase any of that and I'd much rather you reasoned it through in front of me than pointed me at references. And write it in British English throughout, please — spellings and everything.
