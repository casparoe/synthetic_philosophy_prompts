ok, so I'm auditing an international criminal law course this semester and simultaneously sitting in on a friend's reading group about the ethics of assertion, and these two things have collided in my head over one episode and I can't get it out. The episode is Washington in April–June 1994, i.e. the weeks when the killing in Rwanda was underway and the US government was arguing with itself about whether to use the word. The facts I want you to work from, since you can't look anything up: the State Department and NSC drafted guidance telling spokesmen to say only that "acts of genocide may have occurred"; the US ambassador in Kigali defended that formula on the grounds that "as a responsible Government, you don't just go around hollering 'genocide'"; a senior official explained that "Genocide is a word that carries an enormous amount of responsibility" and that using it would make voters expect troops; officials conceded in private that the public posture reflected a certain lack of candor, and argued the treaty imposed no absolute obligation to act; and the whole naming fight ran in parallel with the US pushing to draw down the UN force. Then, years later — 1998, I'm pretty sure — a president goes to Kigali and expresses regret that the US hadn't acted or named things quickly enough. Set against that, Lemkin's 1944 formulation: genocide as "a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves," whose objectives include the "disintegration of the political and social institutions, of culture, language, national feelings, religion, and the economic existence of national groups" — versus the 1948 Convention's much narrower legal test, with its protected-group list and its specific-intent requirement.

Here's the framework I've been building with my friend, which I want you to treat as a proposal to test, not as something established. First, distinguish naming-as-report from naming-as-action: because the Convention makes the term load-bearing (duty to prevent and punish), saying "this is genocide" from a position of state authority isn't describing the world, it's pulling a trigger, so the ordinary epistemic norms for assertions may not even be the right norms to apply. Second, what I've been calling lexical free-riding: reaching for near-neighbors of the loaded term — "acts of genocide," "ethnic cleansing," "mass atrocity," "tribal violence," "chaos" — in order to keep the descriptive accuracy and the moral gravity while shedding the legal entailments. My claim, which may be wrong, is that the free-rider is worse than the flat denier, because the denier violates a norm and the free-rider exploits it, using the term's accumulated authority as camouflage. Third, an evidential hedge has a floor: "we're not in a position to determine X" starts out true and then, at some point, becomes false as the evidence accumulates, and I want to know whether there's a principled way to locate that crossing point or whether it's just a matter of degree. Fourth, delayed naming isn't partial compliance: naming something in 1998 does not do even a fraction of what naming it in May 1994 would have done, because the function of the name was to trigger a response, so retroactive naming is a different speech act entirely — maybe an apology, maybe a self-exculpation, but not a late installment of the same obligation.
