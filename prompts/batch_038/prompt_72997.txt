I am a fourth-year PhD student in a philosophy department with a strong history-of-philosophy group, and in three weeks I give a twenty-five-minute paper, with fifteen minutes for questions, at an interdisciplinary workshop on self-knowledge whose audience is not what I am used to. My paper argues that Avicenna's Flying Man argument is systematically misread by the literature that treats it as an ancestor of Descartes, because that literature treats the deprivation scenario as a modal one — a description of a possible subject from which a conclusion about the nature of that subject is supposed to follow. On my reading the scenario does no modal work at all: it is a subtraction procedure, a device for isolating the one awareness that survives the removal of every other, and the real load-bearing premise is the claim that self-awareness is uninterrupted — that it persists in sleep, in intoxication, and in total absorption in an object, so that there is no moment at which the subject fails to be aware of its own self. That continuity premise, not the suspended man, is what licenses the inference that self-awareness is not the activity of any bodily organ, since nothing that depends on an organ whose function can be wholly suspended could itself be uninterrupted. Two consequences follow, and they are the ones I want stress-tested: first, the stock objections to the thought experiment — that the floating man would still enjoy vestibular, proprioceptive, and interoceptive awareness, that he would feel the air, that the scenario is not genuinely conceivable given what we know about cortical function — all misfire, because they mistake a limiting idealization for a description of a possible person; second, and much less comfortably, my reading leaves the scenario doing almost no argumentative work, which makes it look rhetorical, and it relocates the whole weight of the dualist conclusion onto a premise that sounds like an introspective report about actual human beings.

The room will be roughly four analytic philosophers of mind working on consciousness and self-awareness (at least one of them a committed higher-order-representation theorist who will say that a self-awareness involving no second-order act is either empty or incoherent, and another who works on embodiment and the minimal self and will say that subtracting sensation from a living human body is not an idealization but an impossibility that smuggles in the result), one historian of philosophy in the Islamic world who knows the Shifāʾ, the shorter restatements, and the post-Avicennan reception far better than I do and who will suspect that I am reading the later vocabulary of knowledge by presence back into a text that does not contain it, two people who work on personal identity and will want to know whether uninterrupted self-awareness can deliver numerical identity rather than mere continuous acquaintance, and a chair who is a Descartes specialist and who has already told me that whatever I say, he wants to know whether my reading destroys the comparison with the Second Meditation or simply makes it more interesting. I work from English translations and secondary summaries, so my textual claims are the softest part of the paper and I need to know where to hedge out loud rather than be caught out.

Please produce a Q&A preparation sheet for this specific room rather than a defense of the thesis. I want roughly eight objections in the order I should expect them, weighted by how much damage each would actually do to the talk, each phrased in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it — including the vocabulary they would reach for, whether that is higher-order representation, transparency, the minimal self, question-begging, or anachronism — and each followed by a reply I could deliver in about two minutes standing up, a note on how much of the objection I should concede in my opening remarks before anyone has to extract it from me, what I must refuse to concede, and precisely where the reply is weakest.
