I run a small plumbing and heating outfit, which means a large part of my week is spent chasing invoices that have not been paid, and somewhere along the line that got me interested in what money actually is rather than what it buys. A podcast mentioned that an eighteenth-century Irish bishop, the same George Berkeley who is famous for denying that matter exists, had written a pamphlet of rhetorical questions about Irish poverty in the 1730s, and I found the thing online and have now read it twice with a notebook. I have no philosophical training, so please correct me wherever I have garbled him, but here is what I think I got: that the true idea of money is altogether that of a "ticket or counter"; that money is worth nothing except insofar as it stirs up industry and lets people share in one another's labour; that a people cannot be called poor if the common sort are well fed, clothed and lodged, while an industrious nation is never poor and an idle one never rich; that wealth comes from the elements and the labour worked on them rather than from bullion; that the state would do well to manufacture wants, since appetites drive action; and that what men really aim at is power, so a man who could have everything at his will would not value money at all. On the last point in particular I want you to tell me whether I am overreaching, because I am about to hang a good deal on it.

The reason I am writing is that our monthly film evening at my house is doing The Treasure of the Sierra Madre, John Huston's 1948 picture after Traven's novel, and I have become convinced, possibly too cleverly, that it is a Berkeleyan film about money. My evidence, which I would like you to test and build out: it begins with a labour contractor who vanishes owing Dobbs and Curtin their wages, so labour has been performed and no counter has been issued against it, and the only ways the two men recover value are a bar fight and a winning lottery ticket, both of them outside the ticket system entirely; once they are in the mountains the three men divide their digging and hide their separate shares, converting gold from something that circulates into something inert, which strikes me as exactly the condition Berkeley thought money must never be in; the bandits cut open the bags at the waterhole, mistake the dust for sand and dump it, which seems to me the cleanest possible demonstration that there is nothing in the stuff itself that makes it valuable, since its whole worth depends on what buyers in Durango will hand over for it; and Howard finishes with a permanent home and a position of honour among the villagers and not an ounce of gold, while Dobbs finishes dead over bags of dirt. I would like a scene-by-scene reading along these lines, pitched so that I can carry it into a living room with about twelve people who know the picture mainly from its endlessly parodied exchange about the bandits' credentials, and who will otherwise sit through it as a straightforward fable about greed corrupting the soul.

What I most want, though, is the honest resistance, because I have two hunches that may contradict each other. First, Howard's talk at the beginning about what striking gold does to a man, and the way the picture treats the mountain itself, seem to imply that gold has an agency over the human soul that is not a matter of convention at all; if that is right, the film may believe something Berkeley explicitly denies, and I would like you to say whether that is a genuine tension or whether I am reading a moral into a metaphor. Second, Berkeley wanted appetites deliberately created because appetite is what produces industry, and he was scathing about a kind of do-nothing contentment among the Irish poor; yet the film's last image is two men laughing in a dust storm over their total loss, one of them walking away from money altogether into a community that will feed and honour him. So does the picture vindicate Berkeley's theory of what money is while contradicting his theory of what money is for, and if it does, is that a flaw in my reading or an interesting thing about the film? Give me your verdict on which of my two hunches is better supported, three or four specific moments I should stop the group at and what to say at each, and a clear statement of anywhere the Berkeleyan frame is being forced onto material that does not want it — I have no interest in flattening a good film into an illustration. End by telling me what the picture contributes to the question of money that Berkeley's pamphlet cannot: something the argument alone misses that the story of three men and a bag of dust makes visible. I am not asking for quotations from the script or from the pamphlet, and please do not send me off to read anything; work from what you already know, and flag the places where you are unsure of a detail rather than filling them in.
