I am setting the closed-book final for an upper-level undergraduate jurisprudence seminar on self-reference in legal systems. There are twenty-two students; most have read Hart on the rule of recognition and some Kelsen, and exactly two have encountered Peter Suber's Nomic. I have drafted a miniature charter that generates a self-amendment problem of my own devising, modelled loosely on the way Nomic separates mutable from immutable rules, and I would like your help turning it into the central essay question of the exam, together with a model answer and a marking scheme I can hand to my teaching assistant.

The charter will be printed on the exam paper, so students need no prior reading:

Article I (Amendment). Any Article may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly, provided the full text of the proposed amendment was circulated to all members at least seven days before the vote.

Article II (Entrenchment). Article I may not be amended, repealed, or suspended. No Article may be adopted whose adoption would have the effect of rendering Article I amendable, repealable, or suspendable.

Article III (Emergency). Upon a declaration of emergency by the Chair, any Article other than Article II may be suspended for a period not exceeding thirty days. Declarations of emergency are not subject to review.

Article IV (Interpretation). Any question concerning the meaning or application of this Charter shall be decided by the Tribunal, whose decision is final and not subject to appeal. A decision of the Tribunal is not an amendment.

Three episodes are to be assessed:

1. The Assembly, with proper notice and by a two-thirds vote, adopts Article V: "Article II is repealed."

2. That failing, the Assembly instead adopts Article V: "The Assembly may, by two-thirds vote, designate any Article as unentrenched." No designation is made at the time. Six months later, acting under Article V, the Assembly designates Article I as unentrenched; the following week, acting under Article I, it repeals Article II.

3. The Chair declares an emergency and suspends Article I for thirty days. On the eighth day, the Assembly passes a resolution by simple majority purporting to repeal Article II. On the thirty-first day, the Assembly adopts Article VI, retroactively validating that resolution.

Note that Article II entrenches Article I but conspicuously fails to entrench itself. That omission is deliberate, and I want the question to exploit it rather than paper over it.

Please produce four things.

First, the exam question. It is worth 30 of the paper's 100 marks and should be answerable in about an hour. It must be entirely self-contained, must be phrased so as not to signal which reading of Article II is correct, and must avoid the vocabulary of any famous paradox, so that students who recognise the shape of the case gain no advantage over those who do not. It should ask students to determine the validity of each episode, to say what they would decide if they sat on the Tribunal in episode 2, and to state the general principle about the binding force of self-imposed limits to which their determinations commit them.

Second, a model answer of 800 to 1000 words. The crux I want tested is not whether students can see that Article I and Article II conflict — that much is easy — but whether they can see that episode 2 turns on whether the phrase "would have the effect of" attaches to the consequences a rule has at the moment of its adoption or to its foreseeable downstream consequences, and that each reading carries a cost: the first makes entrenchment circumventible by anyone patient enough to legislate in stages, the second converts Article II into an open-ended prohibition on legislating about the future at all. Episode 3 should be treated as a different kind of problem, concerning whether validity is a matter of pedigree at the instant of the act or a property of the practice taken as a whole, and whether a later competent act can cure what was void when done. Article IV should not be left dangling: if the final interpreter may hold that Article II permits anything, the marking scheme ought to register that entrenchment may survive its own interpretation only nominally.

Third, a marking scheme allocating the 30 marks across named points, stating explicitly which combination of points constitutes an A and which a B. It should also list the errors I wish to penalise, each with a short name I can reuse in feedback — for instance, the student who invalidates episode 2 simply because Article II is entrenched and never engages the staging; the student who dissolves the whole difficulty into an infinite regress of higher rules; the student who observes that the matter is really about who holds power, without noticing that this observation presupposes a rule for when power counts. Please add others that the structure of this particular charter invites. Award marks for the underlying distinctions even where the student does not know their names, and award nothing for invoking Hart, Kelsen, or the debate between continuing and self-embracing sovereignty unless the student actually deploys the distinction.

Fourth, one variant of the question, with a two-line note on how the marking scheme shifts, that I can rotate into next year's paper so that this one cannot simply be circulated.

Do not include a bibliography or a list of further readings; I will supply those separately. If you judge one of the three episodes to be philosophically inert as drafted, say so and propose a replacement rather than quietly dropping it.
