Spent 35 years in a national metrology lab, retired a couple of years back, and now I'm auditing a philosophy of science masters seminar where the convenor has (foolishly) asked me to set one of the take-home questions. The week's topic is the interpretations of probability, and I want the question built out of an actual uncertainty budget, because that's where I think the philosophy gets uncomfortable. In a GUM-style budget you publish one number, but the pieces going into it are arrived at two ways: Type A from a series of repeat measurements, Type B from a calibration certificate, a maker's spec sheet, or somebody's judgement about bounds converted into a distribution. Then you add them all in quadrature as if they were the same species of thing. And the guide itself says the A/B split is just a convenience and doesn't imply the components differ in nature — which reads to me as either a fudge or a substantive claim its authors didn't want to defend.

So: write a question worth 25 marks that a student could do in about 90 minutes, asking them to decide whether that quadrature step is legitimate and which interpretation each kind of component actually belongs to. I don't want it answerable by reciting the frequentist/subjectivist/propensity definitions. Put the weight on a single case — say the probability that this particular reference artifact drifts out of tolerance within the next year — where there's no repeatable sequence, no obvious reference class, and yet the number gets used by other labs. And make them say why propensity, which feels natural enough for one decaying nucleus, buys you nothing at all for the drift and judgement components.

Then give a model answer of roughly 700 words, plus a marking scheme that spells out which specific moves an A answer makes that a B answer misses — for instance whether they see that Type B isn't automatically subjectivist, and whether they can state what would have to be true for combining the components to be coherent rather than merely conventional. Also list the mistakes that should cost marks. No references needed in the answer, I'd much rather they argue than name-drop.
