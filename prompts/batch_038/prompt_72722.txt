I am a junior at a public high school and I serve as secretary of our Student Senate, a body of eleven delegates, five of whom were elected for the first time this fall. Our bylaws say that when a vote comes out even, the chair casts a second, deciding vote in addition to her own. At last Tuesday's meeting a sophomore delegate proposed replacing that rule: on a tie, either flip a coin in front of the room or rotate the deciding vote between the two halves of the chamber. My objection was practical rather than principled — every club, team, and committee in this building resolves ties the same way, our minutes and bylaws are written around it, delegates have learned to predict how close votes will settle, and I could not identify any tie the new rule would have broken better than the old one did. When I made that argument, a senior who has read a fair amount of philosophy cut in and said that it was just a convention, that conventions are arbitrary, and that therefore there was no reason at all to keep ours. He compared it to which side of the road people drive on. Several delegates nodded, the motion to change the rule was tabled rather than defeated, and I did not have a reply.

My difficulty is that his sentence sounds like a claim with content rather than an insult, and I suspect the word arbitrary is carrying several different loads at once. My own hunch is that it means at least two things that keep getting confused: something about how a rule like ours got started in the first place, and something quite separate about whether it is worth keeping now that it exists. I am fairly confident there are more readings than those two, and I am also unsure which of them are simply true but beside the point, which are true and would genuinely undermine me, and which are false. What I want is not a defense of my position but an honest sorting of the claim he made.

Could you lay this out as a numbered menu I can bring to the next Senate meeting? For each distinct precise thesis that conventions are arbitrary might express, state exactly what it claims, say whether it is a platitude everyone should accept, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and then say whether that particular reading would actually support the conclusion he drew, that there is no reason to keep the chair's double vote. Please also tell me which reading he was most likely invoking when he said it, and whether there is a stronger, more careful version of his point that I should prepare for, since I suspect that if he presses the idea properly he may have something worth answering. Include what I could have said back in one or two sentences for each reading that does threaten my argument. Begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether his move was legitimate — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
