I'm retired — thirty-four years in benthic ecology, mostly mudflats and infauna, with some taxonomy on the side — and about two years ago I started auditing a philosophy of science seminar at the university near where I live, becuase I'd read a bit on my own and wanted to find out whether it was as good as people said. Mostly it is. Last week the reading was the Gould and Lewontin spandrels paper, and after class two of the students, Nadia and Ellis, got into it while the rest of us were packing up. The professor had allready left. I took notes, because I've spent my whole career in the middle of this fight from the science side and nobody had ever laid it out for me like this, and I still can't tell who won.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Nadia said the paper is methodological hygiene dressed up as a discovery. Her case: it never identifies a trait in a real organism and demonstrates that it's a spandrel. The worked examples are architecture — vaults, arches, that church in Venice — and the biology is all generic gestures at allometry, pleiotropy and drift. So what's left is "remember to consider alternatives," which no working adaptationist ever denied, and the paper's lasting influence is a matter of prose and timing rather than content.

(2) Ellis said she was reading it like a case report when it's a critique of a procedure, and he walked through what he took the argument to be: atomize the organism into separate traits; ask what each one is optimized for; if the first adaptive story fails, tell another; if those fail, change what you're counting as the trait's function; and if even that fails, fall back on a non-adaptive explanation without treating the retreat as evidence against the approach itself. That's a claim about how a research program digests its own failures, and it's the sort of thing you could check against the record. He added that the architecture is not decoration: the point is that a design program can begin from a space that has no function at all, so asking what the space is for is a category error — which is exactly what people are doing when they ask what the human chin is for.

(3) Nadia said "digests its own failures" is just underdetermination with a better wardrobe, and question-begging to boot, because failed optimal-design stories plainly do lower confidence in particular adaptive hypotheses. Then, on his own account, recognizing a spandrel means showing the trait is a necessary consequence of some other feature of the organism's form — which requires precisely the positive, comparative, functional work the paper never does. So the critique is parasitic on the program it attacks. And "necessary consequence of form" quietly assumes you can identify constraints in advance, when in fact you only ever learn a trait was constrained after you've found the constraint.

(4) Ellis half-conceded the parasitism point but said the real disagreement is over the default assumption, and that the two candidate defaults aren't operationally identical. Hers: treat a trait as an adaptation until testing rules otherwise. His: make no assumption about adaptive status until you've actually run the alternatives. He said the difference shows up in what counts as a surprising result, in which data gets collected, and in which story gets told first, and that a program's defaults are a legitimate target of criticism even when every individual move inside the program is perfectly reasonable.

(5) Nadia said that's a sociology claim, not a methodological one, and it can't be settled by arguing in a seminar — it needs a study of actual practice, which she doubted he could produce. Then she made what I thought was her best move: that "spandrel" in actual use has come to mean "any non-adaptive feature," which collapses three quite different things — a by-product that never had a function, a feature whose present use differs from whatever it originated for, and a feature that is an adaptation whose story nobody has found yet. She said Gould blurred these himself afterwards, in the exaptation business he did with Vrba, so the vocabulary the paper gave us is part of the confusion rather than a cure for it.

(6) Ellis said the blur is real but that's an argument for sharpening the terms, and sharpening them requires exactly the distinction the paper insisted on: why a feature came to exist versus what it's useful for now. He said that's the one thing that survives every objection she raised, and that her point (5) actually depends on it.

Nobody budged and the room cleared out.

I'd like you to referee this, not summarize it. For each of the six moves, say whether it lands, half-lands, or misses, and identify which part of it is doing the work — a move can be right for a bad reason. Where a move rests on a factual or historical claim about the paper or about what happened afterwards, tell me whether that claim is safe or shaky, since I can't chase the literature and I have a feeling at least one of them is overstating to win a point. Then give me an overall verdict on who came out ahead and why. Finally, tell me what neither of them said that a decent referee would expect: my own hunch is that they both skipped over whether "spandrel" names a real category at all or is just a placeholder for "not yet explained," but I might be wrong about that too, and I'd rather you tell me my hunch is bad than agree with it out of politeness. I've read the paper twice, so don't spend space re-telling me what it says, and don't hand me a bibliography at the end — I'm not going to chase it, and I'd much rather you say where you're uncertain.
