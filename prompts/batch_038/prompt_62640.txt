I edit a small popular-history magazine and I've been asked to write the one-page test our readers' panel uses to decide whether a submission is pseudohistory or just an unfashionable opinion. Every draft I write falls over on some case I bring to the next meeting, so before I try again I want the spec rather than another draft: what does a criterion for calling something pseudohistory actually have to deliver?

Give me the requirements, with the case or the consideration behind each, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are really just one camp's taste dressed up as a standard. I already suspect that must-be-falsifiable and must-have-a-mechanism are carrying more weight than they can bear.

Here's the pile my drafts keep tripping on. Atlantis: taken as a bare claim, that there was an advanced civilisation somewhere that collapsed, it's an ordinary empirical hypothesis, so the defect can't be improbability — and I cant seperate the claim from the practice, where the site relocates to Thera or Antarctica or the Richat Structure every single time somebody goes and looks. Göbekli Tepe and the other genuinely spectacular recent digs, which are peer-reviewed and real and also load-bearing for exactly that kind of story, so a criterion keyed on fabricated evidence is out too. Fomenko's chronology: credentialed mathematician, wall of statistics, testable, tested, failed — which is why 'they arent testable' can't be the test either. Schliemann, who wrecked strata, overclaimed a hoard from the wrong millennium, and still came out roughly right about Troy. The Norse in Newfoundland, treated as saga nonsense until the Ingstads dug it up. And that 2021 peer-reviewed paper about an airburst flattening a Jordan Valley city, which the journal retracted four years later after a documentary machine had been running behind it the whole time.

One more constraint that I think gets ignored: the criterion can't classify ordinary historical inference as pseudohistorical. Historians abduce motives out of thin records all day and the past doesn't rerun, so whatever the difference is, it can't just be thin evidence plus no repeat experiments.

My suspicion, and tell me if I'm wrong, is that you can have at most two of these: a rule a smart non-specialist can apply before the digging is done; a rule that isn't just whatever the experts currently say; and a rule that doesn't retroactively stamp the Vinland vindication as pseudohistory. Say which pair you'd keep and why.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking out existing criteria, and give me two lines each on how the falsifiability move, the degenerating-research-programme move, and the sociological one about refusing to engage the scholarly community do against the list. No reading list, I'm not going to read it.
