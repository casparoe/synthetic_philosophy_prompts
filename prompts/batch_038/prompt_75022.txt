My flatmate is three years into a PhD on the pragmatists and we have this game where I hand him claims and he has to say whether they're true. His record so far, as near as I can reconstruct it:

- "the coffee you buy has more caffeine than the cheap stuff" — something he has never tested and never will — true, and he said he'd bet money on it.
- "the number of grains of sand on that beach last Tuesday was even" — not a question with an answer, don't pass go.
- "there are infinitely many twin primes" — open problem, but he said that one is true or false right now, and that unlike the sand it's a real question because there's something that would count as settling it.
- His brother taps the doorframe four times before driving and hasn't had an accident in eleven years. Not true, he said. Then: "but I'm not going to be the one who tells him."
- Was the settled cosmology of 1870 true in 1870? He said "true then, in the only sense I care about," and then changed the subject and wouldn't be pinned down, which is very unlike him.
- A friend's religious belief that has kept the man off drink for a decade and that, as far as I can tell, makes no difference to anything either of us could ever check. He said "true for him," and then said he wasn't sure I'd asked a question that has an answer.

We've both read enough Peirce and James to be annoying about it, and I keep flip flopping on which one he actually is. My hunch is that at least two pretty different rules fit all six of those verdicts, and possibly a third that isn't either of them.

Here's the case I'm bringing him on Thursday. My sister wrote her combinatorics thesis around a conjecture — call it K. Nobody had proved K and nobody had refuted it. There was a heuristic argument plus an analogy from a neighbouring field that made most people think it probably false, but nothing in writing. She assumed K anyway and it paid: three results that are now in the textbooks, results nobody would have gone looking for without K, one of them used in coding theory. Two years ago a grad student posted a 200 page disproof. Everyone in the field privately thinks it's right, nobody has checked it, and the student has since left academia. So: is K true? And seperate question — was K true while she was writing the thesis, and does the answer change on the day somebody finally reads those 200 pages? And do her three results survive all this, or did they just get unproved?

What I want from you: your guess at his verdict on each of those parts; the candidate rules that generate all six of his earlier verdicts, stated sharply enough that I could tell them apart in practice; which rule you think he's really running and why — or, if this record genuinely can't decide it, say so plainly; and the one further case I should bring next month that would force the surviving rules apart. Please no survey of pragmatism and no rundown of who said what in 1907. I just want to know what his own track record commits him to.
