I'm the TA for a second-year political philosophy course — eighteen students, one 75-minute section that is mine to run — and Dewey comes up the following week (The Public and Its Problems, plus the material on inquiry in Logic: The Theory of Inquiry). I want an activity they do before they have read a word of him, so that the two claims I find hardest to get across arrive as something they have already lived through rather than as doctrine to be recited back.

The first claim: inquiry begins in an indeterminate situation, and the criterion of success is itself constituted and revised inside the inquiry — so a group that insists on fixing its ends in advance and then executing them is not being rigorous, it is failing to inquire. The second: a public comes into existence only when people recognize that the indirect consequences of transactions they never chose have affected them, and organize around that recognition — and usually they do not.

Design the activity. The rules and incentives, not my narration, have to produce the failure or the recognition as an outcome of what they actually do. My own instinct is a first phase that feels like a well-posed optimization task with a success criterion each group writes down and is then strictly held to, and a second phase in which those written criteria are precisely what generate a new, unanticipated situation affecting other groups; if you think there is a cleaner structure that produces the same discovery, design that instead and tell me why. Give me roles, materials (paper and pens only, no money), a scoring scheme with small but real stakes, and a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes.

Then: a debrief that maps particular moments onto each claim and only afterwards names them; an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover mid-session; and a frank account of what the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that a timed game can stage only the procedural shadow of what Dewey means by democracy as a way of life — tell me whether you agree, or whether the design can get closer than that.
