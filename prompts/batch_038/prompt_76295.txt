You are a social epistemologist who has spent a decade designing and running simulations for undergraduate seminars and for newsroom training workshops. Treat this as a design problem rather than an exposition problem, and think carefully through the mechanics before you write a word of the runbook.

**The setting.** I teach a 75-minute session in a second-year seminar on knowledge and society. Twenty-four students, seated at six tables of four, in a room with movable chairs. Available materials: a whiteboard, index cards, pens, two decks of playing cards, and a set of six-sided dice. No computers, no phones during the activity, no real money. I am the sole facilitator. The students have had one prior session on testimony and expertise, but I will not tell them in advance what today's topic is.

**The target.** I want them to feel, rather than be told, the difference between two failure modes that ordinary talk collapses into the single phrase "echo chamber":

- a structure that merely *omits* relevant voices — a failure of connectivity, which exposure to the missing voices can in principle repair;
- a structure that *actively discredits* relevant voices — a failure of trust, in which exposure to those same voices does not repair anything and may entrench the structure further.

**Non-negotiable design constraints.**

1. The activity has two acts, using the same twenty-four people and the same underlying task. Act I must instantiate the omission structure. Between the acts you may change only the rules and the communication topology: you may not instruct anyone to distrust anyone, plant a rumor, or have me make any announcement concerning the reliability of a group or table. Act II must instantiate the discrediting structure, and the distrust must arise endogenously from the incentives and from what participants can genuinely observe from where they sit.
2. The single change that most improves performance in Act I — adding links, allowing tables to communicate — must be among the changes that make Act II worse. Participants should be able to read this off their own scores, not off my mouth.
3. Participants must have evidence. The game should produce, from the inside, the felt sense that the other group is unreliable: an inference from data the participants actually collected, where the data have been corrupted by the structure rather than by anyone's bad faith. Build an explicit mechanism that produces this.
4. Nothing gets named until the debrief. The words "echo chamber," "epistemic bubble," "testimony," and "source" must not appear on any handout, card, or instruction sheet that a participant sees.

**What I want, in this order.**

(a) A short brainstorming section, for my eyes only, weighing at least three candidate mechanisms for satisfying constraints 1–3 — for instance a messenger or relay role, a scoring rule that rewards being nominated as an informant by one's peers, a lossy transmission channel, a costly verification token — and stating which you selected and precisely where the others fail.

(b) The hidden state of the world and the private-signal mechanism: exactly what each participant receives, how a single facilitator generates it with cards or dice in under four minutes, and what the best achievable accuracy is for an isolated table versus a fully connected room, so that I can predict the spread of scores before I run it.

(c) The payoff scheme, given as a table: the accuracy term, the social term, their relative weights, and how points convert into something students actually care about (a modest grade bonus, first choice of next week's reading, or whatever you recommend) without using cash. Follow it with a one-sentence statement of what an individual's rational move is at each decision point, in each act, separately.

(d) Roles, including any relay or messenger role: what the occupant may say, what they may not say, what they are required to write down, and what happens if they deviate.

(e) A minute-by-minute timeline for all 75 minutes, including the transition between acts and the exact wording of every announcement I make aloud.

(f) A materials list, plus the verbatim text of every card, slip, and instruction sheet participants will see.

(g) The debrief: an ordered sequence of questions, what answer each is meant to elicit, the moment at which the two structures finally get their names, and how to handle the student who protests that of course they distrusted the other table, since that table was feeding them garbage. That protest is the correct response and the most important moment of the session; tell me how to convert it into the payoff of the exercise rather than let it function as an escape hatch.

(h) Failure modes: at least five things that predictably go wrong the first time this is run, each with a repair I can execute mid-session. Address at minimum a table that reverse-engineers the mechanism early; a group that refuses on principle to distrust anyone; a round in which the dice make the honest table look bad and the debrief thereby loses its bite; and the risk that competitive scoring leaves students genuinely irritated with one another when they walk out.

(i) Honest limits: which features of the real phenomenon this activity cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally, and where the analogy actively misleads. Address at least the facts that the game has a determinate ground truth known to the facilitator, that real discrediting is bound up with identity and moral standing rather than points, that a twenty-minute trust structure is not a lifetime one, and that distrust inside the game is instrumentally rational in a way distrust in the world often is not.

(j) Adaptations: what to cut for twelve students in fifty minutes, and what to add if I have forty students and a second facilitator.

Write for a facilitator who is competent but has never run this activity before, and be concrete enough that I could run it tomorrow from your document alone. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; refer to positions and arguments by description only and I will supply the reading myself. Wherever a part of the design rests on an assumption about how students will actually behave, mark it explicitly as an assumption and state what to do if it proves false.
