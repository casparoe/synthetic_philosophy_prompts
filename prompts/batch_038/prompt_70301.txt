You are to act as a philosopher of mind who also reads the empirical literature on visual awareness with professional care — backward masking, iconic memory and partial report, change and inattentional blindness, blindsight, and the neuroimaging work on reportability and ignition. I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar that will devote two sessions to whether phenomenal character can outstrip everything a subject is able to report, attend to, or otherwise use. The seminar is divided, but the division does not track the labels people reach for, so I need an instrument that reveals what a person is already committed to by what they say about cases — not an instrument that lets them self-report an allegiance.

Deliverable: a decision tree that sorts a seminar member into a named position on the access/phenomenal consciousness debate, plus a key to the leaves. Take as fixed common ground, and begin the tree after it, that everyone in the room accepts these two things: some visual discrimination occurs with no accompanying report and no feeling of seeing, and a briefly presented display can support partial report of items the subject did not say they had seen.

Work in two stages and show me both.

Stage 1, brainstorming the logical space. List the independent claims that genuinely separate the positions — about reportability, whether attention is necessary for conscious visual experience, whether working-memory capacity bounds what is experienced, whether phenomenal character can be more fine-grained than any discriminative or reportive ability, what a higher-order state is supposed to add to a first-order one, whether there is any further fact beyond poised representational content, and anything else you judge to belong. For each pair of claims, state whether they are logically independent or whether one entails or undermines the other; where two candidate questions are dependent, say which you keep as a node and why. End the stage with a rough count of the answer-combinations your claims permit.

Stage 2, the tree. Constraints:
- No more than twelve decision nodes and eight to twelve leaves; nodes may be two- or three-way.
- Every node must be phrased as a claim about a case or a paradigm that a person who has never heard of any of the theories can answer in one or two sentences. No node question may contain the vocabulary of the theories; if a node genuinely needs a term of art, define it inside the question in a single clause.
- The branch material must include at least one fine-grained-property case of the speckled-hen type and at least one case in which a subject performs above chance while denying any visual experience, and it must not include any question about what the subject would call their own state.
- At least two nodes must turn on a question that is empirical rather than conceptual. For each of those, say in one or two sentences what result would decide the branch and what result would leave it permanently open, and describe the paradigm in plain words.

Required leaves: global workspace theory, on which consciousness requires ignition and broadcast; a localist or recurrent-processing view on which local visual processing suffices and can outrun report and attention; a higher-order thought theory; a first-order representationalism that grants a real functional distinction but denies any further phenomenal fact; a "rich" view on which phenomenology exceeds access even in ordinary, attentive seeing; an illusionist or deflationary view on which talk of inaccessible phenomenology is a misdescription manufactured by the access machinery itself. Add any further leaf the logical space permits, and if a cell is permitted but nobody occupies it, mark it unoccupied and diagnose whether that is because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Also flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate, and say what further question, if any, would separate them. I expect at least one such pair; a tree that sorts everything cleanly has probably been built from labels rather than from claims.

Format: the tree as indented text (node question, then lettered branches), followed by a key giving each leaf's name, one sentence on its central commitment, and one sentence on its most awkward consequence. Roughly two pages of handout after the brainstorming stage. No bibliography, no citations, no author-date tags, and no URLs — anything I cannot check from your prose is worse than useless here, so describe studies rather than naming them. Before you finish, reread the tree and report two things: that every leaf is reachable by at least one complete path, and that no node question contains a word that only makes sense if the reader already favors one of the leaves.
