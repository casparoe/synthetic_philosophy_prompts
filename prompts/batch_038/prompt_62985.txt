I am a high school junior and I run the game-theory club at my school. I know what a payoff matrix is, I can follow an induction argument, and I have heard the term subgame-perfect equilibrium used in conversation, but I have had no formal training, so please do not assume more than that.

Last month we played the centipede game at a meeting: four decision points, players alternating, the pot roughly doubling whenever someone passes; taking ends the game and pays the taker substantially more than it pays the other player; and if both players pass all the way to the end, each finishes with more than the first player would have collected by taking at the very first node. In eight of our ten games the first player passed. Three of those ran past the second node, and in two of them both players ended up better off than the backward-induction outcome would have left them.

Our faculty adviser's response was a line he has repeated several times since, always as though it closed the discussion: if both players are rational, the game ends at the first move. He has now put that same sentence to work in three separate arguments. First, to explain away our results — he said the ten games simply showed that the players were not rational, or did not believe one another to be rational, or were playing some other game without noticing it. Second, as practical advice: to a member negotiating a one-off arrangement with someone she will never deal with again, he said that leaving anything on the table for the other side is irrational by definition, since there is no last round in which cooperation could pay off. Third, as a concession to a member who pushed back: he agreed that the centipede game is called a paradox, and said the paradox shows that rationality is self-defeating, because two rational players finish worse off than two who reason badly.

I am not convinced that one sentence can carry all three of those jobs, and I suspect that in at least one of them it is doing no work whatsoever. Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring to the next meeting, the distinct precise claims that "if both players are rational, the game ends at the first move" could be making, running from the readings that are true by stipulation through the substantive ones to the ones that are simply false? For each candidate reading I would like three things: a one-sentence statement of exactly what it asserts; an explicit list of the assumptions it needs beyond the payoff table — in particular what it takes "rational" to mean (maximizing expected payoff given one's beliefs, holding correct beliefs about the other player's later choices, being disposed to reason backward from the end, something else), how deep the knowledge of the other's rationality has to go, and what a player is supposed to conclude about her opponent after a move that the reasoning says cannot occur; and a verdict, namely whether that reading is a theorem, an empirical prediction about how people will actually play, a recommendation, a definition that renders the sentence immune to counterexample, or false.

Then tell me which readings would license each of my adviser's three uses and which would not. Two things interest me especially. First: whether there is any reading on which our ten games could count as evidence against the sentence, or whether every reading strong enough to be interesting has already ruled that evidence out by stipulation — and if it is the latter, whether that makes the sentence empty or merely not the sort of thing an experiment can test. Second: I have built a variant for next week in which the first player is told only that her opponent is either someone who has played before and knows the argument, or a first-timer who passes the first two nodes out of politeness and only then begins calculating, without being told which of the two she faces. Does the same sentence predict an immediate take in that version? I would like to know which of your readings change their answer when I change the game in this way and which do not, and whether the ones that do not change are precisely the ones I should distrust.

Please do not give me names, dates, journal articles, or a reading list; I cannot chase those down, and what I want is the claims themselves rather than the history of who advanced them.
