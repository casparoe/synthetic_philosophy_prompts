A week-long workshop for early-career AI-policy people includes one 90-minute session that is mine to design. There will be 18 participants of mixed backgrounds — a few with economics training, most without any game theory — seated as six teams of three, and the room has no laptops, only paper, index cards, and one die per team. The session concerns the pressure that competitive dynamics put on safety work, and I want the participants to run into that pressure rather than be told about it. The terms alignment tax and competitiveness constraint must not be spoken aloud until the debrief.

Design the game they play. My sketch of the core: over several rounds teams accumulate capability points; they may convert some of those points into verification steps, which lower their chance of causing an incident but slow their accumulation; and an incident is not private — it damages every team's final score, not just the responsible one. On top of that core, build in three features I have not seen handled well in anything comparable:

1. The lead is only imperfectly visible. Each round, teams see their own total exactly and everyone else's total with some noise, so nobody is certain who is ahead.
2. Trailing is subsidized. Teams further behind gain a slipstream bonus on accumulation, which should make appearing to be behind a strategic asset and tempt at least one team to sandbag.
3. At least one round must confront a comfortably leading team with a choice that is genuinely close on the payoffs — a verification step that is worth skipping for a capability-maximizer and worth buying for an incident-avoider — so that the debrief can ask them why they did what they did and get a real answer.

What I most want the room to discover by playing is that the familiar picture, in which everyone is uniformly forced to cut corners, is wrong in a specific asymmetric way: the constraint binds hardest on whoever is second, and least on whoever is far enough ahead to stop caring about the prize. Ideally the session ends with an argument about whether the sandbagging team was clever or a collective disaster.

Then supply the following. Exact numbers: capability gains per round, the cost of each verification step, the die table for incidents and how verification shifts it, the winning threshold, and the score damage an incident inflicts on all teams, together with enough of the probability arithmetic that I can sanity-check it myself. A minute-by-minute timeline. The debrief questions in the order I should ask them, and an explicit mapping from moments in play to the two concepts I was not permitted to name. What will predictably go wrong on a first run — including the risk that one team moralizes rather than plays, and that the dice make the outcome feel arbitrary — and what I should have ready for each. Finally, an honest account of which features of real competitive pressure the game cannot reproduce and I would have to supply by argument instead. My own worry is the known endpoint: participants who can see the session end in ninety minutes will accept risks no real lab would.
