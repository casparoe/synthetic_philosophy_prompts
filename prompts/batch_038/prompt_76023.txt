My reading group has been arguing about the centipede game for like two years and we are nowhere. Set-up, since I want you inside the weeds: two players alternate, at each node you can either take the pot and end the game or pass, the pot grows every time someone passes, and taking always beats passing for whoever's turn it is. Backward induction says take at the very first node. Half my group says that's obviously correct and the lab results where people pass for a bunch of nodes just show humans are noisy and haven't learned the game yet — sharpen the incentives, repeat it, and they converge. The other half says the argument is rotten at the root: by the time you're standing at node two, the shared assumption of common knowledge of rationality has already been falsified by the history, so the counterfactual "what would he do at node three" has no determinate answer, and backward induction is reasoning from a premise its own conclusion makes false. A third person keeps insisting both of them are missing the point — put even a tiny probability on your opponent being the sort who passes for non-strategic reasons and passing stays rational for dozens of nodes, so the result isn't deep, its just fragile to how you wrote the game down.

Here's the bit I really want you to chew on. I built a variant to try and break the tie and I'm worried it made things worse. At every node, before deciding, each player types one sentence about what they think is going on, and the other player reads it before choosing. The idea was to drag the off-path epistemics out into the open so nobody has to guess. What I found: the games that run longest are the ones where the sentences stop being about the pot and start being about the person — something like "I don't think you're the type to cut this off early." Both camps claim this as a win. The orthodoxy people say it proves social preferences are contaminating the payoffs, i.e. a modelling failure, not a logical one. The belief-revision people say it proves exactly their point: players were repairing an incoherent common ground on the fly, which is what they said would have to happen. Same data, two readings, nobody budged an inch.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My working hypothesis — attack it, don't just accept it — is that the two main camps aren't disagreeing about the game at all but about what a supposition licenses: one side treats "suppose both players are rational and know it" as a static constraint you get to carry unchanged into any sub-history, the other treats it as a common ground that gets revised the moment the history contradicts it. If that's right, is the dispute merely verbal, or is it a real fight about counterfactual semantics wearing a game theory costume? And seperately: is my variant even the sort of thing that could settle it, or is that data interpretively underdetermined no matter how I design the experiment — meaning no experiment I could run would count? Tell me what would count as progress here, a distinction, a formal result, a differently posed question, or argue that no progress should be expected and why.
