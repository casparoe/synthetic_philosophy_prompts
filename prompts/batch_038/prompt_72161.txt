For the past three years I have been running what the field calls no-report paradigms in visual working memory, and I have reached a point where I need someone to tell me what kind of disagreement I am actually in the middle of.

My paradigm, concretely. An array of four oriented gratings is flashed for 50 ms and masked. On half the trials a cue, presented after the mask, selects one location, and the observer reports that item's orientation in a two-alternative forced choice, with an explicit guess option so that we can separate "not stored" from "stored but not usable." The dispute the paradigm is supposed to bear on is the old rich-versus-sparse question about how much of a briefly presented array a person is conscious of at a given moment: everything that leaves a trace, or only the two or three items that can be reported.

The traces we look for in the uncued items are indirect: a bias in a subsequent saccade, a change in the precision of the cued report when the uncued items are manipulated, and a late posterior EEG deflection. Here is what has become unbearable. Every result we obtain is claimed by both camps. When we find a trace of an uncued item, the sparse camp says the trace is not perceptual but a sensory aftereffect or motor preparation, and correctly notes that comparable traces appear in conditions nobody would call conscious. When we find no trace, the rich camp says our measure lacked sensitivity, or that the retro-cue destroyed the very state we were trying to detect. In three years of reading this literature I have never seen anyone revise a substantive position in response to a result produced by the other side's paradigm. I now have to write the discussion section of a paper whose central finding is a null, and I can honestly write it either way — which is what makes me suspect that the problem is in the setup rather than in the data.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than adopted, is that the dispute is not about how many items sit in some store at all, but about the epistemic status of report: whether reporting is a measure of consciousness or a component of it. On the first view a failure to report is evidence of absence; on the second it is no evidence either way, because the state being measured and the state being reported are not the same state. I have taken to calling this the contamination asymmetry, since each side treats the other's paradigm as contaminated while treating its own as clean, and the two contaminations are not symmetric in any way I can articulate. Block's separation of access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness is supposed to adjudicate something in this neighborhood, but in practice it seems to get invoked by both sides as a license rather than as a constraint.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement — not a verdict on who is right, and not a survey of the theories or of the experimental literature, all of which I have read; I am not asking for references or a bibliography, and none should appear. Specifically: is this a verbal dispute about what it means to be conscious of an item; a substantive empirical dispute that better instruments could in principle settle; a methodological dispute about which paradigms count as evidence, in which case what deeper commitment is it a proxy for; or two different questions pursued under one name? Say which, and argue for it by engaging what each side actually claims rather than caricatures of their claims. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a new distinction, a new kind of experiment, a formal model specifying what these trace measures are measures of, a change of question, or nothing at all — and be honest if the answer is nothing. If you think my hypothesis about report is wrong, or only half right, say so and say what should replace it.
