I've been reading about the history of machine translation on my own, and two episodes have stuck with me: the Georgetown–IBM demonstration of January 1954, where reporters watched an IBM 701 turn some sixty-odd carefully selected Russian sentences into English using six grammar rules and a vocabulary of a few hundred stems and endings, with its organisers suggesting the problem might be solved within five years; and the 1966 report of the ALPAC committee under John Pierce, which found that a decade of work had not delivered and recommended that money go instead to basic research in computational linguistics and to machine-aided translation, after which American funding collapsed. I'd like that twelve-year arc analyzed through Douglas Hofstadter's account of why machine translation is shallow — the argument he develops in Le Ton beau de Marot (1997), and names with the Eliza effect, that translating is not substituting words but reconstructing what a speaker meant, which calls for a rich model of the world rather than of the language; and through the related earlier claim of Bar-Hillel that fully automatic high-quality translation is unattainable because sentences underdetermine their own interpretation.

Begin with a disciplined mapping: what, on this theory, the 1954 system could and could not have been doing, and whether ALPAC's reasoning was that reasoning or something different — the committee was weighing cost and usefulness against human translators, and I doubt Hofstadter's framework has any vocabulary for a trade-off of that kind, so say where it goes silent rather than forcing it to speak.

Then take up a suspicion of mine. The argument seems to be about the tail of a distribution, while every test the field has devised measures its average: the sentences picked for a demonstration, and later corpus-wide quality scores, are exactly the evidence the theory predicts will look favorable. Does that make the position unfalsifiable, and is it any help in explaining why statistical and then neural methods eventually worked well enough that Hofstadter's conclusion now reads as too strong? Close by separating the parts of the episode the theory genuinely illuminates from the parts it never anticipated, and by answering this: is a shallow system that is right almost always a counterexample to the theory, or precisely what the theory said would happen?
