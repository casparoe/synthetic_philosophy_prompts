Write a philosophical dialogue between two theorists of pain who disagree about what pain asymbolia shows, if anything, about why pains are bad for the people who have them.

Begin from a clinical picture that both of them accept, and do not let either one dispute it: there are patients — typically after damage involving the insular cortex or its connections to limbic structures, and in something like the same way after cingulotomy, prefrontal lobotomy, or heavy opioid analgesia — who present a dissociation. Their ability to localize a noxious stimulus and to judge its intensity is intact; their withdrawal reflexes are intact; they frequently laugh, smile, or seem amused when pinpricked; they are undistressed and show no inclination to escape the stimulation or to have it stopped; and yet, when asked, they will say that they do feel pain, that it hurts, while adding that they do not mind it, or that it does not bother them.

The disagreement between your two characters is this. One is an evaluation theorist of unpleasantness: what makes a sensory episode a pain, a genuinely unpleasant one, is not any intrinsic property of the episode but the subject's disvaluing of it — the subject's experiencing it as bad and being thereby moved to want it to stop. On this view the asymbolic patient's sensation has lost precisely the feature that made it a pain, and it is the patient's continued use of the word that now stands in need of explanation. The other is an intrinsic-quality theorist: unpleasantness is a felt property of the experience itself, present in virtue of how the experience feels, and aversion, distress, and the desire for relief are its normal downstream consequences rather than its constituents. On this view the asymbolic patient still feels a pain that is bad for her; what the lesion has destroyed is her capacity to respond to that badness.

Requirements for the dialogue:

1. The two characters are equally sharp, equally well-informed, and equally fair-minded. Neither is a strawman, and neither may prevail through a slip or an inattention on the other's part.

2. The exchange must run on genuine back-and-forth. Each character takes up the immediately preceding move specifically: answering the actual objection, conceding what is right in it, and then pressing where the other is now exposed. No parallel monologues, no restating a position at greater length in place of answering.

3. Both must treat the patient's own report as a problem to be solved rather than as evidence handed to them on a plate. Each should have a worked-out account of what the asymbolic patient is doing when she says she feels pain but does not mind it, and each should be pushed by the other on whether that account is anything more than a device for immunizing a theory from the data.

4. At least once apiece, they should press the mirror-image hypothetical case: a patient who cannot localize or characterize a sensation at all, who cannot say where it is or what it feels like, but who is intensely distressed and desperate for it to stop. Each must say what their own theory implies about whether she is in pain, and absorb the other's reply.

5. They must confront the two standard worries head-on. For the evaluation theorist: if disvaluing is itself a felt episode, does it require a further disvaluing in order to be unpleasant, and how is that regress to be stopped without smuggling in an intrinsically bad feel? For the intrinsic-quality theorist: if an experience is bad purely in virtue of how it feels, independently of any disposition to mind it, what makes that feel a badness rather than a mere intensity — and is there any possible case in which the two theories could come apart in their verdicts?

6. The dialogue should close without either character converting the other, but with each stating what they now take the real disagreement to be, and ideally with one point on which they discover that they had been talking past each other.

Length: 1,100 to 1,500 words. You may give the characters ordinary names. They should argue in their own terms and should not cite books or papers, attribute views to named philosophers, or gesture at a literature; everything they need is in the case and in each other's reasoning.
