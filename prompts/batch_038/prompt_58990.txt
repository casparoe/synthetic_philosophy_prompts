In my rational choice seminar we just did Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma — the one where an eccentric trillionaire tells 20 people that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars, but if more than one sends, or none does, nobody gets anything, and they're not allowed to communicate. Two people in my section got into a pretty intense argument afterward and I couldn't follow half of it, so I figured I'd ask you to referee instead of guessing.

Nate said the answer is easy: everyone randomizes and sends with probability 1/20, because that's the value that maximizes the chance of exactly one telegram (a bit under 40%), and everyone can see it's the same for all of them, so nothing about superrationality is threatened. Priya said that isn't a solution to the dilemma, it's a surrender: the whole content of superrationality was supposed to be that identical reasoners land on identical conclusions, and that this correlation does work ordinary expected-value reasoning can't. Randomizing gets you a slightly-better-than-one-in-three shot at a total loss, which is roughly what any self-interested calculator would have said anyway, so the dilemma shows the idea buys nothing here.

Nate: notice the deterministic options are provably hopeless though. If everyone follows the same rule and the rule issues a definite yes or no, you get twenty telegrams or zero, both guaranteed losses. So mixing isn't a fallback, it's the only non-zero play, and that's exactly what the superrational reasoning delivered.

Priya: you can keep the determinism. The sameness that matters is sameness of procedure, not sameness of action. Rule: send iff your first name starts with the alphabetically earliest letter among the twenty. All twenty apply one identical rule, exactly one sends, no coin flips.

Nate: that can't work. Nobody knows the other nineteen names, so from inside your own head you can't tell whether you're the unique satisfier. And "they'll all apply the same rule to their own data" assumes things about their data and their rule that the setup never gave you — you've smuggled in a coordination assumption.

Priya: agreed, and that's my point in the end. Every determinate route needs extra assumptions about the group that aren't in the description, and what's left over is just mixing, which is ordinary EV talk wearing a costume. So this is a counterexample to superrationality, not an application of it.

Nate's last move was that she's overgeneralizing: the failure is local to this payoff structure, which rewards being *different* from everybody else, whereas superrationality earns its keep in games that reward doing the same thing, like the prisoner's dilemma. So it's no surprise correlated reasoning is useless here.
