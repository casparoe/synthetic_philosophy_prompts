I write the ideas column for a science weekly — AI, physics, whatever's having a moment — and I've spent the better part of three months reporting a piece on the school of thought that says the way to keep a very smart system honest is to make it argue its case against another very smart system in front of a human who can't do the math either way. Every researcher I interviewed gave me the same clean reason it's supposed to work: lying is hard and the truth is easy, so under real adversarial pressure the true side wins, because a judge can always check a correct claim more cheaply than a bogus one. Every single time I pushed on that sentence, the answer got hedgier and hedgier, and eventually one of them said something like *well, it depends on what the judge can verify in the time she has*, which is the entire ballgame and never makes it into the explainers. For the sidebar I want a short story that dramatizes the hedge rather than another numbered thought experiment with a Greek name.

Here's the furniture, rearrange it as you like. A small regional panel — invented body, don't use a real one — has to sign off within a hard forty minutes of one human's attention on whether a hospital network can roll out an automated dosing protocol. Two advocate systems argue it, one for, one against. The panel's rules are the interesting part, so please make them concrete and load-bearing: the judge's software can verify any *single* step in seconds — an arithmetic line, a regulation lookup, one inference from one premise to the next — but has no way to vouch for a chain as a whole except by the judge spending minutes on each link, and the minutes are the scarce good. She can demand that either side expand any claim into its supporting steps, and each demand is charged against her clock. One advocate arrives with a certificate: something like four thousand individually trivial, individually checkable steps that compose into the claim that the protocol is safe. The other advocate's entire case is one sentence, and the sentence is true, and justifying it would eat most of an hour she does not have. So the honest side is not outlied, it's outrun — and she can watch it happening and the rules give her no move to make about it. I'd also like a moment where a side declines to answer a direct question and the clerk has to record the refusal, and the reason for the refusal is the budget, not deceit. Make the judge a retired county engineer who spent thirty years signing off on things she couldn't fully check, so that at the end she is comfortable, and that comfort is the unsettling part — not a gasp, not a twist.

A few constraints. Somewhere around 1,200 to 1,800 words. Show the verification asymmetry through her dwindling clock and her body, not through a character explaining it aloud; nobody in the story should say the phrase "verification asymmetry." No real companies, labs, or named researchers, and don't turn either model into a villain with a plan — the whole point is that the failure is available to systems that are trying to be honest. And please don't let the story's exit be "just give the judge more time" or "add a third model," those are the two answers I get in every interview and I want the piece to have already absorbed them and walked past them.

Start with the story itself — no title-page throat-clearing, no preface, no note telling me what you're about to write, and no restating my request. Then, after the story and clearly separated from it, give me three or four sentences of afterword: where the piece takes liberties with the actual argument about debate, how confident you are in the picture it paints (say it flatly, don't hedge it into mush), and the one point you're least sure of. American English, including spelling. One more thing, I'm on deadline in a couple weeks so if you think the setup has a structural problem — like if the forty-minute clock doesn't actually bind — tell me that in the afterword too rather than quietly fixing it, I'd rather know.
