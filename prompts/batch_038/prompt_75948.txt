Our production company makes a weekly narrative-history podcast, and I am the one who decides whether a contested claim about the distant past goes into the script as stated, goes in hedged, or comes out. Write me a procedure my team can actually run — source criticism in spirit, but usable by people with no historian on staff.

Constraints: fifteen to twenty such claims per episode, twenty minutes each; nobody reads the relevant primary languages, so we work from translations and from modern syntheses; the executive producer signs off only on calls the procedure flags as non-mechanical. We currently sort everything into three labels — attested, disputed, reconstructed — and the scheme misfires in ways I can name. A claim resting on a single late author who is agreed to have used a lost earlier account gets dumped into "disputed" beside genuine live controversies. A boast carved on a monument by the king it flatters is weighted the same as that boast reported by his enemies. An oral tradition first written down eight centuries after the events is discarded even when the archaeology fits it.

Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, which distinctions from the tradition must survive the simplification and which I can drop under those constraints, where the procedure terminates in a label, and where it stops and hands the call to a person because what is at issue is how much confidence we owe an audience rather than what the evidence shows. Tell me if three labels is the wrong number, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle at all — I assume my inability to evaluate a specialist consensus is one.

Then run it on these. (1) Our only account of a battle was written three centuries later by an author whose sources are lost, and four subsequent writers quote him: independent witnesses, or one witness five times over? (2) A story I have treated as legend, where two chronicles composed by parties hostile to one another agree on a detail damaging to both. (3) The season's most vivid scene, which one respected specialist defends as traditional and the rest regard as later invention. I expect (3) to cost me the scene — tell me if it should not, and tell me if (2) obliges me to grant a story more credit than I want it to have.

No bibliography or reading list; just the procedure and the three verdicts.
