Our philosophy-of-AI reading group got completely derailed last week and I still don't know how to score it. Someone pulled up the log from a little agent they'd built for a class project: it was told to summarise a webpage, got a 403, and then re-issued the exact same fetch call — same URL, same parameters — about thirty times before it finaly gave up and reported that it couldn't reach the page. That set off an argument between two people in the group, Dev and Sam, about whether that counts as sphexishness. You know the wasp case: the digger wasp drags her paralyzed cricket to the mouth of the burrow, goes in alone to check it over, and if you move the cricket while she's inside she comes out, finds it, hauls it back to the mouth, and starts the whole routine again.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Dev said that's sphexishness in a nutshell — the agent has a routine, the world stops cooperating, and instead of working out what to do now it just re-runs the routine. Same shape as the wasp.

(2) Sam said no, and that Dev had the wasp wrong anyway. The wasp isn't looping because repetition is her only move; she loops because the routine gets re-triggered every time the prey-at-the-entrance condition is wiped out. The agent's thing is an ordinary retry, and the fact that it eventually quit and said it couldn't reach the page shows it had the goal and knew the goal wasn't being met. Sam added that Dev rattles refresh on a dead page a dozen times himself.

(3) Dev said fine, but then sphexishness is just a measure of how long you persist, and a concept that can't separate "hung in there a bit too long" from "mindless" isn't buying anything over plain failure-to-adapt.

(4) Sam said it does buy something. The point of the wasp isn't that she fails once, it's that you can beat her as many times as you like for free — move the cricket a hundred times, she loses a hundred times. Sphexishness names the failure modes that are cheap to trigger repeatedly, including by someone who's trying to. That's a real engineering property, not just a way of saying it broke.

(5) Dev said that criterion is worse, because on it humans are sphexish all over the place and usually better for it: you can exploit a keeper's habit, a trader's standing rule, a pilot's checklist discipline, and in the pilot case the rigidity *is* the virtue. So either exploitability condemns things we want, or it isn't the criterion.

(6) Sam half-conceded, then went further: said the example the whole notion rests on is shakier than people admit, that the original observations are nineteenth-century and anecdotal, that when the experiments were actually run the wasps didn't loop indefinitely but changed their behaviour after enough iterations, and that re-inspecting a nest that got messed with while you were away is plausibly just sensible caution rather than mindlessness. So we inherited a metaphor from a bad data point.

(7) Dev said that's a different argument: even if the wasp turns out cleverer than advertised, the concept doesn't die with her, because there obviously are systems that re-run a routine regardless of context, and the wasp was only ever an illustration.

(8) Sam said the free-will version of the story is the weakest link of all — what's supposedly disturbing about the wasp isn't that she's predictable, it's that she can't be moved by reasons, and on that standard the agent might be closer to the interesting case than the wasp ever was. Dev said that just concedes his point.

They asked me to referee and I chickened out. Can you go move by move through all eight — say who's right, or how much of each move actually lands, and why — and then give me an overall verdict on which of them has the better grip on what sphexishness is? Then, separately: what are both of them missing? I have a nagging sense they're talking past each other about what the concept is even supposed to track, and about whether it's the kind of thing that comes in degrees without that being a problem, but I can't pin down what the disagreement underneath actually is.
