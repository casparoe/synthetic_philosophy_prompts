I work in policy on the trust-and-safety side of a mid-size platform (not engineering, I mostly write the rules and then argue about them), and last week we had a genuinely ugly four-hour meeting about a loophole: a coordinated campaign had figured out how to use a documented feature exactly as documented, so acting against them meant breaking a rule we had published and told users we'd follow. My colleague ended the meeting by writing "let justice be done though the heavens fall" on the whiteboard and then just kind of standing there until people gave up. Since then I've noticed the line getting thrown around our Slack constantly, doing wildly different jobs — sometimes it means "follow the rule, full stop," sometimes "it's not our place to weigh the consequences," sometimes something more like "if the system burns down because we followed its own rules, that's the system's fault not ours," and once, weirdly, someone used it to argue *for* an exception.

Can you take it apart for me? What I want is a menu of the distinct precise theses one sentence like that could be standing for, not an essay about the history of the maxim. So: is it a claim about agent-relative restrictions that bind me even when breaking them would produce more of what the restriction is protecting? A claim about who is allowed to aggregate harms across different people? An epistemics claim — that we're just too bad at predicting consequences to trade rules for outcomes? A claim about keeping one's own hands clean in a sense that has nothing to do with how the world turns out? A threshold view where the heavens actually falling is exactly the circumstance in which the rule stops binding? A rule-consequentialist move where the ends justify the *rule* but not the exception? Something about integrity, or about the difference between doing and allowing, or about not taking on responsibility for other people's choices? For each one, tell me whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's just false — and then say which readings would actually have earned him his conclusion (that we don't touch the loophole) and which ones wouldn't, because I suspect he was sliding between at least two.

There's also a version of this where I think the maxim backfires on him and I can't tell if I'm just being cute. If the content of the slogan is that justice should be *done*, and the relevant heavens-falling scenario is a lot of people getting away with a much bigger violation than ours, then isn't "let justice be done" telling us to intervene? Is that a legitimate reading or am I equivocating on "justice" — treating it as a state of the world to be promoted when the whole point was that it's a way of acting? Relatedly: the standard objection to deontological constraints is that they seem to forbid the one act that would prevent the most constraint-violations. Where does that land on your menu — does it kill one of the readings, all of them, or does it just push everything onto the question of what the constraint is even for? And is there a reading of the maxim on which that objection simply doesn't get a grip, which I imagine is the one he thinks he's using.
