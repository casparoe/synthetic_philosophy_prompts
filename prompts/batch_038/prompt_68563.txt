I teach a 90-minute session on rational choice to 20 third-year undergraduates in an interdisciplinary program. About half have taken one probability course; two have never used a logarithm outside high school. They know expected-value reasoning and the basics of expected utility. I want them to run headlong into the difference between maximizing expected value and maximizing expected growth rate, and I do not want the phrase Kelly criterion to appear — from me or from a handout — until after they have lost tokens to it.

Design a runnable in-class betting game that does this with paper tokens. No real money, no computers, no projected spreadsheets: everything must work with tokens, a deck of cards or a die, a whiteboard, and handouts I can print the night before.

Here is the core loop I have in mind. Check it, repair it, or replace it if it does not work, but tell me which you did and why.

- Each player begins with 128 tokens — a power of two, so staking everything stays integral for several rounds.
- Each round, every player simultaneously and privately chooses how much of their bankroll to stake. A public, verifiably fair chance device decides: a win adds the stake, a loss subtracts half the stake.
- Staking everything therefore has an expected multiplier of 1.25 per round and a median outcome of exactly no growth. That gap is the engine of the whole activity.

Now solve the bookkeeping honestly. How do players record a stake so I can audit it afterward? What happens when someone holding 54 tokens wants to stake half of it — do I need a bank that makes change, a rounding rule that favors the house, or a paper ledger where half-tokens are legal and only converted at the end? What does a player do on the rounds after they have been reduced to a single token? I need arithmetic that survives twenty real rounds with twenty real people, not arithmetic that merely looks tidy on the page.

Three further requirements.

First, role cards. Deal three or four of them at random at the start, kept secret from the rest of the room: someone who must withdraw a fixed number of tokens every round or leave the game; someone whose payout depends on their rank relative to the room at a specified round rather than on their absolute total; someone who plays only the first round and is paid on its expected value; and someone who is told, privately, that the chance device may not be exactly fair, without being told which way it leans. The debrief must use these to show that the growth-optimal stake is a function of the situation, not a law of nature — and the design should make each of those players behave in a way that looks irrational from the outside until their card is revealed.

Second, a public cross-sectional record. After each round, the mean and the median bankroll in the room go on the board, in that order, before anyone explains anything. The divergence should become visible as a fact about the room rather than as a claim from the podium.

Third, a short closing phase in which participants compare averaging across players at a single moment with averaging across moments for a single player, using only the token records they have already generated. I want that distinction to be something they compute with their own numbers, not something I assert.

Also specify: what the tokens are actually worth at the end, with a real but trivial stake that humiliates no one; a round-by-round timeline in minutes; the exact point at which a ruined player is re-entered or given a job, so nobody sits idle for a quarter of the session; and a debrief script that maps named moments from the run onto the ideas — including where the growth-optimal fraction for this particular gamble comes from, why it is half the bankroll, and why maximizing expected log wealth and maximizing the long-run growth rate are two different justifications that this game cannot tell apart.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I expect at least two things: one player who stakes everything, gets hot, finishes with several hundred tokens, and argues — correctly — that they had the highest expected value of anyone in the room; and a room that draws the lesson as a temperance slogan about greed rather than anything sharper. Give me the intervention for each, and tell me whether I should play myself, and if so, what I should stake.

Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce at all, and that I will have to supply by hand: finite rounds, a known payoff distribution, no consumption, and a room of twenty are not the asymptotic setting the optimality claim lives in.

One epistemic request for the debrief notes. Separate clearly what is a theorem under stated assumptions from what is a live disagreement among people who work on this — in particular, whether long-run growth maximization is the right normative target at all, and whether reframing the issue in terms of time averages versus ensemble averages adds anything to standard expected-utility theory or merely relabels a logarithmic utility function. Do not present either side of that second dispute as settled, and flag any place where my mechanics — the fair device, the halving rule, the finite horizon, the absence of withdrawals — quietly assume the conclusion I want the students to discover. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations; if you invoke a named result or a well-known objection, describe it in a sentence and move on.
