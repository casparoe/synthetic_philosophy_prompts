A friend of mine runs intuition surveys on slurs, but she deliberately uses an invented one so people's real-world feelings don't contaminate the data. In her scenario the dockside city of Ostre calls people from the silt flats "silts," and that's the slur. Her own recorded verdicts so far:

- "Marek is a silt" and "Marek is from the flats" come out true in exactly the same situations, but only the first is an awful thing to say.
- "Marek isn't a silt," "if Marek is a silt then…," and "is Marek a silt?" all keep the bite. Embedding doesn't launder it.
- "Ilse said that Marek is a silt" keeps it too, even though Ilse is an uplander and clearly disapproves of Marek's enemies — the reporter doesn't get off just by embedding.
- A flats-born speaker can use "silt" of herself and her friends with zero derogation, straight descriptively. She's adamant about this one.
- A historian reading a 1920s broadside aloud verbatim in a lecture: derogation present, but not attributable to the historian.

Next week she has to rule on this: a flats-born witness at a discrimination tribunal says "silt" of himself, on purpose, in front of an upland panel, to make them feel what the word does. He isn't describing anything, he's weaponising it at them. Derogatory or not?

I care less about the answer than the machinery. Which of the standard packages — truth-conditional, presuppositional, conventional-implicature, purely use-based/pragmatic, and the one that makes the slur an indexical of the speaker's own group identity — is she implicitly running? Which others fit all five verdicts just as well, and where do they split on the tribunal case? Give me your best guess about her verdict and why you prefer that rule, then name the one further case I should hand her that would break the tie.
