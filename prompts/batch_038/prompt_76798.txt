I spent thirty-five years in comparative physiology, most of it on crustaceans, and have taken up the philosophy of animal welfare only since retiring, where I have run into what looks to me like a contradiction in the literature on farmed fish and shellfish. The position I keep meeting is that nociception and pain come apart: an animal can mount a complete nociceptive withdrawal with no felt experience accompanying it, which is precisely why reflex measures are said to be insufficient. Granting that, however, I cannot see what the standard welfare tests are testing. Operant choice tasks, trade-offs between a noxious stimulus and feed, the disappearance of rocking and wound-rubbing once an analgesic is given, animals returning to a location where they once received relief: these are all behavioral measures, and the skeptic's argument is that elaborate behavioral flexibility is fully compatible with nothing being felt at all. So either the nociception/pain distinction does no work in this debate, or these tests are aimed at the wrong target and we ought to be resting our conclusions on something else entirely. Which is it?

If your answer is that the tests do escape the skeptic's charge, please say exactly what they measure that nociception alone does not, and why the same measures are treated as probative for a trout but as considerably weaker evidence for a prawn. Close by telling me what would change your mind about any of this: which consideration would lead you to revise the verdict you give me, or where you suspect your own reasoning is most likely to break down.
