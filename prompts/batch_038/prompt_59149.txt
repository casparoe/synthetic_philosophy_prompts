I cover AI and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I'm doing a long-form Q&A with a researcher who's been circulating a proposal for how a deployed system should decide whose values to serve. She isn't a public figure and you won't know the proposal, so here it is in my words — assume this account is accurate and treat it as the whole of the view:

- She gives up on liberal neutrality. Trying to be neutral among conceptions of the good is, on her account, a dodge, because any procedure you pick already favors some ways of living. So instead the system aggregates burdens: for any decision, each person's stake is weighted by what it would cost them to go along with the alternative — what they'd have to give up, not how much they'd gain.
- Her central claim is that burdens are interpersonally comparable in a way values aren't. You can compare what people lose without ever ranking what they care about.
- There's a floor: no coalition, however large its summed sacrifice, can make a small group *act* against its core commitments. But the floor doesn't cover decisions that merely make those commitments expensive to sustain. Those get compensated and logged, not vetoed.
- The aggregation base is only people who currently have formed commitments. No future generations, no small children, no uploaded or non-human minds — you can't burden someone who has nothing to frustrate yet.
- Every decision ships with a publically available ledger saying who is burdened and by how much, and she thinks the publicity of that ledger is doing most of the normative work.

She also has a tic I want to break: any objection about measurement gets the same answer, "the point isn't to get the weights exactly right, it's to make the tradeoffs visible."

Write me eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question each on:

1. how you elicit burdens without the metric collapsing into ability to pay, and what happens to people whose expectations were trained down so they report almost no cost to complying;
2. the act/sustain line at the floor — bring a concrete case, something like a language community nobody compels to do anything but that can no longer afford schools, courts or media in its own tongue, and press on why that isn't the same harm wearing a different hat;
3. whether her burden metric is itself a substantive view of the person that a perfectionist or a utilitarian has no reason to accept, given she already conceded that procedures are never neutral;
4. whether the veto floor and the weighting rule can contradict each other, and what the scheme outputs when two groups are incompatible in the stronger sense that accommodating one *is* maximally burdening the other;
5. whether excluding the not-yet-formed squares with the fact that her system will run for decades and shape which commitments people end up having;
6. what the framework predicts that a straight bargain among the well-organized wouldn't, and who actually runs the elicitation.

Then give me a two or three question sequence that makes "make the tradeoffs visible" progressively harder to fall back on, ending with this: is there any concrete decision her scheme would endorse that she personally would find repulsive? If she says no, what should I take that to mean about the scheme — and if she says yes, why keep it?

No warm-up questions about her career, and no suggestions of books or papers for me to read beforehand; I want questions I can ask cold, with nothing to look up.
