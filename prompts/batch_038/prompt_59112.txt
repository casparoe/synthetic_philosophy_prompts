Half my department says Rome fell and the other half says nothing fell, it just got reorganised, and after two semesters of this nobody has budged anybody. What's maddening is that they're citing the same evidence. Both sides grant that long-distance trade contracted hard — far fewer Mediterranean shipwrecks after the second century, coarser and simpler pottery, cattle shrinking back toward pre-Roman sizes, tiled roofs basically disappearing from rural Britain — and both sides equally grant that Latin stayed the language of law and administration, bishops carried on governing cities, barbarian kings ruled in thoroughly Roman style, Odoacer shipped the imperial regalia off to Constantinople in 476 insted of taking the throne himself, and the eastern half ran for another thousand years. Material-decline people say that's a fall with a nicer soundtrack; transformation people say the pots are noise and the institutions are the actual thing; each thinks the other is cherry-picking the record.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the fight isn't about evidence at all, it's about which thing "Rome" is — a fiscal-military state, a material standard of living, a legal-political order, or an idea about legitimacy — and each camp has quietly chosen a different subject and then argues as though that choice were neutral. Two corollaries I can't decide between. Either the question is contaminated because we inherited it from Renaissance humanists who needed a dark middle to flatter themselves, so "did Rome fall" is partly a dispute about a periodization scheme rather than about the past. Or the stall has little to do with Rome and a lot to do with the fact that the story is load-bearing for modern declinism and immigration arguments, so nobody can afford to lose.

So: diagnose it. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal fight over "fall" and "Rome", two camps answering different questions under one label, a clash about which kinds of evidence count as decisive, a hidden empirical question hiding under it (the causal-mechanism stuff — post-536 climate cooling, plague, lead, migration pressure — which everyone concedes is underdetermined), or an ill-posed question that should be dissolved? Argue from what each side actually says; don't hand me a survey of late antiquity.

Exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion. The fifth has to be the one about what would count as progress — a distinction, a new kind of data, a reformulation of the question, or nothing at all if you think the impasse is stable for reasons that have nothing to do with Rome.
