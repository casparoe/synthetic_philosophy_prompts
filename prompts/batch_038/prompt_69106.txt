I teach a third-year seminar in practical philosophy: sixteen students, a single hundred-minute session, a room with movable furniture and no budget beyond paper and printing. The students have encountered standard material on coercion and on informed consent, and roughly the tripartite structure common in biomedical ethics — adequate information, absence of controlling pressure, sufficient decisional capacity. They have not met the apparatus below, and I do not want it presented to them as coming from the literature.

I want the session built around a model I have sketched for my own use, which we may call the dial-and-floor model. On it, any episode of apparent consent can be scored on three dials: **Information** (how accurate and adequate the consenting party's picture of the transaction was), **Pressure** (how much genuine room there was to decline), and **Capacity** (how far the party's decisional abilities were intact at the moment of assent), each on a scale of 0 to 10. By the end of the session three rival hypotheses about how validity relates to those scores should be live and contestable:

- **Additive**: validity tracks a weighted sum of the three scores, so a deficit on one dial can be paid for out of surplus on another.
- **Floor**: each dial has its own threshold, and falling below it invalidates the consent however high the other two stand; above the floors, further surplus does no normative work.
- **Shifting floor**: the thresholds are not constants but move with the stakes of the transaction and with the relationship between the parties, so the Capacity score that suffices for agreeing to be photographed does not suffice for agreeing to a lumbar puncture.

A fourth variable is to be introduced late rather than at the outset: **Detectability** — whether the party on the receiving end of the consent could reasonably have spotted the defect. Its purpose is to drive apart two questions my students reliably collapse: whether the consent was valid, and whether the recipient did wrong in relying on it.

Please design the whole session. In particular:

1. **A staged outline with minute allocations**, in which the students generate the data themselves before any of the three hypotheses is named. I want a blind rating phase in which each student scores unlabelled vignettes on the three dials and records a verdict, so that the aggregate can then be interrogated for structure.
2. **At least eight fully written vignettes**, each short enough to be read aloud in under a minute, presented in a table giving the text, the scores you intend them to carry, and the verdict you expect under each of the three hypotheses.
3. **Two crossing pairs** within that set: pairs of vignettes on which the Additive and Floor hypotheses give opposite verdicts. Explain what a divergence in the class's own responses across such a pair is supposed to show, and — importantly — what it would show if the class does *not* diverge, since I suspect a coherent group might simply absorb the tension without noticing it.
4. **A surplus-trading round** in which students are permitted to move points between dials on a given card to reach a validity threshold, with a rule set that makes the permissibility or impermissibility of such trades the thing they are actually deciding. Say what absurd verdicts this round is engineered to produce, and at what point I should stop it.
5. **The Detectability phase**, and a debrief sequence that maps specific moments of the activity onto the distinctions between a condition being necessary and being sufficient, between invalidity and impermissible reliance, and between a threshold being fixed and being stakes-relative.
6. **An optional closing module** in which, at the very start of the session, students are invited to sign a sheet agreeing in advance that one feature of the class will be withheld from them, and the withholding is then disclosed in the final minutes so that the class can rule on whether its own consent to participate was valid. I want your assessment of whether advance agreement to a later non-disclosure can cure that non-disclosure, and whether running this module is ethically defensible in a graded seminar; if you judge it indefensible or merely risky, supply a non-deceptive substitute that still puts the students on the receiving end of the question rather than only discussing it.
7. **A frank failure list**: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. At minimum, address the spurious precision invited by a 0–10 scale, and the objection a sharp student will raise that Pressure is not really a separate dial at all, because coercive offers either are or produce Capacity deficits. Tell me whether that collapse is a bug to be managed or a discovery to be welcomed, and how to keep the session running either way.
8. **An honest limitation note** on what this activity cannot reproduce about consent and what I must supply in words — I expect the absence of the real consenting party from the room, and the fact that validity is normally adjudicated by someone with standing or authority rather than by a committee of peers, to figure here.
9. **A short reflexive assessment** of whether the consistency test in (3) gives the students any genuine evidence about which hypothesis is correct, or whether it only dramatises the question; if the latter, say so plainly and explain what the exercise is worth anyway.

Write in a formal register, and write the entire response in British English, using British spelling and British institutional vocabulary where the setting calls for it. Do not supply a bibliography or a list of further reading, and do not attribute any of the apparatus above to a named author.
