I facilitate a standing peer-accountability panel at a research consortium. Its most common derailment is a respondent declining to engage on the ground that the complainant is in no position to raise the matter at all: the complainant did the same thing, or took part in the very episode complained of, or was sanctioned for comparable conduct and never owned it, or simply wants the respondent to lose. Some of these objections are real, some are pure deflection, and some are real but only as to part of the complaint. The panel has no shared way of telling which is which, so I want a triage instrument we can run aloud in the room. Please build it in the following sections, in this order.

**1. Vignettes (about 300 words total).** Five cases, roughly 50 words each, that between them cover: a complainant who committed the identical conduct more recently than the respondent did; a complainant who was a knowing participant in the episode being complained about; a complainant whose own comparable conduct is years old, publicly acknowledged, and already sanctioned; a complainant who is clean on this matter but has a reputation for keeping score; and a complaint lodged by an institute rather than by a person, where several of that institute's own members are implicated in the conduct complained of.

**2. The questions (about 600 words).** Nine to eleven numbered items, each with two or three answer options and no free text. Label each item as addressed to the complainant, to the respondent, or to the panel. Hard constraint: no item may contain the words "standing," "hypocrisy," "tu quoque," or "blame," and no item may name a philosopher, a book, or an -ism; the instrument has to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like. At least one item must probe whether the objection depends on the relative gravity of the two episodes at all, since that is where the underlying views genuinely come apart, and at least one must probe whether the complaint concerns what the respondent privately thinks versus what the complainant says out loud to the respondent and to third parties.

**3. The tree (about 350 words).** Present the items as an ordered branching structure in path notation, with the question at each node, the answer options as branches, and the leaf reached at the end of each path. Where an answer makes further questions irrelevant, show the path terminating early.

**4. The leaves (about 400 words).** Seven to nine terminal positions, each with a descriptive name of your choosing (not an attribution to anyone) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the thing its proponent has to say that they would rather not.

**5. Unoccupied cells (about 200 words).** Identify at least three combinations of answers the question set makes available that no leaf corresponds to, and diagnose each: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored?

**6. What this instrument cannot separate (about 150 words).** Name any pair of leaves that the questions as designed fail to distinguish, and say what further question would be needed — or why it cannot be asked without breaking the constraint in section 2.

**7. Two worked runs (about 250 words).** Trace the institute-level vignette and the years-ago-and-sanctioned vignette through the tree, question by question, and state where each lands and why the result is or is not satisfying.

Keep the whole thing under 2,000 words. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations of any kind — the panel will be arguing from the cases, not from the literature.
