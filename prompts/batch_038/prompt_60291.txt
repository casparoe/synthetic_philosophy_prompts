Bit of background first. I've been running a philosophy evening group in the back room of our local library for about two years now — fortnightly, anywhere between 12 and 20 people depending on the weather and whether there's a match on, ages from about 19 to 70, and only two of them have ever done any philsophy formally. We normally just sit round and talk about a printed article, which is fine but has got predictable. I want to do something they actually have to take part in for once: 90 minutes including the tea break, chairs in a circle, a table at the front, no budget to speak of, so nothing that needs real money (beans, tokens or a scoring sheet are all fine).

The reason it's on my mind: I've just got back from five months working abroad. I'm deliberately not going to name the country, partly because it doesn't matter and partly because I don't want the evening turning into a chat about national character. Three things happened that I'm still chewing on. One — I crossed on a red pedestrian light when the road was completely empty, nothing moving for a hundred metres either way, and a woman behind me made a noise about it, and I felt genuinely bad, which surprised me, because I couldn't for the life of me say who I'd wronged or what I'd damaged. Two — there was some service-charge business in restaurants that I never got the hang of, so I did what a colleague told me was done, and felt like a tight git on the nights I didn't. Three, and this is the one I really want the activity to get at: the colleague had given me a fairly specific rule about how you behave in a particular everyday situation, and I followed it faithfully for months, and then it turned out, more or less by accident, that it wasn't a rule at all. She'd half-invented it. Nobody else was doing it. And yet the whole time I was doing it I felt bound by it, and faintly ashamed when I skipped it, and I caught myself mentally ticking off other visitors who didn't. A norm with nothing behind it but everyone's belief in it felt, from the inside, exactly like the real ones. That's the bit I don't have a handle on, and its the bit I want them to run into rather than be told about.

So — design me an activity, a game or a structured thing with roles, whatever you think works, where the group hits this stuff themselves before anyone names it. I want them to come out the other end having felt at least three things without being led to them: that some of the everyday conventions we're weighing up are pure coordination and break down harmlessly the moment you break them, while others seem to generate something you actually owe to particular other people, and you can't tell from the inside which is which; that the pressure to comply slides about depending on how many other people you can see complying, so there's a threshold hidden in the obligation that we don't usually admit to; and the big one, that a rule existing only because everyone believes everyone else is following it is indistinguishable, in the moment, from a rule with a real basis behind it. I'm quite keen on planting a fake custom in the room — invent one, brief one or two people in it as though it were simply how things are done here, and let everyone else pick it up and start enforcing it on each other — but I've no idea whether that survives contact with fifteen real adults, or whether someone spots it in two minutes and announces it to the room. And I'd like a few of them put in the position of the visitor who was never briefed at all, because I want the group to have to argue afterwards about whether that visitor did anything wrong.

What I need back: the rules and the roles; a materials list I could assemble from a stationery cupboard; a token or scoring scheme that generates genuine social pressure without real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; the actual text of any cards or briefs I'd hand out (write them out in full, don't describe them); and the debrief. The debrief matters more than the game. I want it to map particular things that will have just happened in that room onto the questions, rather than being a little talk from me at the front, and I want them to hear any vocabulary worth giving them only after they've hit the thing. There's some psychology distinction between what people actually do and what people approve of that I think is floating around this territory, but I don't want to open with it. Then be straight with me about two things. What will go wrong the first time I run it — the bloke who won't play, the person who turns it into a competition and flattens the point, the group that cooperates all the way through and makes it dull, and the moment someone says "but none of this was real so nothing I felt in there counts" — and what I should actually say or do at each of those. And what the activity can't reproduce that I'll have to supply by hand: my guess is that it's the fact that real conventions are backed by people you'll see again next week and next year, and by consequences that follow you home, which a one-off evening with a room full of volunteers can't touch. Tell me if that's the real gap or if there's a bigger one — maybe the fact that in ordinary life you can go and look at how many people are complying, whereas in my room I'd be feeding them that number.
