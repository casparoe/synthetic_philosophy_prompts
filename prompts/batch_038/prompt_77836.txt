I teach a graduate seminar on the early modern philosophy of mathematics, and for our closing session I want to stage an anachronistic confrontation. My question is this: how would Hobbes — not the Hobbes of the political treatises, but the Hobbes of *De Corpore* and of his two decades of quarrel with Wallis — have judged the geometry that came after him?

Let me fix the two doctrines I take to be load-bearing in his philosophy of mathematics, so that the exercise engages those rather than his politics. First, his account of what makes demonstration genuine: philosophy proceeds either from the generation of a thing to its properties, or from properties back to a possible generation, and we have science in the strict sense only where we know causes because we ourselves construct the object. Geometry is his paradigm case precisely because a figure is generated by motion and the geometer is the author of that motion; his nominalism then supplies the rest, since the definitions standing at the head of a science are stipulations fixing the signification of names, and reasoning is the reckoning of the consequences of names agreed upon. Second, his hostility to the analytic methods of his contemporaries: he regarded the manipulation of algebraic symbols as a device for getting answers without acquiring understanding, marks on paper doing duty for conceptions the operator never actually frames — which is much of why he judged Wallis's work, and the infinitesimal methods built on it, ingenious but unscientific.

Do not confine yourself to those two. His refusal to grant any conception of an actually infinite magnitude, his treatment of a point and a line as bodies considered in a certain way, and his reworking of the Euclidean theory of proportion all bear on what follows, and you will likely want to add commitments of your own.

Now bring in three things he never saw. (1) Consistent non-Euclidean geometries, together with the eventual demonstration that the parallel postulate cannot be derived from the remaining assumptions. (2) Hilbert-style axiomatics, on which the primitive terms of a theory are fixed solely by their position in the axioms and are, as Hilbert told Frege, interchangeable with anything you care to name. (3) Computer-assisted proofs: the four-colour theorem as established by Appel and Haken, and the Kepler conjecture as certified by Hales's formalization project — in both of which the verification is too long for any individual mathematician to survey.

I want an essay predicting his verdict on each of the three, and it may not hedge into "he would have had mixed feelings." Commit to a verdict in each case and then identify which of his own commitments does the work in producing it. Resolve, in particular, the tension my students will spot at once: his nominalism about definitions looks like a charter for stipulating whatever geometry one pleases, which casts him as Hilbert's ancestor; his constructivism, and his insistence that names be tied to conceptions the reasoner can frame and follow, look like a standing objection to a mathematics whose steps no human being can inspect, which casts him instead as Brouwer's. Say why one strand dominates, or specify what distinguishes the cases in which each is triggered.

Then turn to the case that I think will decide the question. Suppose Hobbes had been shown Lindemann's proof of the transcendence of π, and with it the demonstration that quadrature of the circle by straightedge and compass is impossible. Would he have taken this as finally settling the problem he pressed from *De Corpore* until his death, or would he have dismissed it as one more piece of symbol-mongering that never generates the magnitude it purports to speak of? Answer that, and use the answer to say something general about whether his philosophy of mathematics was fundamentally a theory of proof, a theory of understanding, or a theory of authority — for I suspect that what he ultimately wanted from geometry was not the tracking of truth but the end of dispute, and I want to know whether the modern developments would have served that aim or sabotaged it.

Two constraints on the submission. Paraphrase Hobbes rather than quoting him; I want the doctrines, not the diction. And I want argument, not a survey of what other scholars have said about him.
