For two years I have sat on the documentation standards committee of a state health agency, and I have become increasingly unable to articulate what our central practice actually assumes. The practice is this: every document that goes out to the public — benefit notices, eligibility letters, discharge instructions, the text of a public comment page — must be run through a readability checker that returns a United States school grade level, computed essentially from average sentence length and average syllables per word. Body text has to come in at or below the eighth-grade level; anything a patient must act on, at or below the sixth. Alongside the score we maintain a flagged-word list covering Latinate abstractions, nominalizations, passive constructions, and any term that has an approved equivalent in the plain-language glossary. Writers revise until the checker turns green, a reviewer records the number, and the document is then certified as plain. The certification attaches to the number rather than to any judgment about whether a particular reader could work out where they stood.

Two things about the results have struck me. First, the metric is easy to satisfy without touching the content. Our writers have learned to chop sentences, multiply bullet points, and address the reader in the second person, all of which move the score sharply; and when a paragraph contains genuinely irreducible conditionality — an exception to an exception, a discretion reserved to a caseworker — that material migrates into footnotes, cross-references, hyperlinked policy manuals, or simply into the judgment of the person at the front desk. Sentence-level difficulty falls, and the reader's ability to determine what applies to them often does not improve. Second, the checker is blind precisely where I am most uneasy. A short, monosyllabic, syntactically simple sentence can still be a way of not saying something: a notice telling a family that their 'coverage will be adjusted' is perfectly plain by every measure we apply and tells them nothing at all. Meanwhile the same agency, when it reviews consent documents for a research protocol, insists on comprehension testing — real readers drawn from the target population, asked afterwards what they understood — and treats a grade-level score as no evidence whatsoever. So we hold one view of clarity in the communications office and a flatly different view in the research office, and I cannot tell whether that is an inconsistency or two legitimate specializations.

What I want is not a summary of Orwell's Politics and the English Language, which I have read, but a reconstruction of the theory of language our practice is standing on. What does it presuppose about where meaning lives — in the sentence, in the vocabulary, or in the relation between a particular writer and a particular reader? Does the grade-level gate assume that obscurity is produced by word choice and syntax rather than by structure, by reserved discretion, or by an interest in not being understood? And is the practice genuinely Orwellian, or does it invert him? His rules about short words, the active voice, and cutting words out are exactly the ones we operationalize, but the essay's larger argument — that linguistic decline has political and economic causes, that the resulting slovenliness feeds back and makes it easier to think foolishly, that a writer's first task is to see clearly and choose words for their meaning, that the final rule licenses breaking the others rather than committing a barbarism, and that the worst political language arises where there is something that cannot be defended openly — seems to point somewhere else entirely. I would particularly like you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably. Democratic accountability, informed consent, reducing avoidable calls to the helpline, and limiting litigation exposure are all offered internally as justifications, and I suspect that some of them need nothing beyond comprehension testing while only one of them actually motivates the numeric gate; tell me which, and say why. Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible theories of communication would equally license it, and where it commits us to something specific that a rival theory would forbid. Finally, tell me what would have to change institutionally — who certifies a document, what counts as evidence of clarity, what happens to the footnotes and to the discretion buried inside them — if the agency adopted the view that clarity is a property of a reader's capacity to act on a text rather than a property of the text's surface.
