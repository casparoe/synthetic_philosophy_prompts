I spent thirty-five years in integrative physiology — homeostasis, thermoregulation, the neuroendocrine control of the internal milieu — and since retiring I have been reading the philosophical and clinical literature on the determination of death. I expected a technical question with a settled empirical answer. What I found is an argument that has not moved in forty years, conducted by people who agree on essentially all of the physiology. I am not looking for a survey of the positions or for a verdict on which is right; I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement.

Here is the terrain as I understand it. One camp holds that death is the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole, that the brain is the body's principal integrator, and that a brain-dead body on a ventilator is no more an organism than a warm flask of living cells: the parts persist, the whole is gone. A second camp observes that such bodies heal wounds, mount immune responses, digest and excrete, hold their temperature with support, and have sustained pregnancies for months at a stretch — which they take to show that somatic integration demonstrably continues, and that the whole-brain criterion is consequently a policy preference dressed in biological language. A third camp abandons the biological framing entirely and defines death as the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, which selects largely the same patients for a wholly different reason. There is also a separate quarrel about retrieval after circulatory death, where the argument turns on whether the cessation of circulation is irreversible in the sense that it could not be restored, or merely permanent in the sense that no one will attempt to restore it.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two main camps are answering different questions under one word: one is asking what has happened to a biological individual, the other is asking when a body may be treated as a corpse for purposes of withdrawing support and procuring organs. A second, weaker part of my hypothesis is that much of the heat comes from the ambiguity between irreversible and permanent.

Test that hypothesis rather than flatter it. Specifically: is there a residual disagreement that survives the disambiguation — a genuine conflict about what an organism is, or about whether organismic unity admits of degrees — and if so, state it precisely. Does the extension of the rival criteria actually diverge anywhere: in the persistent vegetative state, in anencephaly, in the patient who retains some neuroendocrine function? If the criteria never come apart in practice, does that make the dispute verbal, or merely about the rationale rather than the verdict? And given whatever diagnosis you settle on, say what would count as progress — a distinction, an empirical result, the abandonment of the requirement that donors be dead, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected because the concept is being asked to do two incompatible jobs at once.
