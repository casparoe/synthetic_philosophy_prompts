I spent thirty-five years doing field and lab work on solitary wasps and their relatives, and have only picked up philosophy properly since retiring — mostly philosophy of mind, rather badly, at a Tuesday morning group I joined at the local U3A. Last week we landed on the digger wasp example. The wasp stings and paralyses a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it lying outside while she goes in to check everything is in order, then comes out and hauls it down. If you shift the cricket a few inches while she's underground she finds it again, but the sequence starts over: back to the entrance, back inside to re-inspect, and so on, apparently for as long as you keep interfering. The version our group read reaches the philosophers by way of Hofstadter and Dennett and, behind them, an engineer's book about the brain from the early sixties; the name for the property — sphexishness — is Hofstadter's, meaning mindlessness got up as purposeful activity.

Being the entomologist in the room I put in a few things of my own. That the observations go back to Fabre, and that a philosopher called Keijzer has argued the example survives in the literature because it's rhetorically handy rather than because it's accurate, since wasps do begin to adapt after enough repetitions. That re-inspecting a nest which appears to have been disturbed isn't obviously stupid — it may be the sensible move. And that from my own seasons at the nest banks the picture is patchy: some individuals look locked in, others give up and improvise. I dont know how much weight any of that should carry in what followed, and I didn't pretend to.

What followed was between two other members, Helen and Dev, and I mostly kept my mouth shut. Helen is a retired anaesthetist; Dev is about thirty-five and designs control systems for process plant.

(1) Dev said the wasp shows free will is just a matter of how much behavioural flexibility a system has: the wasp fails a flexibility test, we pass one, no mystery left over. Helen said he'd quietly swapped the words — coping with novelty isn't the same thing as being free, and on his criterion a clever enough adaptive controller would have free will, which he surely wouldn't grant.

(2) Dev said fine, then go up a level: real agents don't only revise behaviour, they revise the rules by which they revise behaviour, so it's a hierarchy of loops, and the wasp's loop closes at the bottom while ours closes much higher up. Helen said any finite stack has a top, and whatever sits at the top isn't itself revised by anything — it simply runs, exactly like the wasp's routine, only buried where nobody bothers to look. And if he made the stack infinite, nothing ever gets settled and there's no agent left to be free.

(3) Dev said the real criterion isn't height, it's whether the system can be surprised — whether anything in it functions as *that shouldn't have happened*. Prediction error, mismatch, that sort of thing; and the wasp has some of it, since she goes and retrieves the moved cricket. Helen said then every homeostatic organism is free, including a woodlouse veering away from dry air, and besides the wasp can only be surprised about the world, never about her own rule — whereas we can catch ourselves having done the same thing forty times.

(4) Dev agreed and said that's precisely the difference: we can aim the error signal at the error-correcting machinery itself. Helen said that makes the criterion self-monitoring rather than flexibility, and it's still quantitative — a monitor watching a monitor watching a monitor. She added a case from her working life: patients under light anaesthesia who carry out well-learned sequences of movement competently and afterwards have no recollection of them and no sense of having chosen; plus everyone has driven the last mile home without the faintest memory of doing it. So the wasp/human boundary isn't a boundary, it's a smear.

(5) Dev said then maybe the honest conclusion is that we are all partly sphexish, and free will is just what we call the parts that aren't. Helen said that isn't a deflation of free will but an evaporation of it: if freedom is only whatever a given explanation hasn't yet swallowed, it's defined by our ignorance, and it shrinks every time the neuroscientists do better. That's a placeholder, not a faculty.

They both stoped there and looked at me, which was awkward. Could you go through their exchange move by move — the five numbered points — and say who's right at each, with reasons, and then say who comes out better overall? I'd also like your view on what both of them are missing, including whether the actual facts about the wasp make any difference to the arguement at all, or whether Helen and Dev would have had exactly the same row if the insect turned out to be flexible after all. British English please, I keep having to translate.
