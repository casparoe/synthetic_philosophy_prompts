You are to act as a specialist in late-antique moral theology who also serves as an experienced university examiner: someone who has taught Augustine's two treatises on lying repeatedly and who knows how to write an essay question that cannot be answered by producing a competent summary. Work carefully, and plan explicitly before you draft.

**The context.** I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar, "Truthfulness and Its Exceptions," and I am writing this year's closed-book final. Three weeks of the course were spent on Augustine's positions on lying: the claim that lying is always sinful and never to be done for the sake of any good outcome; the account of what makes an utterance a lie, located in the doubleness of the heart rather than in the truth-value of the sentence uttered; the permission to conceal the truth alongside the prohibition on asserting what one does not believe; the graded ranking of kinds of lie by seriousness, running from lies about religious doctrine down to lies told to shield another person from death or from sexual violation; and the later treatise written to block a scheme for infiltrating a heretical sect by pretending to belong to it. Students also read the standard secondary literature on absolutism and on the lying/deception distinction. The exam is 90 minutes, one compulsory essay question, worth 30 marks, with an expected length of about 1,400 words. Students are told in the rubric that they may identify arguments descriptively — no chapter numbers, no page references, no recalled quotations are expected or rewarded.

**The case I want the question built around.** Please use the following constructed case (it is a teaching device of mine, not a historical report, and the question should present it as such). During a persecution, an officer asks a Christian householder point-blank whether a fugitive is hidden somewhere in the house. Minutes earlier the householder moved the fugitive from the cellar to a concealed space in the roof. The householder has four replies available:

- **(A)** A flat denial that anyone is hidden in the house — false, and believed by the speaker to be false.
- **(B)** A statement that the cellar is empty — literally true, believed true, and chosen precisely because the officer will infer from it that no one is hidden anywhere in the house.
- **(C)** A refusal to answer, or silence.
- **(D)** An ambiguous utterance whose sense the speaker resolves privately in a way he does not disclose, so that what he believes and what he says are matched only by an interpretation the hearer has no access to.

**What the question must make students do.** Write the question so that a student has to: (i) determine, using Augustine's own criteria and not general moral intuitions, which of (A)–(D) he would rule out outright, which he would permit, and which would fall in between, saying which element of the criteria is doing the work in each case; (ii) examine whether the doubleness-of-heart criterion and the underlying rationale that signs exist to convey what is in the mind pull apart in (B) and (D) — that is, whether his framework has the resources to forbid engineered misleading once every assertion has been made true, or whether it licenses exactly that; (iii) explain what the graded ranking of lies does and does not concede to consequences, and whether ranking by seriousness is compatible with holding that no lie is ever permissible; and (iv) end with a defended verdict on which reconstruction of the position is best: an absolutism about the integrity of the speaker that has no notion of an epistemic entitlement owed to the hearer; an absolutism about the proper function of signs that does generate such a notion; or a view that oscillates unstably between the two, with the student saying what in the framework decides. You may polish the wording, add sub-parts, and allocate the 30 marks across the parts as you judge best, but the four tasks above must all be genuinely required for full credit, and the question must fit the time limit.
