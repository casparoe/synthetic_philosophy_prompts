I sit on the appeals panel of a municipal disaster-relief board. Our grant guidelines reduce an award where the claimant's losses were "reasonably avoidable" — that is, where the claimant had a genuine opportunity to reduce the risk and did not take it. Two appeals arrived from the same flood. The first is from a homeowner who twice declined a voluntary buyout of her riverfront property; she says the offer left her to cover transaction and moving costs she did not have, and that her adult son, who has a severe cognitive disability, could not be relocated without losing his day program and the neighbors who knew how to help him. The second is from a renter three blocks away who lost everything and argues he never had a choice at all, because rents on the high ground were beyond him. The panel is split: some members hold that neither claimant had the genuine opportunity the guideline requires, others that the homeowner weighed the offer and declined it, and that a guideline which ignores that fact will simply stop anyone from ever being answerable for their own decisions.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend a disposition for each appeal under the guideline as it stands, and if you judge that the guideline cannot sort these two cases without producing an outcome we could not defend in public, say so plainly and propose the narrowest revision that would. State the strongest form of the panel position you are arguing against and explain why it does not carry the day. Please write it as continuous prose, without headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold text, or tables — in the manner of a considered letter rather than a briefing document.
