I never attended university. I left school at seventeen, spent twenty-odd years working in municipal administration, and educated myself in the evenings — history and the methodology of the social sciences, in an undisciplined but stubborn way. For the past five years I have convened a Friday-evening discussion group in a borrowed room at our local library: roughly a dozen people, mostly analysts and programme officers at the city and at two charities, plus a couple of retirees. They are entirely comfortable with spreadsheets, logic models and evaluation reports, and almost none of them has read any history or philosophy since school. There is a whiteboard, no projector, and no one reads anything in advance. I have ninety minutes.

Next month I want to take up a single question that I have been turning over privately for some time and that I think this group is peculiarly badly equipped to handle: whether the values a culture holds can be a cause of anything at all. I want to work through it by way of Max Weber's thesis about the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism — not as a museum piece of intellectual history, but because it seems to me the sharpest available test case for a general methodological puzzle, and because my colleagues write sentences like "uptake in that neighbourhood is low because of stigma" without ever asking what sort of explanation they have just given.

The puzzle, as I have come to see it: when an explanation cites what people valued — a calling, an ascetic discipline, a conviction that worldly success in one's station is a sign rather than a means — the thing cited is not an event. It has no date, it cannot be assigned to a moment, and it seems to justify the conduct rather than to push it about. So either such explanations are not causal explanations at all, or our picture of what causation requires is too narrow. I suspect Weber's method of the deliberately one-sided ideal type is his answer to this difficulty rather than a fudge for it, but I could be wrong, and I would rather you tell me so.

Please do not explain the thesis to me; I know it as well as my reading allows. Plan how I should explain it. Specifically:

1. The order the pieces must go in, and what genuinely depends on what. Where does the general problem belong — stated up front as an abstraction, or allowed to surface only through the historical case? I worry that starting with the abstraction loses them in five minutes, and that starting with the case turns the evening into a history lecture they will remember as a story about Calvinists.

2. The single case that should carry the whole thing, and what that case must have and must lack. Tell me which of these you would choose and why: Weber's own opening gambit of laying out the spirit through a set of maxims about money, time and credit; the pastoral chain by which a doctrine about who is already saved produces a way of life; or a contemporary example from my colleagues' own work, such as why an eligible family does not claim a benefit it is entitled to. I am drawn to the third and suspicious of my own instinct.

3. The misconceptions they will arrive with or form on the spot, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect: that Weber holds religion caused capitalism; that the thesis amounts to the platitude that culture matters; that it is unfalsifiable because a suitable value can always be found after the fact; that the Protestants were already the merchants, so it is a selection effect; that an ideal type is an average or a stereotype; that values cannot be causes because they cannot be measured. Correct my diagnosis where it is wrong, and tell me which ones I have missed that are more damaging than these.

4. How much of the ninety minutes the philosophical hinge deserves, and how to state it to people who have never met the idea that explaining an action by giving the agent's reasons might be a different kind of thing from explaining an event by giving its cause. Say plainly whether naming that wider debate helps them or swamps the room.

5. One question I can put at the end that would show whether the methodological point has landed, rather than whether they enjoyed the evening. It should be answerable by someone who works in programme evaluation and should fail if all they took away is that Weber was interesting.

6. What I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so out loud: I assume the theology for its own sake, the subsequent empirical literature, the comparison with Marx, and the wider interpretive method, but tell me if one of those is load-bearing after all.

Please give me no readings, no bibliography and no suggested assignments; the group never reads beforehand and I have my own shelves. I want a plan I can hold in my head at a whiteboard.
