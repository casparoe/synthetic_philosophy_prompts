I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and at a workshop dinner last week two of the panelists got into it about a forecaster they both know — a guy who runs a small prediction market and posts prices publicly. Someone had noticed he sold a ticket on "rain in Boise tomorrow" at 62 cents and another on "no rain" at 41 cents, so a buyer who takes both pays $1.03 for a guaranteed $1. He's out three cents no matter what the weather does.

That's where it went sideways. Dana said that's a proof of irrationality, full stop: his numbers aren't probabilities, and the sure loss is what makes that so, not a colorful way of putting it. Ravi said hold on — nobody has to bet both sides at his posted prices; a bookie has to get him to accept the whole package, and he's free to say no, so no actual loss ever happens. Dana said that's beside the point, because the bets are how we find out what he really believes: if he'd take both, then the credences he reports aren't the ones he has, so coherence is constitutive of belief rather than a piece of practical advice. Ravi shot back that this makes the argument either empty or absurd — on that standard nobody is coherent, since all of us would happily pay a cent rather than bet on some logical truth we haven't bothered to check, and now the Dutch book argument is condemning every finite mind for failing to be an ideal logician. Dana said fine, relativize coherence to what the agent can actually compute. Ravi said that if you have to bolt on that clause by hand, the betting argument isn't doing any of the work — something else is, and Dana has just smuggled it in.

Both of them think they won. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, not just overall — and what neither of them seems to be addressing?
