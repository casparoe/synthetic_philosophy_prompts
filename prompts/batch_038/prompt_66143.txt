I am at work on a novel set in the State Planning Committee of an invented socialist republic, in a decade roughly analogous to our own 1970s, and my principal character is the deputy chair responsible for the balance of material supplies — the man who signs the tables. Because I refuse to write economists as caricatures, I have been reading into the calculation debate, and a consultant I work with, an economist of broadly Hayekian sympathies, has pressed on me a formulation of the conclusion considerably stronger than the paraphrase one usually meets. He states it as three linked claims. First, a substantial share of the knowledge that determines what should be produced, where, and by which method exists only in situated, non-propositional form: a shift foreman's feel for the bearing that is about to go, a dispatcher's sense of which yard will foul on a wet Tuesday, a buyer's read on a supplier's true slack. Second, the loss incurred in reporting such knowledge is principled rather than merely practical — no questionnaire, telemetry array, reporting mandate, or incentive-compatible elicitation scheme can carry it to a centre, because stating it forces it into categories that misdescribe the very situation it tracks. Third, therefore prices are not one coordination device among several and not merely a conveniently cheap one: they are the only mechanism known that can transmit that content at all, so the objection to central planning is not about computing power, bureaucratic capture, or motivation, but about the absence of a channel. On this view a planning board with unlimited hardware confronts an impossibility, not a difficulty. I find the argument rhetorically seductive and I want to know precisely where it is exposed before I put it in a character's mouth.

What would count against it? Please work through the three premises separately as well as jointly, and identify the observations, results, and arguments that would move a reasonable holder, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward. I want you to engage, at minimum, with the following lines, and to say for each whether it genuinely damages the thesis or can be absorbed: the existence of enormous internally administered firms that allocate stock among thousands of outlets and dozens of depots by algorithm with no internal market and no price on a transfer between their own warehouses — and what, if anything, distinguishes that achievement from what my deputy chair attempts, or whether it survives only by feeding on market prices at its boundary; the possibility of recovering practical knowledge from behaviour rather than testimony, since a system can infer the foreman's judgement from maintenance overrides and sensor traces without his ever articulating it, which seems to strike at the second premise directly; the computational-feasibility counterattack, both the older trial-and-error proposal in which a board iterates prices against reported surpluses and shortages and the more recent claim that labour-time values for a whole economy reduce to a linear-algebra problem solvable on ordinary hardware — and whether defeating a complexity objection can touch a thesis that was framed to be indifferent to complexity, or whether that indifference is itself a symptom; and the collapse worry, that everything the knowledge thesis explains is equally explained by absent residual claimancy, soft budget constraints, or the distinct argument that without market prices for capital goods no comparison of production methods is possible at all, in which case the epistemic framing adds nothing.

I would also like a candid assessment of insulation. Any apparently successful non-market allocation can be redescribed as too small, too coarse-grained, too slow-changing, or parasitic on the markets surrounding it; tell me what discipline would separate a legitimate restriction of scope from a saving clause, and whether the thesis as I have stated it passes that test. Say what this position predicts that its rivals do not — for instance, something about which sectors a planned economy should handle adequately and which it should mangle, and about how performance should vary with the rate at which circumstances change and the number of distinguishable product varieties — and identify which of those predictions could come out wrong. Distinguish the objections a serious defender would concede as damaging from those he would wave away, and say why. Do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading; where you lean on a real work, episode, or episode-like fact, describe it rather than cite it, and if you are unsure of a title, a name, or a date, omit it, since invented specifics would be worse for my purposes than none. Finally, for the three defeaters you rank highest, add one sentence each on how the thing could be dramatized inside a planning ministry — a document, a meeting, a discrepancy in a returns table — since that is what I will actually have to write. End with a single line beginning Verdict: and containing \answer{...}, where the braces hold exactly one of FATAL-FLAW, SERIOUSLY-EXPOSED, WELL-INSULATED-LEGITIMATELY, or WELL-INSULATED-VACUOUSLY, followed by no more than one sentence of justification.
