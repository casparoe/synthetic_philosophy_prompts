I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized infrastructure company, and for the past two years I've run an internal forecasting guild: roughly forty engineers who file quarterly probability estimates on things like whether a database migration will land on schedule, whether a service will breach its error budget, or whether a vendor will miss a contractual commitment. We score the forecasts, but the part I actually care about is that we also adjudicate disputes over whether a member's *response to feedback about their own reasoning* was legitimate — cases where someone is told, in one way or another, that the thinking behind their number may not be trustworthy. One senior member, whom I'll call Marta, has final say in those adjudications, and I've kept a log of her rulings because I suspect she's applying a fairly consistent set of principles she has never written down. I have no formal training in philosophy and only an undergraduate probability course, so please define any term of art you end up using, and reason from the cases below rather than from the literature; I'm explicitly not looking for a reading list or for the names of people who have written about this.

Here are her rulings so far, in the order they occurred.

1. Ana filed a 0.70 estimate that our payments service would breach its latency budget, based on a careful trace review. Two days later she disclosed that she had done the review during a week of four-hour nights while taking a sedating antihistamine. She voluntarily cut her number to 0.45. Marta ruled this rational, and added that leaving it at 0.70 would not have been.
2. Ben and Carla, with comparable track records, independently filed 0.80 and 0.30 on the same question. Each read the other's write-up closely and reported that they could find no flaw in it. Ben moved to 0.60 and Carla to 0.55. Marta ruled both rational, and said in passing that either of them simply standing pat would also have been rational.
3. Deepak filed 0.70 that we would see a reportable security incident in Q3. Our audit tooling then surfaced a base-rate finding: estimates filed by members coming straight off an on-call rotation had been badly overconfident in roughly forty percent of past instances. Deepak had just finished a rotation. Two senior guild members re-read his write-up and found it sound. He moved to 0.60 and noted in his log that the flag seemed real but that he could not locate anything wrong with his reasoning. Marta ruled this rational, and specifically praised the *shape* of the response: moving partway while recording that he might be wrong.
4. Elena reasoned her way to "the vendor will default," then, working back through the same chain of reasoning, concluded that one of her steps had been sloppy and that the conclusion was therefore probably wrong. She withdrew to 0.50. Marta ruled this irrational, on the grounds that you cannot use the reasoning you are in the act of distrusting to certify the distrust.
5. Farid filed 0.75 with the log line: "I believe this, and I also believe that believing it is irrational for me right now." Marta refused the submission outright, said she would never sign off on a filing in which those two claims sit side by side, and accepted a resubmission at 0.50 with the line deleted.
6. Gil filed 0.80 that a rollout would fail; his analysis was endorsed by two colleagues who had checked it. It then emerged that the metrics pipeline feeding his analysis had been misconfigured for eleven days, so the historical numbers he had reasoned from were partly fabricated by the tooling. He held at 0.80, citing his colleagues' endorsement. Marta ruled this irrational: vouching for the reasoning does not repair a broken input.

The case now in front of her, and the one I need help with, is Dario's. Dario filed 0.85 that our storage migration will miss its announced date. His analysis was read line by line by two colleagues who found no error, and it is corroborated by an independent source — the vendor's own published incident history — so it does not rest solely on our internal data. After he filed, the audit tool flagged that his last six estimates, all made inside a five-week window that he now describes as involving very poor sleep and the start of a new medication, were systematically overconfident, and that the window fully contains the two weeks in which he did this analysis. Dario responded by lowering to 0.60 and writing the following in his log: "I accept the flag as real. On reflection I think continuing to hold this estimate at all is irrational for me right now. But I cannot bring myself to abandon the analysis, which I still find compelling when I reread it." His two reviewers now disagree about the flag itself: one argues that the tool's baseline window happened to include an unusually predictable quarter, so the flag is an artifact; the other argues that the tool's historical accuracy is good and the flag should be taken at face value. Marta has to rule by Friday, and the guild's practice is to either accept a filing, accept it with a corrected number, or send it back.

What I want is a prediction and a recommendation, not a survey. First: predict Marta's ruling on Dario's filing as written — both on whether the state he is in is one she'll call rational, and, if she accepts it at all, on whether she'll require a different number. Second, and more importantly: lay out at least two, ideally three, candidate rules that are each compatible with all six of the precedents above, and show what each of them implies about Dario. I know the precedents underdetermine things — ruling 2 is permissive in a way that seems to pull against rulings 1 and 5, and ruling 3 looks like an endorsement of exactly the posture Dario is taking while ruling 5 refuses something very close to it — so please say explicitly how much weight you put on 3 versus 5, and whether you read the difference between them as principled or as an inconsistency in Marta's record. Third: identify which single precedent is the closest analog to Dario's, and say what the guild commits itself to by treating that one as controlling. Fourth: commit to a best guess about her verdict and defend it, rather than hedging across the rules. Fifth: describe one further concrete case — same setting, invented facts, a few sentences — that would discriminate between your candidate rules, and state what Marta would have to say about it on each rule if she is applying that rule consistently. Finally, tell me what I should actually do: if the guild wants to be internally consistent, should we accept Dario's filing as filed, accept it with a different number, or send it back — and articulate, in plain language and without reference to any named position, the general principle about trusting your own reasoning that we would be adopting with each of those choices.
