So I spent 35 years as a stream ecologist — mostly counting fish and measuring nutrient fluxes in Appalachian headwaters — and since I retired I've fallen into philosophy headfirst, which my wife (died three years ago) would have found hilarious. Lately I've been working through the problem of evil stuff: Plantinga's free will defense, the skeptical theists, and Marilyn McCord Adams on horrendous evils (that Cornell book from the late 90s), whose framing I find much more gripping than the counting-up-pleasures-and-pains versions because she asks whether a particular ruined life can still add up to a great good for the person living it.

Then I read Cormac McCarthy's The Road, and it wrecked me, and now I can't stop thinking about the two together. I'd like a real piece of criticism — call it 1300 to 1600 words, with section headings — that reads the novel through the theodicy debate and doesn't flinch.

Here's what's got me going. The man in the book occupies, functionally, God's position with respect to the boy: he decides what the boy is told, he decides who lives, he carries the pistol and the plan for the boy's own head, and he keeps saying they're the good ones and that they carry the fire. The boy meanwhile asks the questions — over and over, and none of them get answered. So: is McCarthy running a theodicy in miniature with the parent in the deity's slot? And does putting a *finite* providence in that slot expose something the parent-God analogy usually hides — that a human father can't promise a later defeat of the horror, can't offer an afterlife as compensation, and can only keep walking south?

Second, the ecology, which is my home turf. There's no photosynthesis worth mentioning: ash in the air, dead rivers, no insects, nothing on the hoof. Everything edible is either canned — that is, stored sunlight from the old world, finite and being drawn down — or it's other people. So the novel's world is a closed, non-renewable system in which every calorie you keep is a calorie somebody else loses. What does that do to the free will defense, which as I understand it requires a regular, law-governed, resource-adequate environment for moral agency to mean anything? And notice the inversion: the cannibalism in that book isn't free-floating wickedness, it's downstream of the collapse of the biosphere — moral evil as a *consequence* of natural evil, rather than as the price paid for creaturely freedom. I'd like you to push on that hard, including the awkward implication that the boy's generosity (sharing their food with the old man on the road) is only affordable because they found the bunker. Does virtue require surplus, and if so what's left of the soul-making story?

Third, Adams specifically. The locked cellar, the infant on the spit, the thief they strip naked and leave on the road — treat those as candidate horrendous evils in her technical sense, for both the victims and the perpetrators, and ask whether the novel offers anything like a defeat of them. Does the ending, where the boy is taken in by the man with the shotgun and his family, function as a theodicist resolution or is it just luck wearing a nice coat?

Fourth, the mother. She refuses the whole enterprise and kills herself, and the book leaves it genuinely unclear whether she's clear-eyed or merely broken. Take a position on how McCarthy treats her, and on whether the reader is allowed to agree with her without being condemned alongside her.

Fifth — and this is the part I care about most as a critic — tell me where the lens breaks. The book never names the catastrophe, barely says God's name, and refuses explanation at every turn. If the theodicy reading is something I'm bringing rather than finding, say so plainly and say which scenes resist it. I don't want to be the retired scientist who colonized a novel with his new hobby.

And finish with that last page, the passage about brook trout in mountain streams and the markings on their backs as maps of the world in its becoming, something that could not be put back. After thirty-five years of electrofishing those exact animals, I need to know whether you read that coda as the reintroduction of pattern and providence into an otherwise meaningless world, or as pure elegy — a theodicist gesture or its opposite. Then close by saying what the novel contributes to the debate that argument alone can't: I suspect it has to do with the fact that the only answer in the book is a practice rather than a proposition.

Two practical things: don't quote the novel verbatim, I don't have the text in front of you either way, just describe scenes and I'll know which ones you mean; and no reading list or citations at the end, I'll chase down whatever you mention myself. If you're unsure about some plot detail, flag it instead of papering over it.
