I'm an engineer at a ~250-person company and I sit on our architecture review board — three of us, appointed by the CTO, no term limits, no elections, and we can veto any technical decision a team makes. Basically every team resents us and honestly I think they're half right. This is the unelected-judges-overruling-the-legislature problem in miniature and I want to figure out what I actually think by listing my gut calls on cases:

- A team votes 14-0 to build a new service in Rust when everything else here is Python and Go. We block it. Fine, I'd defend that.
- A team votes to change a schema in a way that breaks another team's consumer without asking them. We block. Fine.
- A team votes on something purely internal, nobody else affected, and we block it because the design is over-clever and two of us wouldn't have done it that way. Not fine — even though I think we were technically right about the engineering.
- We block a design because the team that will inherit the on-call for it didn't get a vote and can't yet exist. Fine.
- We block something because it violates a security policy every team signed off on four years ago. Fine.
- We block something because it violates the *spirit* of that same policy in a case the text doesn't cover. Not fine. Feels like legislating from the bench.
- We block something because of a risk the policy's authors plainly never anticipated (prompt injection, whatever). Fine.

Those last two are the pair I can't reconcile — same move as far as I can tell, opposite verdicts from me. And the one I keep flip-flopping on: we vetoed a feature every affected team wanted unanimously, because it would leak user data in a way no user was ever asked about, and users have no seat at any table here.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? I want it stated precisely, with which clause is doing the work in each case, what it says about the flip-flop one, and what it'd say about a case I haven't listed — like us vetoing a proposal to let teams self-certify, which would end the board. Be honest about which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and if the simple principle and the one that saves everything come apart, tell me which to take.
