I'm teaching a philosophy of science seminar — 18 students, one 75-minute class, they've heard the phrase "carving nature at its joints" but haven't read anything technical. I want an activity where they run into the problem of whether a classification is out there in the world or just tracks what you happen to care about, instead of hearing me assert it.

Here's the rough setup, change it if you have something better: no real chemistry or biology. Make up a deck of cards for about 24 fictional samples from some survey — a moon, a mine, a wreck, whatever — each card listing maybe six properties. Split the class into groups and hand each group a different client brief, so one group is being paid to predict which samples dissolve in something and another needs to predict which ones are safe to handle. The trick I want built into the data: both briefs should pick out real, stable clusters, but the clusters crosscut each other, so when the groups present their taxonomies at the end neither one can claim the joints and neither can say the other is just wrong.

So I need the actual card contents (a compact table is fine), the wording of the briefs, a scoring rule for a final prediction round on fresh samples that doesn't involve real money, and debrief questions that map what happened in the room onto the distinction. One group will almost certainly try to declare the other's kinds arbitrary or subjective — how do I steer that so it becomes the lesson instead of a stalemate? And be straight with me about which part of the natural kinds debate this can't actually stage, and what I'd have to supply by hand.
