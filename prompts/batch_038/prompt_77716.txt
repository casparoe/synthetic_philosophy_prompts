**Q3 (20 points).** Sen's 1970 paper "The Impossibility of a Paretian Liberal" derives a contradiction from three conditions. (a) Name the three conditions and state in your own words what the liberalism condition requires — in particular, over what it makes an individual decisive. [4] (b) Reconstruct Sen's example involving a copy of *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, the Prude, and the Lewd. Give each person's ranking of the three states explicitly, say which pair of states each is decisive over, and derive the social ranking step by step, indicating exactly where and why the contradiction arises. [6] (c) Explain one proposed response that attempts to dissolve the paradox rather than merely lament it, and say what that response concedes. [6] (d) In three or four sentences, state whether you think the result reveals something genuine about the relation between rights and welfare, or whether it is largely an artifact of the formal apparatus, and justify your verdict. [4]

Here is the student's answer, in full (I have corrected two typos and nothing else):

> **(a)** The three conditions are unrestricted domain (every logically possible profile of individual orderings is admissible), the Pareto condition (if everyone prefers x to y, society ranks x above y), and minimal liberalism (each person is decisive over at least one pair of social states, meaning there is some pair such that whichever way that person ranks its two members, society ranks them the same way). Each looks individually unobjectionable, which is what makes the result striking. Minimal liberalism should also not be confused with Arrow's non-dictatorship condition, although it entails it: since Arrow already proved that no social welfare function can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship, adding liberalism delivers the impossibility by way of Arrow's theorem anyway, so Sen's real contribution is to identify a philosophically vivid instance of a result that was in place.
>
> **(b)** Let a be the state in which the Prude reads the book, b the state in which the Lewd reads it, and c the state in which nobody reads it and the book is disposed of. The Prude ranks c first, a second, and b last: he would rather nobody read it, but if someone must, he would rather it were himself than the Lewd, since at least he would read it in disgust. The Lewd ranks a first, b second, and c last: his greatest pleasure would be to get the Prude to read it, failing which he would read it himself, and he would rather read it than see it go unread. The Prude is decisive over the pair (a, c), because those two states differ only in whether he himself reads; the Lewd is decisive over the pair (b, c), because those two differ only in whether he himself reads. The Prude prefers c to a, and being decisive over that pair he gets c above a socially. The Lewd prefers b to c, and being decisive over that pair he gets b above c socially. Both prefer a to b, so Pareto forces a above b socially. Chaining these gives b above c above a above b, a cycle; no transitive social ordering exists, so the three conditions cannot all be met. The upshot is usually summarized by saying that a society which respects rights can land in a Pareto-inefficient state, since the rights-driven ranking favors b and both parties agree a is better than b; which in turn suggests the remedy is to let the two of them bargain and trade or waive their rights.
>
> **(c)** Gibbard's response restricts the domain. The example works only because each of the two has other-regarding preferences: the Prude cares what the Lewd does with the book and the Lewd cares what the Prude does with it. If admissible profiles are limited to those in which each person's preferences are self-regarding — each cares only about what happens inside his own protected sphere — then a rule satisfying both Pareto and liberalism exists, and the paradox dissolves. This is now universally regarded as the standard resolution, and Sen accepted it as a complete answer. Nozick, coming from a libertarian direction, took a different line: he argued that the Pareto condition is what has to go, because if rights are side constraints then a unanimous preference that would override someone's rights must be ignored however unanimous it is. On his view Sen's mistake was to suppose that unanimity has any force at all where rights are in play.
>
> **(d)** I think the paradox is largely an artifact. It depends on unrestricted domain, which admits preferences nobody need have, and on modeling a right as a slice of decisiveness over a pair of states that gets fed into an aggregation rule. Once rights are treated as constraints on what may be done rather than as inputs to a social ranking, and once the domain is narrowed to self-regarding preferences, nothing contradictory is left. So the theorem is best read as a warning about how not to formalize liberty, not as a demonstration that liberty and welfare are genuinely incompatible.
