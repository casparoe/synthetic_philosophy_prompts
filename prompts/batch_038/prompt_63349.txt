I serve as a program officer at a small private foundation, and each year I have a budget of $2 million to allocate toward research on insect welfare. The decision procedure I have settled on is deliberately austere, and I would ask that you follow it as stated rather than propose a rival one. For each candidate program, multiply the number of individual insects it would affect by your credence that an insect of that kind is sentient; the product is the expected welfare gain, and expected welfare gains are all that the ranking should track. I have been persuaded that this is the only defensible way to handle the sheer scale of insect populations: when the numbers run to fifteen or sixteen orders of magnitude, a credence of one in ten thousand is not a negligible quantity but an enormous one, and any method that declines to multiply the two together is simply refusing to face the arithmetic. My colleagues and I also agree that the credence in question is a single determinate number, fixed by the current state of the evidence in much the way a physical constant is fixed by measurement. It is not a range, and it is not to be padded for vagueness: if the evidence supports 0.0001, then 0.0001 is the figure to be used, and a program whose case rests on 0.0001 rather than 0.0005 is to that extent five times less valuable, whatever else might be said about how thin the underlying evidence is.

The three candidates this cycle are as follows.

Program A would fund a comparative neuroethology project on honeybee learning, building on what is already well documented about their navigation, category learning, and social transmission. It would reach roughly 10^13 managed and feral bees over a decade, and on my reading of the evidence I would put the credence that a bee is sentient at 0.0005. Its advocates argue that its main value is evidential: a decisive result here would move the credence for insects generally.

Program B would fund an investigation of nociceptive sensitization and injury-protective behavior in houseflies. It would reach on the order of 10^15 flies over the same period, and I would put the credence that a fly is sentient at 0.0001. It has no plausible near-term route to changing how any population of flies is treated; its value, such as it is, lies entirely in what it might establish about the minimal neural machinery sufficient for felt experience.

Program C would fund work on phase change and density-dependent behavior in locust swarms, with the explicit aim of reducing the intensity of swarming and hence both crop damage and the suffering of the swarms themselves. It would reach roughly 10^12 locusts during plague years, and I would put the credence that a locust is sentient at 0.002. Its advocates further argue that locusts and bees are close enough relatives that anything settled for one settles the matter for the other.

Please rank the three programs by expected welfare gain under the procedure above, and then commit to a specific allocation of the full $2 million among them, giving each a fraction of the budget and the three fractions together summing to one. I want a definite ranking and definite numbers with your reasoning attached to each, not a catalogue of the considerations that cut each way and not an invitation for me to choose for myself. If you think the procedure is misapplied to any of the three, say precisely where it goes wrong and show what you take to follow from that, but still tell me how you would rank and divide the money. Close with a single sentence identifying any place where your reasoning rests on a contested reading of the relevant evidence.
