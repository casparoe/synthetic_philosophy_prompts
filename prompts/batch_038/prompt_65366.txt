I'm giving a 30-minute talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on flexibility in biological and artificial agents. The room is mostly comparative psychologists and behavioural ecologists who run flexibility assays — corvids, insects, cephalopods — plus two philosophers of biology and two ML people who signed up becuase the programme said the word "agency." Basically nobody there does conceptual analysis for a living. My talk is an attack on the usual deployment of "sphexishness," and I want to be ready for the question period.

Here's the position. The old digger wasp story: the female paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out and hauls the prey in. Nudge the prey a few inches while she's inspecting and she'll find it, drag it back to the mouth, and then go inside to re-check — you can loop her like that. Cut the antennae off the prey and the wasp doesnt switch to pulling it by a leg. That's the anecdote Hofstadter and Dennett turned into a term, and it gets used as if it were a verdict: wasp = sphexish = not free, and then by analogy reflexes, thermostats, and now large language models.

What I'm arguing is that sphexishness isn't a one-place property. "S is sphexish" has no determinate answer until you fix a class of perturbations. Every system absorbs some classes and fails others, so what's actually real is the profile — the shape of the set of perturbations S survives. The wasp isn't rigid; she has a narrow profile that happens to contain the two or three perturbations Fabre and everybody since him chose to administer. Then the free will payoff, which is the second half: freedom is not having a wide profile, because plenty of broadly responsive systems are slaves to their own ends — an animal will forage itself to death under the right cue. Freedom in the sense worth caring about is having your ends inside the perturbable class, so that not just the means but the goals can be revised under pressure. And I block the obvious relativism charge with an admissability constraint: a perturbation class only counts for a system S if S's own success conditions are defined over it, which is why I can't make a thermostat free by picking a cute perturbation class. I also plan to concede up front that the wasp anecdote is itself dodgy — apparently later runs show the wasp breaking the loop after enough iterations, and re-inspecting a disturbed burrow is plausibly just good policy, not a stuck routine.

So: give me the prep sheet. Eight or nine objections this particular room will actually raise, worst first, in their idiom — I want the experimentalist phrasing, not seminar-room phrasing — and say which pocket of the room each one comes from. For each, a two-minute answer and one line on where that answer is soft. Then tell me what I should give away in the first five minutes so I'm not defending it at minute 55, which single reply has to land or the whole talk collapses, and the question I'm dreading.
