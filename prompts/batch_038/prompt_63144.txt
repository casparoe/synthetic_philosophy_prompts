I teach a third-year philosophy of language module and I'm putting together the final. Act as a working philosopher of language who has set and marked a lot of exams: before you write anything, spend a few bullets working out where the real crux of the case below is, so the question ends up testing that rather than labels.

I want one essay question on vagueness that a student cannot answer by listing supervaluationism vs. epistemicism vs. contextualism. Build it around this case:

"Tall" is being applied to a closed population of 1,000 people. 500 of them are between 150 and 168 cm; the other 500 are between 173 and 190 cm. Nobody is in the 5 cm gap.

Part one should make students decide whether a sorites series can get going here at all, and what the answer says about where the persuasive force of the sorites actually comes from — is it the predicate, or the density of the series? Part two: suppose the only measurements we have were taken with a device good to ±3 cm, so a person reading 170 could really be anywhere from 167 to 173. Ask whether that re-imports vagueness, and if so wether it's the same phenomenon as ordinary sorites vagueness or a different one (ignorance about the world vs. indeterminacy in the predicate). Students should have to say which of the three theories the gap case helps and which it embarrasses, and why — e.g. what constrains the admissible precisifications to land inside the gap, and whether that's a resource or a lucky accident.

Deliver: (1) the question exactly as students would see it, 20 marks, answerable in 45 minutes; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; (3) the marking scheme as a markdown table with exactly these columns — `Criterion` | `Marks` | `What a first-class answer does here` | `The specific mistake that costs these marks`. Four or five rows summing to 20, ordered by marks descending.
