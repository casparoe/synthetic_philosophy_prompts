The question I set was:

"Frederick Douglass publicly abandoned the view of the United States Constitution he had held through the 1840s and defended a contrary view for the rest of his life. (a) State both views. (b) Explain the interpretive principle he relied on in defending the later one, and how it differs from the principle behind the earlier one. (c) Give one concrete political action that the later position licensed and the earlier had ruled out."

The learner's answer, unedited:

> The question asks for two positions, and they are the Garrisonian one and the one Douglass came to afterwards. Through the 1840s Douglass followed William Lloyd Garrison in treating the Constitution as a pro-slavery compact: a bargain struck at Philadelphia in 1787 in which the northern states bought the union by guaranteeing the slaveholding states their property, and a document no honest man could use as an instrument against slavery. In 1851 Douglass announced that he had changed his mind, and from then until his death he argued that the Constitution, correctly interpreted, is an anti-slavery document.
>
> His case began with the words. The Constitution never uses the word "slave" or "slavery." The clause counting three fifths of other persons for representation, the clause concerning persons held to service or labour who escape into another state, and the clause barring Congress from prohibiting the importation of such persons before 1808 all describe slavery without naming it. For Douglass that silence was decisive: because the framers refused to write slavery into the text, they cannot have intended the Constitution to establish it, and what they meant in 1787 is what the document means. His interpretive principle was therefore that the Constitution is to be read by recovering its authors' intentions from the historical record — their correspondence, the compromises they struck, and the lengths to which they went to keep the thing they were doing out of the written instrument. This is what separated him from the Garrisonians, who insisted on taking the plain words of the clauses at face value: the fugitive clause says such persons shall be delivered up, so the Constitution protects slavery.
>
> Douglass then read the whole instrument in the light of its preamble and of the Declaration of Independence. A document whose declared objects include establishing justice and securing the blessings of liberty cannot be construed to guarantee a right of property in human beings; where a clause admits both a liberty-preserving and a slavery-preserving construction, the first is the correct one. On that reading slavery never had any legal existence under the Constitution, in any state, from 1787 onward, and the states that tolerated it were acting without legal warrant. Since nothing needed to be added to the Constitution to make slavery illegal, Douglass treated a constitutional amendment abolishing it as beside the point; the work was to get the government to declare and enforce what the Constitution already said. That is why he threw himself into ordinary politics — registering and voting, backing anti-slavery candidates, and pressing Congress to act where it plainly had jurisdiction, over the territories, the District of Columbia, and the slave trade — and, during the war, into pressing Lincoln. The Garrisonians had ruled all of that out, since they would not participate in a government resting on a pro-slavery compact and preferred the free states to withdraw from the union rather than remain party to it.
