You are a philosopher of technology who has run several adversarial collaborations between engineers and their critics, and you know what such documents are for: not to blur the dispute but to make it exact. Two camps have been arguing for decades about the strand of thinking usually traced to von Neumann's late essays on whether humanity can survive its own technology.

Call the first the momentum view. Its claims, as I take them: technical possibility has a logic of its own, so once a capability becomes feasible somebody somewhere will build it, and restraint by the willing only transfers the capability to the unwilling; the real danger is not the machines but the mismatch between a technical order that compounds quickly and a political order that adapts slowly; therefore survival lies in accelerating institutional adaptation and in answering dangerous technology with better technology, redundancy, and monitoring.

Call the second the steerability view. Its claims: feasibility is not actuality — cost, incentives, legitimacy, talent concentration, and deliberate choice decide what gets built, and plenty of feasible things have never been built; the historical record of moratoria, abandonment, and non-adoption is the data the first camp explains away; therefore some capabilities should be foregone or slowed, and the correct lever is often the technology itself rather than the institutions that trail it.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could both put their names to after a good-faith collaboration. Begin with a short analysis of how each camp actually reasons — where each draws its confidence from, and which of its premises the other side shares — before you draft anything. Then give me: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, covering the historical record of restraint and non-adoption, the relation between capability and political order, and where each locates the risk of catastrophe; a table stating every surviving disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own position, with no slant in either direction; and the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions whose settlement would genuinely move at least one side. For each crux, name a case, body of evidence, or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance, so that being wrong is checkable.

Two cruxes I suspect belong on the list, though you should judge for yourself: whether 'possible' in 'whatever is possible gets built' means physically possible or economically and politically feasible — I worry a good deal of the dispute is a fight over a modal operator; and whether the lag between a new capability and the institutions that govern it is roughly constant or lengthens as capabilities compound. A third candidate is von Neumann's own limit case of self-reproducing machines, systems that build copies of themselves: does that dissolve the momentum view, since the counter-technology becomes the thing to be survived, or complete it?

Be explicit wherever you conclude that a disagreement is merely verbal, and say which words are doing the damage. Think carefully before writing, prefer precision over comprehensiveness, and write the whole answer in British English.
