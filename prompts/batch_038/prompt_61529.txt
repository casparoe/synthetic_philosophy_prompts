**Draft: Counting, weighting, and where the numbers live**

§1. The scale argument, roughly. There are on the order of 10^18 terrestrial arthropods, and considerably more if you fold in marine ones. Take even a very low credence that any given individual has experiences that matter to it, multiply by that count, and the expected amount of morally relevant suffering in the insect world swamps anything involving vertebrates, humans included. Critics generally go after one of two things. Some go after the multiplier: your credence should be far lower than the argument needs, because the neuroanatomy and the behavioral evidence don't get you there. Others go after the weights: even if insects have experiences, those experiences are so faint or so disunified that no plausible aggregation lets vast numbers beat intensity. I think both lines of attack miss something structural, and that missing it is why the debate keeps going in circles.

§2. The structural point is that the argument treats *p* — the credence that a given animal is sentient — as a scalar, and then multiplies it by *N*. But *p* is not a scalar. It is a compression of two very different bodies of evidence, and those two behave completely differently under intervention.

Call them dial A and dial B. **Dial A** is architectural: does this animal have the kind of nervous system that could plausibly support unified experience — cross-modal integration, recurrent processing, centralized modulation of responses? **Dial B** is behavioral and stakes-based: does it learn from noxious stimuli, trade off costs against avoidance, protect an injured part, reorganize its motivational priorities under threat?

The crucial asymmetry: dial A is essentially fixed. No land-management practice, no change in planting regime, no alteration of a hedgerow changes a beetle's neuroanatomy. Dial B is not fixed. The behavioral profile that generates my evidence is measured in conditions, and those conditions vary enormously.

§3. So here's the bite. Almost everyone with a non-zero *p* got there from a mixture of A and B, but the mixture is not stable across environments, because dial B is habitat-sensitive. An animal in a dense monoculture under pesticide pressure and crowding may express a different damage-response and trade-off profile than a conspecific in a woodland margin. If my B-driven credence is tracking that profile — and for most of us it is doing most of the work — then my credence is indexed to habitat. Call it *p_h*.

§4. That means the right aggregate is not *N × p*. It is a sum over habitats of *n_h × p_h*. And this changes the shape of the conclusion, not just its size. Under the count framing, the moral landscape is one enormous homogeneous lump: wild insect suffering dominates, and the only responses that look proportionate are cosmic ones — either despair, or hunting for interventions that scale to everything at once. Under the habitat framing, the total count matters much less than the *distribution* of animals across behavioral profiles. And the natural response becomes stubbornly local: change the profile of the habitats we already manage, because those are the very ones where we generate both the numbers and, on dial B, the evidence that the numbers matter.

§5. A worry I want to press on myself. If *p_h* tracks a behavioral profile, it tracks however finely I carve habitats and however I describe the behavior. Carve finely enough, describe generously enough, and I can engineer whatever aggregate I want. Call this the gerrymandering worry. My reply is that the carvings aren't free. Dial A constrains from above: no description of a hedgerow turns an animal with a diffuse nerve net into a serious candidate for unified experience. Dial B constrains from below, since the measures I'd use are the standard ones — chosen, in the honest case, before anyone knew what they'd imply for the aggregate. Gerrymandering is therefore possible but detectable, and it shows up as a mismatch between A and B. That mismatch is itself information: it tells you which habitat descriptions are load-bearing for your credence and which are just doing rhetorical work.

§6. What I take to follow. The scale argument is right that the numbers matter and wrong that they're the main thing. Expected moral weight is a sum over habitat-indexed credences, and the practical upshot is far less cosmic than the literature makes out — closer to agronomy than to theodicy.
