I spent about thirty-five years on a synchrotron beamline doing crystallography, retired four years back, and since then I've fallen into philosophy of science pretty hard, plus a lot of reading on mechanistic interpretability of neural nets — which turns out to be the same fight I had in the 80's about whether a blob in an electron density map is a real atom or an artifact of how you phased the data. My reading group (mostly ex-lab people, no professional philosophers) has asked everyone to write down their actual position so the others can poke at it, and I want a check on whether mine hangs together before I present it. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. When interpretability work finds a direction in a model's activations that tracks, say, whether the sentence its reading is in French, and the finding is robust, that's a discovery about the model rather than a projection of the researcher's own taxonomy. Some maps latch onto the territory.
2. But I only hand out reality credit through what I've started calling the bench test: you have to be able to perturb the thing and get a deficit you called in advance. Patch it, steer it, break it, watch the behavior fail the way you predicted. So correlational decoding on its own doesn't count for me — a linear probe can dig out information that's just lying around unused, and decodable isn't the same as represented.
3. At the same time I find the Dennett-ish line convincing: ascribing representations is taking a stance, and what counts as a system's belief is settled by whether that way of describing it pays off predictively. I think the same goes for the bug-detector cells in a frog's retina. There's no view from nowhere about what anything represents.
4. I accept superposition — more features than dimensions, polysemantic units, features smeared across directions — and I take from it that no grain of description is ontologically privileged. I don't believe there's a level at which the model's computation is "really" written.
5. And yet when an intervention at some particular grain hands me a mechanism that explains a whole class of behaviors, I treat that mechanism as a real object, in roughly the way I treated a scattering pathway as real. Mechanisms get found where they can be found.
6. I'm an anti-realist about the intended-function story. Talk of what the model "was trained to do", what the objective "wanted", what the designers meant it to compute — useful fiction for me, no more.
7. I don't think a finding has to travel across architectures to be a discovery. If it holds for this family of models and not others, fine, that's just what's true of them.
8. Concrete case: I do count the result that a transformer carries a linear direction for position-in-sequence as a genuine representation, purely on the robustness of the decoding and how well it lets me predict the model's off-by-one errors. Nobody has patched it in the models I care about.

So which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Im sure several are compatible and I'd hate to be told to give up something I can keep. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to go and what the cheapest repair costs me. Deal with item 8 explicitly, because I suspect it's a problem and I don't know which way I'd want to fix it. Then finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. No primer on what interpretability or the intentional stance is — assume I've read enough to be dangerous.

Please answer me like a letter: continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way you'd reply to an email. Just give me your overall verdict in the first few sentences so I know where I stand, then work through the list in whatever order makes the argument clearest.
