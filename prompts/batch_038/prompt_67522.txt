I cover neuroscience, artificial intelligence, and the philosophy of mind for a weekly magazine, and for the past four years I have also served as the outside journalist on the curatorial advisory panel of a municipal science centre — six of us in all: a developmental psychologist, a philosopher of mind who works on perception, a museum-studies specialist, two bench scientists, and me. Our mandate is narrow but real. We approve, condition, or decline proposed installations for the permanent galleries, and although the director can technically override us, she has never done so and has told me privately that she would prefer not to start. On Thursday we vote on a proposal I have been turning over for three weeks, and I need to circulate a written opinion the night before, because our practice is that panel members arrive having read each other's positions rather than hearing them for the first time in the room.

The proposal is an interactive installation called The Thin Interface, built by a media artist in collaboration with a cognitive neuroscientist and partly underwritten by a technology firm's cultural programme; it is funded, fabricated, and slated to open in eleven weeks. It has three stations. In the first, visitors watch a screen avatar that mirrors their own movements with a lag that lengthens from roughly eighty milliseconds to a full second, and by the end most report that the avatar has begun to act on its own and that they are no longer certain they initiated their own gestures. In the second, visitors choose between two photographs of faces, are then handed the one they did not choose and asked to explain their preference, and in a large share of trials fail to notice the swap and offer fluent reasons for a choice they never made. In the third, visitors press a button while a cap-based readout displays a marker that appears, they are told, before their own report of deciding — the label reads that the decision has already been taken by the time they experience taking it. The copy in the entry gallery is what has split the panel. In draft it runs, in three sentences: "Your conscious mind is not a witness. It is a control panel." / "Everything you take for direct acquaintance with yourself is a rendering, built to be used and not to be true." / "There is no one behind the interface." The closing panel adds: "You are not the user. You are the illusion." Our psychologist and our philosopher object that the stations establish local claims — that introspective reports can be unreliable under specific manipulation, that the sense of authorship depends on the timing of feedback — while the entry copy converts those into a global thesis about phenomenal consciousness and presents it as a settled result, in a venue whose audiences read wall text as authority. They also note that the demonstrations were engineered to produce the effect they display, so they cannot distinguish between the modest claim that the brain's self-model is simplified for control and the radical claim that there is nothing it is like to be the subject of it. The artists reply that deflation is precisely the artistic purpose, that the copy is a provocation of a kind the panel has welcomed before, and — this is the part I keep coming back to — that two years ago the panel approved an infant-cognition installation over near-identical objections about its framing of what babies "know," and nothing whatever came of it. The director has two further worries: school groups are already booked for the first term and the accompanying teacher's guide contains a worksheet asking students to describe an occasion when they were "not really in charge of themselves"; and a clinician who works with patients whose sense of agency is genuinely impaired, and who use delay-and-attribution technology of exactly this kind in motor rehabilitation, has written ahead of opening to say she finds the framing demeaning to the people she treats.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate Wednesday evening, in about nine hundred words, opening with a single paragraph of bottom line that I could read aloud without notes. It must recommend one disposition among four — approve as designed; approve subject to revised entry-gallery copy and station labels; approve as a time-limited installation in the gallery we reserve for explicitly argumentative work, with a moderated public programme attached; or decline — and it must give reasons the artists could actually answer rather than reasons that merely restate our committee's anxieties. Go station by station and separate what each demonstration licenses from what the artists want it to show, then say concretely what the entry copy would have to become if your recommendation involves revising it; I want sentences I can put in front of the fabricators, not a general call for nuance. Confront the strongest form of the artists' objection, which is not that they should be allowed to overclaim but that the panel has no principled place to draw the line between interpretation and overclaim, since every exhibit's design choices embed philosophical assumptions nobody votes on — and tell me how much force that argument really has, and whether it defeats the conditions I am contemplating or only limits how confidently we may impose them. Weigh the infant-cognition precedent honestly, including the possibility that it cuts against us. Of the director's two worries, identify which one a condition can actually address and which one, if it is to matter at all, would have to function as a reason to decline; do not let both do quiet work in the argument. And address, in a sentence or two, the reflexive difficulty our philosopher will raise if I do not raise it first: whether the claim that experience is an illusion is one that can coherently be addressed to an audience in the business of having experiences. Two housekeeping constraints: I am provisionally drawn to the argumentative-gallery option, but say so plainly if you think that is the timid choice and one of the others is better defended. And I want no bibliography, no reading list, and no named authorities — the panel is deciding an installation, not sitting a seminar, and I would rather have the argument laid out than have it shored up with attributions I cannot check before Thursday.
