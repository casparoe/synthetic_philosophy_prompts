You are a philosopher working at the intersection of value theory, metaethics, and the philosophy of history, and specifically someone who has thought long and hard about whether large-scale atrocities can be ranked at all. Please think this through carefully rather than answering from first impressions; the description below contains a subtlety that a quick response will miss.

I direct an advanced seminar on historical memory and comparative atrocity. Over several years of watching the same discussion go around, I have started collecting an argumentative move that I cannot name. I will describe it as plainly as I can and deliberately avoid the vocabulary of your field, because I want to find out whether your field has a word for it.

First instance. A student proposes to compare two campaigns of mass killing — say, by asking which was worse. Someone objects that the question is itself indecent: that putting these events on a single scale demeans the dead of both, that no metric can hold them, that the attempt to rank is exactly what converts atrocity into arithmetic. The room accepts this and moves on. But notice what has happened. In the act of refusing to rank, the objector has assigned the events a position relative to everything that could be ranked; the objection carries weight only if the events sit at an extreme of some ordering, since they are said to be beyond comparison precisely because nothing else reaches them. The refusal is a ranking dressed as a refusal to rank. And the striking part is that no one in the room can say so, because there is no term for it: the objector sounds morally serious, and the student who says "but you have just ranked them" sounds like a pedant.

Second instance, which I suspect is a different thing but might be the same thing wearing different clothes. When the comparison does go ahead, the parties keep changing which feature of badness is doing the work, while continuing to treat it as one and the same question. One week worse means more people killed. The next week it means whether the killing was the aim or a byproduct. Then whether a language and a way of life were erased rather than merely a population reduced. Then whether the harm is finished or still running. Then whether the victims were targeted as such or caught incidentally. Each of these is a defensible dimension, and each can reverse the verdict; none of the parties announces the switch, and none of them seems to notice it, because in ordinary speech every one of them is simply "worse." So a dispute that began about numbers ends as a dispute about intent, and both sides report that they were disagreeing about the same thing all along.

What I would like from you:

1. Begin with an explicit brainstorming pass. List the established concepts — in philosophy, in rhetoric and the study of fallacies, in the historiography of memory, in social psychology, wherever they live — that come closest to each of the two patterns, with a sentence on why each candidate is near and a sentence on why it is not quite it. Do not stop at the first candidate that sounds plausible, and do not settle on a fit merely because it is respectable.

2. Then tell me which of them, if any, is actually the right label. Be candid if none is. I would rather be told plainly that there is no established concept than be handed a loose fit that I will regret printing. I know about incommensurability, and I know there is a long-running argument about whether the Holocaust is unique, but neither of those is what I am describing: I am describing something about the argument used to shut comparison down, not a thesis about the events themselves.

3. If there is no established term, coin one. Give me a definition tight enough that I could teach from it, and then specify the boundary — what would a version of this objection look like that survives your diagnosis? I suspect such a version exists, something along the lines of granting that a scale could be built while denying that it can represent the features that matter in this case, and I want to know exactly where the line falls, because the difference between the legitimate and the illegitimate use of the move is the entire point of the exercise. Please also give me at least one example of a use that looks like the pattern but is not, so that I can see where the concept's edges are.

4. Finally, tell me whether the two instances are two distinct phenomena, one phenomenon with two faces, or one phenomenon and one merely adjacent habit. This determines how I structure the syllabus.

To be clear about what would count as a good answer: the name is not what I am after, the carving is. A bare label would be useless to me, and so would a general meditation on the ethics of comparison. Work from the cases rather than from an abstract account. Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I am not asking you to source anything, only to identify and delimit concepts; if you invoke a term of art from some literature, gloss it in a sentence or two so that I can judge whether it really fits.
