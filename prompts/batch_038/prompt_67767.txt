I chair a faculty–student committee that runs our university's emergency hardship fund. Small pot, about $35k a round, and this term we got something like 90 applications against maybe 20 awards. I'm the philosopher on it; we also have a health economist, and she's just circulated a proposal that's split us down the middle. I need to put something in writing before we vote on Tuesday.

Here's the setup. Right now we score each file two ways: an objective checklist (rent arrears, days without a proper meal, unexpected medical or dental bill, sudden loss of hours at work) plus a self-reported distress rating the student assigns themselves on a 0–10 scale. My colleague's complaint, which I think is basically right, is that the 0–10 doesn't mean the same thing across people. One student's 8 is another's 3; students who've been scraping by their whole lives have recalibrated and report modest numbers for objectively awful situations; and anyone who suspects the number matters has an obvious incentive to inflate it. So she wants to replace it with what she calls a relief-price schedule. We'd list a fixed menu of concrete supports — a month of rent, a course of dental treatment, a term-long transit pass, a week of emergency housing — and each applicant would state the smallest cash amount they'd accept instead of that item. Everything then comes out in dollars, which is at least a common currency, and each applicant gets to describe their situation on their own terms rather than in our categories.

The objections floating around the room, roughly: (a) the dollar answers will track how comfortable a student is with money talk rather than how bad their situation is — a first-gen student may have no idea what anything should cost, and a finance major will; (b) it has the same adaption problem as the 0–10, just better hidden, because someone who has never had dental work will price it low; (c) a couple of members object to ranking students against each other at all and want to go to a lottery among everyone who clears a minimum eligibility bar; (d) someone pointed out that asking a broke student to state what they'd take to forgo dental treatment is bit of a dignity problem.

Could you draft the memo I'd circulate? Call it 900–1200 words, addressed to the other committee members — most of whom aren't philosophers and one of whom wrote the proposal, so it has to be readable and it has to take her argument seriously rather than strawman it. I want a clear recommendation up top: adopt the schedule, keep the self-report, run some hybrid, or go to the lottery. Then the reasoning.

Two things I especially want you to get into. First, whether the money metric actually buys interpersonal comparability or just relocates the comparison problem somewhere less visible — I want the mechanism spelled out, not just asserted. Second, the claim that the lottery isn't the comparison-free option people think it is: choosing a threshold, and choosing what counts toward eligibility, already ranks people, and I want that pressed as hard as it honestly can be. Give me a decision rule specific enough that a committee member could apply it to a real file on a Wednesday afternoon without improvising, and close with the strongest case against whatever you're recommending — argued better than the person making it in the room managed to argue it.
