I spent thirty-four years as a benthic ecologist, the last dozen of them running a small marine field station on the west coast, and since retiring I have given two days a week to a county record office, where I am helping to catalogue the papers and shell collection of an Edwardian landowner who was also a serious amateur conchologist. Philosophy is a late enthusiasm for me and I have no formal training, so I should be grateful if you would take on the role of a philosopher of history and of recordkeeping — someone equally at home with testimony, with the evidential weight of documents, and with the metaphysics of institutional facts — and think carefully before answering. Please begin with a short, unpolished brainstorm of the readings you can see in the phrase before you commit to a list; I would much rather catch a distinction you have missed than receive a fluent answer that never engages with what actually happened here.

The phrase that now dominates our volunteer meetings is "if it isn't written down, it didn't happen". In my working life it was unremarkable: under good laboratory practice it meant that an unlogged step could not be audited, and my wife, who was a hospital pharmacist, used to report the clinical version — a dose not charted is a dose not given. Last month, however, it was deployed to settle a substantive historical question, and I am not satisfied that it did any such thing. The shell labels credit only the squire. His own notebooks record payments to two farm labourers for specimens brought in, and describe a housekeeper who prepared and set the material; a great-granddaughter has written to us with a family account of the same; and one drawer of unlabelled shells appears, from a covering letter, to have arrived through an exchange with a society in Lisbon. A local history group wants the online catalogue to carry "collected by" and "prepared by" fields with the basis of each attribution stated openly. Our cataloguer refused on the grounds above, the objection was accepted without argument, and I sit on the committee that revisits the matter next month. I have half-read a book by a Haitian anthropologist suggesting that silence enters the making of history at several distinguishable points, which I suspect is relevant, but I want the readings stated in terms I can argue with across a table rather than in that vocabulary. One habit from my old field may help or may mislead: in survey work we treated a zero less as a report about the species than as a joint report about the species and the probability of detecting it, and I cannot shake the feeling that a missing record is similarly double-barrelled.

So, working as a philosopher rather than as an archivist, please produce the following. First, prise apart the terms doing the work: "written down" (contemporaneous or later; created, preserved, extant, accessible, or credible; in whose hand and under whose authority), "it" (the event, a person's role in the event, or a status conferred by the record), and, above all, "didn't happen" — where my own suspicion is that the equivocation lives, since a metaphysical, an epistemic and an institutional reading seem to me to be running together. Then give me five to eight distinct readings, ordered from the near-platitudinous to the most radical, each stated as an explicit proposition with its quantifiers visible, in the manner of "for any event of kind K, if no record of it survives, then no claim about it can be justified to standard S". For each reading, deliver a verdict: trivially true, true but irrelevant to our dispute, substantive and defensible, true only for particular kinds of record, or false; where it is false, say what the counterexample would look like, and note any reading on which the slogan turns out to be self-undermining, given that it appears nowhere in our written procedures. Then tell me which readings genuinely license the cataloguer's refusal, and which — if she in fact holds them — would oblige her to the opposite conclusion, for instance a reading on which the squire's payment entries are exactly the kind of document that establishes the labourers' involvement, or on which the unlabelled drawer counts as a record of a different sort. Test the readings against three cases: the housekeeper named only in an entry in someone else's hand; the descendant's letter; the unlabelled drawer with its covering correspondence. Where two readings give identical verdicts on all three, flag the pair, since those are the ones our committee cannot separate by argument about the material. Finally, tell me what you think she most probably means, given that she is a careful professional worried about the evidential weight of a public catalogue entry and about laundering conjecture into an authoritative record — which strikes me as a far more respectable worry than the slogan lets on — and say plainly what the sentence was asked to settle that it has no bearing upon.

Please write in British English, in a form I can convert into a two-page handout for the committee, and do not supply a bibliography, a reading list or citations of any kind: I have no library access, and I would rather you reasoned the matter through than gestured at sources I cannot check.
