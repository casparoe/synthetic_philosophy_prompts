I'm writing a novel and one of my POV characters is a third-year philosophy PhD student. Chapter eleven more or less reproduces the fellowship proposal she submits — the reader gets the document, and then there's a scene where her advisor takes it apart. So it has to be good, or at least good-looking from the inside. I don't want anyone who's actually sat on a fellowship committee to read it and snort.

Here's her project as I've got it, plus a hunch of hers I want you to develop into the real thing. The hunch: the debate over whether introspection is reliable — the confabulation and choice-blindness results on one side, defenders of some kind of priveleged or at least specially secure first-person access on the other, and a middle view where self-report is a theory-laden interpretation of yourself — all of it quietly assumes introspection is a measuring instrument aimed at a target that's already fixed. Her idea is to split that in two: (a) getting the measurement wrong about a state that's sitting there independently of your looking, and (b) the target itself being partly made by the attending, so there's no settled prior fact for the report to match or miss. If (b) is true of a decent chunk of cases, then "accurate" stops being well-defined for them, and the experimental paradigms both camps lean on can't decide anything — which would explain why the debate feels stuck rather than merely unresolved. And then the live question becomes what you'd put in accuracy's place as the standard a first-person report can be judged by: coherence across time, predictive usefulness for the person's own behaviour, narrative stability, some combination, something else entirely.

Turn that into the proposal she'd actually submit. About 1000 words, nine-month fellowship, and I need: a precise statement of the question (precise enough that it could genuinely come out either way); why the existing positions don't already settle it; the method — what she does, month by month, and why that instead of the obvious alternative, which is just designing a cleverer experiment; what a result would look like; and an honest failure condition, meaning what would tell her around month five that the (a)/(b) distinction isn't doing any work and she's only redescribing. Also flag where the scope is too big for nine months.

No bibliography and don't invent one — she can name positions and arguements freely, but anything that looks like a citation I'll handle myself, probably by cutting it. Then at the end, separate from the proposal, give me a short list of the spots where a working philosopher would smell that a novelist wrote this, so I can sand them down.
