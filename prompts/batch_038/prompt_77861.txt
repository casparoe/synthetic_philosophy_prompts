There is a two-day workshop on self-revising autonomous systems in six weeks, and I have the second slot on the first day, right after a keynote on open-ended learning in which the word bootstrapping will have come up perhaps a dozen times. The room will be about forty people: mostly engineers and advanced PhD students working on self-play, models that generate their own training data, and agents that edit their own scaffolding and evaluation code; three or four people from formal methods and programming-language semantics who think in fixed points and reflective oracles; one philosopher of mind who studies consciousness and has been put on the program as a designated provocateur; and a scattering of policy people who will arrive only on the second day. Nobody there has read Hofstadter past the Escher on the cover, and several have absorbed the popular version of strange loops as a species of mysticism, so I expect the term inviolate level to sound like an incantation the first time I say it aloud. My advisor thinks the Escher slide is a mistake and I am keeping it anyway. What I need from you is not help defending the thesis but a map of the question period — and specifically the questions this audience will ask, not the ones a philosophy seminar would.

The claim I am defending is deliberately narrow. Escher's Drawing Hands is the picture everyone reaches for when describing recursive self-improvement: two hands, each drawing the other into existence, apparently needing no outside help. The point of Hofstadter's treatment of pictures like that one is that the apparent closure is an artifact of where you cut the description — the loop looks self-sustaining only because some level of the hierarchy has been silently exempted from it (the sheet, Escher's own hand, the viewer who supplies the sense of draws). I want to argue that this is not an aesthetic curiosity but a structural claim about self-revision, and that it transfers. My argument runs in three steps. First, separate levels by what is editable at them: parameters are editable by training; the objective is editable by whatever rewrites objectives; the criterion for ranking candidate objectives is editable only by a further move up, and so on. Second, argue that at each level an edit must be scored by something not currently up for edit, because a standard that moves along with the thing it measures cannot separate better from merely different — so a system that revises all the way down does not get more improved, it gets less capable of being described as improving at all. Third, draw the consequence I actually care about: the live question is never whether a self-revising system has an inviolate level — it must — but which level that is, who put it there, and how fast it drifts out of the designers' field of view as the system grows. In the talk I plan to walk through three real configurations (a model generating its own training data; a self-rewarding model that writes and grades its own tasks; an agent that patches its own evaluation harness) and name the exempted level in each: frozen base weights, an untouched reward model, the human-authored scaffold and the compute budget.
