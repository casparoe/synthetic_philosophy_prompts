So I'm the program director at a small family foundation in Ohio, we give away about eight million a year, mostly in one metro area plus a handful of rural counties. Last fall we got a bequest with no strings attached, roughly $2M, and the board wants a recomendation from me in April. There's two obvious places to put it. One is a job-training program we've funded for six years; it went through a proper randomized evaluation a couple years back and the results were good — not transformative, but real, people who completed it were earning meaningfully more three years out than the control group. It's the only thing in our portfolio where I can point at a number and not feel like I'm lying. The other option is a scrappy nonprofit that does long-term mentoring for kids who have a parent incarcerated. No evaluation, no theory-of-change document, they've never even tracked how many of the kids stay in the program past a year. But everyone who's seen it up close says it changes lives, and my sister basically had one of these — a neighbor who raised her for four years while my mom was in and out of treatment, and my sister is the only person in my family who ended up stable.

My friend R, who is deep in the effective altruism thing, says this is an easy call and that I'm being sentimental: multiply out the expected value, weight by the strength of the evidence, fund the proven thing, and if you feel bad about your sister's situation do something about it on your own dime. I tried to take him seriously. I spent a whole weekend on a spreadsheet. And here's what happened — every time I extended the consequences past about five years, the sign flipped depending on assumptions I had no way of checking. Does the guy earning $12k more a year have more kids or fewer? Does he move out of the county, hollowing out the place we were trying to help, or stay and become the kind of person who coaches little league and shows up at council meetings? Does the mentored kid go to college and leave, or stay and eventually run for school board? I could build a case in either direction on every one of these with equal confidence and equal evidence, which I've started to think means I wasn't building a case at all, I was just writing down what I already wanted and adding decimals. And the honest truth is that the numbers we put in our grant templates past year three are made up. Everyone in this field knows they're made up. We just don't say it out loud.

What bugs me even more, though, is that R's advice seems to have this property where it can never lose. If I fund the training program and it scales, someone will run another evaluation in ten years and it will show a positive effect, because that's the effect we know how to measure, and everything else will have washed out invisibly. If I fund the mentoring program and it turns out to have been the most important $2M anyone ever spent, nobody will ever know, including me. So the decision procedure recommends itself and then marks its own homework. So: is R wrong, and if he is what do I actually put in front of the board in April? Because "you can't know the consequences so go with your gut" is not something I can say to people who have a fiduciary duty and will ask me, reasonably, why I didn't fund the one program with an RCT behind it. Is there a way to make this call that isn't either fake math or vibes? Or is the real answer that I should be arguing to the board on some basis other than consequences entirely — and if so, what would I even say?
