I'm moderating a fireside conversation at a small workshop on judgment and forecasting — audience is mostly public-policy grad students plus a handful of people who do cost estimation for a living. My guest is a methodologist who runs an outside-view-first practice for cost and schedule estimates. Her position, as I understand it (tell me if I've caricatured any of this):

- reference class first, and literally first. Her teams lock in the reference class and its outcome distribution in a sealed envelope before anyone reads the project-specific file, because she wants to seperate picking the class from reading the case — picking the class using inside information looks circular to her.
- the base rate is a constraint, not a prior. The final number has to land inside the central 80% of the reference distribution unless the team can argue a causal difference from the class, and that argument itself has to be backed by data from other projects. The burden sits entirely on the deviation.
- when people ask whether *this* one is different, her answer is basically that you can't know that in advance, and the pretense that you can is what blows up budgets. So epistemic modesty is doing structural work here, its not just a vibe.
- she's in the Flyvbjerg lineage on megaprojects, but she pushes the method well past construction — R&D timelines, how long wars last, product launches.

I don't want soft questions and I don't want her restating the planning fallacy at me. Write six questions, ordered so the difficulty ramps: open with something she'll field comfortably, close with one I suspect she can't get out of. For each one, name the pressure point you're actually targeting, give me the reply she's most likely to give, and then a follow-up that survives that specific reply — assume her first answer is the good one, not a strawman answer, she's sharp.

Make sure these get covered, though I don't want the set to read like a checklist:

- the sealed envelope. Locking the class before you know anything about the case seems to fix the circularity, but somebody still had to decide which class, and that decision appears to need exactly the information she's refusing to look at. Also nested classes: all wars, or all wars since 1945, or all wars since 1945 with comparable technology? The distributions differ enormously and she's barred from using case knowledge to choose among them.
- the constraint reading. If the base rate is really a prior you update, then "outside view first" just means "don't neglect the prior," and the entire substance is the size of the adjustment, which is inside-view work. If it's a hard constraint instead, it can conflict with information that's about as good as information gets. Concretely: a project under a genuine fixed-price turnkey contract has close to no cost uncertainty. Does her protocol make her widen the interval anyway? If yes, the method is producing worse estimates on purpose. If no, she's letting case-specific information override the class, which is the one thing she forbids.
- first-of-a-kind cases. The risk-versus-uncertainty line: base rates belong to situations that repeat, and forcing a genuine one-off into a distribution assembled from non-comparable cases may be false precision wearing modesty as a costume. Ask what makes reporting a wide interval more honest than saying the distribution doesn't apply here.
- performativity. If her method becomes standard practice, the reference distribution changes, because part of what it records is overruns caused by people not using the method. Which distribution is she supposed to use once the method is widespread, and is a method whose success erodes its own evidence base a feature or a defect?
- turn it on her. Her view is itself a claim about how to make estimates better, so what's the reference class for methodological interventions like hers, and what's the base rate for those actually improving outcomes in the wild? Debiasing training tends to wash out fast and forecasting-improvement methods have a graveyard. She can't exempt her own thesis from the outside view without special pleading.

Then two things I want as much as the questions. First: tell me straight which of the six you think she has a real answer to, and what that answer is — I don't want to walk in believing I've got a knockout when I've got a nuisance. Second: say what would change your mind about the ranking. Which detail of her view, if I've described it wrong above, would flip your judgment about which question is the killer, and where do you think my framing is most likely to have smuggled in a strawman?
