A departmental reading group has been deadlocked for a year between two camps on Dutch book arguments. The first holds that vulnerability to a Dutch book is what gives the probability axioms their normative force, so that incoherent credences are defective precisely as credences. The second holds that the theorems show only that certain packages of betting dispositions are self-defeating, and that nothing about belief as such follows: on this view coherence is neither necessary nor sufficient for epistemically good credences.

Draft the joint statement they should have produced. Begin with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, and make these non-trivial — I would expect at least the point that no finite book can enforce countable additivity, the point that coherence leaves priors wide open, and whatever the two sides genuinely share about the diachronic case. Then a disagreements table, one row per live dispute, each row giving the first camp's preferred wording, the second camp's preferred wording, and a verdict on whether the row is substantive or merely verbal.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one side. I suspect three belong on the list. First, whether an agent with interval-valued credences who accepts a bet only when the offered odds fall strictly outside the interval is a counterexample to the link between incoherence and exploitability, or merely a coherence requirement in disguise. Second, whether the money-pump argument for conditionalization survives the objection that it assumes the agent can attach no value to planning and can take no account of its own predictable future choices. Third, whether "susceptible to a Dutch book" is even well-defined for an agent with unbounded utility. For each crux, state the concrete case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about that case in advance.

Close with a short section on what each camp would count as evidence against its own position.
