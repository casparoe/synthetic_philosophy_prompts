Here's how the essay lays out the Allais business, more or less verbatim off my notes:

"In 1953 the French economist Maurice Allais put two choice problems in front of a group of economists and watched the foundations of rational choice come apart. First pair: a million dollars for certain, or a lottery with a 10 percent chance of five million, an 89 percent chance of a million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing. Almost everyone takes the certain million. Second pair: an 11 percent chance of a million, or a 10 percent chance of five million with the other 90 percent paying nothing. Almost everyone takes the long shot. Now cancel the 89 percent that both options share in each pair, and the two experiments turn out to be literally the same choice — which means the subjects are flat-out contradicting themselves. The mechanism is unexciting: money has diminishing value, so a sure million beats a lottery, and once you take the sure million away the jackpot does the tempting. Independence — the rule that adding the same odds to both options can't change which one you prefer — is just consistency written in math, and anybody who breaks it can be bled a nickel at a time by a bookie who knows what he's doing, which is the technical sense of 'irrational.' Allais intended it as a demonstration that ordinary people are simply bad at thinking about risk, and expected utility theory has been a zombie ever since: nobody in the field believes it, but it's still taught because there's nothing to put in its place."

The essay then spends the rest of its word count landing the real point: since a preference that flips when you add identical odds to both sides isn't a preference at all, there's no stable fact about what people want, only about what they'll do in the moment — so policy should be built entirely out of observed behavior, and economists' modelled agents should be retired.
