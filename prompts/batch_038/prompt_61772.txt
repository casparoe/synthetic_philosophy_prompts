I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy working on the normative foundations of decision-making under uncertainty, and for a chapter draft I need a number from you rather than a survey of positions.

Begin with a canonical case. An agent is offered the same bet indefinitely: a fair coin is flipped, and the agent stakes a fixed fraction f of current wealth each round; on heads the stake is returned plus twice its amount, on tails the stake is lost. Staking everything maximizes expected wealth, since the expected wealth multiplier is 1 + f/2 and thus increasing in f, yet ruin then arrives almost surely. Staking f = 0.25 instead maximizes the almost-sure long-run growth rate of wealth, about 0.059 in log terms per round, while f = 0.5 yields a growth rate of exactly zero. Now suppose the proposition at issue is this:

G: For an agent who repeatedly faces a favorable bet of this multiplicative form, selecting the fraction that maximizes the long-run growth rate of wealth is required by practical rationality, and this requirement binds the agent regardless of what the agent's utility function over wealth happens to be.

State your credence in G as a single number, then show me the derivation rather than the conclusion. I want a probability for each of the sub-questions G appears to depend on:

1. That norms of rationality can constrain an agent's ends instead of merely serving them — this is the clause that makes G contentious at all.
2. That the divergence between the ensemble average and the time average, the non-ergodicity on which growth-rate arguments trade, carries normative weight for the single agent living one life rather than being only a descriptive fact about collections of agents.
3. That the decision problems we actually face are of the relevant form: repeated, multiplicative, with ruin absorbing and no re-entry at the same odds.
4. That growth-rate maximization is not simply expected-utility maximization under a logarithmic utility function in disguise — or, if it is, that this does not reduce G to a claim about one particular agent's tastes.
5. That the need to estimate both the odds and the payoffs leaves G's normative claim intact even though it destroys its practical applicability.
6. That the dispute is not merely verbal: that there is a determinate notion of rationality about which growth-rate theorists and expected-utility theorists genuinely disagree.

Then tell me how you combine these into your headline figure (if not multiplicatively, say what you do instead), which term your number is most sensitive to, and what argument or empirical finding would move it furthest. For calibration: my own credence is 0.55, while a colleague in the economics department puts it at 0.15. Say which of the six terms most likely accounts for that gap, and whether the gap is substantive or a difference in how each of us parses G. If you judge any of my terms redundant, mis-stated, or in need of a seventh, restructure the decomposition and explain why.
