I'm giving a 40-minute paper plus a 30-minute question period at a workshop co-hosted by a law school's constitutional law center and a divinity school's religion-and-public-life program, and I need a prep sheet for the Q&A because the room is going to be a mess in a very specific way. Here's the thesis. Every liberal state that keeps its hands off religious money and religious recognition thinks that abstention is what neutrality looks like, but abstention isn't neutral, it's a survival filter: voluntary funding rewards communities whose members are wealthy, organized, high-demand and willing to fill out forms, and it quietly starves the diffuse, the poor and the recently arrived. So the state does end up deciding which convictions survive — it just does it with its hands in its pockets. My proposal is a mandatory but tiny directed levy: every taxpayer has to point a half percent of their income tax at one recognized community of conviction, religious or explicitly non-religious, with a civic-commons option. The interesting part is the default rule. Italy already does something adjacent — under the otto per mille, 0.8% of your income tax gets allocated to a recognized religion or to a state social-assistance scheme, and if you tick no box at all, your share isn't held back, it's redistributed among the recipients in proportion to the choices that actually got expressed, which in practice means silence is counted for whoever spoke. Only about 43 percent of people ticked a box in the mid-2000s. I keep bringing this up at dinner partys. My twist is to keep the mandatory designation, kill the proportional default, and replace it with a lottery over all eligible recipients, so a community's revenue tracks the number of citizens rather than the number of administrators. And a second twist that I know is the controversial one: there is no "none" box, because a recorded none-category becomes a census fact that majorities then weaponize — I've been calling this the null-category problem, and the principle underneath it is that no community should live or die on its members' paperwork.

The audience is why I'm asking. There will be two US constitutional litigators who have spent their careers on Establishment Clause cases and who will treat any state channeling of money toward religious bodies as doctrinally dead on arrival before I've finished the second slide. There's a French political scientist of laïcité who will say the entire framing is a Protestant hangover and that a state competent to define "conviction" is already the problem. There's a public-finance economist who studies charitable giving and tax expenditures and who will want to know about crowding out, and a canon lawyer worried about what happens to a church that becomes a budget line — the gilded cage worry. Plus a sociologist of religion who works on the unaffiliated, several grad students, and one or two people from the German side who'll point out that the Kirchensteuer is basically my proposal with worse paperwork and a formal exit declaration in front of a state office. I already know where I'm soft: the compelled-support/compelled-expression angle, and the fact that my criterion for which groups are eligible to recieve money is a mess I've never solved.

So give me the prep sheet, not a defense. Eight objections or so, in the order they'll actually land, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the economist's version of the crowding-out worry and the canon lawyer's version should not sound alike — with a two-minute reply I can deliver standing up, and a one-line note on exactly where that reply is softest. Flag which two I should concede inside the talk itself, before anybody gets the chance to score off them. Then tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks: my guess is some version of what happens to a Muslim taxpayer in a country where there's no single Islamic authority to tick a box for, but I suspect there's a worse one and I'd rather hear it from you than from the French guy. Last line of the whole thing should be exactly `Verdict: #N — six words`, naming whichever objection you think is most likely to sink the talk.
