So I was up till like 1am last night arguing with a friend from my program — she's a Mīmāṃsā person, very much a Kumārila partisan — and we never actually resolved it, which is a problem because I give this talk in three weeks and she basically was the audience. The talk defends a pretty specific claim: that Nyāya is right to treat *śabda* as a pramāṇa in its own right rather than a species of *anumāna*, but for a reason contemporary epistemologists of testimony almost never reach for. The usual anti-reductionist moves are either entitlement-by-default or the circularity complaint (you can't establish a speaker's reliability without more testimony, so you must be entitled to trust primitively). My line is different and it's basically causal: what makes a pramāṇa its own pramāṇa for the Naiyāyikas is its *karaṇa*, the special instrumental cause that produces the cognition. Inference runs through a *parāmarśa* — grasping the reason as pervaded by the property to be proved. Testimony runs through the comprehension of a sentence, which has its own enabling conditions (mutual syntactic expectancy between the words, semantic fitness so the combination isn't nonsense, contiguity so the words arrive as one utterance rather than scattered, and the speaker's *tātparya*, what they intend the words to be doing). Different instrument, different pramāṇa, even where the contents overlap. Then the part my friend went after, which is my own spin and not something you'll find in the commentaries: I want to read the *āpta* clause in the sūtra definition not as a psychological claim about the speaker's honesty or expertise but as a claim about the utterance — a structural or register-level track record, a property of the speech community and the genre the utterance comes out of, so that *āptatva* is more like an institutional reliability than a virtue of a person. That's what I need help bracing for.
