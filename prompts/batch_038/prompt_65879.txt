I co-run a monthly reading group on AI and society at a mid-size university — next session is two hours, probably around 22 people, and it's a weird mix: philosophy undergrads, four or five CS grad students who have actually trained models, and a couple of law school people who have never seen a line of code. The topic is human oversight of AI systems, and the specific slice I want to hit is the difference between grading a system's final output versus grading its intermediate steps/reasoning. I've been reading a bit about process reward models and the argument that step-level feedback is much better signal, and I sort of buy it, but I also keep running into the objection that step-level labels are brutally expensive per data point and that a system can write gorgeous confident steps that don't reflect anything real. I don't want to lecture on any of that. I want them to hit the tradeoff themselves in the room, ideally before anyone says the words "process supervision" out loud.

The skeleton I have in mind, feel free to improve or replace it: teams, each with a couple of people playing "graders" and one or two playing the "model," working on a paper-and-pen multi-step task where every step is checkable but the final answer can be fluked or guessed — a chain of arithmetic word problems, a short logic derivation, a guess-the-hidden-rule-from-examples puzzle, whatever you think holds up best with no laptops (the room's projector is broken, so whiteboard, pens, paper, and a phone timer is all we've got). The thing I really want engineered in is a fixed annotation budget: each team gets something like 30 grading tokens, and they can burn them on final answers of lots of cheap attempts or on every step of a small number of attempts, and they have to commit to a split before they see what the "model" does. And I want the model players to have real scoring on their side, so they discover through play that under outcome grading, spraying sloppy guesses is a perfectly good strategy, while under process grading, writing plausible-looking steps pays off even when the reasoning is wrong. Then teams compare notes and I get to ask which regime they'd want if they were deploying the thing.
