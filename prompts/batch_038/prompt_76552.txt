Our department runs a recorded interview series that pairs a visiting scholar with an undergraduate who has written a seminar paper in the visitor's area, and this term I drew the long straw. Our guest is a mid-career philosopher whose first monograph develops an account of the cogito that I want to test rather than merely admire. Her thesis, as I read it, is that the certainty Descartes claims for his own existence owes nothing to inference and nothing to introspective reporting on one's mental acts: it comes from the thought's token-reflexive content, where the content is about the very event of its own occurring, so that the thought cannot be entertained without thereby being made true. From this she draws a consequence she is quite happy to own and that most of my classmates think is a disaster — that the certainty is non-transferable. Because clarity and distinctness do no work in the mechanism, the cogito cannot be cashed in as a criterion of truth; on her reading Descartes's real error was not the cogito but the foundational use he made of it. I have twenty-five minutes, the transcript will be posted beside the audio, and the audience is second-years who have read the first two Meditations and nothing else, so the questions may speak of clear and distinct perception, of the thinking thing, and of the evil demon without glossing, but should not assume the Principles, the Objections and Replies, or the correspondence.

Please draft the question list: ten questions, ordered so that the first lets her state the positive account in her own terms and the last asks what evidence or argument would change her mind. Because she has heard the standard lines many times, each question has to be built so that a rehearsed answer does not end the exchange — it needs a hook that survives the reply. I want pressure applied at the following points, and I leave it to you to decide which deserve two questions apiece: (i) the size of the class she has bought into, since token-reflexive contents look plentiful — 'this very thought is occurring', 'there is thinking', 'someone is thinking' — which threatens either to make the cogito one unremarkable instance among many or to force her into a restriction to the first-person singular that looks tailored to the result she wants; (ii) the old objection, usually credited to Lichtenberg, that the same self-verifying structure is available to an impersonal 'it thinks', so ask what in her machinery licenses a thinker rather than a thinking, and whether the first person in her account is anything more than the grammatical subject of a reflexive clause; (iii) the sentence 'I think that I do not exist', which her account seems obliged to classify one way or another, together with 'I am imagining a unicorn', which shares the form of the cogito without sharing its conclusion; (iv) duration — the mechanism delivers certainty only while the tokening is under way, so what entitles her to the claim that she existed yesterday, and what work memory can possibly do at a point in the book where memory is precisely what remains in doubt; and (v) the amputation worry: if the cogito cannot ground a criterion of truth, ask what she thinks is left standing — God, the external world, the wax — and whether she regards that stripped-down picture as the cost of her reading or as its main discovery.

For three of the questions — the one on the size of the class, the one on the impersonal 'it thinks', and the one on the amputation — write the answer you expect from her in a single sentence, in her voice and using her own vocabulary, and then give the follow-up question keyed to that answer. On the second of these, assume she replies that the reflexive content is a de se self-ascription rather than an impersonal event report, and make the follow-up ask whether de se content requires an owner that persists or only one that is present. Keep every question under sixty words, do not smuggle three sub-questions behind a single question mark, and leave out anything biographical: I am not going to ask what drew her to Descartes, what she is working on next, or how she feels about the state of the discipline.
