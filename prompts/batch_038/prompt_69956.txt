Two years ago our city council established a seven-member Behavioral Design Review Panel whose job is to examine any proposed change of defaults, framing, or notice design put forward by a city department or by the municipally owned utility, before it reaches a council vote. Its founding resolution supplies two tests, both stated loosely: a design should be one that residents would accept if they had the information, the time, and the attention to consider it; and a design should be rejected where leaving is harder than arriving. I am the newest member and the only one with any philosophy training — the rest are two economists, a former utility regulator, a tenant advocate, a city lawyer, a retired school principal, and a public-health nurse — and the chair has asked me to circulate a memo before Thursday's meeting on the newest proposal. What I want from you is not a survey of the nudging literature, which I have read and the panel has not; I want the reasoning to run entirely through our own record, using the ordinary vocabulary of the debate (choice architecture, libertarian paternalism, reflective endorsement, manipulation, active choosing, publicity) only where it does real work.

Our record so far is as follows. (1) Automatic enrollment of new city employees in the retirement plan at six percent, with automatic one-point increases for three years, exit available from day one by phone or portal with no forms and no waiting period, the design published in the employee handbook, and an annual notice to those who had left telling them they could return: approved unanimously, the written rationale resting on the disclosure and on a staff survey in which a large majority said they were glad to have been enrolled. (2) Changing the default on all municipal printers and copiers to duplex, after an open consultation, with a one-click per-job override and the paper savings returned to the departments that incurred them: approved six to one, the dissenter writing that the measure's actual purpose was the city's emissions target and that an incidental budget saving should not be allowed to launder it. (3) Letters and texts to residents who appeared eligible for a heating subsidy but were not receiving it: approved on the condition that each message state what the subsidy is, why the recipient appears eligible, and how to stop receiving messages. (4) A proposal to place residents in the most expensive tier of the municipal parking permit unless they installed a trip-logging smartphone app and kept it running for ninety days, exit by notarized form: rejected unanimously, on the ground that leaving was far harder than arriving and that the arrangement functioned as a fine. (5) Automatic enrollment of all staff in the employee assistance program with the usual bulletin notice suppressed, so that employees would first encounter the program as an unfamiliar line on their payslip: rejected five to two, the majority holding that a design which depends on people not finding out cannot be defended in public and again makes exit depend on discovery, the two dissenters writing that the program plainly benefited those enrolled and that uptake would otherwise have been a third as high. (6) A letter to households behind on a municipal tax bill, illustrated with a photograph of the recipient's own street and worded in vivid loss language about what the household stood to forfeit, timed for the week before the deadline: not approved as submitted and sent back; the redesigned letter, which kept the deadline salient but used plain information, was approved. The minutes record only that the panel was divided on whether vivid emotional framing is objectionable in itself or merely objectionable when combined with non-disclosure, and no further guidance was ever written.

Thursday's proposal is this. The utility wants every household that registers a change of address — roughly thirty thousand registrations a year, heavily concentrated in late summer, and a moment when people are dealing with a dozen administrative tasks at once — to be placed automatically on the hundred-percent renewable tariff, which costs about four percent more per unit than the standard tariff. Exit would be available at any time by portal or phone in three clicks, with no forms, no waiting period, no retention script, and no closing window; the default would be disclosed in one sentence inside the twelve-item bundle of notices every moving household receives, and again on the first bill. The council debated the program in open session in March and the local paper covered it twice; a citywide survey that month found seventy-one percent of respondents approving of households being defaulted into clean electricity, but when a follow-up question specified the four percent premium, support fell to forty-four percent. A year-long pilot in two districts found eighty-eight percent of defaulted households still on the green tariff at twelve months, three percent having actively opted out, and nine percent having never opened the portal at all, with household emissions in those districts falling slightly more than in matched comparison districts. The premium is regressive in absolute terms, because larger detached homes consume more, and although a low-income discount exists that would offset it, that discount must be applied for separately and only about forty percent of those eligible take it up. Two alternatives are formally on the table alongside approval: an active-choice variant, under which the address change cannot be completed until the household selects one of the two tariffs on a screen displaying both prices, which elsewhere yields roughly a third choosing green; and outright rejection. Please tell me, first, which of the three options the panel is most likely to adopt and how I expect the votes to fall, with the ground on which each likely dissenter dissents; second, which of our six precedents is the closest analog and precisely where the analogy breaks, since in the retirement case the money was the employee's own and exit was available from day one rather than at a moment of maximal cognitive load; third, the candidate principles that are jointly consistent with our whole record — I count at least five, including a publicity or public-defensibility principle, an option-set or exit-asymmetry principle, a reflective-endorsement principle, a reason-giving principle that treats the bypassing of deliberation as the objection, and a principle requiring that the good aimed at be the subject's own rather than a third party's or the state's — together with what each implies for approval, for active choice, and for rejection; fourth, which decision best coheres with the panel's own record, and, stated explicitly rather than buried, what principle the panel would thereby be committing itself to and what it would have to say about a future case in which the same reasoning would be uncomfortable; fifth, whether the survey numbers settle the reflective-endorsement question or merely relocate it, since it matters a great deal which description of the residents' counterfactual reflection is the relevant one; sixth, whether requiring active choice is more or less paternalistic than the default it replaces, given that it imposes a real decision cost in order to protect a choice nobody asked to make; and finally, where our record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, plus one further case, described in a sentence or two, that would discriminate between the principles that are currently tied. I would rather have an honest account of the underdetermination than a confident memo that quietly picks a principle and presents it as our precedent.
