Right, I've just come off about a three-hour arguement with a mate of mine — he does economic history, I do philosophy — and neither of us budged, so I want a proper second opinion from someone who isn't invested in either of our egos.

Here's my position, in as precise a form as I can manage. I think the dynastic-cycle tradition — Polybius's anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's account of a ruling house's asabiyya running down over roughly three generations of forty years or so — is not a literary genre or a mood about the human condition, but a claim about an actual mechanism, and the mechanism is this: what a founding coalition produces is cohesion, and cohesion is the one asset that cannot be bequeathed. The heirs inherit the *outputs* of cohesion — security, rents, offices, prestige, a state that functions — but never the *inputs*: shared hardship, mutual hostages, reputations built under conditions where defection gets you killed. So the asset depreciates at a rate set by demographic turnover rather than by anything the rulers do or fail to do, which is why the classical figures cluster around three generations instead of, say, seven. Call it the amortisation view; that's my own coinage, not a school I'm borrowing. What I like about it is that it makes a couple of asymmetric predictions the usual rise-and-fall pieties don't. Cycle *length* should track generation length rather than wealth or state capacity, so polities with early succession and high elite mortality should compress and long-lived, late-succeeding elites should stretch. And a cycle should be interruptible wherever an institution manufactures fresh bonded cohorts — conscription, competitive examinations that build a new peer group, frontier expansion, a revolutionary ideology — which is also how I read Ibn Khaldun's point about dynasties getting renewed by fresh solidarity from the periphery. My mate's line was that this is astrology for historians: fitted after the fact, with the start and end points of any given dynasty chosen by the victors' chroniclers, and maybe a dozen usable cases in world history so that any curve will fit. I'll concede one thing up front — the long Chinese dynasties, Han, Tang, Ming, Qing, all ran two or three times the 120 years the classical scheme wants, and my instinct is to call them multi-cycle polities with renewals rather than single stretched cycles, which I admit sounds like an escape hatch. Tell me whether that instinct is defensible or already the beginning of the end.
