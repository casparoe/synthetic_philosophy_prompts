I am in the middle of a master's dissertation in political philosophy on conditionality in the British welfare state, and before I commit anything to print I would like to know what my own intuitions actually add up to, because they appear to be pulling in two directions at once. Most of the cases below come from the machinery of Universal Credit as it operates, and I have recorded my verdict on each as I find it rather than as I think a consistent person ought to find it. A claimant who misses a work-search appointment because he is sitting at his mother's bedside in hospital, is sanctioned, and is vindicated on reconsideration some weeks later, by which time the money has gone and the rent arrears it created have not: impermissible, and the existence of an appeal route that eventually finds in his favour does not repair it. A woman with a degree and fifteen years in her profession, unemployed for two years, who declines a full-time job at the minimum wage because she experiences it as beneath her and is sanctioned for it: permissible, or at least I cannot bring myself to condemn it, even though her sanction bites on her rent exactly as hard as his. A man working thirty-eight hours a week across three zero-hours contracts, whose monthly earnings swing above and below the threshold at which his in-work support is adjusted, and who is nevertheless required to attend meetings about looking for more work: impermissible, and this is the case that angers me most, since what is being supervised here is a man who is already working nearly full time. Someone who claims as a single person while living with a partner whose income they conceal: the conduct is wrong, and investigation and deterrence aimed at it are appropriate. A claimant with a fluctuating condition, multiple sclerosis or recurrent depression, who is assessed on a comparatively good day and found capable of work: impermissible, though here, unlike the previous case, nobody has behaved badly and what has failed is the instrument rather than the person. A woman caring full time for an adult son with profound disabilities who is required to demonstrate job search: impermissible, obviously; yet a mother of two school-age children who works part time and is required to move toward what the system takes to be her full earning capacity: borderline, and on reflection I lean permissible. I cannot say what separates them beyond degree of caring responsibility. The rule that withdraws support for a third or subsequent child whatever the parents' circumstances: impermissible. The overall cap on what a household may receive in total benefits, defended on the ground that a household should not be better off on benefits than in work: permissible in principle. Both adjust entitlement by reference to facts about household size and composition that the claimants partly control, and both fall hardest on the children, so I do not know what I am tracking. A young man leaving the care system at eighteen who has never worked and never contributed, set beside a claimant with thirty years of contributions: their claims on the system strike me as equally strong; but I also think a contribution record may permissibly affect the level or duration of support, and those two thoughts sit badly together.

Here is the principle I keep reaching for, and I suspect it is a rationalisation of whichever verdict I have most recently stated rather than something I independently believe: conditionality is permissible only where the conduct it targets lies genuinely within the claimant's control, where the system's finding of non-compliance is reliable, and where the cost of the sanction falls on the person whose conduct is at issue rather than on third parties who had no say in it. Please tell me whether that delivers every verdict I have listed, clause by clause, saying which case each clause is doing the work for, and where it fails. I expect it fails on the man at his mother's bedside, since he did choose to be there, was correctly identified, and bore the cost himself; if so, say what I am really tracking in that case, whether it is the quality of the claimant's reasons, whether the requirement serves anyone's good at all, or something about what the demand expresses on behalf of the community. Then state one or two candidate principles precisely, and be honest about which of my verdicts each cannot accommodate. I would far rather be told that the woman who declines the minimum-wage job and the man at the hospital bedside cannot both be saved than be handed a principle with four epicycles bolted onto it to rescue everything.

Alongside that, give me the resulting account's verdicts on cases I have not considered: mandatory drug testing as a condition of receipt; an algorithmic risk score that assigns intensive conditionality to those predicted to become long-term unemployed; sanctioning someone who misreported their circumstances where the misreport would have left them no better off anyway; requiring attendance at a job-search programme run by a religious provider; and an unconditional basic income that abolishes conditionality altogether. Finally, say where my pattern of verdicts sits among the families of view one meets in this literature, meaning responsibility-sensitive or luck-egalitarian accounts, the relational and democratic-equality critique of them, reciprocity-based defences of a civic minimum, and straightforwardly contractualist or bargaining accounts, and tell me whether the distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor can be reconstructed on the basis of what I have said, or whether these verdicts show me to be someone who ought to abandon it. Please write the answer in British English.
