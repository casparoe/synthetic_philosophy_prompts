The question, worth 25 marks, was as follows.

> In his 1939 British Academy lecture Moore claimed to have given a rigorous proof of the existence of an external world, and set out the conditions he took such a proof to satisfy. State the proof and those conditions. What is at issue in the charge that the proof begs the question against the sceptic, and does that charge succeed? Refer to at least one response to Moore written after 1939.

The candidate's answer, reproduced in full below, ran rather short for the time available; the paper asked for four answers in two hours, and this student appears to have hurried.

> Moore's proof is set out in "A Defence of Common Sense" (1925), where he lists a series of truisms, among them that there exists at present a living human body which is his body, and again, more dramatically, in the 1939 lecture, where he holds up each hand in turn and says "here is one hand" and "here is another". From these premisses he argues that at least two external things exist, and hence that an external world exists. A rigorous proof, Moore says, must meet three conditions: the premiss must be different from the conclusion; the premiss must be known to be true; and the conclusion must follow from the premiss. He holds that all three are satisfied.
>
> The premisses are, properly understood, claims about appearance. Moore seems to see two hands, and the question is whether he is entitled to pass from that seeming to the existence of mind-independent objects. That step is not deductive but inductive, resting on the general reliability of perception, which is why the argument has been thought to require the very thing it is supposed to establish.
>
> The charge of begging the question can be put in terms of the sceptical argument Moore is answering. The sceptic reasons: if I know that there are hands, then I know that I am not dreaming; I do not know that I am not dreaming; therefore I do not know that there are hands. Moore performs what has come to be called the Moorean shift, denying the second premiss rather than the first and arguing in the opposite direction. But this does not answer the sceptic, who simply refuses the premiss. Moore concedes that his proof may fail to convince someone who denies its premisses, and so the charge of circularity is fatal: an argument that cannot persuade anyone who doubts it has not done what a proof is meant to do, and the proof is question-begging in exactly this sense. In any case, any argument for scepticism is less plausible than the premiss that here is one hand, so the sceptical position cannot even be coherently stated.
>
> Wittgenstein's objection in On Certainty is the same charge pressed further. He holds that Moore cannot truly say that he knows here is one hand, since knowledge presupposes the possibility of doubt and there is no room for doubt in this case; so Moore does not know that he has hands, and the sceptic is right that propositions of this kind are not known.
>
> Crispin Wright developed the point into the doctrine of transmission failure. Moore's argument carries warrant for its premisses only if one is already entitled to accept its conclusion, so the warrant does not transmit. Wright concludes, with Dretske and Nozick, that closure — the principle that if one knows that p and knows that p entails q, one is thereby in a position to know that q — must be abandoned, since a Moorean argument cannot at once fail to transmit and preserve closure.
>
> On balance the charge succeeds, though not for the reasons usually given: the argument is valid and Moore's three conditions are met, but the proof is dialectically useless.
