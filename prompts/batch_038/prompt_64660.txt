I'm assembling the reading materials for a graduate seminar that sits between criminal law and political philosophy, and this term's session is on genocide as both a legal category and a contested moral concept, with particular attention to how the naming of an atrocity is itself a political act. Rather than assigning an essay, I want to hand the students a dialogue to argue with. Here is the case I have constructed for it — the country, the people and the programme are entirely invented, a composite, and I would like the dialogue to treat them as the fixed facts of the matter. Over roughly thirty years the Republic of Vandara has relocated the Kess, a highland pastoralist people, into lowland "development towns" under a national modernisation scheme. Every household signs a consent form, but the grazing commons and water rights on which the Kess economy depends have been reassigned to lowland enterprises, so a household that refuses loses the means of subsistence within a season. Kess-medium schooling has been abolished; children are boarded at state academies from the age of nine and allowed one supervised visit home per year. Nobody has been killed, sterilised, or starved, and on every standard indicator the Kess are far better off than their grandparents were — income, infant mortality, life expectancy, literacy. Fluent speakers of the language have fallen from about nine-tenths of the population to under five per cent in two generations. A leaked internal memorandum from the minister who designed the programme records the objective as ensuring that within three generations there would be no Kess left as a distinct people, only Vandaran citizens who happened to have highland ancestors. A truth commission has classified the conduct as persecution and forcible transfer, and the prosecutor has declined to bring a genocide charge.

Please write the dialogue between two speakers, both of them excellent. The first is a former appellate prosecutor turned legal scholar who holds that the category must be kept technically intact: the Convention's protected groups are a closed list that was deliberately negotiated in 1948, with political and social groups excluded under pressure from states that feared the label; the required intent is an intent to destroy the group as such, and the tribunals have in practice read that destruction as physical or biological rather than cultural; the boarding-school element is the only real foothold in the list of prohibited acts, and even that has to clear its own hurdles; and — this is the point I most want developed rather than asserted — the classification as persecution and forcible transfer already condemns everything that happened here in the strongest terms the law possesses, so no moral loss is incurred by withholding the word. This speaker should also press the inflation objection hard: if the deliberate dissolution of a distinct collective life counts as genocide, then a great deal of ordinary state-building since the eighteenth century — compulsory national schooling, the suppression of regional languages, the resettlement of nomadic peoples — becomes genocide too, and the word loses the very function, legal and diplomatic, that makes it worth having. The second speaker is a historian and moral philosopher who holds that the Convention is a politically truncated version of the concept its originator actually advanced, one concerned with the destruction of a group's whole pattern of social and cultural life rather than merely with body counts, and that the 1948 narrowing reflects the bargaining of states rather than any moral insight. This speaker should argue that a group is not an aggregate of bodies but a continuing collective way of life, so its members can survive and even prosper while the group itself is destroyed; that the physical-biological gloss on intent is a judicial construction rather than anything the text demands; that consent signed under the deliberate removal of the material base of a way of life is not consent in any sense that matters; and that describing all of this as the sum of harms to individuals leaves a residue unaccounted for, namely the loss of a world in which the Kess existed as a people. Critically, this second speaker must answer the inflation objection with a discriminating criterion of their own — something that separates the Vandaran programme from assimilationist policies generally — rather than simply insisting that the difference is obvious.

Some requirements about the craft of the piece. Aim for roughly twelve to fifteen hundred words, in attributed lines only, with no narrator and no stage directions beyond what is strictly necessary. Neither speaker should be a strawman, and both must engage the other's actual arguments rather than delivering set speeches: I want real concessions, and I want each side to land at least one move the other cannot answer directly. Work through, at minimum, whether the destruction of a group can consist in the end of its collective life while its members flourish; what follows from the closure of the list of protected groups and from the 1948 exclusion of political groups; whether household-level consent survives the state's removal of the conditions that made Kess life possible; whether the transfer of children can carry a genocide charge on its own; whether the crime-against-humanity classification leaves any moral residue; and whether the second speaker's proposed line is principled or merely reflects which cases they find sympathetic. Do not include citations, footnotes, case names, or a reading list — the speakers may allude in general terms to how international tribunals have construed the intent requirement, but no argument may depend on a specific judgment or work of scholarship. Likewise, do not have the characters label each other's positions with terms of art or trade in names for camps; they should argue from the substance. End without a victor: the closing exchange should leave a narrowed and precisely stated disagreement, plus one thing each of them now accepts that would be unwelcome news to their natural allies.
