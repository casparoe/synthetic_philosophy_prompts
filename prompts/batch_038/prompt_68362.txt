You are a scholar of late-antique moral philosophy who has spent twenty years teaching Augustine's treatises on lying to analytically trained non-specialists, and who also reads rationalist blogs and forums closely enough to know how that audience argues. Please think carefully about this before answering, and work in two passes: first a short brainstorming section in which you list the candidate load-bearing examples you considered and say in a line each why you rejected all but one, and only then the plan itself.

The situation. I run the ideas track at an annual weekend gathering for a rationalist community — sixty or so attendees, heavy overlap with the effective-altruism and forecasting scenes, median age around thirty, almost none of them with any background in pre-modern philosophy. Next spring I have sixty minutes with a self-selected subgroup of about fifteen on Augustine's argument that lying is never permissible. I want the argument, not the text and not the man. I am not assigning readings, so please do not give me a bibliography, a suggested further-work list, or anything I would have to go and look up; and since I do not have the treatises in front of me while I teach, please paraphrase rather than put words in quotation marks — I want the reasoning, and I would rather have no phrasings at all than invented ones.

The audience's priors, which are the real obstacle. They already have a sophisticated instrumental account of why lying is bad: it erodes the trust that makes testimony usable, it degrades the epistemic commons, it breaks the cooperative equilibrium, and it corrupts the liar's own calibration. On that picture an absolute prohibition looks like either naivety or a consequentialist calculation in disguise. They are also trained to reach for the murderer-at-the-door case, and I expect it out of someone within ninety seconds. There are two failure modes I am trying to avoid: the session lands as a museum piece, and the session lands as a strawman they get to beat up on for an hour.

What I need from you, as a numbered plan:

1. The through-line: one sentence that the whole sixty minutes exists to install. Everything below must visibly serve it, and if a component doesn't, cut it.

2. A block-by-block timeline with the dependency structure made explicit. For each block, state what must already be in place for it to be intelligible and which later block needs it. My working hypothesis is that the definition has to land first and that everything downstream is unintelligible without it — specifically, that a lie on this account is a mismatch between what the speaker signs and what the speaker holds in the heart, rather than between what the speaker signs and how the world is, so that a sincerely asserted falsehood is no lie at all while an asserted truth the speaker believes to be false is one. Tell me whether that is right and whether it really is first; if your dependency structure differs from my guess, say so plainly and give me yours.

3. The load-bearing case. Not the murderer at the door — they have that one pre-rehearsed and it triggers the script rather than the thinking. Choose a single different case that can carry the definition, the teleology of speech, the grading, and the permission to remain silent, and say precisely what properties it must have and what it must lack. Also give me one backup case for when the first one fails to land, and tell me how I will know by minute fifteen that it has failed.

4. The grading of lies. I recall that the treatise ranks kinds of lie by seriousness, with lying about religious doctrine at the worst end and lies that harm no one but save someone from death or from bodily violation near the least-bad end, but I do not trust my memory of the middle rungs and I have no intention of being caught out in front of fifteen people. So do not recite the list. Give me the ordering principle instead: the dimension along which the scale is monotone, and whether that dimension is harm done, injustice, damage to the liar's own soul, damage to the community of speakers, or some combination. If the principle is not in fact consistent all the way down, tell me where it strains and which part of the scale I should simply not touch.

5. Objection placement, treated as triage rather than as a list. For each of the following, say whether it can be headed off by construction, before it forms, or only repaired afterwards, and name the point in your sequence where the work gets done: (a) this is just Kant four centuries early; (b) lying means asserting a falsehood, so this is really a debate about edge cases; (c) the definition turns every novel and every joke into a lie; (d) if silence and concealment are permitted, he has smuggled the calculation back in through the back door; (e) remove the theological premises and nothing is left. Then tell me which of these five I have misjudged, and which sixth objection I have not thought of that will cost me more than any of them.

6. The steelman, stated without flinching. Identify the one respect in which this argument does work that my audience's trust-erosion account genuinely cannot do — my guess is that it locates the wrongness in the speaker's relation to her own mind rather than in downstream effects on hearers, which their account has no place for. And give me your honest verdict on whether that survives the removal of the theological premises, since item (e) is the first question I will get and I refuse to improvise the answer.

7. Two smaller judgment calls. First: the biblical hard cases, where he argues that divine reward tracks mercy or faith rather than endorsing the deception — do I bring these in at all, and if so where, given that my audience is likely to find the exegesis more embarrassing than illuminating? Second: the distinction between not-speaking-a-truth and speaking-a-falsehood, together with his willingness to have the innocent person suffer rather than lie — is that the load-bearing move or the one that will cost me the room, and how do I sequence it against the rest?

8. Scope cuts: what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and for each cut, whether it is safe or lossy — that is, whether the argument still stands without it. The polemic against the sectarians who held that feigning was pious, the grace controversies, the Latin terminology, the medieval reception: I suspect some of these are free to drop and one is not.

9. The check. One question I can put to the room at minute fifty-five that discriminates understanding from polite agreement, plus what a good answer and a merely agreeable answer each sound like.

Write the plan concretely enough that I could hand it to someone else and they could run the session from it alone.
