Our graduate reading group in Buddhist philosophy has spent the last several weeks on the interpretive dispute over whether Buddhist ethics is best read as a form of consequentialism — roughly, Keown's insistence that the tradition is better understood on an Aristotelian virtue model, against the consequentialist readings defended by Goodman and, in a different key, by Siderits's Buddhist reductionism about persons. Two members of the group, Julian (fourth year, working on Madhyamaka ethics) and Meera (a postdoc in comparative philosophy), got into a sustained argument that the rest of us mostly watched. I am reporting it here because I could not determine at any stage which of them had the better of the exchange, and I would like a careful adjudication rather than a summary of the two readings.

The argument started from the skillful-means case everyone reaches for: the bodhisattva who, as captain of a merchant ship, learns that one passenger intends to massacre the five hundred merchants aboard, kills him preemptively, and accepts the resulting bad karma and hell rebirth upon himself. Julian opened by saying this settles the interpretive question, since the captain weighs one life against five hundred and chooses the greater aggregate good, and the tradition not only permits but praises the calculation, so we have an explicit act-consequentialist permission inside the corpus. Meera replied that the case proves close to the opposite: nothing in a maximizing calculus requires the captain to absorb the karmic cost himself, so the point of the story is the agent-relative, self-sacrificial element that aggregation cannot capture — he does not treat suffering as transferable between persons, he takes it onto his own account. Julian said self-sacrifice is no counterexample, because a utilitarian can praise willingness to bear costs when bearing them produces the best outcome, and accepting a hell rebirth is instrumentally rational if it saves five hundred lives while also sparing the bandit a far worse karma of his own. Meera answered that describing the choice as instrumentally rational already presupposes that the good being promoted is agent-neutral, whereas the captain reasons as this agent about this deed that he will have to own — which is precisely the separateness of persons that the no-self doctrine denies, so the tradition cannot coherently be described as maximizing across persons.

She then generalized: Buddhist ethics is intention-based, since intention is what constitutes karma, and if moral status attaches to the mental act rather than to the state of the world it produces, the structure is anti-consequentialist. Julian said that intentions are individuated by their objects — to intend to relieve suffering is to represent suffering as to be relieved, an orientation toward a value that is not indexed to the agent — so intention-centrality is a claim in moral psychology rather than about right-making, and it presupposes a value theory that tells us which intentions are skillful (kuśala) in the first place. Meera then pressed what she took to be her strongest point, the aggregation problem: if persons are reducible and suffering, in Śāntideva's argument for altruism, has no owner, there is no site across which to sum welfare; ownerlessness licenses the elimination of suffering as such, which is not the same as maximizing aggregate well-being, and nirvāṇa is emphatically not a sum of pleasant states, so the consequentialist label smuggles in a positive welfare metric the tradition lacks. Julian said that is a quarrel about content rather than form — if what makes acts right is their bearing on the cessation of suffering, the view is consequentialist with an unusual axiology, and she had thereby conceded the structural point — adding the Parfit-style claim that reductionism about persons undercuts agent-relative constraints, so no-self should push toward agent-neutral reasons. Meera's last move was that reductionism undercuts egoism and aggregation in a single stroke: the insight that dissolves the boundary between my pain and yours equally dissolves the domain over which one sums, leaving compassion as a disposition rather than a decision procedure. At that point a third member suggested they were simply using consequentialism in different senses and that the dispute was verbal; neither accepted the suggestion.

Could you work through the argument turn by turn, saying where each of them is right and where each is wrong, rather than merely naming a winner? In particular: (a) does the captain case favor one reading, or is it neutral between them once the detail about the captain's absorbing the karma himself is handled carefully; (b) is Julian's move from the object-directedness of intention to the compatibility of intention-centrality with consequentialism sound, or does it change the subject; (c) does the ownerlessness-of-suffering argument support maximization, undermine it, or do neither; and (d) is Meera's closing reductionism point a genuine argument or a restatement of Parfit's? Please also say explicitly where either of them equivocates among a theory of right action, a theory of value, and a theory of moral psychology; whether the third member's charge that the dispute is merely verbal is fair; which of the two is more right overall and about what; and what both of them leave out of account — including whether the disagreement would survive if the captain case were bracketed entirely.
