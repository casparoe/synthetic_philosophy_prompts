So, background: second year as an assistant principal at a mid-size public high school, and I'm the person everything social lands on. I grew up two counties over from here, which matters, because half my family would have handled every one of these cases completely differently than I do. I've been reading a bit about the honor culture / dignity culture / victimhood culture distinction — the sociologists who push it argue that honor cultures make your worth depend on what other people think of you, so you're expected to answer a slight yourself, publicly; dignity cultures say your worth is inherent and can't actually be damaged by an insult, so the virtuous response to being mocked is to shrug and only involve authority for real crimes; and victimhood cultures are a hybrid — sensitive to slight the way honor cultures are, but instead of retaliating you publicize the harm and get a third party to intervene. If I've got that wrong, correct me, because the whole thing below depends on it.

Here's what I actually did or would do, case by case. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. Sophomore shoves a freshman in the hallway after the freshman calls him a name in front of his friends. I wrote up the sophomore for the shove and told the freshman he was out of line, but no consequence for him. Words aren't a wrong; hands are.
2. Structurally identical shove, except it happened in an empty stairwell with no one watching. On paper I'd handle it exactly the same. In my gut, the kid who got shoved in front of an audience is more excusable than the one who got shoved where nobody saw. I don't like that I feel that.
3. A girl screenshots another girl's text and posts it, forty kids pile on in the replies. The target tells me she's fine, she muted it, please don't do anything. I didn't. But I walked away feeling like I'd abandoned her, and I know I'd have acted instantly if she'd asked me to.
4. A senior tells me he's been called a slur in the locker room since sophomore year and never reported it because he doesn't let that stuff get to him. I respect him and I'm also annoyed at him — like he owed the next kid in that locker room a report. Not coming forward was, to me, a small failure on his part.
5. A kid punches another kid for joking about his mother, who died last spring. No consequence, just come find me next time. Privately I thought he was right. Compare to 1: over there a shove earns a writeup, here a punch earns nothing, and the difference is entirely what the insult was about.
6. A teacher sarcastically demolishes a student's answer in front of the whole class and the parent complains. I backed the family and made the teacher apologize. When a student sarcastically demolishes a teacher, I tell the teacher it's teenage noise, let it go, don't put it in writing. I think that asymmetry is correct and that it tracks power, not content.
7. A parent wants me to discipline a kid for an Instagram post made off campus on a Saturday about her daughter. I said no, not mine to police, and told the daughter to let it roll off. Six weeks later, basically the same kind of post about a kid with an IEP who's already on our radar: I called the family and had it down in a day. I'd do both again.
8. The one I can't settle. A freshman tells me another kid used a slur about her at a party. He denies it, three witnesses give three different stories, nobody recorded anything. I believed her and gave him a consequence. If the accuser were a kid with a reputation for stretching the truth and the accused were a quiet girl in her band, I honestly don't know that I'd do the same, and I know how bad that sounds.

My working principle, which I've never written down until now: how much fuss you're allowed to make depends on whether you could have handled it yourself and whether the institution is actually going to do something. If you can shrug it off, you should; if you can't, you're obligated to tell someone; and hitting back is only excusable when no adult with authority is going to protect you.

Please play the role of a moral philosopher who has also done real applied work in schools — someone who can handle the theory and the mess at the same time. Think carefully, and before you commit, brainstorm three or four candidate principles out loud, saying what each one is tracking (audience? the target's own say-so? relative power? vulnerability? whether the institution is competent?), and only then pick the best and test it against all eight. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts it can't save and which one I should give up. Also: is case 3 or case 4 the one that's wrong, since they look alike and I judged them oppositely? And invent one case I didn't list that your principle scores differently from what I'd probably say on instinct — I want something that would actually be hard for me on a Tuesday morning. No bibliography or reading list, please, I can't look anything up and don't want to guess at sources.

Last thing, and it's why I'm asking instead of just living with the inconsistency. My own kid is 14 and told me last month that someone's been calling her a name at the bus stop and she's just ignoring it. I said good, ignore it. Then I sat there feeling like a fraud because of case 4, and my brother's advice to her was the exact opposite of mine. Which of us is right, and does the principle you land on say anything about what I should tell her tonight?
