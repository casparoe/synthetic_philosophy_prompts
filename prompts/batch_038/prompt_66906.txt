Write a patient information leaflet — the folded insert that comes with a prescription medicine, with the usual sections: what it is used for, before you take it, how to take it, what to do if you miss a dose, possible side effects, and a closing note addressed to the patient — for a newly approved drug whose only effect is to make the patient's reasoning unreliable in ways she cannot detect from the inside. About 650 words, in a flat regulatory register, with no winking at the reader.

The manufacturer understands the epistemology and gives honest instructions: the warning label tells the patient that her reasoning is compromised, and that warning is itself evidence she must weigh using the very faculty it impugns. Have the leaflet commit, in the small print, to a definite view about a patient who goes on believing what she has carefully worked out while taking the drug — irrational, rationally akratic, or neither — and make at least one section self-undermining in the way the underlying problem is, so that following the advice correctly requires discounting it. The missed-dose section should note that reasoning improves while the patient's evidence about her own recent reliability does not.

Include a contraindication for patients who have just received a devastating assessment of their own competence; an adverse-reactions list that contains believing *p* while believing that your evidence does not support *p*; and a rebate form that is void if it was completed by someone who believed in it at the time. A reader who knows the literature on higher-order evidence should be able to see exactly which positions the document is taking; a reader who doesn't should still get a coherent and funny piece of packaging.

Begin your response with the leaflet itself: no preface, no commentary, and no note about which view you chose.
