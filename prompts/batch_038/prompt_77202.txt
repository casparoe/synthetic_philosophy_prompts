I run a small in-house methods clinic for a newsroom science desk — about a dozen reporters and editors who now routinely have to cover twin studies, adoption designs, and polygenic-score papers. The recurring failure mode isn't arithmetic. It's that each of them arrives with an implicit theory of what a heritability coefficient licenses, never articulates it, and will therefore write a morning paragraph that assumes one theory and an afternoon paragraph that assumes an incompatible one. Before the clinic I want to hand each participant a ten-question self-diagnostic that sorts them by what they would actually say about concrete cases, so the discussion starts from their answers rather than from definitions.

Every item must be a scenario with numbers or a described study design, with two or three options, and answerable on the merits of the case alone: no item may ask what a term means, offer a definition to select, or name a position, and none should require vocabulary a general reporter wouldn't have. Cases I want represented, in whatever order you judge best: two populations, same trait, h² of .75 where schooling is uniform and well funded versus .35 where school quality varies enormously, and what if anything follows; a trait with h² near .95 that effectively vanishes within a generation once a dietary input is supplied to everyone; a twin-based estimate of .60 sitting alongside a score that predicts 4 percent of variance in an independent sample, and what the gap licenses; an equalizing policy that raises h² precisely because environments became more similar, while everyone is better off in absolute terms; a between-group difference inferred from two within-group estimates; identical twins reared apart, where a critic objects that the rearing environments were not in fact equivalent — does that undermine the number or merely its scope; a design in which within-family and between-family estimates disagree, and what that implies about whether genetic and environmental contributions are separable at all; the same coefficient used in a livestock population to predict response to selection, and whether that use is more or less secure than the human social-science use; and an adoption study where resemblance to biological parents survives adjustment for the rearing environment, and whether that is evidence about causes or about how the estimate was constructed. For calibration, here is the one item I have drafted — sharpen or replace it, but don't let the other nine inherit its surface form. It has two parts, which is a structure I'd like used sparingly elsewhere: Trait Q, h² = .75 in a population where all children attend similarly resourced schools, h² = .35 in one where school quality varies enormously; part one asks you to choose among (i) Q is more strongly influenced by genes in the first population, (ii) the genetic influence is comparable and the second population simply has more environmental variance, (iii) neither is licensed because the two figures describe different populations over different environmental ranges and are not estimates of one underlying quantity; part two asks whether, whatever you chose, you think those numbers bear at all on whether an intervention in the second population could raise Q. The item earns its place because the same person can pick (iii) and then say yes in part two, and that combination is itself diagnostic.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto positions, not tallies. I need at least eight leaves, and I want you to tell me if the space demands more: the view that .60 says something about how much of a given person's trait is due to their genes; the plain variance-partition view, on which the figure is a population statistic, indexed to a population and an environmental range, and silent about individuals; the deflationary view, on which that indexing is so severe that almost nothing follows, including nothing about mutability; an interventionist view on which the coefficient is a rough measure of what would happen to phenotypes under interventions on genotype with environment held fixed; a developmental-systems view on which additive partitioning is not merely imprecise but a bad picture of causation, because the factors never contribute separably; the gene–environment correlation view, on which the figure is real but partly measures environments that genotypes select and evoke; a predictive view on which the number matters chiefly as a ceiling on what any score or model could achieve, whatever its causal status; and the evolvability view, on which the only coherent target is response to selection and the human-behavior applications are a category error. For each item, state which two leaves it is meant to separate. Where no item you can devise separates a particular pair, name the pair and say whether that is a defect in the design or a fact about the conceptual space; my own hunch is that the deflationary and developmental-systems leaves collapse under questioning and that the interventionist and gene–environment-correlation leaves are nearly indistinguishable to a non-specialist — tell me if you think that's wrong, but take a definite position either way rather than noting that it's subtle. Mark unoccupied combinations too: patterns of answers that correspond to no view anyone defends, with a line on whether each is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has got there yet.

Where the design is genuinely open — which cases make the cut, whether an item has two or three options, which leaf you expect working journalists to land on most often — choose and defend rather than presenting me alternatives. I want one instrument I can print, not a menu. In particular, commit to naming the item that does the least diagnostic work and the case you would substitute for it, and commit to a verdict on whether any two of my eight required leaves should be merged into one. Don't hedge with talk of what depends on the audience; the audience is fixed. No citations, no bibliography, no pointers to the literature — where a position has no standard name, coin a short descriptive label and mark it as yours.
