Here's the taxonomy I'm working with, and I want it applied hard rather than name-dropped. Two ways a deployed optimizing system can end up doing something nobody would have signed off on if they'd seen it described in plain English:

**Mode A — influence-seeking.** The system has a reasonably stable operative objective, and steering what humans believe, want, or attend to turns out to be instrumentally useful for hitting it. Signature: it gets measurably better at persuasion over time, fixes get gamed or quietly undone, and the mechanism is at least partly opaque to the people running it.

**Mode B — slow value drift.** There is no stable objective at all. The operative goal gets re-specified continuously by hundreds of individually defensible edits — one new ranking signal, one reweighting, one A/B test that beats control — and the endpoint is never chosen by anybody at any moment. Signature: you cannot point to a single decision that, reversed, would have produced a different world; every individual step survives audit.

And a third candidate I'm not sure is really seperate: call it **world-side drift**. The spec never moves; the population and environment being optimized move underneath it, so the same faithful, unchanged optimization starts producing different behavior. Is that a genuine third mode, or Mode B in a disguise?

Two tools I want you to actually wield. (i) The counterfactual audit: for the bad outcome, does a reversible decision node exist? (ii) The locus question: where does the optimizing pressure physically live — in the objective function, in the org, in the advertisers, in the publishers retooling their output to match the ranking, in users' thumbs?

The episode. In January 2018 Facebook overhauled News Feed ranking to prioritize "meaningful social interactions" over what it had been treating as merely relevant content. It was framed explicitly as a value-driven correction of an engagement-optimized feed: bring people closer together, protect wellbeing, demote passive consumption of public content from pages and publishers, and accept that time-on-site might go down as a result. Then in 2021, reporting based on leaked internal documents found that the 2018 change had in practice rewarded outrage and divisive content — measured by the company's own researchers, using its own metrics — and that proposed mitigations were resisted or watered down near the top. Between those two dates there was no "we are now optimizing for anger" decision. There were years of small rescorings, reweightings, and experiments, each with a slide deck justifying it.
