I'm putting together a seminar section on the scale argument for wild insect welfare — you know the move: even if the chance that any given insect is sentient is really low, multiply that by the sheer number of insects and you get an enormous expected amount of suffering, so it ought to be way up the priority list. Every version I've read writes it as *one* probability times *one* population. I want to pressure-test exactly that bit of shorthand, and I want a case, not an essay.

Build me a scenario where getting **better evidence for insect sentience** makes the pro-intervention conclusion come out worse, not better. Specs:

- Realistic, present day, one paragraph max. A regional agriculture or conservation body deciding whether to roll out a pest-suppression program — mating disruption, sterile-insect release, a parasitoid wasp, whatever, but it has to be something that actually gets done somewhere. No gene drives, no sci-fi, no futuristic monitoring. Ordinary moths or beetles; no exotic cognition, no hive minds, no cephalopods sneaking in.
- Then a study lands. It has to be unambiguously *good news* for insect sentience: everyone in the case agrees it raises the rational credence that the animals in question are sentient, and it says nothing whatsoever about the program's cost, its efficacy, or its side effects. Purely about the animals' capacities.
- Before the study, rolling out the program is the highest-EV option on the table. After it, doing nothing — or the smaller alternative — strictly beats it. And the flip has to survive arithmetic a reader can redo with a calculator: give me actual numbers, keep any two insect populations within an order of magnitude of each other, keep credences to two decimals. No 10^18s, no hand-waving about astronomical stakes.
- You may **not** get the flip by assuming a sentient insect's life is net-negative, or that death is worse for an insect than never existing. You also may not just declare that the non-target insects matter more per capita. If there's an asymmetry doing the work it has to be generated by the evidence in the story, not imported into it.
- Both sides in the case use the scale argument's own logic. The disagreement should be about the *structure* of the credence, not about whether numbers matter.

Then run the check: lay out the before-and-after expected values in a small table so I can see the crossover happen; name the single feature of the case that does the work; tell me what happens to the verdict if I delete it — specifically, if the study had landed on the pest taxon instead of wherever you put it, does the whole thing collapse back into the standard scale-argument picture? And finish with how someone who holds that better evidence about sentience can only ever *strengthen* the case for acting would attack your construction, plus your honest read on whether that attack lands.

So you know what counts as cheating: the point is to get ecology grad students (basically no decision theory background) to see that the scale argument quietly assumes there's a single probability attached to "an insect" that you can just lift out and multiply by a headcount, and that this is a substantive assumption doing real work, not a notational convenience. So a case that stipulates "some of these insects are more probably sentient than others" without giving a reason *in the story* is a fail, even if the arithmetic is right.
