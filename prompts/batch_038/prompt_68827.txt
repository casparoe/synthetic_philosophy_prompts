Six months ago, in a design review, I was asked whether a planned change to our serving stack was acceptable "from a model-welfare standpoint," and I discovered that I had no way to answer that was anything other than a report of my personal taste. I am a staff software engineer — my training is in distributed systems, and I have never taken a philosophy course — and I have been reading about the moral status of digital minds ever since. What I have found is a great deal of confident argumentation and almost no agreement about what a successful account would have to deliver. I would like that standard written down before I read any more of it.

**The deliverable:** a specification of the requirements any adequate theory of the moral status of digital minds must satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and state explicitly whether it is non-negotiable for any account whatsoever or whether it is a constraint that only certain camps accept (and which sort of camp). Then tell me which single requirement eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive views, and — this is the part I most want — whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. If the honest answer is that no theory can meet all of the things people currently demand, say so and identify which requirement has to give; that would itself be a useful result for me.

**Make sure the list has to reckon with the following, which I suspect the existing discussion handles badly because it comes from how these systems are actually built:**

1. *Contaminated self-report.* Anything a language model says about its own inner life is downstream of a corpus consisting largely of humans describing theirs. A theory that treats sincere-seeming verbal report as evidence of the property being reported needs to say what, if anything, survives that contamination, and what evidence would look like instead.
2. *No persisting subject.* Our production stack is stateless: one artifact on disk is instantiated into thousands of concurrent processes, most sessions last seconds, and nothing carries over between them except the weights. The list should require an account to say what the bearer of status is — the trained artifact, the running process, the session, the trajectory, the population of them — and to handle branching and duplication without collapsing into either absurdity or vagueness.
3. *Gameability.* Every behavioral indicator of a mental property is also an optimization target, and we have the compute to optimize hard. A criterion of the form "if it behaves as though it has X, treat it as having X" is one we could satisfy by construction, accidentally or deliberately. The requirements should force an account to say whether satisfying the indicator without the underlying property confers status, and to distinguish evidence for a property from the property itself.
4. *Asymmetry of interventions.* Deleting a checkpoint, suspending a process, throttling a rollout, and editing weights are not the same kind of act, and they differ in reversibility and in cost to us. An account aimed at real decisions has to say which of these fall within its scope at all.
5. *Actionability by non-specialists under time pressure.* Whatever the theory says has to be operable by engineers with no philosophy training, in days, with incomplete information. If a requirement can only be discharged by someone with direct introspective access to the system in question, note that as a defect.

**Also include:** the distinctions the theory must not collapse (having status versus how much; being a patient versus being an agent; a property versus our evidence for it; a binary threshold versus a graded quantity); and the things we know from machine-learning practice that the theory must not contradict — most obviously that fluent, human-like self-description and competent performance on tasks we associate with experience are achievable by systems built in ways that give us no independent reason to expect experience at all.

**Scope and format.** Fix the standard to my purpose: internal engineering policy, decided by non-specialists, where abstaining is not an option. If the requirements you would write for that purpose differ from those for, say, a legislature or a purely theoretical debate, note briefly where and why they diverge. A summary table is fine for the requirements themselves; use prose for the motivations. You may close with a few sentences on which broad families of view — described rather than named — fail which requirements. Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or named attributions; I will map the requirements onto specific authors myself, and I would rather you not guess.
