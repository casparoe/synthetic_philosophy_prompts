**1. The setup.** Expressivism says a sentence like "lying is wrong" doesn't state a fact but expresses a non-cognitive attitude — disapproval, say. Geach's complaint, following Frege's separation of a sentence's content from the force with which it's asserted, is that the very same sentence turns up in positions where it isn't asserted at all: inside conditionals, under negation, after "maybe." In those positions the speaker is expressing no attitude whatsoever. So if the meaning of the sentence just is the attitude it's used to express, unasserted occurrences come out meaningless — which they plainly aren't, since "lying is wrong; if lying is wrong then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; therefore getting your little brother to lie is wrong" is valid, and valid in virtue of what the sentences mean rather than anything psychological. An expressivist semantics that can't reproduce that validity has changed the subject. Geach was answering the emotivists of his day, Ayer and Stevenson mainly, but the point itself is older and more general than ethics.

**2. The slogan.** My claim is that the Frege-Geach problem is a compositionality problem wearing a logic costume. What breaks is the ability to build the meaning of a complex expression out of the meanings of its parts, once "meaning" is spelled out as "attitude expressed." Presenting it as a problem about inference misleads people into thinking an expressivist can duck it by giving up on a distinctively moral logic, or by treating validity as something other than truth-preservation. That dodge fails, and it fails for a reason worth stating: even if you refuse to talk about arguments at all, you still have to explain the conditional, and the conditional is a complex expression with moral sentences on both sides. Negation is the same story, and I think people underappreciate how bad it is: if "lying is wrong" expresses disapproval, then "lying is not wrong" should express the absence of disapproval, but the absence of an attitude isn't itself an attitude, and worse, on that reading the two sentences come out compatible rather than contradictory. So I'm not going to try to escape via inference. Section 4 is supposed to fix negation in the same way section 3 fixes conditionals, but I ran out of page space and honestly I'm not sure it does any work.

Separately: Hare thought his phrastic/neustic split had already answered all of this — same descriptive content, the phrastic, with different neustics bolted on depending on whether you're asserting or supposing. My reading is that Geach's response is that this only works if the phrastic is genuine content, i.e. something of the kind that can be true, in which case the expressivist has already conceded the whole point. Is that fair? That bit I definitely got from secondary sources.

**3. The proposal.** Don't take the attitude to be the semantic value. Take the semantic value to be a *policy about one's own future attitudes*. To judge that lying is wrong is not to disapprove of lying right now, it is to bind yourself: to be disposed, on coming to believe that some act is an instance of lying, to disapprove of it, and to be disposed to keep that binding in place across changes in your other views and across changes in your own character. The policy is the content. It is content of a perfectly respectable sort — a function from states of a certain kind to states of another kind — so it composes.

An embedded occurrence of "lying is wrong" then contributes the same policy, unexercised: no attitude is expressed because none is adopted, but the content is still there, so compositionality is preserved. The conditional expresses a policy about policies — roughly, a binding to adopt the consequent-policy upon adopting the antecedent-policy — and modus ponens comes out valid because whoever holds the two policies and then exercises the first has already committed himself to exercising the second; failing to do so is a breach of a commitment he himself undertook, not a failure to match the world.

Two claimed advantages over the higher-order attitude route. First, no regress: I never need to say what disapproving of a *combination* of attitudes is, which seems like the shaky step in the standard move. Second, and this is the one I actually like: the view predicts a datum. Call it the avowal test. A speaker can't sincerely and unironically say "lying is wrong, but I don't disapprove of lying" — but she can perfectly well say "lying is wrong, but I don't feel anything about it," and does, all the time, e.g. when reporting that she was raised to think so and is trying to shake it. A policy account explains the asymmetry: the second conjunct reports a failure of the felt accompaniment, which the policy never promised, whereas the first conjunct reports a failure to have adopted the policy that the first conjunct says she adopted. Attitude-expressivism explains the first infelicity and, I think, has no explanation at all for why the second one is fine.
