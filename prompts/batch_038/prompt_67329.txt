I cover evolutionary science for a general-interest magazine, and I'm about to file a personal essay staking out my own position in the units-of-selection debate. Before it goes up I'd like a consistency check, not a refresher on the literature. Here is what I hold:

— Gene-centred and group-centred descriptions of the same evolutionary process are formally intertranslatable: one underlying dynamic, two bookkeeping conventions, not rival theories.
— What makes something a unit of selection is high-fidelity replication; everything else is a vehicle expressing a replicator's phenotype.
— Kin selection accounts for the whole of animal altruism, and invoking group selection adds no explanatory power to it.
— Kin selection is nonetheless a limiting case of group selection, the same covariance partition read from the other end.
— Human cultural evolution is the exception. Conformist transmission and institutional punishment let group-beneficial norms spread by routes that individual-level selection cannot reproduce, so cultural groups do selective work that is not reducible to what the individuals inside them are doing.
— In the artificial-life experiments I've been reporting on, where whole populations are periodically culled according to their group-level output, I take it that genuinely group-level adaptations emerge which a strictly replicator-centred account has no room for.

Please audit the set rather than defend or attack it. Which of these pull against each other, and which pairs only look inconsistent and dissolve once you distinguish the claim being made in each? For every real tension, say what the cheapest revision is and what it would cost me elsewhere in the list, then order the tensions by severity. I'm deliberately not telling you which item I'd be least reluctant to drop — part of what I want from the audit is whether that turns out to be obvious.
