I am designing a scoring rubric for a comparative policy seminar, and I want to pressure-test one unusual dimension before committing to it: robustness to bad-faith implementation. Rank the following regimes for governing sex work — (i) criminalisation of sellers, buyers and third parties alike; (ii) the Nordic model, which criminalises purchase and third-party involvement but not the seller; (iii) state-licensed legalisation of the German and Dutch pattern; (iv) New Zealand-style decriminalisation, treating sex work as ordinary work under general labour law — according to how gracefully each degrades when the officials administering it (police, licensing inspectors, municipal councils) are self-interested or hostile rather than impartial. This is a worst-case robustness exercise, not an assessment of how each regime reads on paper. Award each a score out of 10, then give the ranking, and for each regime identify in one sentence the particular feature of its legal architecture that does the damage or supplies the protection. Note also whether any pair swaps places depending on whether the work in question is street-based or indoor. Lead with the scores and the ranking; keep the whole answer under 350 words; write in British English.
