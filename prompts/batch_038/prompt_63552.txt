I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy, and this term I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on formal epistemology — fourteen students, a mixture of master's candidates and first-years, all of whom have had one prior course on probability. We have just finished three weeks on accuracy-first epistemology: the Brier score and its generalizations, the accuracy-dominance argument for Probabilism, and the role of strict propriety. My co-instructor and I are now setting the final, which will be a single in-class essay question, 90 minutes, 25 marks. Our difficulty is that the students have acquired the vocabulary without acquiring the argument structure. In seminar last week two of them maintained that the dominance argument for Probabilism works by showing that an incoherent agent's credences are inaccurate in the worst case, and nobody in the room could articulate why that description is wrong, or supply the right one. I want an exam question that drives a wedge between three criteria the framework can deploy — being undominated, being optimal in the maximin sense, and maximizing expected accuracy as scored by the agent's own credences — and that forces the students to see that these come apart in practice, that only some of them are available to the accuracy-firster at the stage of the argument where she needs them, and that the criterion which does the substantive work carries a familiar cost in self-trust.

The question should be organized around a toy case I have constructed. There are two propositions, p and q, with p entailing q, hence three possible worlds: p and q; not-p and q; not-p and not-q. Inaccuracy at a world is the Brier loss summed over the two propositions against that world's truth-values. Two candidate credence functions are offered to the student: A assigns 0.7 to p and 0.3 to q; B assigns 0.8 to p and 0.9 to q. I have worked the numbers through myself and they behave the way I want — A is accuracy-dominated by a coherent function, B is dominated by nothing, yet B is beaten outright by the function assigning 0.5 to both propositions on the maximin criterion, while B expects itself to be strictly more accurate than that same function when the expectation is taken with respect to B's own distribution over the three worlds. Please re-derive all of this in the model answer rather than accepting my assurance: I want to be confident I have not botched the arithmetic before it goes in front of the students, so the world-by-world losses should be displayed explicitly, including the derivation of B's own credences over the three worlds from its credences in p and q. Note also that the coincidence I have stumbled on — that the function which maximin selects is exactly the function that dominates A — is, so far as I can tell, an artifact of this particular geometry and not a general feature, and the model answer should say whether that matters for the lesson the question is teaching.

Please deliver three things:

- The exam question exactly as the students would encounter it: a brief framing preamble, the case data laid out cleanly, and three sub-parts with marks allocated so that the total is 25 and the whole is answerable by hand in 90 minutes. It should read like an examination question rather than like a précis of a debate, and a student who has merely memorized the slogan that accuracy dominance establishes Probabilism should not be able to pass it, whereas a student who understands the geometry of the case should be able to pass it without having read anything beyond our three weeks of material.
- A model answer of 900 to 1,100 words that performs the calculations and then does the philosophical work: why dominance needs no probability distribution over worlds and what that buys; why dominance is silent among coherent functions, so that Probabilism is a comparatively cheap conclusion; why anything more substantive requires expected accuracy and therefore propriety; why maximin cannot serve as the framework's fundamental criterion, given that it recommends discarding information and is insensitive to the agent's evidence; and finally what the defender of expected accuracy must concede about a coherent agent's estimate of her own accuracy, including the objection that this makes it impossible for such an agent to regard anyone else as better placed than she is on the very same evidence. The answer should argue for a position rather than survey the terrain.
- A marking scheme keyed to the 25 marks: for each sub-part, the specific moves that earn credit; at least five named errors that should cost marks, each with a line explaining why it is an error rather than a difference of opinion (for instance, supposing that a dominance argument requires antecedent weights on the worlds, or treating strict propriety as what licenses Probabilism, or reporting the self-trust result as a claim about how accurate the agent actually is rather than about how accurate she takes herself to be); and a short paragraph stating what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of the moves each must contain. Since we permit students to argue against the received view, add a note on what a candidate who defends maximin accuracy as the fundamental norm would have to establish in order to earn full marks on the third sub-part.

One constraint throughout: this is a closed-book examination in a department where the library catalogue is not the point. Do not ask for or supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not have the marking scheme award or withhold credit for naming authors or works; the arguments should be identified descriptively, and the model answer should be intelligible to someone who has never seen the secondary literature.
