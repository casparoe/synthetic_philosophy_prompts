I'm grading finals for my philosophy of religion seminar (upper-level undergrad, mostly seniors) and I've got 30 of these to get through, so I could use a second pair of eyes on one of them. The exam was closed book, 50 minutes, and this was the single essay question, worth 25 points:

"Set out Gödel's ontological argument in prose: the primitive notion, the three definitions, and the axioms governing 'positive property'. Say which modal logic the argument presupposes and identify the step at which it is needed. Then compare Gödel's argument with Plantinga's modal ontological argument, and assess the two most serious objections to Gödel's."

Here's the answer:

---

Gödel's argument can only be run in higher-order modal logic, because the definition of God quantifies over properties; that's the first thing that seperates it from Anselm's, which is at least gesturable at in ordinary language. The one primitive is P, 'is a positive property'. Gödel never analyses it — he axiomatises it instead, and his own notes say the concept has to be extralogical. The axioms are (1) for any property, either it or its negation is positive, but not both; (2) if a positive property entails another property, that one is positive too; (3) being God-like is positive; (4) if a property is positive, it is necessarily positive; (5) necessary existence is positive. There are three definitions. A God-like being is one that has every positive property. An essence of x is a property x has that necessarily entails every other property x has. And x exists necessarily just in case every essence of x is necessarily exemplified by something.

The proof runs in two stages. Stage one: a God-like being is possible. Like Plantinga, Gödel has to start here — the axioms about positivity only tell you what the concept involves, and the possibility itself has to be granted before anything can be derived from it. This is also where the choice of modal system matters: he needs S5, with a universal accessibility relation, so that the world in which the God-like being lives is reachable from ours. Stage two is then internal. Take a God-like being in some world. God-likeness is an essence of it; necessary existence is positive (axiom 5), so a God-like being has it; so by the third definition that essence is exemplified in every world. No further assumption about the modal system is needed here, since axiom 4 already carries positivity across worlds on its own. So a God-like being exists necessarily, and hence exists.

Compared with Plantinga: both are modal, and both are exposed to the same charge of begging the question at the possibility premise. Plantinga's is neater — maximal greatness in one world gives omnipotence, omniscience and moral perfection in all of them — but Gödel's is more economical, since he doesn't build the divine attributes in by hand; they drop out of positivity. Gödel's version also has a pedigree: he was working on Leibniz's attempt to patch Descartes, and treated his own argument as a cleanup of it.

The most serious objection is Sobel's modal collapse. Because the first axiom applies to every property whatsoever, whatever is true of a God-like being is true of it necessarily, and the axioms end up making every truth necessary. That proves too much, so the argument is invalid — a valid argument can't have consequences that destroy the distinction its own premises trade on. The second is Kant's, that existence isn't a predicate; it's real, but it bites less here than people assume, since what axiom 5 calls positive is a defined technical property rather than bare existence.

Gödel didn't publish any of this. Dana Scott copied the proof out in 1970, it circulated privately, and it only appeared in print after his death, in the Collected Works.
