My roommate is doing an AI policy fellowship and she came home last Thursday and whiteboarded this at me over dinner, and I still cant tell whether it's a good argument or a trick. Her pitch is that it's just the responsibility-gap argument followed all the way down, and that if you follow it you have to give up the idea that foresight is a condition on blame. Roughly as she gave it:

(1) A person is morally responsible for a harm only if they could reasonably have foreseen that their conduct would produce it.
(2) A system that acquires its behavior from data — a driving stack, a triage model, a targeting system — produces particular behaviors that its designers, its manufacturer, and its deployers could not have foreseen, because the mapping from training procedure and dataset to deployed behavior is opaque even to the people who built it.
(3) So when such a system causes a harm, no human in the chain is responsible for it: not the engineers, not the company, not the operator who switched it on, not the regulator who cleared it.
(4) Generalize. Every harm any of us causes reaches the world through processes we don't understand and can't predict — our own nervous systems, the other people involved, the hospital, the supply chain, the traffic system, the courts.
(5) So on the same reasoning, nobody is responsible for any harm, ever.
(6) That's obviously false; it can't be that no one is ever responsible for anything.
(7) So (1) goes. Foreseeability isn't a condition on responsibility — and notice that's not a special lesson about machines, it's just the epistemic condition doing what it always did.

She also likes the fact that (7) is provocative, which makes me suspect her a bit. But I'll admit (2) just seems true to me, anyone who's actually used these systems knows the behavior surprises you, and I'm pretty confident (1) is doing real work in ordinary cases: if I hand you a drink at a party and it turns out somebody slipped something into it at the bar and you die, I dont think I'm blameworthy for your death, and (1) is exactly why. If I reject (1) I seem to lose that, and I'm not willing to. On the other hand (4) looks like the weakest link to me, since going from "the thing I built is a black box" to "my own nervous system is a black box" feels like a stretch — but she says that's just squeamishness about the word "system," and honestly I don't have an answer to that. I also don't know what to do with (3): it slides from "no individual foresaw the harm" to "no one is responsible," and there's a company standing right there in the sentence.

Then price it: what does the person who accepts your diagnosis have to give up, and is there anything left worth calling a responsibility gap once the slide is blocked, or does the gap just evaporate and we're back to ordinary negligence?
