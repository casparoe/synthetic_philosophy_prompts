I am preparing a short essay for a general-interest politics magazine and want to get the underlying philosophy straight before I start drafting. Simone Weil's late essay arguing for the general suppression of political parties has been sitting on my desk. My reading of her charge, which may be crude, is threefold: a party functions as a machine for manufacturing collective passion; it necessarily pursues its own growth as an end, without any internal limit; and the discipline it exacts from its members makes independent judgment about what is true and what is just structurally unavailable to them. Rather than test that charge against "parties" in the abstract, I want to test it against one concrete institutional mechanism — the whip system in the British House of Commons — which I take to be the sharpest available case.

The mechanism, described plainly: each week the party whips circulate the coming business to their members with the leadership's instruction underlined once, twice, or three times, the number of lines indicating how seriously attendance and compliance are demanded. A three-line whip marks the votes that matter most to the government's survival or to its program, and a member who defies one risks having the whip withdrawn — expulsion from the parliamentary party, loss of the party label at the next election, and usually a long period in the political wilderness before readmission. Yet on a scattered list of subjects, historically including abortion, capital punishment, embryo research, fox hunting, and more recently assisted dying, the same leadership declares a free vote and the whips stand down. What strikes me as peculiar is that this freedom is granted from above rather than claimed from below: the leadership decides, case by case, which questions are matters of conscience.

Against that background, three things I need from you.

First, what theory of political judgment does the whip system presuppose? When a member votes as instructed on a bill whose merits they have not independently assessed — or whose merits they have assessed differently — what is the practice taking them to be doing: deferring to an epistemic authority better placed to judge, honoring a mandate conferred on the party by its published program, keeping a promise made on joining, or pooling with others in a way that only makes sense if the party is a single agent rather than a coalition of agents? Tell me which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits comfortably with, and say whether the practice is underdetermined — whether several inconsistent theories could each license exactly the behavior we observe.

Second, what does the free-vote carve-out presuppose that the whip system does not, and is the combination coherent? Does the carve-out amount to a concession to a rival theory of judgment, one on which certain questions can only be answered alone and cannot be delegated at all? Or is it a managerial device for absorbing backbench pressure on questions where the party has no coherent line, no manifesto commitment, and no electoral interest in taking one? Test both readings against the fact that the freedom is revocable at the leadership's discretion, and tell me which is more plausible or whether the practice really does admit of both.

Third, where does Weil's charge land and where does it overshoot? If the whip system is best understood as promise-keeping between members who campaigned on a program and voters who elected them for it, does her objection from suppressed judgment still bite, or has it been answered? And what would a legislature actually look like if the presupposition were replaced outright — no whips, no party labels on the ballot, members deciding question by question on their own judgment — and which of the functions the present arrangement silently performs, such as keeping a government stable, giving voters a coherent record to hold someone to, and managing the rotation of power without violence, would be lost in the exchange?

Format: begin with the bottom line — state the presupposed theory in no more than two sentences, before any argument — and then develop it under the three headings above. Hard limit of 700 words in total; I would rather you cut a good point than exceed it. No quotations from Weil are needed, and please do not supply a reading list.
