Last weekend I ended up in a two-hour argument with my flatmate about an essay he had sent me, and I came away frustrated rather than convinced. Some background: I am in the first year of a master's programme in mathematical logic, so I can follow the formal apparatus, but I have almost no training in philosophy of religion, whereas he has read a great deal of it on his own and is considerably better at dialectic than I am. The essay is by an academic philosopher writing for a general-audience magazine, on the supposed revival of the ontological argument, and the section on Gödel's version is what he thinks settles things. His position, stated repeatedly over the course of the evening, was that once you see the axiom list laid out properly there is nothing left for a sceptic to grab hold of except one axiom, and that refusing that axiom is nothing but prejudice. I do not believe that, but every time I tried to say why, he simply recited the structure of the proof back at me — and I could not find a step to object to, because as far as I can tell each individual step is correct. I suspect the essay's rhetorical moves are doing the work, not the logic, but I cannot locate them. Here is the passage as I typed it out from the magazine:

"Gödel's argument is the first ontological proof that is genuinely rigorous rather than merely rhetorical. It is set in a quantified modal logic of the S5 type, and it takes a single undefined notion — that of a positive property — together with five axioms about it: that positivity is inherited by whatever a positive property necessarily implies; that for every property either it or its negation is positive but never both; that being God-like is itself a positive property; that whatever is positive is necessarily positive; and that necessary existence is positive. A God-like being is then defined as one that has all the positive properties. The elegant part is what Gödel does not need: he does not have to smuggle in the possibility of God as a premise. Since a positive property cannot be self-contradictory, every positive property is possibly exemplified; being God-like is positive; therefore a God-like being is possible. After that the modal logic does the work. God-likeness turns out to be an essence of any God-like being, necessary existence belongs to such a being because it is a positive property, and so a God-like being exists in every world whatsoever — the conclusion is actual and necessary existence, not a bare possibility. Notice too that Kant's objection, which sank the earlier versions of the argument, has no purchase here: Gödel is not treating existence as a perfection that adds something to the concept of a thing, but as a modal status attaching to the way an essence is realized across worlds. That is why the residual dispute concerns only the axioms. The inference is valid, so anyone who denies the conclusion is obliged to reject one of the five, and the only candidate that lacks independent support is the fifth, the one declaring necessary existence positive. Gödel himself never published the argument, apparently because he was uneasy about how it would be received."

Could you go through that passage clause by clause and tell me what to trust? Specifically, I would like each claim sorted into three piles: what a logician or philosopher of religion working on this material would sign off on without qualification; what is a defensible simplification for a general audience that becomes a distortion only when it is used to make the essay's larger points; and what is simply wrong or question-begging. Three things in particular are what my flatmate and I actually fought about. First, is he right that the possibility of a God-like being is genuinely derived rather than assumed, and does that derivation lean on the axiom saying that for every property either it or its negation is positive — because if it does, I want to know how much of the argument's weight rests on that axiom rather than on the fifth. Second, is the essay's treatment of Kant fair? It strikes me that one of the five axioms declares necessary existence to be a positive property, which looks like treating existence as a property of a being after all, but I could not articulate this against him and he said I was confusing two different senses of "predicate." Third, is the claim that the only remaining dispute concerns the axioms, and that only the fifth lacks independent support, accurate? Can someone reject the conclusion while keeping all five axioms — by objecting to the underlying modal logic, or to the second-order quantification over properties, or to the identification of "a God-like being" with the God anybody actually worships? Someone in our reading group mentioned in passing a problem called modal collapse and I never understood what it had to do with any of this, so if it bears on the essay's closing move, please explain it to me from scratch. I would also value a verdict on the historical aside about Gödel's motives, which I suspect is tidier than the record.
