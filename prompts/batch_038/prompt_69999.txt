I teach a seventy-five-minute methods seminar to second-year economics graduate students — fluent in Bayesian updating, able to derive a posterior without thinking, and entirely innocent of philosophy. The assigned topic is Keynes's conception of probability and uncertainty: the doctrine of the 1921 Treatise on Probability together with the treatment of long-term expectation in the 1936 General Theory. I do not want you to explain Keynes to me; I want a plan for how I should explain him, and I want it to be honest about the order in which the pieces can actually be received.

Specifically:

1. Give me the dependency structure of the sequence. I take it that at least the following have to be introduced: the claim that probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a proposition rather than a property of an event or a state of the world; the possibility of probabilities that are non-numerical; the stronger and much less digestible claim that some probability relations are not even comparable in magnitude, so that no ordering exists to maximize over; the separate notion of the weight of an argument, which can increase while the probability stays the same or even falls; and Keynes's restrictions on the principle of indifference. Tell me what genuinely depends on what, which of these can be postponed without cost, and which one collapses if introduced out of order. Then tell me where the General Theory material belongs: is the move from the Treatise's logical relations to talk of spontaneous optimism and the urge to action rather than inaction a consequence of the earlier argument, an application of it, or a change of subject that I should announce as such?

2. I want to run the whole seminar on one example, and my instinct is to take Keynes's own case of the walker deciding whether to carry an umbrella when the barometer reads high but the clouds are black. Assess that choice. Can it carry non-comparability, or does it only illustrate conflicting evidence that any competent agent will simply weigh? Can it carry weight, or does weight need a different case — one in which the probability is perfectly determinate and the supporting evidence is nevertheless thin? State what the carrying example must have and what it must conspicuously lack, and if the umbrella cannot do both jobs, propose the single alternative that can, or say plainly that I need two examples and where to switch.

3. I want to open by writing a slogan on the board — that probability belongs to arguments, not to events — and I suspect this will do damage I cannot foresee, most likely by making the view look like a species of subjectivism about belief. Tell me whether the slogan is safe, and if it is not, what to write instead.

4. Map the misconceptions, and for each one say where in the sequence it must be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. The ones I anticipate: that weight of argument is simply confidence, or the variance of a posterior, and so nothing new; that the right way to absorb Keynes is to formalize weight as a second-order prior, a distribution over distributions, in which case his objection evaporates — I want you to take a position on whether that translation is a legitimate concession I should grant in class or a substitution of a different problem for his, and how to handle the student who presses it; that Keynes is simply a Bayesian who doubted measurability; that he is saying what Knight said about risk versus uncertainty, only less crisply; that the Ellsberg urn already disposed of the matter; that animal spirits is psychology smuggled in where an argument about probability had been promised; and, finally, the flat conclusion that if probability is not numerical then decision theory is bankrupt.

5. Give me one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would discriminate a student who has grasped the distinction between probability and weight from a student who can merely repeat the words back — ideally a question whose answer would come out differently under the two states.

6. Tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out, rather than gesture at: the formal apparatus of the middle part of the Treatise, the polemic against the frequency theory, the question of whether Keynes is committed to the existence of probability relations as entities, and the later logical-empiricist uptake of his view are all candidates, but I want your judgment on which of these are genuinely dispensable and which one will leave a hole that the students will fall into later.

Please do not supply a reading list, references, or page numbers; I have the texts and want only the pedagogy.
