I have recently taken a seat on the animal research ethics panel at a European institute, and after a month of reading protocols rather than philosophy I find myself unable to settle a question about the shape of the rules themselves.

Our remit is fixed by statute, and the list of animals inside it is short. A project needs authorisation, a stated justification for the species chosen, anaesthesia and analgesia where pain is foreseeable, a defined humane endpoint, and an approved method of killing, if it uses any live vertebrate — including larval fish from the moment they begin feeding independently, and mammalian fetuses from the last third of gestation — or a live cephalopod. Within that class there is also a severity floor: procedures no worse than a needle stick given in good veterinary practice need no authorisation at all. Outside that class there is no floor, because there is no class. A colleague two doors down runs conditioning experiments on cockroaches and crayfish, disposes of her subjects by freezing or crushing, keeps no tally of how many die, and files nothing with us. A genetics group on the third floor breeds and discards several million fruit flies and nematodes a year outside any record whatever. In the same week that those protocols pass unexamined, I have watched this panel spend forty minutes on whether a particular mouse strain would find a proposed nesting material aversive, and I have seen projects refused for failing to show that they had minimised numbers to the statistical limit.

Two features of the boundary trouble me. It has moved: cephalopods were not on the list when my predecessors started, and were added at the last major revision on the strength of new findings about their nervous systems; one legislature has since recognised crabs, lobsters and octopuses as sentient under a separate welfare statute, and there is pressure to bring decapods into the research rules too. And it is drawn by lineage and by developmental stage rather than case by case — a crayfish and an octopus do not look obviously further apart in the relevant respects than an octopus and a perch, yet one of those pairs sits on opposite sides of the line while the other does not.

What I would like is an account of what this practice actually presupposes about animal minds and about whose experience counts. Is it committed to the view that there is a genuine threshold in nature, such that animals on one side have experiences that matter morally and animals on the other have none, or none that matter? Or does it merely adopt a workable boundary while remaining neutral about where experience really shades off, so that the list is an administrative artefact rather than a claim? Is the allocation of the burden of proof the thing doing the real work — an animal is treated as having no inner life until somebody demonstrates otherwise — and is that burden applied evenly, or only to the phyla nobody loves? Which rival pictures of animal minds would make our list indefensible, and which would leave it exactly as it is? Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible accounts of minds would license the same rules. Then tell me what would have to change on a Tuesday morning if the panel adopted a view on which experience comes in degrees and our confidence in its presence comes in degrees too: what would we begin to count, which procedures would become impermissible, and what could we no longer do at all?
