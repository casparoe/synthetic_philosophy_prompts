the question as the students got it:

PHIL 3140, Essay 2. Max 800 words.

Cognitivist theories of emotion hold that emotions are, or essentially involve, evaluative judgments. Perceptual theories hold that emotions are more like perceptions of value: they have evaluative content, but not of the sort that requires the subject to endorse a proposition. Feeling theories hold that an emotion is the feeling of a pattern of bodily changes.

In lecture we discussed a case we labelled "hollow fear": a patient who, on being shown a spider, exhibits the full autonomic profile of fear and reports the characteristic felt quality of being afraid, but sincerely disavows any evaluation of the spider as dangerous or threatening, and makes no move to withdraw or avoid.

(a) Which of the three families of theory does hollow fear create the most serious difficulty for, and which does it leave comparatively unscathed? Say why.
(b) Is the patient in hollow fear afraid? Explain what you take to settle the answer.

the student's essay, in full:

"Cognitivism, defended in its strongest form by Solomon and developed by Nussbaum, identifies an emotion with a judgment; Nussbaum adds that the relevant judgment is eudaemonistic, tracking the way an event bears on the subject's own flourishing. On this account my fear of the spider just is my judging that the spider threatens me. Perceptual theories retain the idea that emotions carry evaluative content but deny that the content is conceptual: fear presents the spider as dangerous in something like the way a visual experience presents a surface as red, non-conceptually and involuntarily. Feeling theories, going back to James, deny both. On James's telling the order of explanation is reversed from what common sense assumes: the bodily changes come first, and the emotion is the feeling of them, so what makes my state fear rather than excitement is which changes I am feeling, not anything I take the world to be like.

Hollow fear is obviously most damaging for cognitivism. There is no judgment in the case at all — the patient disavows the evaluation — and yet everything we would want to call fear is present, so the cognitivist either has to say the patient is not afraid, which looks like a bullet too big to swallow, or has to posit a judgment the patient cannot report, which makes the theory unfalsifiable. Perceptual theories come out second best. Because their content is non-conceptual, it can outlive the loss of the judgment in the same way that an illusion outlives our knowing that the two lines are the same length. Prinz's embodied appraisal version makes this explicit: since appraisals are non-conceptual they operate below the level of consciousness, so the theory treats the hollow fear patient as having an appraisal that simply has not been attended to, and it predicts the patient will report fear once the arousal is brought to attention. That is a testable prediction in the theory's favour. It is also worth noticing that James was in effect already a cognitivist, since on his view the feeling is a judgment about my own body; the dispute between feeling theories and judgment theories is thus only about whether the judgment is directed at the world or at the body.

So feeling theories are left unscathed, and they are what hollow fear supports. But note the mirror-image point: hollow fear is the reverse of the ordinary recalitrant case, where the subject judges the spider harmless and is afraid anyway. Whether those are two distinct problems depends on whether the disavowal in hollow fear is a judgment or just a report about what the patient is disposed to do.

The upshot, then, is that emotions are not evaluations of anything. They are bodily feelings, and the appearance of evaluative content is a story we tell afterwards. This also means that questions about whether a fear is appropriate or an anger fitting do not really arise, any more than they arise for an itch. The genuine cost is that bodily feelings may be too coarse-grained to separate fear from excitement, but hollow fear suggests that cost is worth paying."
