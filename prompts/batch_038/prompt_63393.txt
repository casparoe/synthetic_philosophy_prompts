My applied-ethics reading group has an argument that's supposed to show consent to enrol in a sponsor-funded drug trial in a poor country is never valid, and about half of us now think it proves way too much. Our resident defender layed it out like this:

(1) Consent is valid only if the person consenting has a reasonable alternative to consenting.
(2) Whether an alternative counts as reasonable is judged against the option set she actually faces, not against some better set she might have faced.
(3) That option set — what care is available, what she owns, what she can earn — was shaped by a distribution of wealth and infrastructure she had no hand in.
(4) That distribution is unjust.
(5) An option set produced by injustice contains no reasonable alternatives: everything on the menu is something she's being unjustly made to choose between.
(6) So by (1) her consent to the trial is invalid.
(7) And the reasoning transfers — the warehouse worker's consent to the rota, the tenant's consent to the lease, the user's tap on 'accept', each of them given from a menu somebody else's injustice wrote.
(8) But it would be worse for the enrollee, the worker and the tenant if we treated those consents as void: no trial access, no wages, no flat.
(9) Therefore (1) is false, and all this coercion talk about background injustice is empty.

The defender says (8) is beside the point and we should just bite the bullet on (6) and (7); I suspect the real trouble is earlier, somewhere around (5), but I can't say what. Which step do you reject? Tell me why it looked innocent going in, then price each of the candidate rejections — including what we'd lose if we dropped (1) altogether. And is there any way for the defender to keep the moral complaint about the trial without the invalidity claim, or does the complaint depend on it?
