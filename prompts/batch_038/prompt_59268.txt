I've been keeping a running list in my notes of the imprecise-credence cases I actually have verdicts about — it's going to be the spine of a workshop talk — and its a mess. Here it is, roughly in the order I wrote it down.

1. A coin comes out of a mint I know nothing about: no symmetry information, no track record, no manufacturing story. I refuse to hold a sharp 0.5 for heads. Something like an interval that comfortably straddles a half, maybe [0.3, 0.7]. And refusing to bet either side at even money, for any stake, seems to me like the correct call rather than cowardice.

2. But now: a coin I'm told is either double-headed or double-tailed, and nothing further. Here I think my credence in heads should be sharp, exactly 0.5, by symmetry — even though I also know for certain the true chance is 0 or 1, so my sharp 0.5 is guaranteed to misdescribe the chance. I'm completely at peace with that. Cases 1 and 2 look to me like the same evidence situation getting opposite treatment, and I'm not embarrassed by it, but I need to say why out loud.

3. The two-machine case. A coin is tossed twice by a machine I know is either sticky (likes to repeat) or anti-sticky (likes to alternate), with no clue which. Say sticky means P(HH)=P(TT)=3/8 and anti-sticky means P(HT)=P(TH)=3/8, so each toss is marginally even either way. My credence that the second toss is heads is a sharp 1/2. Now I'm offered the first result for free — and whichever way it comes, my interval for the second toss blows open to [1/4, 3/4]. I think declining the free look is rational. I also think, separately, that costless no-strings information is never rationally declined. Both sentences are on the list.

4. Betting on an interval. Offered $1 if heads for 55c: refuse. Offered $1 if heads for 45c: refuse again. So I turn down both sides and leave money on the table, and I think that's fine. But a scheme that guarantees I lose no matter how things turn out — that I think is genuinely irrational, not merely unlucky. Turning down both sides and getting suckered are supposed to be different things, and I want the principle that makes them different.

5. Sampling. I've watched the mint's coin land heads 500 times in 1000. My interval for the next head should now be narrow, call it [0.47, 0.53], and it should keep narrowing with more data, approaching a point arbitrarily closely without ever being one — even though the data never logically excludes a bias of 0.9.

6. Same interval, different history. You and I both sit at [0.2, 0.8] for "this urn is mostly red." You got there by drawing twenty balls; I got there by knowing nothing at all. I think these are not the same epistemic state, and that a view on which a credal state just *is* a set of numbers cannot see the difference. I'd pay for one more observation in both cases, but not the same amount, and one of us is much closer to being squeezed into precision.

7. If I know one of E1…En must obtain, and my conditional interval for H given each Ei lies inside [0.4, 0.6], then my unconditional interval for H had better not stick out past that. I'm told this requirement fails in some infinite setups and that I should learn to live with it. I don't want to.

My tentative principle is: the credal set is exactly the probability functions compatible with your total evidence; update by conditionalizing each member; accept a bet iff every member recommends it. It handles 1 and 4 tolerably. It gives the wrong answer on 2, since compatibility alone leaves heads anywhere in [0,1] there. It's silent on 5, because every bias is compatible with 500/1000. It can't even express 6. It's silent on 7. And I suspect it turns the refusal of free information in 3 from permissible into mandatory, which is the opposite of what I want.

So: what principle actually fits this list? State it precisely enough that I could run a new case through it myself, say which clause is doing the work in which numbered case, and tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't keep — I'd much rather be told that 3 or 7 has to go than be handed something with six epicycles bolted on. If 3 and 7 are in tension, I want that said explicitly, since I currently believe both.

Then run your principle on two cases that aren't on the list. (a) A forecaster I know to be well-calibrated reports 0.3 on a claim about which I otherwise have nothing at all: do I end up sharp at 0.3, or interval-shaped around it, and what in the principle decides that? (b) Same sticky/anti-sticky machine as in 3, except the first toss gets announced over a loudspeaker whether I want it or not, and I'm allowed to lock in my bet on the second toss beforehand — is walking out of the room still permitted, and if it is, what does that say about whether the information in 3 has negative value or merely no value?

I know the names of the standard camps here; I don't need the labels, I need the machinery.
