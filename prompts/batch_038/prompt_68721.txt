I'm prepping a reading-group session on Williams's internalism about reasons — the claim that an agent has a reason to do something only if doing it could serve some element of her actual subjective motivational set, by way of a sound deliberative route. Rather than just listing the usual lines of attack, I want them ranked by how much trouble they actually cause the view. Here are five candidates:

1. The sound-deliberative-route clause is so elastic that it smuggles external reasons back in: if the route can include imagining unfamiliar experiences and thereby acquiring new desires, then almost anything can be a reason for anyone, and internalism loses its distinctive bite.
2. It can't do justice to blame. If the amoralist genuinely has no reason to act morally, then blaming him anyway looks like nothing more than an expression of our own tastes, which sits badly with the way we actually practise blame.
3. It conflates two different questions — whether a reason exists, and whether this particular agent could be led to acknowledge it. Justification isn't a psychological matter.
4. It makes reasons hostage to luck in one's upbrining: two people in identical situations get different verdicts purely because of how they happened to be raised, and that arbitrariness is a cost, not a feature.
5. The self-conversion problem (this one's mine, I think): internalism can't explain why an agent has a reason to acquire a motive she currently lacks and has no reason to want — say, becoming someone who cares about her craft for its own sake — since every route out of the motivational set has to start inside it.

Could you sort these into tiers by how damaging they are to the view, with a sentence or two on why each lands where it does, and then give Williams's thesis an overall survival score out of 10? Please be explicit about which of your judgements track something close to a settled consensus among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested — I don't want a minority reading served up as the standard line. Answer in British English.
