Norway's 2003 law requires that at least 40 percent of the directors on the boards of its public limited companies be of each sex, and the outer-limit sanction for refusing to comply was that the company itself could be wound up. Two features of how it worked have stayed with me. First, the same law was defended in two quite different voices: as a matter of fairness to women, and on the ground that mixed boards simply run better businesses. Second, the requirement was checked firm by firm, by counting heads, and nothing in it cared whether the same small number of women supplied the missing 40 percent everywhere — within a few years, one study counted eight women holding more than sixteen directorships each over a four-year span, against two men doing the same — or whether the pool from which directors are drawn changed at all.

What does this arrangement presuppose about what was wrong with the old boardrooms? Work through the candidate readings: that women were being kept from their share of a valuable good; that a board on which everyone has had the same life deliberates badly; that a room composed of one sex asserts something false about who is fit to govern; or something else entirely. For each, say whether the practice genuinely requires it or is merely consistent with it, and where the practice leaves the question open so that several incompatible views could license the very same rule. The directorship-hoarding pattern seems to me to tell these readings apart, though I cannot work out how; show me what it does and does not settle. Finally, describe what a policy built on each of the other readings would have had to look like instead.
