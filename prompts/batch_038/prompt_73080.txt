I run ethics and compliance at an industrial company, about 2,400 people — procurement, field service in six countries, a small commodities desk, and a few roles that touch cash or customer funds. Every quarter I get somewhere between six and ten situations that are either already bad or about to be: a category manager who steered business to a supplier he had an arrangement with, a service tech invoicing hours nobody on site could contradict, a regional manager whose downtime numbers improved every month for two years and then fell apart in a week. The retro always lands in the same place — he was trusted, he seemed great, and the fix is more training or one more approval layer. Six months later its the same story somewhere else.

I want to build the intake tool on a different idea. Glaucon's challenge in Book II of the Republic: the shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible and promptly uses it to get into the queen's bed and kill the king. But Glaucon's actual move isn't "people are rotten" — it's to hand the same power to a just man and an unjust man and predict they end up walking the same road, because nobody is just willingly, only for want of the ring. He also treats justice as a compromise between the best outcome (doing wrong and paying nothing for it) and the worst (suffering wrong with no way to hit back). So the question I want my team asking about a role isn't "is this person honest," which we can't answer and which everyone finds insulting — it's "how much of a ring does this role hand whoever sits in it."

Write me a procedure we can run on a role or a process before the incident: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where a role stops at "this is a ring and the fix is to redesign it or kill it," and where the procedure has to hand the call to a person because what's left is a question of values, not facts. Tell me what to actually look at — how detectable the act is, whether the actor is individually identifiable to anyone who'd care, how far the harm sits from the decision, whether some reputational community we don't control is watching, and whether the existing controls are independent of the person they're supposed to constrain.

Constraints, because these decide whether we use it at all: 30–45 minutes per role, run by a compliance analyst who has never read Plato and isn't going to, off a spreadsheet and one 40-minute conversation with the role's manager. It has to be presentable to the business without reading as "we assume you're a crook" — if the first slide implies that, my COO kills it. Legal signs off on anything that adds monitoring or moves a reporting line, and I don't get headcount, so a "yes" that requires a new permanent reviewer isn't a yes I can act on.

Then be honest about the translation. Which of Glaucon's distinctions has to survive into a checklist and which can I drop to save time — especially his brother's separate point right after him, that what people actually want is to be thought just while doing as they please, which sounds like a different lever than invisibility and may be the one I control. Where does the thing misfire: roles that score high but are held down by feedback loops you can't see in an org chart, and roles that look controlled because there are four approvals in the workflow when all four approvers report to the same person or just rubber-stamp. And mark any step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume.

Finally, run it on three cases. One: field service techs in a country where we have no supervisor, billing labor and parts from a phone against customer sites, approved by an analyst six time zones away off a spreadsheet. Two: a sole-source procurement lead who owns a category with one qualified supplier and writes the technical specs himself. Three: my own function — intake reports land on one analyst's desk, we have read access to basically everything, nobody audits us, and our numbers look better the fewer substantiated findings we close out. I expect the third one to be the answer I don't like. Then tell me what the tool can't do. I already suspect that scoring the ring quietly concedes Glaucon's whole point, and that the single most important input — real detection probability — is a number I can never measure, only guess at. Say where guessing is good enough and where it isn't.
