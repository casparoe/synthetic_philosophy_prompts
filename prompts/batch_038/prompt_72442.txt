A thread I have been reading for the better part of two weeks has ground to a halt on a single sentence, and I want that sentence taken apart before I post in it.

The setup: someone proposed that a hand-specified prior for a hierarchical model could be objectively better — better in the sense of being truer to how the domain is actually structured — than a prior fitted automatically to a large corpus of previous datasets. The reply that closed the discussion was, in substance, that there ain't no free lunch: averaged over everything one might be trying to predict, every method performs exactly as well as every other, so the question of which prior is correct is aesthetics wearing a lab coat. Several people seconded it and the thread moved on to implementation details. In the communities I read, this sentence gets pointed at anything that claims one inductive bias is better than another — a universal prior, an appeal to Occam's razor, the whole project of arguing about priors as though arguing were possible — so I do not expect this to be the last time I meet it.

I am confident that the sentence was doing at least three separate jobs and that at most one of them survives contact with the results it invokes. I am not confident enough to say which is which in public. What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a rebuttal.

Take the slogan — in whichever phrasing you find most tractable among *there ain't no free lunch*, *no method beats any other on average*, *all priors are equally arbitrary*, *without assumptions there is no learning* — and separate the precise claims that could be inside it. For each reading: put it in a form capable of being true or false; state what has to be pinned down before it is even evalable (averaged over which set of targets, under what weighting, measured on the points the learner was shown or on the ones withheld, comparing deterministic learners or randomized ones, and benchmarked against another learner or against chance itself); and attach a verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, false as stated, or unfalsifiable rhetoric. Please flag the trivial readings as trivial rather than dignifying them with careful exposition, because my suspicion is that the sentence carried its weight in that thread by being heard in a substantive register while being licensed only in a trivial one.

Three places I would like the menu to reach.

First, the results for black-box optimization and the results for supervised learning are not the same theorem, and the thread moved between search heuristics and generalization error without ever marking the change. Say what each family actually licenses and what each leaves untouched.

Second, the argument slid from *no method is prior-free* to *no prior is better than any other*. I believe the slide is where it dies, but I want the intermediate step spelled out rather than asserted: what would have to be true of the world for one prior to be better than another, whether that is an empirical hypothesis about the world or a stipulation about our preferences, and whether the averaging inside these results says anything about it in either direction.

Third, someone wrote that the theorems are Hume's problem of induction rendered in mathematical notation, and I think that has it backwards. The uniform weighting over targets that makes the results go through looks to me like precisely what the inductive skeptic declines to grant, which would mean the theorems presuppose the contested assumption rather than establish it. Tell me whether that objection holds, or whether I am the confused one.

One further wrinkle, recalled imperfectly and quite possibly mangled: I have some memory that these results need care with randomized learners, something about whether the average is taken over the learner's own internal coin flips. My instinct is that this cannot matter, since a randomized learner is just a distribution over deterministic ones and expectation is linear. If the instinct is sound, say so plainly; if there is a genuine technicality behind the memory, describe it, because I would rather be told than post something confidently wrong.

Deliverable: a numbered menu, ordered from the most defensible reading to the least, with the verdict attached to each and a short note on which readings the argument in that thread actually depended on. Close with the briefest honest reply I could post — one that concedes what the slogan gets right and does not concede the rest.
