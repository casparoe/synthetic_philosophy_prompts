I host a philosophy-of-religion podcast that records in front of a live audience of about forty people — mostly graduate students, but with a persistent contingent of lay readers from a local theology group. Next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent the past decade building a positive account of the atonement out of David Lewis's 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" Her view, as I understand it from her articles, runs in three stages. First, she accepts Lewis's central diagnosis: the classic picture on which an innocent volunteer takes the sentence the offender deserved, and justice is thereby satisfied, is not something we can now believe, because its intelligibility depended on a social world of kinship and honor in which a kinsman's suffering could be accepted as the offender's own — the substitute was never a mere unrelated third party, and that is what did the work. Second, she thinks Lewis's own preferred alternative, where the one who was wronged declines to exact and instead absorbs the cost himself, is left underdeveloped in the paper. Third, she develops it into the claim that what the cross displays is not the transfer of a penalty but the victim's absorption of a loss: God, as the party wronged, takes the damage rather than calling it in, so that forgiveness stays forgiveness instead of becoming a three-party transaction. She is a careful and gracious guest, but she has a characteristic move — she reframes hostile questions as category errors, saying that the questioner is putting a retributivist question to a non-retributivist account — and I want questions she cannot dissolve that way.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, not to the answer I would like. I want the set to cover, at minimum: whether absorption is any less a substitution than punishment, given that on her own telling the bearer of the cost and the party wronged are not fully distinct persons in the Trinitarian case — and if she answers that this identification is precisely what makes the arrangement acceptable, whether she has not simply re-licensed the doctrine Lewis rejected; whether her claim about honor cultures is conceptual or anthropological, and what she means by "we cannot believe it" if a person raised inside a clan-based compensation system would find the transfer perfectly intelligible; the retributivist insistence that punishment is non-transferable even with the offender's wholehearted consent, and whether her reply — that what is owed is a debt of honor rather than a penalty — quietly returns her to Anselm's satisfaction and so buys coherence by changing the subject; whether a victim who must incur a price before the relationship can be restored is forgiving at all, or merely settling, and what exactly is absorbed by a God who cannot be made worse off; what her account needs the crucifixion for, and whether the cross does any explanatory work beyond illustrating a willingness to forgive that could have been declared; and what her model says about the offender's side — whether absorption cancels the debt, relocates it, or leaves it outstanding with the victim carrying it, and what repentance contributes. I would also like one question that runs the whole issue without theology at all: a friend who volunteers to serve a sentence, a family that compensates for a member's offense, an employer who eats a subordinate's liability — so that she has to say whether her objection is to substitution as such or only to substitution enforced by a state. Close with a question about what would count against her view. No biographical softballs, no invitations to summarize the state of the debate, and nothing that asks her to recommend sources, since the audience will have read the paper.

Two further things, and here I want you to commit rather than hand me a menu. First: tell me plainly which two or three of these questions you judge will actually force a concession from a philosopher of her caliber, and say why those rather than the others — I would rather run a tight forty minutes on the questions that bite than cover everything and be deflected. Second: flag where my gloss above is uncharitable, imprecise, or simply wrong about Lewis or about the position she is likely to hold, and if you think one of the pressure points I have listed is a fake problem that she will dispatch in a sentence, say so and tell me what to substitute for it. If you can see a stronger line of attack on the absorption model than any I have listed, put it in the set and explain what makes it stronger.
