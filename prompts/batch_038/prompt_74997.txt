**Purpose.** I am preparing teaching materials on interpersonal utility comparison for a graduate seminar, and I need a case that isolates the *scaling convention* as the sole source of disagreement about a distributional choice. The case should show students that two respectable procedures for making utilities comparable across persons can be applied to exactly the same body of intrapersonal facts — the same people, the same outcomes, the same ordinal rankings, the same evidence — and still point in opposite directions. Anything that produces the divergence by some other route counts as cheating: no stipulated verdicts, no character whose gain we are simply told is the larger one, no smuggled moral asymmetry between the two claimants, and no device that reads utilities off directly.

**The scenario.** Write a single allocation problem in at most 200 words of continuous prose, satisfying all of the following:

1. One indivisible benefit and one binary decision: it goes to A or to B. No third parties are affected, and there is no uncertainty — given the decision, each individual's resulting state is certain.
2. Although the decision is binary, each individual's situation must be described over a range of at least four distinguishable states rather than two. For each of them the case must locate their worst attainable state, their best attainable state, where they currently stand, and where the benefit would put them. The endpoints may lie beyond what this decision can deliver; their job is to fix the range.
3. An ordinary institutional setting — an employer, a housing authority, a municipal committee. Explicitly not medical triage, not pain management, and nothing turning on how long either person has to live.
4. Neither individual's state set may include death or permanent severe impairment. The stakes must be of a kind that could in principle be compensated for in money or in time.
5. Both individuals strictly prefer receiving the benefit to not receiving it, and their ordinal rankings must be stated explicitly.
6. The case must supply enough ordinary, non-question-begging evidence — self-reports, observed behaviour, what each says about their own alternatives — for a reader to construct both (a) each individual's own attainable range, and (b) a single metric on which the two gains can be compared directly. The evidence for (b) may not itself presuppose that the comparison has already been made.
7. The engine of the divergence must be a difference in the *spread* of the two attainable ranges: one individual's states lie close together on the common metric, the other's are far apart. This difference must be a matter of temperament, not of adaptation — the narrow-range individual must not be someone who has lowered their expectations in response to deprivation or discrimination, and the details of the case must make clear how a reader is supposed to know that.
8. Both individuals must currently sit at the same level of the common metric, so that the case cannot be settled by prioritarian concern for the worse off, and must be matched on every other dimension a student might use to break the tie: equally deserving, equally needy in the ordinary sense, equally entitled, and equal in disposable income.
9. The common metric must be able to locate the endpoints of *both* ranges, and the case must indicate what enables it to do so. If it cannot place those endpoints on one scale, the ranges are not commensurable and whatever divergence you get is an artifact.

**The demonstration.** After the scenario, in numbered steps:

1. *Range normalization.* Define the convention in one sentence. Assign each individual 0 at their worst attainable state and 1 at their best, place their current and post-benefit states accordingly, and report which allocation the convention favors. Show the arithmetic.
2. *Common-scale comparison.* State which single metric you are using — hedonic intensity, a shared self-report scale, a capability-style measure of what each person can actually do and be, or another — and say briefly why it can bear interpersonal weight here. Report which allocation it favors. Show the arithmetic.
3. Confirm explicitly that steps 1 and 2 disagree, and that they disagree on identical ordinal facts and identical evidence.
4. *Money-metric check.* Run a willingness-to-pay or willingness-to-accept comparison on the same case, state which side it lands on, and say whether an income correction changes anything.
5. *Impartial-observer check.* Run the extended-sympathy procedure: an observer choosing as though they had an equal chance of being either individual, with that individual's preferences. Report which side it favors — or, if it comes out indeterminate, say precisely why and name the convention that would have to be supplied to make it determinate.
6. *The load-bearing feature.* Name the single feature of the case that generates the divergence, remove it while holding everything else fixed, re-run steps 1 and 2, and report whether the divergence collapses.
7. *The loser's reply.* Give the strongest attack available to the side that comes out badly — for instance, that the common metric is really two scales in disguise, that the evidence in step 2 covertly stipulates what it was supposed to establish, that the intuition the case elicits is a hedonic one which a preference-based theorist need not share, or that the exercise proves too much because every such comparison is a value judgment dressed as an empirical claim. Then say how much of that attack lands.
8. *Generality.* Is the divergence structural — arising whenever two individuals' attainable ranges differ in size under a common metric — or an artifact of your particular numbers? If structural, say what the two conventions would have to agree on in order to stop disagreeing.

Write for readers who know expected utility theory and have encountered the impossibility results but have never had to perform an interpersonal comparison by hand. Define each convention on first use. No citations, no reading list.
