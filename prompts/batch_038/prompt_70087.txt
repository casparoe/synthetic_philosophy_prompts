My film-and-philosophy group is doing Citizen Kane next month, and I want to run it against something I've been reading on the side lately, which is Plutarch. I'm a longtime LessWrong reader so this isn't my home turf — I'm much better on decision theory than on ancient biography — but I've been going through the Parallel Lives and the modern argument about whether you can actually learn ethics from lives, and Kane keeps looking like the perfect test case. I'd like a real reading, not a plot summary with philosophy sprinkled on it.

Here's the Plutarch side I want you to take seriously. The proem to the Life of Alexander is where he says he's writing lives rather than histories, and that a slight thing — a remark, a joke, the way a man behaves at a dinner party — often discloses character better than a battle where thousands died. The proem to Pericles makes the moral machinery explicit: reading about good men produces admiration, and admiration produces an impulse to imitate, so the biography is a piece of ethical technology rather than a record. Then there's the synkrisis, the formal comparison at the end of each pair, which is basically Plutarch grading two lives against each other. And in the Demetrius/Antony proem he defends writing up bad men at all, on the grounds that there's something to learn from what to avoid. Set that against the hagiographic worry — saints' lives flatten the subject into a type, edit out the unedifying parts, and in doing so make imitation impossible, because you can't imitate someone who is never shown choosing — and against the contemporary debate: exemplarist accounts that treat admiration as a route to moral knowledge, the moral saints objection about whether the admirable life is even the life you should want, and the plain epistemic problem that a biography gives you behaviour and testimony, not character.

Now the film. Kane is structured as a failed biography: a reporter trying to build the man out of five witnesses, each producing a different Kane — Bernstein the loyal disciple who is basically a hagiographer and doesn't seem to notice it, Leland the moralist who can't finish the review and gets fired for it, Susan the intimate who resents him, Raymond the butler who observes everything and concludes nothing, Thatcher's written memoir as the institutional life. And then Rosebud, which is precisely the trivial Plutarchan detail — except the only person who gets it is the audience, and the biographer inside the film never does. So: does the film vindicate Plutarch's claim about slight things, or refute it, given that the detail turns out to be unshareable and to yield no verdict on the man? Is Bernstein's distortion treated as a failure of the method or as the only form love takes? And here's the trap I most want handled honestly rather than skated past: Kane being a bad exemplar doesn't by itself refute exemplarism, since a single life can be unimitable without the practice of learning from lives being mistaken — so tell me whether the film is making a claim about the method or only about this man, and whether Kane belongs in Plutarch's Demetrius slot as a cautionary life he'd have been happy to write. Finally, say what the film shows about character and testimony that the theories miss, and where my framing strains — flag anywhere I'm being anachronistic, e.g. reading Plutarch as an exemplarist or Welles as making a deliberate epistemology-of-biography argument. I don't want a survey of the options; take a position on whether Kane vindicates or refutes the Plutarchan project and argue for it, and note where your reading takes liberties with either the film or the Lives. Last line, exactly, in the form Verdict: VINDICATES-PLUTARCH or Verdict: REFUTES-PLUTARCH or Verdict: ORTHOGONAL, followed by a parenthetical of no more than ten words giving your reason.
