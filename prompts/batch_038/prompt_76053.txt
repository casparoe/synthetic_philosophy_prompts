I am setting Section B of the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the ethics of war, and I need one essay question, worth 25 marks, to be answered in 50 minutes. The seminar has spent three weeks on the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants and on the revisionist challenge to it, so the question must not be answerable by a competent rehearsal of that debate. I want it to turn on a case I have constructed, so that what is being tested is whether students can track distinctions under unfamiliar pressure rather than recall the two camps.

Please write the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, together with a model answer of roughly 850 words and a full marking scheme.

Here is the case, to be reproduced in the question as a stipulated scenario. Two states, A and B, are at war. Each has fielded a briefing system that prepares the justification dossiers issued to its own combatants. The system operates under a hard design constraint: every proposition it asserts is true, and it is incapable of asserting anything false. What it controls is selection, weighting, sequence, and framing — which true facts about the enemy's conduct are included, how quantitatively prominent they are made, which explanations are offered as live alternatives, and which are omitted as though they were not candidates. The output reliably produces in an attentive, well-trained soldier the settled belief that his own side satisfies jus ad bellum and that the enemy does not. Both systems are equally effective and equally constrained. No soldier on either side has any reason to suspect curation, and there is no available evidence by which he could detect it. Two further stipulations matter and should appear in the question: the soldiers are volunteers, subject to no unusual coercion or threat of punishment; and the political leadership on each side, having outsourced its own assessment to the same kind of system, is in the same epistemic position as its soldiers, so there is no level of the state at which an uncurated judgment about the justice of the war is being made.

The question should have two parts. In part (a), students must say whether symmetric curation of this kind undermines, supports, or leaves untouched the moral equality of combatants; they must identify which of the familiar grounds offered for the doctrine is actually in play — the coercion or authority ground, the epistemic ground, or the convention-and-consequences ground — and they must state explicitly whether their conclusion concerns the justification of killing, permissibility relative to the agent's evidence, liability to be killed, or excuse. In part (b), only A fields such a system; students must say whether the verdict changes for A's combatants, for B's combatants, or for both, and whether A's leadership commits a distinctive wrong, and if so against whom.

The model answer should take a definite line rather than survey, and should grapple with the two features that make the case awkward: that the briefings contain no lies, so it is not obvious that the soldiers are deceived in the sense the standard excuses require; and that the asymmetry between soldier and statesman, on which much of the traditional doctrine depends, has been deliberately removed.

For the marking scheme, allocate the 25 marks across five or six named moves that an excellent answer must make, and for each give one line on the most common way candidates botch it. I am particularly concerned about four failure modes and want them named explicitly: using the labels evidence-relative and fact-relative decoratively without showing which verdicts change between them; sliding from the soldiers are blameless to killing them is impermissible, or from they cannot know to they may kill; treating the truthfulness constraint as window dressing and analysing the case as an ordinary propaganda case; and concluding that because both sides' soldiers have equally good evidence, neither is liable and therefore no one may fight, without noticing that this is a cost their view must absorb rather than a refutation of it. Then state where the boundary lies between a first-class answer and a good-but-not-excellent one, describe what a bare passing answer looks like, and confirm that two opposite conclusions can both earn full marks provided the moves are made — noting in the scheme which of the two you think is stronger and why, so that markers who disagree with my own view can still grade consistently.

Do not include a reading list, footnotes, or citations; I will supply those separately. You may name Walzer and McMahan where that is the clearest way to pick out a position, but refer to arguments by description rather than by source.
