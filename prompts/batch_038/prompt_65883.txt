I worked out an argument about binding your future self, and it seems to prove far too much. I can't locate the mistake.

Two things I want to defend: part of each paycheck is moved automatically into an account I can't touch for a year without paying a fee, and my town's charter says electoral district lines can be changed only by a two-thirds vote plus a delay of one full election cycle. Both strike me as sensible. But consider:

1. A self-binding arrangement does something only if, when it bites, it stops you from doing what you then most want to do. If it never bites, it accomplished nothing.
2. At any moment, what you most want to do is what it is rational for you to do at that moment; if it weren't, you would want something else.
3. So whenever such an arrangement has any effect at all, its effect is to make you act against your own reasons, as you yourself see them at the time.
4. The earlier self installed the arrangement on a guess about a situation it would never be in; the later self is inside that situation and knows what it feels like. A guess is worse evidence than presence.
5. So the earlier self has no standing to overrule the later one, and installing the arrangement is either a blunder or a small act of coercion against someone better informed than you are.
6. Charters work the same way. Either a future majority can repeal the entrenching rule using the rule's own procedure — in which case entrenchment was only a delay, and by (2) a delay that leaves people wanting the same change at the end has done nothing; or it cannot repeal it — in which case the people living under the rule are governed by people who knew strictly less about their situation. Entrenchment is therefore either empty or unjust.
7. Hence neither my savings arrangement nor the two-thirds clause can be defended.

I won't accept (7). If something has to go, I'd rather lose any other premise than (2), which looks like the only thing keeping the whole argument from being arbitrary. Which step fails? I want to know why it looked solid to me, and what I would be committed to if I dropped it instead of one of the others.
