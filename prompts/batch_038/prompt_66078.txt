I serve on my college's Residential Standards Committee, which hears appeals under the Residence Agreement, and I am taking a social choice seminar this term that may be biasing me — but I do not think Thursday's case is merely an awkward fact pattern.

Two residents, R and S, share a suite. Under the Agreement's Personal Sphere Clause each has sole authority over one designated matter in their own room: for R, whether R's door onto the landing stands open or shut in the afternoon; for S, whether S practises cello in S's own room during that period. That yields four arrangements: (i) door open, no cello; (ii) door open, cello; (iii) door shut, no cello; (iv) door shut, cello. The committee must rank all four and write the top one into a standing agreement for the suite.

Each resident has filed a ranking. R: (i) > (iii) > (iv) > (ii) — R wants the light and air, finds the cello intolerable with the door open, and can live with it muffled. S: (ii) > (i) > (iii) > (iv) — S says the instrument only sounds right with the door open, concedes that part of the appeal is that R hears it, and would rather sit in an open, quiet suite than play behind a shut door.

The committee is bound by two further rules: (a) where two arrangements differ only in one resident's designated matter, the committee must rank them as that resident does; (b) where both residents rank one arrangement above another, the committee must too. Rule (a) applied to S's matter puts (ii) above (i); applied to R's matter it puts (iv) above (ii); rule (b) puts (i) above (iv). The ranking the rules require is therefore cyclic and nothing can be written in — and the state the cycle defeats, (i), is Pareto-efficient and is R's first choice.

One member says the committee should simply override S's evident spite and impose (i); another says any arrangement is acceptable provided both clauses are honoured "in spirit." Please draft the memo I will circulate before the hearing. It should recommend a specific disposition for this suite and a specific disposition of rules (a) and (b) going forward — the committee may also refer an amendment to House Council if that is what you think follows — explain the diagnosis in terms a member who has never seen the theorem can follow, and give the override position its strongest case before saying whether it survives and what adopting it would cost.
