I'm preparing a presentation for my philosophy of language seminar on conditionals, and I tried to reconstruct Lewis's triviality result from scratch rather than following the notation-heavy version in our readings. What I ended up with seems to prove far too much, and I cannot find the bad step. Please take on the role of a working philosopher of language and formal epistemologist who has thought hard about triviality results, and reason your way through the argument step by step before committing to a diagnosis: consider at least two candidate culprits, and say why you settle where you do.

Let P be a rational agent's credence function, and let "if A, B" be an ordinary indicative conditional in her language.

1. Adams's thesis: the degree to which she should accept "if A, B" is P(B|A).
2. The degree to which an agent should accept a sentence is her credence in the proposition that sentence expresses.
3. So there is a proposition C with P(C) = P(B|A), and this holds for every A and B and for every credence function she might rationally come to have.
4. Credences in propositions obey the probability calculus, including total probability over any partition.
5. B and its negation form a partition, so P(C) = P(C|B)·P(B) + P(C|¬B)·P(¬B).
6. An agent who became certain of B should accept "if A, B" outright, so P(C|B) = 1.
7. An agent who became certain of the falsity of B should reject it outright, so P(C|¬B) = 0.
8. Hence P(C) = P(B), and so P(B|A) = P(B) for every A and B.
9. That makes every sentence probabilistically independent of every other, which cannot hold in a language anyone actually speaks.
10. So one of steps 1 to 3 fails: indicatives do not express propositions whose credence is the corresponding conditional probability.

To there I think I have essentially Lewis's own short argument. The move I cannot shake is this one. Steps 4 through 9 appeal to nothing special about conditionals, only to the credence in a sentence being fixed by the agent's conditional credences and shifting appropriately as her evidence shifts. But that is true of every sentence: for any S and any E, the credence in S is a weighted average of the credence in S given E and the credence in S given the falsity of E. So the same reasoning appears to show that no sentence expresses a proposition, which is absurd. I want to know whether that generalization is a real threat to Lewis or simply a bad inference of mine, and if it is bad, why the parallel steps do not license it.

Two further things I would like addressed rather than set aside. First, at steps 6 and 7 I pass from what the agent believes to what she would believe after learning something, and I suspect I have left a premise unstated about the range of credence functions step 1 is supposed to cover; tell me whether step 3 already contains that premise or whether I need it separately. Second, step 8 seems to say the conditional is probabilistically equivalent to its own consequent, which would mean that in properly asserting "if the butler did it, the gardener was in on it" I commit myself to being ready to assert that the gardener was in on it. I feel no such commitment when I assert conditionals, and I want to know whether that is evidence against steps 1 to 3 or against step 8.

If you had to reject a single step, which would it be, and what does rejecting it cost? Compare what the main escapes give up: denying that conditionals are truth-apt at all, restricting step 1 to a narrow class of credence functions, or replacing the conditional with something else in the semantics. If forced to choose, I would rather keep step 1, which strikes me as the best-supported claim in the literature, than keep step 2.
