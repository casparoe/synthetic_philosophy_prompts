I write novels for a living, and in about six weeks I'm giving the annual lecture at a theology faculty's institute for religion and the arts — forty minutes, then ten or so minutes of questions, no podium notes, and a room I'd rather not lose. The talk is going to argue something I actually believe but that is going to sound like a novelist trying to get himself off the hook with a dead bishop's help. Here's the thesis: on Augustine's own account of lying, in On Lying and again in Against Lying, what makes an utterance a lie is that it goes against what the speaker holds in the heart, coupled with a will to deceive, and a figurative signification that the hearer is cued not to take literally is not a lie at all. So making things up for a living isn't lying, because the invented content of a novel is never offered to the reader as something the author believes; the convention of the form is the cue. And I want to push further: the reason Augustine would still be uneasy about fiction isn't its falsity, it's what it does to the loves of the person reading it.

The room is roughly a dozen people: patristics scholars and moral theologians who have read On Lying closely and at least two who work on Against Lying and the Priscillianist controversy, one person who has translated Augustine into English, a couple of literary theorists who are there to be suspicious of me, and the institute director who wants the evening to go well. They will not be hostile in a rude way. They'll be precise. Several of them think modern readers smuggle a will-to-deceive requirement into Augustine that isn't doing the work there, and one at least thinks the classification of the eight kinds settles the matter against me before I've started, since the kind that covers saying false things to please an audience is still a lie and still a sin on his ranking, just a lighter sort of sin.

So: give me my prep sheet for the question period. I want the objections this particular room will actually raise, not the ones a general audience or a book festival would — nobody there is going to ask me whether lying is ever okay in a noble cause, they already know the answer he gives and it's no. Order them by how much damage each one does to the talk if it lands badly. Put each one in the mouth of the sort of person who'd raise it, so I can hear the tone, and keep the version I'd hear short, because in ten minutes nobody gets to make a speech. Then for each: a reply I could actually deliver in twenty or thirty seconds out loud without notes, what I should concede straight away to look honest rather than cornered, what I must not concede because the whole argument collapses without it, and where my reply is softest — the sentence in my answer that a patristics person will hear as hand-waving. I especially want to know which objection has real force and which ones only look devastating, because I have a bad habit of defending against the scary-sounding ones and getting run over by the quiet one.

Two things I'm dreading and want handled explicitly. One: my last novel has a character everyone in my town recognises, drawn from a real person who never agreed to it, and I can see someone asking whether bearing false witness against a neighbour is exactly the case my figurative-signification move can't cover, since the cue tells the reader it's invented but the person recognisable in it is being described. Two: somewhere in an anthology foreword I wrote years ago I called the novel a beautiful lie that everyone consents to, and if anyone in that room has read it, I've undercut myself in print. Tell me the answer to that if it comes up, or tell me to steer the talk so it can't.

And end by naming the single question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that would expose the weakest joint in the whole thing, which I suspect has to do with whether a story that is cued as not-to-be-believed can be supposed to carry truth about God or about anything else, or whether Augustine would say I've bought my innocence by giving up the claim that fiction is about anything.

Please write the whole thing to me as a letter — continuous prose, one piece, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists, no tables, nothing like a slide deck. I read better in sentences. Take the objections one after another in the order you've judged them, and let the structure show in the writing rather than in the formatting, the way you'd write an email to a friend who is about to walk into a difficult room. And don't invent scholarship for me to cite, because I won't have read it and I'll get caught — if there's something I should genuinely go and read before that night, say so in a sentence at the end rather than dressing it up as a quotation.
