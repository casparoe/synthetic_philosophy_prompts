So, no philosophy background past one undergrad course here — I write release policy for a team that ships firmware for infusion pumps, and I've gotten weirdly deep into an argument a colleague (ex-philosophy grad student, now does verification) brought to our internal ethics-of-engineering readign group. Our sign-off rule says an engineer may sign a build for release only if that build is safe. He pointed out that safety isn't the sort of thing you can settle by sitting and reflecting: it depends on how the pumps behave in hospitals we've never seen, on nurse workflows, on drug libraries somebody else configured downstream. Two engineers with identical training, identical evidence, identical internal states could each sign a build, one of them safe and one of them not, and neither could tell which situation they were in. From that he ran an argument he says works equally against the idea that assertion is goverened by a knowledge norm, and it lands somewhere I really don't want to be.

His version, cleaned up. He says the motivation for (1) is that constitutive rules are supposed to be what make a practice the practice it is, and a rule you can't check you're following can't be doing that job:

(1) A rule R is constitutive of a practice only if someone engaged in the practice can, in general and by reflection alone, determine whether a given performance complies with R.
(2) For knowledge: two agents can be internally indistinguishable while one knows p and the other merely believes p truly (Gettier setups, fake-barn county, that kind of thing), so no amount of reflecting settles whether you know p.
(3) So knowledge fails (1) and is not a constitutive rule of assertion.
(4) Likewise for truth: internally indistinguishable agents can differ in whether what they say is true. So truth is not a constitutive rule of assertion either.
(5) Belief is different — to find out whether you believe p you just consider p — so of the usual candidates only belief satisfies (1).
(6) So if assertion has a constitutive rule at all, it is: assert p only if you believe p.
(7) But that rule permits flat-out asserting your lottery ticket will lose, and permits asserting something on a total hunch that happens to be true, and those are bad assertions — bad as assertions, not merely imprudent or rude.
(8) So belief is not the constitutive rule either.
(9) Therefore assertion has no constitutive rule, and all the talk about what you may say is just morality and prudence in a rule-shaped costume.

He also thinks (1) condemns our sign-off rule for exactly the same reason, and that we should rewrite it as "you may sign if you believe, on the evidence available to you, that the build is safe." He's confident that's the right fix; it makes me uneasy and I can't say why.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Which step or steps do you reject, and what is the mechanism — I want to know why the bad step looked fine going in, what it equivocates on or what scope it quietly shifts, not just a label for the fallacy. Then price the rejection: if (1) is the problem, what work was (1) doing that now goes unperformed, and what can the knowledge-norm person say to someone who asks how a rule nobody can verify they're following is a rule at all rather than a mere grading standard? And separately, is the sign-off case actually the same case or does the disanalogy do work — is "sign only if the build is safe" genuinely undermined by his reasoning, or does what happens to that rule tell us something about the argument instead? For what it's worth I'd rather keep (9) false and I'd rather keep our rule as written, but I'm not attached to either if the price is too steep.
