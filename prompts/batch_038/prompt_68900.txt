I spent forty years in a national measurement laboratory, first as an experimentalist and latterly as the person called in when two groups could not reconcile their numbers. I retired eighteen months ago and have taken up philosophy with the seriousness one usually reserves for an unfamiliar instrument. I have now worked through Aumann's result that two Bayesian agents sharing a common prior cannot agree to disagree, together with a fair amount of commentary on it, and I should like to put a claim I find myself repeating in committee rooms to the test.

Consider the following, stated as precisely as I can manage:

(P) When two investigators whom I would sincerely describe as epistemic peers have exchanged their subjective probabilities for some empirical claim on several occasions and nevertheless remain far apart, the persistence of that gap is itself evidence that at least one of them is reasoning irrationally, rather than a mere symptom of their having different evidence or different priors.

I should like a single figure for your credence in (P), between 0 and 1, together with the derivation laid open — not the observation that the answer depends on how one defines one's terms. Please decompose (P) into the sub-questions you regard as load-bearing, assign each a rough probability with a sentence or two of justification, and show me how you combine them. Do not trouble yourself with elegance in the combination; I would rather see an honest structure, and where you think the terms are not independent, say so and say which way the dependence runs.

At minimum I expect the following to appear, though you are welcome to add or drop terms as your judgement dictates:

1. The likelihood that a genuine common prior is in play between two people with decades of divergent experience. I understand the objection that a prior is supposed to encode everything an agent has already learned, so that two investigators who have read different papers and run different experiments may not share one in any sense the result requires.

2. The likelihood that ordinary conversation achieves common knowledge in the strict sense — mutual belief iterated without end — rather than merely mutual belief to some high finite order. I have in mind both the argument that a noisy channel adds only finitely many levels per exchange, and the coordination example in which players settle on one equilibrium when a fact is common knowledge and abruptly on another when it falls one level short.

3. The likelihood that the rationality the theorem's premises demand is something stronger than carefulness: namely, common knowledge that each party conditionalizes correctly on everything privately known to them, rather than merely that neither is careless or self-deceiving.

4. Whether a weakened substitute does the work I need anyway. I understand there is a graded notion in which each agent assigns probability at least p to a proposition, and at least p to the other assigning at least p to it, and so on to arbitrary depth, and in which the admissible spread between two agents' posteriors is then bounded by a function of p. If that is right, my two investigators need not be irrational; they need only be separated by more than their degree of mutual belief permits. Tell me how much of (P) survives in that form, and precisely what is lost.

5. Whether the notion of an epistemic peer can be given content that does not already presuppose what (P) is meant to establish — that is, whether I can call someone my peer without having assumed that they share my priors and my competence at updating.

For each of these, and for anything you add, I want a number and a reason. Then tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to, and what single argument or observation would move it furthest.

Two closing requests. My own credence in (P) is about 0.65; a correspondent of mine who teaches philosophy puts hers at about 0.15. We have been exchanging probabilities for years, which strikes me as a case in point. Given your decomposition, tell me on which term she and I are most likely to be disagreeing, and where a careful statistician who has never encountered this literature would most plausibly sit. And please do not furnish me with references: I want your reasoning, not a reading list, and at this remove I have no means of checking one.
