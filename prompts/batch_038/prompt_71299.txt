I cover neuroscience and the philosophical territory adjacent to it for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the better part of a year reporting on pain: a multidisciplinary clinic, a rehabilitation ward, two families living with rare sensory neuropathies. My editor has now commissioned a companion piece, shorter and more argumentative, on the question my reporting kept colliding with — what exactly makes pain bad for the person who feels it? I have no training in philosophy, but I have accumulated a set of verdicts from the cases I covered, and I would like to know whether they hang together into a principle or whether some of them will have to be sacrificed. I should say at the outset that I am not after a tour of the literature or a roster of positions and their champions; I need the reasoning itself, worked through, in prose I could adapt for readers who have never thought about this at all.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A woman whose stroke damaged part of the insula reports pain accurately — she localizes the pinprick, calls it sharp, rates it high — but says it doesn't bother her, does not withdraw or guard the area afterward, and laughs while describing it. I think she is not suffering anything like the harm I would suffer; yet I am unwilling to say her pain is no bad thing at all. It seems to me a small loss, closer to losing the taste of food while keeping its nutrition than to losing nothing. (2) A girl born unable to feel pain has bitten off the tip of her own tongue, scorched her palms, sustained fractures she never noticed, and picked up corneal infections from scratches she could not feel; her life expectancy is shortened, and her family is weighing having some of her teeth removed so she cannot chew through herself. I think this is a grave harm — and the striking thing is that the harm consists in the absence of pains she has never felt. Whatever I say about case 1 has to explain why the same kind of sensation, when it is missing altogether, is a catastrophe. (3) A postoperative patient on opioids rates his pain seven out of ten and declines further analgesia because, he says, it isn't troubling him. I would leave him alone: the number by itself does not strike me as a reason to intervene. And yet if I were shown two patients alike in every other respect, one at seven and unbothered, one at two and unbothered, I would say the first is somewhat worse off. I hold that ordering with no inclination to act on it. (4) Chosen pains — the tattoo, the marathon, the first stage of labor — seem to me bad moment by moment and yet not bad for the person overall, and not something the person is wronged by undergoing. So my account has to say whether badness at a time simply adds up, or whether something else cancels it. (5) Phantom limb pain, where the limb is long gone, strikes me as exactly as bad as an equally intense pain with a peripheral cause, which tells me the badness cannot lie in the signaling. (6) A person who enjoys pain and seeks it out: not bad for them, and perhaps good. But notice that this and case 1 look alike from the inside — felt pain, no aversion — and my verdicts differ. If a principle must treat the absence of aversion uniformly, one of those two verdicts goes, and I suspect it is the masochist's, though I cannot say why I suspect it. (7) A man given a sedative for a procedure recalls being in pain at the time — the amnesia is imperfect — but afterward does not mind that it happened and would not pay to avoid a repeat. I think he was harmed while it was happening and that his later equanimity does not undo it. Compare case 3, where the unbothered attitude is contemporaneous and does, I think, reduce the harm. My intuitions thus appear temporally biased, and I do not know whether that bias can be principled. (8) A woman with a decade of chronic pain has lost her job and most of her friendships to it, and still refuses an intervention that would abolish it, on the grounds that she would no longer be the person — and the writer — she has become. I think the pain was bad for her and that she is not mistaken to refuse its removal, and I cannot presently say how both can be true.

My tentative principle is that pain is bad for a person in proportion to the degree to which it commandeers attention and agency that would otherwise go to that person's own concerns. It handles 1, 3 and 8 well, and I think it can be stretched to handle 4. But it seems to say nothing about 2, where there is no experience at all to disorganize anything, and I worry that it collapses 6 into 1. So: is there a principle, or a short list of competing candidates, that does better? Please state each candidate precisely enough that I could test an unfamiliar case against it; say which of the eight verdicts it delivers and which it forces me to give up; and identify, for each, the clause that is doing the work on the case that motivates it. Then tell me what your preferred candidate says about cases I have not listed — pain felt in a dream and recalled on waking with relief; the body of a patient under general anesthesia, showing every physiological marker of nociception and no experience at all; an infant; and, if it makes a difference, an animal whose inner life I can only infer from behavior. Finally, I have a suspicion that my list conflates two questions, whether pain is unpleasant and whether pain is bad for the person who feels it; if that is right, I would rather know which question each of the eight verdicts is actually answering, and what becomes of the ones that turn out to be answering both at once.
