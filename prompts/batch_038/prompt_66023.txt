I'm in the second year of a science-communication MA, and the online review attached to my department has commissioned me a 1,200-word sidebar on von Neumann's late writing about the brain and what became of it. I've been leaning heavily on one secondary source — a chapter in a well-regarded popular history of computing — whose account of the book that appeared posthumously as *The Computer and the Brain* runs as follows:

'In his last years von Neumann turned from building machines to asking what they could tell us about the mind, and the answer he reached in the lectures he delivered as Yale's Silliman series, published after his death in 1958, was bracing. Strip away the chemistry, he argued, and the brain is a computing machine: a slow, cheap, massively parallel digital processor whose basic components are cruder than anything in his own machines. A neuron, on this picture, is essentially a switch that either fires or does not, and although individual neurons are unreliable, the system as a whole is far more accurate than the best computers of the 1950s — a feat von Neumann explained by redundancy, since the nervous system routinely deploys several cells where one would do, much the same trick that error-correcting codes exploit. The differences between brain and computer therefore turn out to be quantitative rather than qualitative: speed, size, power consumption, arithmetic precision. He was careful not to claim that any of this settled questions about consciousness, but the architectural moral is the one that endured. If the brain does what a stored-program computer does, the way to understand minds is to build larger and faster machines, and the brain's own coding scheme becomes an implementation detail. For that reason the book is usually counted among the founding texts of artificial intelligence.'

My instinct is that this is broadly fair but rhetorically overconfident, and I'd like to know whether that instinct is right or merely flattering to the author. Two things make me uneasy. Skimming the last two chapters of the book in our library copy, I keep finding von Neumann hedging in ways the summary doesn't reflect, and my supervisor, when I showed her the passage, said only that I should check whether he really thought the nervous system was digital. Second, someone in our reading group remarked that the Silliman lectures were never actually given, which would make the opening sentence wrong about a matter of fact rather than interpretation. I've also been reading about the IAS machine and his work on self-reproducing automata, but the sidebar is meant to stay on the brain, so please don't wander into those except where they bear directly on a claim above.

What I need is a claim-by-claim audit I can act on: take the passage sentence by sentence and tell me which statements a historian of computing or a von Neumann specialist would accept as they stand, which are defensible simplifications that lose something important, and which are misrepresentations. Please address specifically whether the brain-as-digital-processor framing survives his own discussion of the nervous system's mixed character; whether the redundancy point is offered by him as an explanation of the system's superior accuracy or rather raised as a difficulty he does not resolve, and whether anything else is doing explanatory work there that the summary omits; whether the quantitative-rather-than-qualitative thesis is his or the popularizer's; whether treating the nervous system's own code as an implementation detail is consistent with the weight he gives it; and whether the closing attribution to the founding texts of artificial intelligence is fair or an anachronism. Then tell me what I can safely reuse, and give me a two- or three-sentence characterization of his position that a specialist would not wince at.
