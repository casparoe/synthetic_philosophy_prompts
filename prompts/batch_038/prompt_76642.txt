I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student in social epistemology, and my dissertation chapter is on peer disagreement. People in my subfield invoke Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result constantly, as though it settles something, and I can follow the algebra but I still dont have a feel for it. Can you walk me through the conceptual structure? A few things I'm specifically stuck on:

1. The proof version I know takes the finest common coarsening of the two agents' partitions and then sums over the cells sitting inside the event where both agents' posteriors equal some fixed number q. In plain language, which step is doing the actual work, and where exactly does common knowledge — as opposed to mutual knowledge to some finite level — enter? I want to be able to say out loud, in seminar, why you can't just run the same argument with level-3 mutual knowledge and a slightly weaker conclusion.

2. Related: is the move from mutual knowledge at level n, for arbitrarily large n, to full common knowledge a cliff or a continuum? I vaguely remember there being relaxations that degrade gracefully — something where the priors are only ε-close and you get ε-close posteriors, and something where you substitute common p-belief for common knowledge and get a bound on the gap between posteriors that shrinks as p goes to 1. So the theorem looks robust along some dimensions and brittle along the knowledge-hierarchy one. Is that right? And if so, why is that the brittle dimension?

3. What is the common prior assumption actually buying? I've read it treated as a substantive, controversial claim about rational agents, Harsanyi-style, and I've read it treated as a harmless normalization that just stipulates the agents didn't start out disagreeing. Which reading is correct, and does the choice matter for what the theorem is supposed to teach us?

4. Please give me a small concrete example: two agents who each know the other's posterior and who nonetheless disagree, where the disagreement fails to be common knowledge. Walk me through which level of the hierarchy breaks down for which agent and why. I want to use it when teaching and I keep botching the ones I improvise.

5. Why doesn't publicly announcing that we disagree have the same force as announcing our posteriors? I know there's a result that iterated announcement of posteriors converges to agreement in finite state spaces — give me the intuition for why the back-and-forth works, and say whether finiteness is doing something essential there or is just a convenience.

6. And the thing I actually care about: does any of this bear on the conciliationism versus steadfastness debate, or is it orthogonal? My suspicion is its a category error — the peer disagreement literature is about agents with genuinely private evidence and no reason to think they share a prior, whereas Aumann is about agents who share everything except their partitions of the state space. But people cite him in that debate all the time. Is there a version of the result that makes a real connection, or is the connection purely rhetorical?

Finally, I keep seeing bounded rationality offered as the escape hatch: real agents can't compute, so the theorem doesn't apply to us. That feels like a cop-out to me, since the theorem is idealized by design and its point is presumably normative — it's supposed to tell you what you'd have to give up in order to keep disagreeing. Do you agree, and can you state the normative reading precisely rather than as a slogan?

Sorry for the wall of text. No bibliography or list of further reading, please — I just want the ideas explained well.
