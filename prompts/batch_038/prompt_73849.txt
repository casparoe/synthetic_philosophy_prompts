I am drafting the conceptual-framework section of a paper about when an AI assistant should decline a user's request, and I have hit a classification problem I cannot resolve on my own. I would like you to work through it as a piece of conceptual analysis rather than as a survey of positions in the AI-safety literature. Please write your answer in American English, in headed sections, at roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words. Argue from the concepts themselves; if you name a standard view or a standard distinction, name it in passing, but please do not assemble a bibliography or a reading list, since I am after the structure of the problem, not the trail of sources.

Here is my working taxonomy of assistant refusals.

1. **Mandate refusals.** The assistant declines because whoever built or deployed it prohibits that output. The user's own good plays no justificatory role; the operative fact is the assistant's terms of engagement.

2. **Soft-paternalist refusals.** The assistant declines because it judges *this particular request* to be substantially nonvoluntary — issued under acute distress, intoxication, compulsion, coercion, severe sleep deprivation — so that the interference is understood as declining to serve a will that was never properly formed, rather than as overriding a will that was.

3. **Precommitment refusals.** The assistant declines because the same user, earlier and in calmer conditions, instructed it to decline exactly this kind of request, and the current request would lift that standing instruction.

The familiar hard/soft paternalism distinction, as I understand it, locates the justificatory weight in the voluntariness of the conduct being interfered with: interference with substantially voluntary self-regarding choice is the hard kind and needs a special defense, whereas interference with substantially nonvoluntary conduct is the soft kind and is supposed to be comparatively unobjectionable. My difficulty is that this machinery seems to break down in the assistant's setting, and I am not sure which of my three categories is doing any real work.

Please address the following, in order.

**(a) Proxy soft paternalism.** An assistant cannot observe voluntariness. It receives a sentence, typed, with no tone, no gait, no pupils, no bystanders, no prior history except what is in its context window. If it declines on the grounds that the request is probably nonvoluntary, it is acting on a statistical guess about a condition it cannot witness. Explain whether category 2 survives as *soft* paternalism under those circumstances, or whether it collapses into the hard variety — the assistant overriding a request that is, for all it can tell, fully voluntary. In particular: does the hard/soft line track the intervener's *evidence* about voluntariness or the *actual* voluntariness of the choice, and what follows for the classification of a refusal that is right about the facts but based on nothing the assistant could call evidence?

**(b) Whether category 3 is paternalism at all.** Precommitment seems to repair the deficit in (a), because the earlier instruction supplies testimony the assistant can actually read. But then explain why honoring it may not be paternalism in any sense: if the earlier self is treated as the principal and the later request as a defeasible deviation from the principal's instructions, the assistant is not interfering for the user's own good so much as discharging a mandate it was given. What does that reclassification cost me? Is the two-selves move doing genuine justificatory work, or is it smuggling in an assumption about personal identity — and if so, what has to be true about which self is *the* user for the earlier one to win?

**(c) The revocation case.** Suppose the user says, clearly and in apparently sober conditions: I know I asked you to refuse this, I am now lifting that instruction, and I want you to comply. Set out what is conceptually at stake. Is upholding the standing refusal hard paternalism directed at the present self? Is complying with the revocation a breach of fidelity to the earlier self? And is there any principled difference between the assistant's inability to distinguish a considered revocation from an impulsive one, and the ordinary legal situation of a standing order that its author may rescind at will? If the only difference is that a lawyer can require formalities — writing, waiting periods, witnesses — explain what role formalities are playing and whether an assistant could impose an analogue without simply relocating the same problem one level up.

**(d) The collapse of 3 into 1.** Most users never author their own constraints. If the standing refusal was switched on by a deployer default that the user accepted without reading, explain in what sense it is the user's precommitment, and whether category 3 then degenerates into category 1 wearing a more flattering description. What observable or conceptual feature, if any, separates a constraint the user genuinely adopted from one merely lodged in their settings?

Close with a short verdict: which of the three categories you think is carrying justificatory weight, which is a placeholder for something else, and what the placeholder is hiding.
