I'm TAing a phil-of-language seminar in the spring and my prof wants each of us to hand in a one-pager saying where we actually stand before we hit the week on slurs, partly so he can pitch the readings right and partly so people can go after each other's views in section instead of the textbook's. Mine has drifted a lot over the past year and I'm honestly not sure it's coherant anymore, so I'd like a real audit before I hand it in. Here's the list, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

(1) The derogatory charge of a slur isn't part of what a sentence containing it literally says. You can negate it, bury it in the antecedent of a conditional, or report it — "she called him the n-word" — and the offensiveness rides along without ever showing up in the truth conditions. So I want something expressive or implicature-ish rather than building contempt into the proposition.

(2) Relatedly, every slur has a neutral counterpart with the same extension, and a sentence with the slur has the same truth conditions as the one you get by swapping the counterpart in. The whole difference is in the attitude the speaker displays.

(3) But I've also been persuaded by the argument that what fixes the property a slur picks out is the racist ideology of the community that coined it, not any neutral social-scientific facts — and since that ideology is mostly false, nothing actually satisfies the slur. Slur sentences come out false, or maybe gappy, for everybody.

(4) Which seems to fit a case verdict I'm attached to: if a completely unprejudiced person said "that guy is the n-word" about a Black person in a flat, descriptive tone, I'd say they hadn't said something true — even though I'd happily say they said something true with "that guy is Black." Truth here seems to track who's talking, and I like that it makes the slur irreducible to its counterpart.

(5) I also want to insist, against the people who say slurs are just forbidden words with nothing interesting in their semantics, that there IS a semantic story to tell — while at the same time holding that the extra badness of slurring, over and above asserting the counterpart, isn't semantic at all. It comes from the history the word drags along and what it does to hearers whether or not you meant anything by it. Consistent with that: a well-meaning lecturer who quotes a slur to a class has wronged nobody but still has to mark the use off somehow, and I don't see a problem there.

(6) Reappropriation is a big part of why I like (1) and (2): when an in-group member uses the word, the derogatory layer just falls away and the extension stays put.

(7) On method: I want a compositional story, I don't want senses multiplied past necessity, and I'm suspicious of any account that makes slurs a sui generis semantic category with no analogue anywhere else in the language.

So go through this and tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do — I have a nagging feeling I've leaned on the same intuition twice for ends that don't fit, but I can't locate it, and I'm not attached to any particular item so don't worry about sparing my feelings. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad, and for each say what has to be given up and what the account loses once it's gone. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that buys the most consistency and whether it's really as cheap as it sounds. Argue it from the positions themselves, not from who holds what. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which commitment goes, or whether you think the set holds — and then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my list back to me before you start.
