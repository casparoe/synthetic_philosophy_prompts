I work in a policy evaluation unit and our appraisal guidance has been quietly driving me nuts. Here's the setup. The national statistics office asks people four questions, each on a 0 to 10 scale: how satisfied they are with their life overall, whether they think the things they do in their life are worthwhile, how happy they felt yesterday, and how anxious they felt yesterday. Then our guidance takes the life satisfaction number and says: if a policy is expected to move it by some fraction of a point, for some number of people, for some number of years, you multiply those three together and get a count of "wellbeing years". That count is then used to compare a road junction scheme against a bereavement counselling service against an apprenticeship programme against a home insulation grant, all on one ruler, and in some cases it gets converted into money at a fixed rate so it can sit in the same cost-benefit table as travel time saved.

The thing I can't get past is what single quantity is supposedly being added up. One person's rating goes up half a point because a heating subsidy landed, someone else's goes up half a point because their kid is out of prison, and we treat those as the same half point and sum them across the population. And the yesterday question is meant to be catching everything from the pleasure of a hot shower to the pleasure of winning an argument to the pleasure of finally following a proof. Do all of those share one property that comes in degrees, or is the word happy just being stretched over a pile of unrelated states that we've agreed to average?

So what theory of pleasure, or of what makes a life go well, does this machinery actually presuppose? Tell me which bits it strictly requires -- is it committed to wellbeing being a single felt quantity, to one person's 7 being the same amount as another person's 7, to happiness and anxiety sitting on one scale so you can net them off against each other, to the numbers surviving being multiplied by years -- and which bits it could do without. Does the arithmetic need a hedonist account of wellbeing at all, or would some very different account license exactly the same table and exactly the same rankings? I'd also like you to point out where the practice fights with itself: one of the four questions asks about worthwhileness, which doesn't sound like a feeling at all, yet it goes on the same dashboard, and the guidance sometimes treats the four numbers as interchangeable and sometimes treats them as competing. And elsewhere in the same manual we flatly refuse to put a number on certain things and park them in a seperate non-monetised box.

Finally: if it turned out that happy states have nothing in common except the name -- no shared feel, no shared metric, just a bunch of quite different things people are all glad about -- what would concretely have to change here? Which of the calculations survive that discovery and which collapse, and what would a version of this guidance look like that didn't pretend to a single scale?

Bottom line in your very first sentence, then the reasoning behind it. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
