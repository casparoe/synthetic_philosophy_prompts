Our organization is building a diagnostic instrument for groups — a school-board coalition, a hospital quality committee, a professional mailing list, a mutual-aid network — that is meant to say something defensible about how well the group's internal epistemic arrangements are working and to recommend interventions on that basis. At present the instrument rests entirely on an inherited folk distinction: a group is in a *bubble* when it simply fails to hear outside voices, and in an *echo chamber* when it has been taught to distrust them. That is too thin to bear the weight we are putting on it. Before I commission a theoretical grounding, I want to know what a grounding would actually have to supply.

Write the desiderata for a theory of epistemic environments of the kind that could underwrite a group-level diagnostic. For each entry in your list, give: the requirement itself, stated precisely; the case or consideration that motivates it; and its status — non-negotiable for the purpose described above, or a demand imported from some particular research program (say which: individual-level vice epistemology, structural accounts of testimony, formal models of information cascades and herding, or something else). A numbered list is the right format.

The following are candidate requirements I have collected. Include the ones you think earn their place, and tell me explicitly which I should drop and why — part of what I want is a judgment about which of these are real constraints and which are the local prejudices of one camp.

- It must license ascriptions to a group rather than only to its members, and must say what makes such an ascription true when the members differ among themselves.
- It must be operationalizable by someone with access only to observable conduct — who cites whom, who stops posting after a disagreement, which sources members link to and which they never mention — and not to private credences.
- It must separate pathology from healthy specialization. A cardiology department that reads cardiology and ignores generalist journalism is not obviously epistemically sick, and a theory that condemns it is useless to us.
- It must yield differential predictions across interventions: broadening exposure, diversifying sources, inoculating members, and raising the cost of defection should not all come out the same way.
- It must accommodate exit. Groups do leave chambers, and a theory that cannot say what changed cannot tell us what to do.
- Its verdicts must be deliverable to the group itself without functioning as an insult. Our reports go back to the people being diagnosed.

Then: identify any subset of your final list that cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that group-level ascription, purely behavioral operationalization, and non-pejorative verdicts pull against one another, but argue the point rather than assume it, and if a subset is genuinely unsatisfiable, say which member should be surrendered. Next, tell me which single desideratum does the most work in eliminating otherwise plausible accounts, and name two or three test cases — concrete groups sketched in a few sentences each — that any adequate theory must classify determinately, together with the classification you think is correct and your reason for it.

Beneath all of this is one substantive question I would like answered by appeal to your desiderata rather than to intuition. We have been toying with a third category alongside bubble and chamber, for which I have no good name: a group whose members do hear outside sources and do not distrust them, but where dissenting from the group's consensus costs a member standing rather than credibility, so that testimony flows inward normally while disagreement never flows outward at all. Is this a distinct kind, a subtype of the chamber, or a distinction without a difference?

Finally, and briefly — one short paragraph each, no more — say how the three families I expect you to reach for (exposure-based structural accounts, active-undermining accounts, and individual-vice accounts) fare against the list. The list is what I want; the scoring is secondary.
