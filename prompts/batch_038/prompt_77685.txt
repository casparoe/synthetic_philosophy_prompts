**The question (take-home, ~600 words, second of three questions on the paper):**

Suppose a methodologically careful cross-cultural study finds that 74% of respondents in a small-scale horticultural society, and 9% of respondents in a metropolitan United States sample, judge it permissible to break a promise made to a stranger in exchange for a modest personal gain. Take the numbers as stipulated. (a) Does this kind of cross-cultural variation in moral judgment provide support for moral non-objectivism or anti-realism, and if so, what is the supporting inference? (b) What, if anything, does the "WEIRD" critique of psychological research add to or subtract from that debate?

**The student's answer** (paragraph numbers added for your convenience; they are not part of the original):

¶1. The WEIRD critique begins from a sampling problem. Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan pointed out that the overwhelming majority of the subjects in the published psychology literature are drawn from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich and Democratic populations, and in practice from American undergraduates, and that this group is among the least representative samples of humanity one could choose if the aim is to generalize about the species. It is not merely that the sample is narrow; it is that the sample is narrow in the specific dimensions that matter for morality, since WEIRD subjects differ from most other human populations in their individualism, in their analytical rather than holistic cognitive style, in the extent to which their prosociality is directed at strangers rather than at kin and known partners, and in their reliance on guilt rather than shame.

¶2. That gives the stipulated result a second edge. If the American respondents' disapproval of promise-breaking to a stranger is an artifact of a psychology shaped by large anonymous markets and by world religions with moralizing high gods, then it is not a neutral baseline from which to measure the horticulturalists' permissiveness. Both groups are reporting outputs of culturally transmitted learning. It is tempting to say that the horticulturalists' judgment is the culturally loaded one and ours is the default, but the WEIRD literature is precisely the demonstration that there is no default.

¶3. From here the inference to non-objectivism looks strong. If both judgments are fully explained by their distinct cultural etiologies, then neither is tracking a moral fact, and there is no genuine disagreement between the groups to be right or wrong about — only two different normative systems making different noises at the same behavior. The objectivist's escape is to insist that the disagreement is real after all, but the only way to do that is to translate the horticulturalists' judgment into our terms, and once you translate it you have stopped describing their morality and started describing ours.

¶4. The usual replies do not obviously help. It is said that variation in moral judgment can be explained by variation in non-moral factual belief combined with a shared principle — people who think an ancestor will punish a broken promise are not applying a different principle from ours, only a different major premise. But this concedes that what the cross-cultural data actually reveal is variation in principle-application, not in principle-possession, which is a much weaker result than the realist wants, because it leaves the content of the shared principle unexplained by anything other than our own reflective endorsement of it. It is also said that disagreement is no argument against objectivity, since experts disagree about cosmology too. But disagreement in cosmology converges over time under evidence, while moral disagreement across societies does not converge at all; it is stable, and stable divergence is the signature of a domain without a fact of the matter.

¶5. Henrich's own positive account supports this reading. In *The Weirdest People in the World* he traces the distinctiveness of WEIRD psychology to the Protestant Reformation's dismantling of the extended family, and he is careful to make the point that WEIRD moral psychology is therefore not merely different but developmentally ahead of the rest of humanity — more cooperative, more trusting, more impartial. That is an admission that the variation tracks something better and worse, which is just what the non-objectivist denies; so either Henrich is inconsistent or his ranking is a leftover of the very WEIRD self-image his book is meant to deflate.

¶6. One more consideration, which cuts the other way. Curry and colleagues coded ethnographic accounts of sixty societies and reported that the several forms of cooperation they tested were treated as morally good wherever they appeared, and that no society in the sample treated any of them as bad. Universals of that kind are a problem for a crude relativism. But they are not by themselves a problem for non-objectivism, because a universal could be universal for reasons that have nothing to do with truth: if the same cooperative dispositions were installed in every human population by shared evolutionary history, their ubiquity would be exactly what we would expect whether or not any of them were correct. Universality without convergence-under-evidence is compatible with the non-objectivist story, so the finding does not rescue the realist.

¶7. A final point about direction of effect, which the realist sometimes uses. The more a society is integrated into markets, the lower its members' offers tend to be in anonymous one-shot bargaining games, because market norms crowd out communal obligations; that is why the American sample looks harsher on promise-breaking than on comparable breaches inside the family. So the correlation between market integration and moral judgment, which the realist treats as evidence that our norms are simply local norms, is really evidence that markets erode them.
