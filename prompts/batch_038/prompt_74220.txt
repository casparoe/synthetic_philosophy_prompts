I am drafting a position memo for an interdisciplinary working group on institutional design, and the convenor has asked each of us to state our commitments compactly so that the others can attack the set rather than the prose. My framework is built on the idea of a safe Pareto improvement (SPI): a modification of some decision situation — a mechanism, a bargaining game, a program with conditional participation — that, under assumptions every party already accepts about how the situation would be resolved by default (say, that nobody plays a strictly dominated strategy, and that structurally identical sub-games are played identically), guarantees that everyone does at least as well as they would have and that someone does strictly better. The guarantee is meant to hold with certainty, and without any assumption about which equilibrium the default play would have landed on; that is the point of the device, since the parties in the cases I care about lack usable probabilistic beliefs about each other's default behavior. My memo claims that such modifications should be adopted as a matter of course and require no further justification, because no participant can identify a state of the world in which they end up worse off than they would have.

Here is the full list I intend to defend. (1) A change is admissible just in case it is an SPI in the sense above; admissible changes are to be enacted, and their enactment needs no appeal to any further distributive or aggregative principle. (2) I reject interpersonal comparisons of utility altogether — this is a large part of why the device appeals to me, since each party's guarantee is expressed in that party's own units, and unanimity does the work that aggregation would otherwise have to do. (3) I accept Arrow's theorem along with its conditions read as fairness desiderata, and I take it to show that no general procedure for aggregating individual orderings into a social ordering can be defended; my program is therefore a politics of unanimity-based, aggregation-free reform rather than a politics of social welfare functions. (4) Safety is assessed ex ante and with certainty: I am unmoved by the objection that a change might turn out badly for someone, provided the guarantee holds under the shared assumptions about default play. (5) The assumptions that generate the guarantee are not all equally good, though. When a party's default behavior is shaped by adaptive preferences, misinformation, or a poor grasp of the game they are in, I think the safety case should be constructed on a corrected model of their default play — the guarantee should hold for the person they would be if better informed — rather than on the assumptions they would actually endorse. (6) The status quo has no special normative weight in my framework; I do not want it to privilege existing entitlements, and whether a reform is admissible should turn on the strength of the guarantee, not on the fact that it disturbs a baseline that happens to be entrenched. (7) Where several admissible SPIs are available, I want to be able to prefer the one that most reduces inequality of outcome; and where two admissible SPIs can be enacted only one after the other, I hold that enacting both is at least as good as enacting either, since a series of safe steps should never leave anyone worse placed than a single safe step would.

I would like a consistency audit of this set, not a defense of it and not a survey of the surrounding literature — you can work entirely from the commitments as I have stated them. Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest available repair is, together with what that repair costs me elsewhere in the list. Separately, identify any pair that only looks contradictory and explain why it dissolves, since a few of my colleagues have already objected on grounds I suspect are confused. Then name the single revision that would clean up the most damage. I am deliberately not telling you which commitment I would be most willing to abandon, because I do not know, and I do not want the audit steered by a hint. Begin with your bottom line in no more than two sentences, then give the ranked audit; keep the entire response under 700 words and use no citations.
