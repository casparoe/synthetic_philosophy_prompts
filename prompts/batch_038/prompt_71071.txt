I TA a first-year seminar, roughly "How Do We Know Anything About the Past" — 20 students, almost none of them majors, one 75-minute section next Thursday, and the week's topic is archaeological inference: how you get from objects in the ground to claims about what people believed and how they were organised. I want them to run into the problem rather than be told about it, so please design a hands-on activity. Small groups get an assemblage of ordinary modern objects and have to produce a written reconstruction of the people who left it — not just "what was this used for" but the genuinely hard inferences: was this space single- or multi-occupancy, is there any sign of a hierarchy or of rules about who could handle or enter what, and is there evidence of anything ritual or non-utilitarian. Hard constraints: no budget, nothing to buy, nothing to print in bulk (I can do maybe 20 single sheets), objects must be things I can scavenge from my own flat and office or that students bring in, roughly 12–18 items total, physically handleable on tables. Room has four tables, one whiteboard, and AV that has never once worked. Give me the object list, the group task sheet wording, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

Here's the bit I actually want the whole thing to turn on, and it must not be announced: I plan to give two of the groups identical objects but different provenance stories — something like "bagged off a desk the morning its owner moved out of the building" versus "swept from a shared lounge bin at the end of term" — and a third group gets the same objects with no provenance at all. Then partway through I want to quietly add one item to each table and remove a different one, so that later they discover their assemblage was never a sealed snapshot and that some of their most confident claims about status or belief were leaning on an object that wasn't part of the original deposit. Design the sequence of moments: what they're doing in the first fifteen minutes, when and how the cross-group comparison happens (I want the reveal to be that the same objects licensed flatly incompatible confident stories, and I want the friction to come from the comparison itself rather than from me saying the word "interpretation"), and when the disturbance surfaces so it lands as their discovery and not as my gotcha. Also tell me how to get groups to commit in writing to specific claims early enough that they can't quietly revise them later — that's the mechanism the whole thing depends on and I can't figure out how to do it without it feeling like a test.

Then the debrief, which is where I'm weakest. I want it to map particular moments in what just happened onto the concepts — equifinality, the Pompeii premise, how much weight analogy and ethnoarchaeological comparison can bear, and the difference between saying an object's function is underdetermined versus saying its social meaning is — but they shouldn't hear any of those terms until the last ten minutes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong and how to handle it: my guesses are that one group refuses to speculate and just writes a description, somebody says "well, look for writing or labels, that settles it," the item swap goes completely unnoticed because nobody is tracking their own pile, and the students treat it as a parlour game and are unfazed by the contradictions. And be straight with me about which feature of real archaeological inference this activity flatly cannot reproduce and I'll just have to supply verbally — I assume it's time depth and the fact that an actual site can be excavated further, but tell me if there's something worse I'm not seeing.
