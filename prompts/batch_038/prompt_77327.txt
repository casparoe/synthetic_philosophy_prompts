I am the staff lead for a two-year working group convened by our county board (roughly 300,000 residents, unremarkable in every respect that matters here). The group was chartered after a botched bond measure, and a private foundation has offered to fund a pilot in which two narrow classes of decision would be settled partly by Hanson-style conditional prediction markets: the annual allocation of our road-maintenance capital budget among four competing repair strategies, and the approval or rejection of two proposed large mixed-use developments. The design on the table is the familiar futarchic one. The board would legislate the value side — a composite index of county well-being, defined and measured after the fact — and speculators would supply the belief side, betting on the index conditional on each candidate policy being adopted, with the conditional legs implemented as called-off bets so that traders can express the comparison rather than the two forecasts separately. Whichever policy carries the higher conditional price is adopted automatically, without a further vote. Three of our eleven members have read the same four or five essays and now argue in circles: one holds that the whole thing founders because the market is asked to price a policy path that will never be observed; another holds that the only real problem is democratic legitimacy and that everything else is plumbing; a third holds that both are downstream of liquidity, since a market with forty thousand dollars of open interest in a small county is just a rumor with a price. Our next session is in three weeks and I want to know what we should be demanding of any such proposal before I let anyone's intuition win.

So: write the desiderata. I want the full specification of what a theory of governance by prediction market has to deliver to count as an adequate answer to the problem we are facing, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and with a clear verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable given our purpose (a small jurisdiction, low liquidity, high public visibility, decisions whose effects are measured in years rather than weeks), which are negotiable, and which are not requirements at all but the settled prejudices of one camp — mechanism designers, calibrationists, welfare economists, or theorists of legitimacy — in which case say whose. I would like you to address at least the following, and to tell me plainly if any of them are pseudo-desiderata: the counterfactual leg of a conditional market, given that the road not taken is never observed; the fact that the resolution variable in our pilot would be computed by the same county budget office whose decisions are being bet on; the way a market weights beliefs by bankroll, illustrated by a local landowners' association that has already announced it will raise a quarter of a million dollars to bet against one of the two developments irrespective of its own analysis; horizons longer than any trader's holding period, as with the twenty-five-year sewer extension one of the repair strategies implies; the reflexive case in which the published price changes the outcome it forecasts, and what counts then as the market having been right or wrong; the requirement of joint coherence across many simultaneous markets on correlated policies; and what the mechanism is obliged to do when it is thin or being manipulated, including whether any fallback rule can itself be gamed. Then identify the largest subset of requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied and argue for it — if the list turns out to rule the project out, that is the finding I need, not a disappointment. Identify also the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate designs. Finally, in no more than a page, say how three broad approaches fare against your list: pure futarchy in which the market decides, a hybrid in which the price is one input to a body that retains an override, and the deflationary view on which prediction markets are only ever instruments for aggregating forecasts and have nothing distinctive to contribute to governance.

Please present the requirements as a single Markdown table with exactly these columns: id (D1, D2, and so on); desideratum (one sentence, phrased as a demand on the theory); motivating case (the concrete scenario or consideration that makes it a requirement, one to three sentences); status (one of non-negotiable, negotiable, camp-specific); camp (which camp treats it as mandatory; leave blank unless status is camp-specific); and conflicts (the ids of any requirements you think cannot be satisfied jointly with this one, or blank). If a case I have not raised forces a requirement I have not thought of, add it as a row and mark it as your addition in the motivating-case column. After the table, give three short headed prose sections: "Largest unsatisfiable subset," "Most exclusionary requirement," and "Three approaches against the list." Write in American English, and do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I cannot check references offline and would rather come to the literature after I know what to look for.
