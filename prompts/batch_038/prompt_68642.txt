The question, as it appeared on the assignment sheet, was this:

**Question 2 (50 points; suggested length 700–900 words).**
(a) State Lewis's best-systems account of lawhood, and explain what it adds to the simple claim that laws of nature are true universal generalizations.
(b) Armstrong holds that a law of nature is a relation of necessitation holding between universals. Explain what this claim adds to the Humean picture, and what Armstrong takes it to cost.
(c) Which of the two accounts better explains the fact that laws support counterfactuals? Justify your answer.

The student's answer, reproduced in full and unedited:

> Lewis's account, usually called the best-systems account, is a refinement of the regularity theory of laws. On his view a law of nature is a true universal generalization over the mosaic of local matters of particular fact — the complete distribution of particular, local qualities across space and time. This thesis is what Lewis calls Humean supervenience: everything there is in the world supervenes on that mosaic, and laws are among the things that so supervene. What makes the account an improvement on a crude regularity theory is that Lewis does not count every true generalization as a law. Instead, laws are those generalizations that appear in the deductive system of truths that best balances simplicity against strength. A system that simply lists every particular fact would be maximally strong but unusably complex; a system that says almost nothing would be beautifully simple but nearly empty. The laws are the theorems of the best compromise between the two. On this view the difference between a law and a mere accident is largely a matter of scope: a genuine regularity is one that holds right across the mosaic and so earns its place in the system, whereas a coincidence covers too few cases to be worth the cost in simplicity.
>
> Three consequences are worth noting. First, the direction of explanation runs from the mosaic to the laws: it is the pattern of particular facts that makes the laws true, not the other way round, and Lewis accepts this direction without thinking it a defect. Second, lawhood on this account is a global matter — nothing about a small region of the mosaic settles what the laws are, since a generalization's status depends on the whole history of the universe. Third, because the account is purely descriptive, laws carry no modal force of their own. They support counterfactuals only because counterfactuals are analysed in terms of similarity between possible worlds, and similarity is partly a matter of sharing laws.
>
> Armstrong rejects the first and third of these. For him a law of nature is not a generalization at all, true or false, but a singular state of affairs in which a relation of necessitation, N, holds between two universals: it is a fact about F-ness and G-ness, not a fact about all the Fs. Because N is a real relation obtaining in the world rather than a way of describing how the world happens to be, Armstrong is committed to the necessity of laws: once N(F, G) obtains it is metaphysically necessary that all Fs are Gs, and the law holds in every possible world in which F and G exist. This is what he means by saying that laws govern rather than summarize, and it is why he thinks his account can explain the truth of counterfactuals where Lewis's can only restate it.
>
> The cost, which Armstrong concedes, is that N does no further explanatory work: it is posited to explain regularities but is itself unexplained, and it cannot be observed. His reply to the second half of this is that we infer laws from the regularities they produce, an ordinary inference to the best explanation rather than a claim to see necessitation happening.
>
> On (c), I think Armstrong is better placed. Lewis must analyse counterfactuals by appeal to similarity, similarity by appeal to shared laws, and laws by appeal to the mosaic whose regularities the counterfactuals were supposed to describe, which looks circular; Armstrong has a single source of modality and no such loop. Against that, Lewis's view is far more parsimonious and does not require belief in universals, so the choice between them may be partly a matter of temperament rather than argument.
