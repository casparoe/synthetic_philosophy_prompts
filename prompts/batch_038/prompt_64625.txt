My flatmate is halfway through a philosophy minor and he reckons he's broken thought experiments. I've written it in his order as best I can, becuase he insists each step is just something I'd already agreed to over the previous one.

1. When somebody says "imagine this", the claim they're leaning on is: if you can coherently picture that p — as much detail as you like, nothing contradictory turning up — then p is metaphysically possible, barring some specific reason to think your imagining went wrong.
2. That caveat is only worth having if it's usable from the inside. If there's no way, while you're doing the imagining, to tell whether yours is one of the bad ones, then the caveat rescues nothing: you can never say of a given act of conceiving that it shows something is possible.
3. There are bad ones. I can picture in complete detail the paper announcing that water isn't H₂O after all — the failed replications, the retracted textbook chapters, the awkward new vocabulary — and nothing in what I picture contradicts anything else in it.
4. But if water is H₂O, it's necessarily H₂O. So what I just pictured isn't possible.
5. And there's no mark on the bad ones. That imagining felt exactly as clean and fully specified as any imagining that's supposed to reveal a real possibility, including the ones I'd want to defend.
6. So nobody ever knows, of a particular conceiving, that it reveals a possibility.
7. Which generalises. Frictionless planes, ideal gases, perfectly rational agents, the veil of ignorance, trolley cases, brains in vats, Twin Earth, the zombie, and the counterfactuals in epidemiology and in history ("had the schools stayed open ...") all trade on the move from "I can coherently suppose it" to "it could have been that way".
8. So all of them establish something about us — what we'll accept saying — and nothing about the world.

He also offered me a case of his own so I couldn't dodge by saying chemistry is special: he can picture, in just as much detail, finding out that the woman he's always called his grandmother isn't one — records wrong, his mother adopted, nobody told anybody — and if where he comes from is fixed by where his mother comes from, and hers by hers, then that picture is of something impossible too. I notice that one has an "if" in it that the water one doesn't, and he says that's beside the point.

I'd much rather give up (8) than (1). I don't want to stop doing this kind of thing, and I don't want to be committed to saying every idealisation in physics is really a report about physicists. Also, he spent twenty minutes walking me through imagined cases to get me to (6), which struck me as awkward given where we landed, but I can't tell whether that's an objection or just a gotcha.
