I co-host a forty-five-minute podcast about the working practice of grantmaking; our listeners are mostly program officers, nonprofit development staff, and a substantial number of graduate students trying to decide whether to go into funding. Next month's guest is a senior officer at a mid-sized foundation who has spent a decade defending one particular way of scoring candidate cause areas: each area receives a whole-number rating from one to five on importance, on tractability, and on neglectedness, and the three ratings are multiplied together to yield a single figure used to sort a shortlist. She is thoughtful and well liked, and she is also unusually good at absorbing criticism by redescribing her own method — her habitual reply to a hard question is some variation on the thought that the rubric exists to discipline a conversation among colleagues rather than to compute an answer, and that anyone who treats the product of three ratings as a quantity with units has already misunderstood it.

I want nine interview questions that make that redescribing move progressively more expensive to keep using, and I want them grounded in the actual content of the rubric rather than in generic skepticism about quantification. Please include at least one question on each of the following, though you may combine or reorder them: whether neglectedness quietly assumes diminishing returns to additional effort, and what happens to the score if a field instead exhibits increasing returns, complementarity, or momentum that only tips past a threshold; whether neglectedness is in truth a claim about other funders' likely responses rather than a property of the cause area, and whether it is therefore indexed to the wrong agent — the world's funders in general instead of her foundation's marginal dollar; what multiplying the three dimensions, as opposed to adding them or ordering them lexicographically, commits her to about their substitutability and about the fact that a product is dominated by its least reliable factor; how importance can be compared at all across cause areas whose outcomes are counted in incommensurable units; how a tractability rating issued in advance of the work is to be distinguished from a rationalization assembled afterward; and one question asking her to walk through a case where the rubric produced a ranking she thought was wrong and to say what she actually did about it.

For each question, give me the pressure point it aims at in a single sentence, the answer she is most likely to give, and a follow-up keyed specifically to that anticipated answer rather than to the question itself. Then close the sequence with a final question built to foreclose the heuristic retreat — something that asks what the rubric has caused her organization to do that it would not otherwise have done, or what observation would oblige her to abandon it — together with a candid note on how she might still wriggle out of that one. Finish by telling me which two questions you would cut if we run short on time, and flagging any of the nine where you expect an audience of practicing program officers to side with her instead of with me.
