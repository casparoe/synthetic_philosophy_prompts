You are a philosopher with serious training in metaethics, philosophy of religion, and the empirical study of moral judgment and wellbeing. I want you to treat the question below as a question about how the world actually works, not as an exercise in exegesis or in the history of ideas. Think carefully before you answer; the deliverable is a number together with the machinery that produces it.

Set aside rebirth, cosmic bookkeeping, and any supernatural enforcement. I am interested in a deliberately deflated, single-lifetime version of the karma thesis. Call it WNK:

> In a world with no supernatural moral governance, an individual's morally virtuous character and conduct make it substantially more likely, through ordinary natural causal pathways, that she will fare well in her own life — where "fare well" means her wellbeing as she herself would judge it, not merely the esteem of others — and this tendency is strong and reliable enough that the world could fairly be described as paying virtue its wages.

Three features of WNK are load-bearing, and I want you to engage with each rather than dissolve the thesis into one of them. First, the claim is causal, not merely correlational: virtuous conduct has to make a difference, not just travel alongside a difference made by something else. Second, the payoff is the agent's own wellbeing, not reputation as such; if virtue pays only by being *seen* to pay, that is a much weaker and much more fragile claim. Third, the phrase "strong and reliable enough" is deliberately underspecified, and I think that vagueness is doing real work in ordinary disputes about karma.

Please do the following, in order.

1. Begin with a short brainstorm. Enumerate the readings WNK might be given — at minimum a constitutive reading on which virtue partly constitutes wellbeing rather than causing it, an instrumental-social reading that runs through reputation and partner choice, an instrumental-psychological reading that runs through the agent's own perception of her circumstances, and a cross-lifetime reading that I have excluded — and say in one line each which of them you think the concept of karma is actually trying to express.

2. List the sub-questions your credence depends on. For each, give a probability and one or two lines of justification. I would expect at least these: whether desert is the sort of relation the world could satisfy or violate at all; how much of the apparent support for WNK is explained by the just-world bias (Lerner) in observers and in the agents themselves; whether the feedback channels that reward virtue are mostly social and therefore hostage to how virtue is *perceived*; and what threshold of reliability the phrase "strong and reliable enough" requires, with your credence conditional on a low threshold and on a high one.

3. I want you to include one further term that I suspect is decisive and that I cannot quite assess myself. Call it the testimonial contamination problem: the evidence that virtuous people fare well reaches us largely through witnesses who have a stake in seeing virtue rewarded, so the data supporting WNK and the data supporting the just-world bias are generated by the same contaminated source. Assign a probability that this contamination accounts for most of the intuitive pull of WNK, and say whether you think the problem is independently testable at all.

4. Combine. Show the arithmetic explicitly — state whether you are multiplying, taking a weighted average, or doing something else, and say why that is the right form of combination rather than an informal gesture. Give one final number and a range you would defend.

5. Identify the sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive, and say roughly how far the number would move if that sub-question resolved against you.

6. Say which single empirical result or philosophical argument would move your number most, and what it would move it to.

7. Flag where you think your number is most likely to be systematically wrong — too high or too low — and why.

For calibration: my own credence in WNK is about 0.4, arrived at without much confidence. Tell me which sub-question you think accounts for the gap between your number and mine.

A note on format: I do not want references, a bibliography, or a reading list, and you should not hedge by observing that the answer depends on one's theory of wellbeing without then saying which theory you are assuming and how that assumption affects the number. Do not answer with a refusal to commit. Give me the number and the derivation.
