I'm in a philosophy-of-physics reading group and one of the members — call him Dave — has this tic where any question about information gets answered with "information is physical," full stop, like that settles it. It came up three seperate times last week and each time the slogan seemed to be doing something completely different. First, someone asked whether a demon-style device could dodge the second law if it just never reset itself and kept an ever-growing notebook instead; Dave said no, because erasing a bit necessarily dumps heat into the environment, and that's what the slogan means. Second, someone pointed out that reversible computing is supposed to let you run a computation with no minimum dissipation per step, and Dave said that's beside the point since the principle is about erasure and not about steps — but then he also said the deeper meaning is that information-processing as such always carries a thermodynamic price, which sounds like he walked the first thing back. Third, near the end of the meeting he used it to endorse the it-from-bit picture, that the world is literally made of information and physics falls out of it, which is... the other direction entirely? Now information is the fundamental stuff rather than something physical systems happen to carry.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that "information is physical" could be standing for, starting from the most deflationary reading I can think of (whatever exists, exists physically, and information is always carried by or is about a physical state, so of course it's physical) and working up through the stronger ones about erasure costs and about what's ontologically basic. For each reading I want a verdict: trivially true, substantive but defensible, genuinely contested, or flat false — and, this is the part I actually care about, whether that reading would support the move Dave was making with it. So: which version does the demon argument need, the weak one or the strong one? Does the erasure-cost claim actually follow from statistical mechanics, or is it an extra assumption somebody has to put in by hand, and does the answer shift depending on which reading of the slogan you adopt? And is there any single reading that could do all three jobs at once, or is he equivating between at least two theses that can't both be held?

I'd like it as a list I can print out and bring next week, each item with a one-line verdict under it. Don't bother with references or citations — I care about the arguments, not the bibliography — but please be concrete about the physics where it matters, e.g. where the kT ln 2 figure comes from, what kind of process it applies to, and whether it applies to a bit that's already correlated with something else the agent knows.
