Its centerpiece is a passage on Fitch's paradox of knowability, which I'll reproduce below. I can follow the argument step by step and it feels airtight, but a friend of mine who works on epistemic logic read the same passage and said only that it was sloppy in a way that matters, then declined to explain on the grounds that I'd learn more by auditing it myself. I can't look anything up while I work through this, so I'm not after references or a bibliography -- just the analysis.

Here is the passage, quoted in full: 'Frederic Fitch's short 1963 paper contains one of the most devastating results in twentieth-century logic. It begins from the anti-realist's central commitment: every truth is knowable, meaning that if a statement is true, then it is possible for someone to know it. Fitch then asks what happens if there is so much as one truth that nobody knows. Take that truth and call it p. Since p is true and unknown, the compound claim that p is true and nobody knows it is itself a truth. But then, by the anti-realist's own principle, that compound claim must be knowable: someone could come to know that p is true and that nobody knows it. Knowledge, however, is factive -- you cannot know something false. So if anyone came to know the compound claim, then p would be true and nobody would know it, while the very knower in question would know it. That is a contradiction, so the compound claim is not knowable after all -- which flatly contradicts the principle we started from. Therefore some truths are unknowable in principle. And here is the kicker: running the reasoning the other way shows that if every truth is knowable, then every truth is already known. The anti-realist is left with two options, both fatal: either accept that we are all omniscient and there are no unknown truths whatsoever, or concede that certain truths lie beyond the reach of any possible knower. Since we are plainly not omniscient, verificationism is dead. Note finally how little the argument depends on the peculiarities of knowledge. Substitute provable, or verifiable, or discoverable for knowable, and the very same reasoning shows that truth outruns proof in general -- which is precisely what Gödel established from within arithmetic.'
