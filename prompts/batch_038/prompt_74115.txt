I teach an upper-level seminar on personal identity — twelve students, mostly seniors, all of them bright and all of them dangerously good at name-dropping. For the final I want one essay question built out of Dennett's "Where Am I?" (the brain-in-the-vat-in-Houston one, with the body he dubs Hamlet and the brain he dubs Yorick, and that output switch on the side of the vat that drops him when you flip it). They'll have read the story and nothing else, and they've had Parfit on fission in week four, so the question has to be one they can't pass by reciting the brain-criterion-vs-body-criterion debate or by saying "well, this is just fission again."

Here's the twist I've been kicking around and want you to build the question out of. Suppose that after the operation the Houston lab starts keeping a complete state-by-state record of Yorick's activity — every transceiver reading, continuously logged. They can feed that log into a second vat-grown brain, a duplicate, and run it with a fixed ten-minute delay, wired to a second, prosthetic body that sits in the next room. So at any given moment there are two live systems: Yorick, receiving and transmitting in real time, and the delayed copy, running exactly what Yorick ran ten minutes ago. Both are interviewed at the same time by a grad student with a clipboard. The delayed copy answers every question the way Yorick answered it ten minutes earlier, which means it can't answer a novel question at all — ask it something new and you get whatever Yorick said to whatever was asked ten minutes back. It has its own output switch too, and flipping it off doesn't disturb anything the narrator reports.

The question you write should force the students to work out what this case does to the psychological-continuity criterion, given that there is no branching moment here — no fission event, just a permanent, fixed offset — so the usual non-branching clause has nothing obvious to grab onto. And it should force them to say what "I" picks out when the delayed copy says "I," and whether the narrator's inability to think himself into the vat (which Dennett makes alot of) is evidence about the metaphysics or just evidence about the limits of introspection. I'd like the question to be answerable in 75 minutes, capped at 1200 words, worth 25 marks.

So please give me:

1. The exam question itself, self-contained — the variant scenario has to be described inside the question text well enough that a student who's read the story but never heard of the case can get going.

2. A model answer of about 700 words that actually takes a position and defends it, rather than surveying the options. It should say explicitly why the offset case is harder or easier than standard fission, not just note that it's different.

3. A marking scheme as a markdown table with exactly these columns, in this order:

   - `criterion` — short name for what's being assessed
   - `marks` — how many of the 25 this row is worth (they should sum to 25)
   - `A_move` — the specific thing a first-class answer does here
   - `B_move` — what a solid-but-unremarkable answer manages instead
   - `loses_marks_when` — the concrete mistake, phrased as something a student would actually write

   Five or six rows. I want the A/B seperation to turn on moves, not on whether they used the right vocabulary.

4. Two or three sentences on how I should handle the student who argues the whole question is malformed — that "where am I?" has no determinate answer and the exam question is asking for one anyway. Is that a full-credit answer, a zero, or something in between, and what would tip it either way?
