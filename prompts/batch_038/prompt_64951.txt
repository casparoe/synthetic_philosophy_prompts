I am two years into a joint program in legal philosophy and public international law, and I have spent this term going in circles on a question in which two distinct complaints are almost always fused into one. When people attack the International Military Tribunal they seem to mean either or both of the following, and they rarely say which: that the Charter criminalized conduct — above all aggressive war — that was not an individual crime under any positive law in 1939, and that the tribunal was constituted, staffed, and jurisdictionally bounded by the victors, so that comparable Allied conduct (the area bombing of cities, the atomic attacks, the Soviet share of the Katyn killings and of the 1939 partition of Poland) was structurally unchargeable. The Dönitz episode is the clearest symptom of the conflation: the tribunal took notice, through answers to interrogatories from an American admiral, that the United States had waged unrestricted submarine warfare in the Pacific, and declined to assess his sentence on that count — which looks like a court conceding the tu quoque objection at the level of sentencing while refusing it at the level of law. At Tokyo a dissenting Indian judge held that the aggression counts were ex post facto and would have acquitted every defendant; the Nuremberg judgment on the law of the Charter, by contrast, treated nullum crimen sine lege as a general principle of justice rather than a limit on sovereign legislative power, and then reasoned that a treaty renouncing war as an instrument of national policy must be read as making those who plan and wage such a war criminals. I have decided to stop arguing about whether these tribunals were legitimate and instead write down, in advance, what any account of legitimate retroactive criminalization in this setting would have to deliver. My purpose is deliberately forward-looking rather than antiquarian: as a seminar exercise I am drafting the statute of a standing international criminal court whose founders know at the moment of drafting that they may one day sit in the defendants' chairs, and who want a statute they would still accept from that chair. Adequacy is relative to that task, and I would rather the list be shaped by it than by the 1945–46 facts, though those facts are the minefield it has to get me across.

So I want the specification itself. Give me a numbered list of desiderata, each entry with three parts: what the theory must do; the concrete case or consideration that generates the requirement — drawn from Nuremberg, from Tokyo, or from a case you stipulate yourself, but specified in enough detail that I could hand it to someone else and they would know what verdict to look for; and a mark, saying whether the requirement is non-negotiable, negotiable (with the cost of giving it up), or in fact a demand belonging to one camp only (in which case say whose, and say what that camp is smuggling in). I would like the list to cover at least the following, and I expect you to add whatever I have missed: the notice the individual defendant is owed, and whether it is notice that the conduct is wrong, that it is illegal, or that it is punishable by this court, and whether those three can come apart; whether retroactivity and selectivity are separable problems or two faces of one problem, i.e. whether a jurisdictional hook that excludes the founders' own conduct is a defect in the law or merely a fact about power; whether pre-jurisdictional atrocity must be reachable — the tribunal found itself unable to declare the persecution inside Germany before September 1939 a crime against humanity because it could not tie it to any crime within its jurisdiction — and if reachability is required, what it costs; whether the anti-retroactivity concern attaches to the definition of the offense or also to the rules for attributing it to a person (heads of state, subordinates acting on orders); and what symmetry could even mean here, given that a court able to try everyone tries no one unless somebody has won. Mark clearly which entries you think are genuine requirements of justice and which are artifacts of a domestic criminal-law model being applied where it does not fit; that sorting is half of what I am after.

Two further things, and they matter more than the list's tidiness. First, tell me which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work — the one that, once you insist on it, rules out the largest number of otherwise attractive accounts. Second, tell me whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that (a) non-retroactivity as against the individual, (b) reach over the founders as well as the defeated, (c) the political possibility of creating the court while the defeated are still occupied and their consent is unavailable, and (d) a punishment that expresses condemnation rather than mere quarantine cannot all be had, and that the choice among them is the substance of the debate rather than a distraction from it. If you agree, say which one you would drop and defend the drop; if you disagree, produce the subset you think is unsatisfiable instead. An impossibility result is a perfectly good answer, and it is the part I most want to see — do not soften it into a list of trade-offs. Finally, briefly and only briefly, score the main contenders against the specification: a legality-first view on which the Charter was simply invalid legislation and the aggression convictions therefore unjust; a view on which conduct so extreme that no valid legal order could authorize it was already criminal; a Kelsen-style view on which retroactive penal legislation is a price worth paying because the alternative is impunity and because the trial can still seed a permanent court; a fair-warning view keyed to what the defendant actually knew and was positioned to know; and a political-legitimacy view of the kind associated with Shklar's treatment of political trials, on which the legal form does normative work of its own even where the underlying legislation is defective. Two or three sentences each, no more. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I want your own reconstruction of the requirements, not references I would have to chase.
