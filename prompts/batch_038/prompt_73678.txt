I sit on the seven-person volunteer committee of a community garden association, and about two hundred members receive the quarterly newsletter we produce. I am writing about an argument I was sitting in the middle of at our last meeting, and I cannot stop turning it over, partly because the argument was about something I had done and partly because I have to speak again on Thursday and still do not know what the thing to say is.

Here are the facts. One of our members, whom I will call Ruth, handles the newsletter. For the second time in a year she missed the printer's deadline, and — the part that mattered more to me — she told nobody. The issue went out three weeks late, we lost our slot and paid a rush fee, and a local business that had paid for an advertisement had it printed in the wrong issue. Ruth replied to the whole committee the following day to apologize and mentioned that she had been at the hospital with her mother; I knew her mother was ill when I wrote. What I wrote was an email to the full committee saying that the lateness was understandable but the silence was not: that failing to tell anyone is what converts a mistake into an insult, because it takes away everyone else's chance to help or to plan around it, and that if you cannot meet a commitment you owe people a word in advance. The last line of the email said that I had done much the same thing the year before and that it had been wrong of me.

Two other members, Nadia and Peter, then spent twenty minutes arguing — not about Ruth, but about whether I had any business sending it.

Nadia's opening move was that eleven months earlier I had let our liability insurance lapse. I did not notice until a member mentioned a delivery van on site, by which time we had been uninsured for ten days. Our treasurer found out, fixed it, and said nothing to the committee; I also said nothing. Nadia says a person who did that, and got away with it, is in no position to tell Ruth that silence is the real offence. She adds that the two lapses are not even of comparable weight — ten days without insurance is graver than a late newsletter — so whatever force the comparison has runs against me rather than for me.

Peter answered that on Nadia's rule no one could ever criticize anyone, since nearly everyone has broken a commitment and kept quiet about it at some point, and that her rule makes the truth of what I said beside the point. The silence did cost the association money and time, and we are worse off if the only people permitted to say so are those with spotless records. He also noted that I had volunteered my own lapse in the same email.

Nadia said that volunteering it makes things worse rather than better, since it turns the email into a display of having improved, and that the reason I never answered for the insurance is not that I owned up but that the treasurer cleaned it up and nobody asked. On her account the difficulty is not simply that I share the fault; it is that I am pressing on Ruth a demand that I managed to avoid having pressed on me.

Peter replied that Nadia was running two questions together: whether what I said was true, and whether I was the right person to say it. He granted the second might be arguable but insisted the first settles what anyone should actually do, and that if a true criticism can be switched off by pointing at the critic's past, then anyone in the wrong has a permanent escape route — find somebody in the room with a comparable stain. He said the charge of a double standard is a dodge, and treating the dodge as decisive simply hands a winning move to whoever actually did the damage.

Nadia said he still was not hearing her. She agreed the email was true and that Ruth already knew it was true; that was precisely her point, because the email was not sent to tell Ruth anything but to make her feel the weight of the failure in front of the entire committee. The same sentences would be one thing coming from the member who has never missed a deadline and quite another coming from me, and the difference is not in the sentences. Blame, she said, is a kind of authority over a person, and it is the sort of thing one can lose.

Peter said that if that is the standard, the only people left holding any authority are the saints and the self-deceived, and those best placed to warn against a mistake — the ones who made it and know exactly what it costs — are barred from speaking. He said the association's best protection against a repeat of last quarter is a culture in which anyone, the previously lapsed included, can name a problem plainly.

At the end they turned to me and asked whose side I was on, and I discovered that I did not know. So: please work through the exchange and tell me who is right at each step. Give me your overall verdict on which of the two has the better of it in the opening paragraph, then take the moves in the order I have set them out, saying for each whether it succeeds, fails, or lands somewhere in between and why. I am not asking whether Ruth was in the wrong — I believe she was, and both of them grant it — and I am not asking for advice about Thursday. What I want is the argument about my email settled move by move, together with whatever Nadia and Peter are both overlooking while they fight about me.

One request about how you write this back to me: plain continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables.
