I work as the sole nurse practitioner at a rural clinic about two hours from the nearest city. Our regional health authority issues a cardiac risk calculator that every clinic in the region is required to use: you enter age, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking status, and a handful of other fields, and it returns a patient's probability of a serious cardiac event within the next ten years. For a patient like the woman I will call Mrs. A, it returns six percent. The authority has audited the instrument repeatedly, and the audits consistently report that of the patients previously scored at six percent, close to six percent went on to have such an event. By the standards the authority recognizes, the figure has earned its standing.

Three things are pulling against it, and I cannot find a principled way to rank them.

First. I have been Mrs. A's clinician for eleven years, and her presentation this spring does not resemble that of the other six-percent patients I have known. I could not write this into a chart note; there is no field for it, and if I were cross-examined I could not name the feature that concerns me. My medical director's position is that a clinician's unease should not move anyone off an audited figure unless the clinician can identify a specific variable the instrument has failed to capture, and that otherwise the figure is the only thing in the room entitled to be called a probability.

Second. When I went looking for the records the calculator was fitted on, I found that they come overwhelmingly from the urban teaching hospitals. The population here differs in ways I can list: agricultural labor, a different ancestral mix, no emergency department within forty minutes, ambulance response averaging better than an hour. So far as I can determine, patients like mine were effectively absent from the data. The authority's documentation acknowledges the gap and instructs that the figures be applied to outlying districts with care, without saying what that care consists in.

Third. Mrs. A's brother had a fatal event at fifty-one. The calculator does not ask about siblings.

From the inside, the first and second worries look like the same sort of thing — my doubts about a number — yet my director dismisses one out of hand and considers the other serious enough to have ordered a review. The third is different again, and I cannot say how or why.

I am not asking for clinical guidance. I know what the treatment guidelines recommend and roughly where the thresholds for starting a statin sit. What I want to understand is what I ought to believe about Mrs. A in particular. Does the six percent survive the discovery that the population it was derived from is not hers? If it does not survive, is there anything left for me to defer to, or am I now simply inventing a number and delivering it in a statistic's accent? And on what basis does the auditors' record of how this instrument has fared across thousands of patients I will never meet bear on this one woman more strongly than eleven years of my own acquaintance with her — or, if you think it does not, less strongly? Please set out as precisely as you can what separates these three kinds of doubt from one another, whether any of them can coherently be set aside, and what someone in my position should conclude.
