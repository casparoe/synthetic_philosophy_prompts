**The announcement as an unsatisfiable specification**

The setup. A teacher tells her class, roughly, that there will be an examination on one of the five coming weekdays, and that on the morning of the examination the class will not know that it is happening that day. One student reasons as follows. If no exam has been given by Thursday evening, only Friday remains, so on Thursday evening we would know the exam is Friday; that would violate the second half of the announcement; so Friday is impossible. But now, if no exam has been given by Wednesday evening, only Thursday remains, and the same reasoning rules it out. Continuing, we rule out Wednesday, Tuesday, and Monday. There is no day on which the exam can be given as promised. The teacher then gives the exam on Wednesday, and the class is genuinely surprised, so both halves of the announcement come out true.

My proposal is that the student has produced a correct proof and then mislabeled its conclusion.

In my line of work we distinguish requirements about a system from requirements about what someone reading the requirements can work out. The second kind is a different object. A specification that says the output shall not be predictable by an observer who has read this document is not a constraint on the output alone; it constrains a pair, the output and a reader holding the document. Requirements of that shape are exactly the ones that turn out to be unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only in ways the author did not intend, because the document is part of the system it is describing.

The teacher's announcement has that shape. So notice what the student's argument actually assumes as its first premise: that the class knows the announcement. Not merely that the teacher said it, and not merely that it is true, but that it is known — because every step of the elimination uses it as a fixed point the students can reason from and reason about. Run the argument with that premise and you derive a contradiction. The disciplined reading of a proof by contradiction is not "therefore the exam cannot be a surprise." It is "therefore one of my premises is false," and the premise under the smallest amount of pressure from the rest of my knowledge is the one that says the class knows an announcement about its own knowledge. So: the class does not know the announcement, and cannot.

That is the whole resolution, and it explains the Wednesday surprise without special pleading. The students do not fail to perform the argument; they perform it, and it consumes the only premise it needed. What they are left holding at the end of the week is not knowledge of a free Friday but a demonstrated inconsistency in their own information. Once you have shown your inputs are inconsistent, everything derived from them is worthless in the same way — including "there will be no exam," including "the exam must be Friday," including "the teacher is lying." Wednesday arrives and they are surprised, which is the correct output of sound reasoning applied to a poisoned input.

The corollary interests me more than the resolution does. An announcement of the form *you will not know X until X happens* transfers authority from the announcer to the listener's own inference. If the listener's inference produces a contradiction, the listener must withdraw confidence from something, and the announcement has arranged things so that the only two candidates are the teacher's word and the listener's reasoning. Which one loses is not decided by the announcement; it is decided by how much the listener trusts their own derivation. A student certain of the argument concludes the teacher misspoke. A student who distrusts the argument — perhaps because it feels too clever by half — keeps the announcement and is surprised. The teacher, if she is subtle, is not predicting the exam day at all; she is exploiting the fact that her class contains both kinds.

Where I suspect I am wrong. First, my account seems to say that a self-referential announcement cannot be known at all, and I cannot tell whether the restriction to self-referential announcements is principled or a patch I invented to keep ordinary cases working. When the teacher says only that there will be an exam sometime next week, with no clause about knowledge, the class plainly can know that, and the reasoning does not run. I want to say the difference is that the second announcement says nothing about the listener's epistemic state, but I am not confident that is a real distinction rather than a restatement of the fact that one breaks and the other doesn't. Second, I have throughout treated knowledge as all-or-nothing and as automatically closed under deduction: know the announcement, know the implication, know the conclusion. I have a vague sense that this assumption is contested, and that if it is dropped either my argument becomes unnecessary or it loses its bite.
