I run a second-year module on nineteenth-century German philosophy and next term the students get the second of the Untimely Meditations, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. The trouble is that by week three they've all read the same two online summaries and can rattle off monumental, antiquarian and critical on command, and then they just start stapling those labels onto anything that moves. Last year somebody called our local history society 'antiquarian' as though that were an insight rather than a tautology. So I want the sorting exercise to land before they've done the reading: a handout, one side of A4 if you can manage it, which works out what relationship to the past a student actually has in their own scholarly practice, from what they say about concrete cases rather than from which word they like the sound of. Spec is this. Eight to ten questions, each a short scenario with two or three options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. None of the questions may contain the words monumental, antiquarian or critical, nor the author's name, nor any synonym that is obviously standing in for them — a student who has never opened the essay has to be able to do it cold. And please build the tree from the three kinds of person the essay says history serves, not from the three labels: the one who is active and striving and wants models to live up to, the one who preserves and reveres and wants to belong somewhere, and the one who is suffering under something inherited and needs to break with it. That gives you a root with three branches, and each branch should then subdivide on whether the attitude is doing the work of sustaining a life or has tipped over into the excess the essay warns about, so six leaves at a minimum. Add two more for the unhistorical and the suprahistorical stances, which is where I expect a couple of my cleverer students to try to hide, and make those questions sharp enough to seperate someone who genuinely can live without the past from someone who has simply decided the past is tedious.

The bit I actually care about is the following. Every leaf gets a name, one line on what a person sorted into it would predictably do next, and its most awkward commitment. Then flag every pair of leaves your instrument can't reliably tell apart, and say why — I suspect that a ruthless critical stance towards one's own training and a devout antiquarian stance towards whatever tradition one has defected to will come out looking identical on paper, and if that's right I'd much rather you said so than smoothed it over. Finally, walk the whole space of combinations your questions make available and point at the cells no leaf occupies: someone monumental about their own past but critical about everybody else's, say, or someone who reveres everything and imitates nothing, or someone who breaks with the past purely in order to preserve it intact somewhere else. For each empty cell tell me whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive and nobody wants to be caught holding it, or because it's merely unexplored and somebody really ought to move in.

For the cases themselves, keep them local and academic, the sort of thing they'll already have an opinion about: whether first-years should be taught out of the primary texts or a good textbook; what to do with the corridor of departmental portraits once a bequest pays for new ones; the student who wants to write a thesis on a figure nobody has read since 1911 and can't quite say why he matters; the student who wants her opening chapter to demolish her supervisor's framework; a town council debating whether to relocate a war memorial; a reading group that has never touched a book published before 2015. Mix in two or three that aren't academic at all, so the instrument isn't secretly just measuring how they feel about their dissertations. And keep the scoring key mechanical enough that I can mark it in a corridor between seminars — I haven't the time to interpret anybody's soul, however much teh essay might encourage me to.
