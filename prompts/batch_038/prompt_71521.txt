I am auditing a chapter from an undergraduate cognitive science textbook that our department has just adopted, and I would like your judgment on how fair its presentation of one position is. The chapter devotes a paragraph to Dennett's account of consciousness, and it reads roughly as follows:

"Dennett's multiple drafts model rejects what he calls the Cartesian theater: the picture according to which there is a single place in the brain where the separate streams of sensory processing come together and are presented to a spectator. Once that picture is abandoned, the model says, there is nothing left to explain. Information is edited in parallel at many sites, and no single edited version ever becomes the one true content of experience; what we call conscious experience is just the verbal report the brain produces afterward. It follows that there is no determinate moment at which you become aware of anything, and no fact of the matter about what your experience was like before you were disposed to describe it. Introspection, on this view, cannot be mistaken, since there is no private object it might misdescribe. This is why Dennett concludes that consciousness is an illusion."

I have read a fair amount of Dennett and the paragraph does not sit right with me, but I cannot say where it goes wrong, and I do not want to be unfair in the other direction either. Please work through it claim by claim and tell me, for each sentence, what a careful defender of the model would accept, what is a simplification that costs something important, and what is simply a misstatement. Two things I especially want addressed: whether the final sentence is something Dennett would endorse in the sense intended, and what exactly the model's thesis about reports is, since I had understood the claim to be that reports are not readings off a prior experience rather than that experience is nothing but a report. I would also like to know whether the assertion that there is no fact of the matter about your experience is presented as Dennett's general view or is a conclusion he draws only in particular experimental cases, and what the moving-color demonstrations are doing for him there.

Then tell me whether the objections an opponent could legitimately press survive once the paragraph is corrected — in particular whether the charge that the multiple drafts model leaves out the felt character of experience, or that it is after all indistinguishable from a global workspace theory that retains a broadcasting moment, still has force against the real position or was only ever forceful against the textbook's version of it.

Where philosophers and cognitive scientists who know this literature disagree among themselves, and I gather that they do, sharply, please say so explicitly, and keep what is settled or at least uncontroversial separate from what is genuinely contested rather than presenting one side's reading of Dennett as the agreed-upon interpretation. If it would help, close with a short repaired version of the paragraph in the same register that a defender of the model could sign off on without wincing.
