I'm a third-year PhD student and I've been handed the final for the philosophy of mind seminar I'm TA'ing — mixed upper-level undergrads and a few MA students, 22 of them, two-hour paper, two questions, they answer one, so roughly 50–55 minutes of actual writing time per answer. I get to write one of the two questions, and I want it to be on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, but the thing I'm dreading is the answer that opens with a definition, recites the standard dialectic (realist says there's something it's like, illusionist says introspection misrepresents, repeat), and then stops. Half the class could do that from their notes without having thought about anything.

So I want the question built around a toy study I've made up, which the students get in the stem and which is supposed to be the thing they actually have to think about. Here's the study, roughly as it'll appear:

48 participants, randomised into two arms matched for total hours on task (30 hours across six weeks). Training arm: guided attention-to-experience sessions in the micro-phenomenological style — an interviewer repeatedly pushes them to describe the unfolding of an experience rather than their judgments about it. Control arm: equal hours of an unrelated skill (sight-reading drills). Afterwards everyone does the same probe task: very briefly presented stimuli, repeated probes asking them to say what the experience was like. Four measures: (i) forced-choice discrimination accuracy on stimulus features, (ii) internal consistency of their reports across repeated probes of the same trial type, (iii) confidence that their experience had determinate detail they could not fully report, (iv) proportion of descriptive versus judgmental vocabulary in their reports.

Results: the trained arm is way more consistent across probes and uses much more descriptive, much less judgmental language. Discrimination accuracy is at chance in both arms. And — the part I care about — confidence in claim (iii) goes *up* in the trained arm relative to controls, not down.

The question should ask whether this result tells against illusionism, and it should force them to say what *would* tell against it. What I really want separated out is whether a student can see that the study hands each side something: the realist gets trained introspection apparently revealing more determinacy, the illusionist gets a reminder that a more consistent instrument isn't thereby a more accurate one, and consistency across probes is exactly what a stable systematic bias would produce too. And underneath that, the harder bit: illusionism has to treat some introspective deliverances as data (otherwise it has no evidence at all) and others as contaminated, so a real answer owes a principled line between them — and this study is designed to make that line look arbitrary if you can't draw it.

What I need from you:

1. The question as it'd actually be printed, stem included — call it 120–160 words, tight enough that they can read it twice in the exam room.
2. A model answer of about 750 words, written as a strong student would write it under time pressure, not as a survey.
3. A marking scheme out of 25. Not generic bands — I want named moves, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note per move on how students typically botch it. Then a short paragraph on what separates a solid pass from a first/A, i.e. the specific extra thing the top answers do.

Things I want actively penalised, so please build them into the scheme as deductions: recapping the hard problem as if that were an argument; treating illusionism as eliminativism about experience-talk generally and then "refuting" it by pointing out that people have experiences; wheeling out the old verbal-reports-are-unreliable literature as though it settled the matter — those studies are about introspection into the causes and contents of judgments, not about the character of experience, and conflating the two domains is the single most common mistake I saw in the practice essays; and a both-sides shrug with no criterion offered.

On tone and commitment: I don't want a diplomatic model answer. If you think one side genuinely comes out ahead on this case as I've described it, say so and argue for it, and let the marking scheme reward students who reach the same verdict for the right reasons. Where you're leaning on a contested reading of illusionism — there's a deflationary version on which it's mostly a claim about concepts and a stronger version on which it's a claim about what the world contains, and the study bites differently on each — flag that in a couple of lines after the model answer, and tell me which reading you wrote it against. Same for anything else where you're taking a liberty with the ideas.

One more thing, and I'd rather you said it than papered over it: if you think my toy study is a dud as a discriminator — for instance if the upswing in confidence is something both views predict anyway, or if the chance-level discrimination plus high confidence combination is just incoherent as a description of a real task — tell me that up front and tell me how to redesign the study so the question keeps its teeth. Don't hand me a polished question built on a setup that can't bear the weight.
