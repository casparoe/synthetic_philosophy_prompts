ok so a weird request. I'm 17 and I go to school in a mid-size town in Baden-Württemberg, and our Volkshochschule runs these panel evenings once a term. This term's is about the church tax. They asked my school to send a student to do the opening remarks — the "young people's perspective" slot, six minutes max, in English because two of the panelists aren't German — and then the actual panel goes for an hour. I got volunteered. Can you write the speech for me?

The room is about 70 people. On the panel: our Catholic parish priest, a Lutheran pastor, the chair of the local humanist society, and a French lecturer who will say laïcité within about four minutes of sitting down. In the audience: a lot of people over 55 who pay the tax and rather like it, three American dads who work for a company here and think the whole arrangement would be flatly unconstitutional where they come from, and around a dozen classmates who came for the free pretzels and the participation credit. I am honestly one of the pretzel people. I was baptised Catholic, I'm still officially on the books, I have a Saturday job so I'll start owing it myself pretty soon.

Here's the part that matters. My social studies teacher made us read one chapter of a 1970 book by an economist, Albert Hirschman — Exit, Voice, and Loyalty — and the argument is: when something you belong to starts going bad, you can leave (exit) or stay and make a fuss (voice), and which one you pick depends on how cheap leaving is and how loyal you feel. Cheap exit smothers voice, because if quitting is easy nobody bothers fighting to fix the thing. I want that to be the actual skeleton of the speech, not a name-check in paragraph four that I then forget about.

Facts I've checked and want you to use — please don't add more facts on top of these, and don't put in any quotations, statistics, court cases or article numbers, because I'll be reading this off paper in front of my priest and if you made something up I'm finished:

- It isn't a donation. The state tax office collects it on behalf of religious communities that hold that special public-law status, as a surcharge on your income tax — 8 percent of it in Bavaria and here, 9 percent in most other states — and it pays for clergy salaries, buildings, kindergartens, old people's homes.
- The only way to stop paying is to formally leave, and you declare that to a state office, not to the church. There's no seperate form for "still a believer, just not funding the institution." The membership data sits with the tax authorities, the employer withholds accordingly, and some churches will then refuse you a church wedding or a church funeral.
- So exit is one page long and basically free — but it's total. It's a switch, not a dial.
- Surveys of people who left put saving the tax near the top of their reasons, but a study done for the bishops found the tax is usually just the trigger, not the cause; the real grievances are older. That's the Hirschman thing exactly — exit is the mechanism that turns years of slow unhappiness into one dated act.
- Italy and Spain work differently: you tick a box on your tax return and a slice goes to a church or to state social purposes, and ticking it has nothing to do with whether you're a member. That's neither exit nor voice, it's a third thing. I want one clean paragraph on that and on whether it's actually better or just quieter.
- Some of the smaller free churches here are legally allowed to collect it and refuse on principle, living off donations instead. That's a real-world test of the comparison, use it.

And the thought I had that nobody in my class had, which I want you to develop and then pressure-test rather than just announce: in a donation-funded church, the equivalent of leaving isn't a dramatic public act, it's quietly giving less and then nothing, and nobody ever counts it. With the church tax, at least the departure is a form, a date, and a number in the annual statistics that the church cannot pretend not to see. So maybe a legally cheap exit is *more* informative than a voluntary system's silent one. Is that right? Does it survive the fact that the leavers say it's about money and not about grievance? Tell me honestly in a short note after the speech, don't just let the speech assert it.

The speech needs to:

- run about six minutes aloud, short sentences, because I'm nervous and a quarter of the room isn't a native English speaker;
- not land on a recommendation. A 17-year-old telling two pastors how to finance their churches is unbearable. But it also can't be "both sides make fair points." It should end by handing the panel one specific question they then have to sit with for an hour, and the question has to fall out of the exit/voice structure — not something generic like how do we make church relevant for young people. I'd also like the question to touch on whether the system is neutral when the price of admission is that the state keeps a register of your members.
- contain one moment where I concede something against my own side, and a real one — something the priest could lift straight into his opening statement. I'm on the side that thinks it's strange for a government to keep a list of who belongs to God.
- work for a split room: the American dads need to hear why this isn't simply the state funding religion, and the humanist chair needs to hear why "everyone leaves, believers donate voluntarily" isn't automatically the cleaner answer. Neither of them should feel set up to be knocked down;
- avoid pluralism, vibrant tapestry, at the end of the day, and journey. One pretzel joke is allowed if it's in the first ten seconds and it's the only joke.

Also don't make me sound like I've read more Hirschman than one chapter. If you need loyalty — his third thing, whatever makes people stay and complain instead of walking — use it, but explain it in a sentence a 15-year-old gets, and let me say once that my teacher assigned it.
