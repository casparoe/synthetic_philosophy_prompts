I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about how one ought to decide whether to accept a gamble, and I should like the matter put to someone who will not simply take his side. He is an orthodox expected-value man. I have come to think that what matters is not the average across a crowd of hypothetical copies of me but the growth rate of the single life I actually get to live; and yet my own verdicts, once I write them down, do not line up neatly with either position. They are as follows. My investable wealth, excluding my house, is about £200,000; on the house I owe a mortgage of roughly nine times the deposit I put down.

1. A colleague offers me a single flip of a fair coin: heads he pays me £11,000, tails I pay him £10,000. I refuse. He is rather hurt. I should refuse it again tomorrow on identical terms.
2. The same colleague offers me a hundred such flips, one settled per week. I accept without hesitation, and I should accept a thousand.
3. A stranger offers me a single, once-and-for-all bet on my whole portfolio: even odds between finishing 40 per cent up and 35 per cent down. I refuse, notwithstanding that the arithmetic mean favours me, and I feel on very solid ground in refusing.
4. The identical wager scaled down to a twentieth of the stakes, so that it moves my total wealth by at most 2 per cent up or 1.75 per cent down: I accept, and I should accept it as often as it is offered.
5. I insure against a low-probability catastrophe — a liability that would cost me about 70 per cent of my wealth in any given year, arising with probability roughly one in four hundred — at a premium of twice the actuarially fair price. I pay it year after year without any sense of having been fleeced.
6. I took out the mortgage described above and have never once regarded it as a reckless punt, although on paper it is a leveraged, single-asset position an order of magnitude more aggressive than anything in (3).
7. Pay £1,000 now for a one-in-a-million chance of £10 billion. I refuse, though the expected value is comfortably positive and my friend says I am being irrational by my own lights.
8. I turned down a secure post paying £30,000 a year more for one paying less in cash but carrying equity in a firm I judged to have perhaps a one-in-ten chance of returning fiftyfold. That, too, I do not regard as inconsistent with (3).

My friend's counter-attack was that all of this is merely a confession about my utility function: if I decline favourable bets, I must simply be sufficiently concave, and concavity does the whole of the explaining without any appeal to growth rates, ergodicity, or a distinction between a bet I face once and a bet I face a hundred times. He invoked Samuelson's argument against the so-called fallacy of large numbers, to the effect that an expected-utility maximiser who rejects one such flip cannot rationally accept a long run of them; I gather, though, that the theorem requires the utility function to be bounded, and I am not persuaded that mine is. He added that (6) and (8) demonstrate that I am in truth perfectly willing to take enormous one-off risks whenever they are dressed up as life decisions rather than wagers, and that my protestations about repeatability are therefore self-serving.

What I want is the principle behind my verdicts, if any such principle exists.

(a) State as precisely as you can a rule that I could apply to a gamble described only by its stakes, its probability, whether and how often it recurs, and what becomes of me if I lose — a rule that reproduces as many of the eight verdicts as possible. Name the quantity to which the rule is sensitive, and say why that quantity rather than the expectation.
(b) For each clause of your rule, identify which of my cases motivated it. Where a clause earns its keep only by handling (5), or only by driving a wedge between (1) and (2), or only by reconciling (3) with (6), say so explicitly: I want to know which clauses are load-bearing and which are decoration.
(c) Tell me which of my verdicts your rule cannot accommodate, and which I ought to abandon rather than purchase with a further clause. I would rather have a simple rule that contradicts one of my intuitions than a baroque one that saves all eight.
(d) Adjudicate the pair (1) and (2) head-on. Either explain how a single principle can refuse one flip and accept a hundred without smuggling in something other than a utility function defined over terminal wealth — and if so, say exactly what that something is and why it is not merely concavity in disguise — or concede that one of the two verdicts must go, and say which.
(e) Give me your rule's verdicts on three questions on which I have deliberately not made up my mind: whether a defined-contribution pension scheme should default its members into a leveraged equity position that is growth-optimal over a thirty-year horizon but would halve the fund in a bad decade; whether I should accept a bet offered once in a lifetime that either pays me ten times my wealth or takes a third of it, at even odds; and whether a government facing a one-in-two-hundred annual chance of a catastrophe that would destroy a tenth of national output should spend twice the actuarially fair sum to prevent it, given that the same government would rightly forbid an individual to make the analogous private arrangement.
(f) Finally, propose one further case of your own devising — one I have not thought of — that would discriminate most sharply between your rule and plain expected-value maximisation, and state what each of the two predicts in it.
