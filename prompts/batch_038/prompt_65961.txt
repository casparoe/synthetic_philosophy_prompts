I'm a fourth-year PhD student in public policy — my work is all program evaluation and cost-benefit stuff, so quantitative — and this term I've been sitting in on a political theory reading group basically as a spectator. Last week it drifted onto electoral systems and I came away with a puzzle I haven't been able to put down, and nobody in the group seemed to think it was interesting, which makes me suspect I'm missing something obvious.

Here's what I can't reconcile. Essentially every country that elects a single head of government or head of state by popular vote insists that the winner have an actual majority: France runs a runoff between the top two, same for most of Latin America and for the post-communist states that elect their presidents directly, and a growing number of US states and cities have moved to ranked-choice or runoff rules for their own offices. A president who wins on 38% in a three-way race is treated almost everywhere as a legitimacy problem. But those same countries fill their legislatures by single-member plurality and don't blink. In the UK a party gets a comfortable working majority of seats on something like 35% of the national vote and everybody — press, opposition, the winning party itself — talks about its mandate, its program, its authority to govern. Nobody proposes holding a runoff in each of 650 constituencies.

And then there's a third practice that seems to cut against both. Referendums are usually settled by a bare plurality of whoever shows up. Brexit was 52-48 on a 72% turnout and got treated as resolving a constitutional question, when a 52-48 result in an election would be written up as a weak mandate. Some countries do impose turnout quorums for referendums and some don't, and I've never seen a principled account of which is which.

So what theory of democratic legitimacy is each of these presupposing? It can't just be majority rule, since the legislature case is explicitly comfortable with plurality winners and the referendum case is comfortable with 52%. Is the thought that one person holding one indivisible office has to be authorized by more than a plurality, because they speak for everybody including the ones who voted against them, whereas an assembly doesn't, because its composition already *is* the representation and the majority requirement gets applied later, inside the chamber, once it's seated? That feels close but I can't tell whether it actually covers the referendum case or just gets contradicted by it.

What I want is a careful reconstruction. What does each of the three practices strictly presuppose about what an election is doing — authorizing, mirroring, aggregating, licensing — as against what it merely fits comfortably with, because I suspect several rival theories would license the plurality legislature. Then tell me where the three genuinely contradict each other rather than just differing, and whether a single principle generates all three; I keep having the feeling there is one and I just cant find it. And finally, what would have to change — the rules, or only how we describe results, or the idea of a mandate itself — if we held all three to one standard? Whichever standard you think survives scrutiny, say what it costs.

No reading list please, I'm on a train and I want the argument, not the references.
