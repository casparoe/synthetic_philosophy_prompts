My sister was in the August 2020 A-level cohort in England, the one where Ofqual graded everyone with an algorithm after the exams got cancelled, and I've spent the last couple of years reading the algorithmic fairness literature — the impossibility results about calibration versus equal false positive rates, group versus individual fairness, the whole taxonomy. I keep going back and forth on wheter any of it actually explains what happened to her, and I'm supposed to be writing a dissertation chapter on measurement in social policy so I can't just leave it alone. Here are the facts as I understand them, since you can't look them up: schools sent in a teacher-assessed grade for each student plus a rank order within each grade; the model then fitted each school's grade distribution to that school's own results over the previous three years, with an adjustment for the cohort's prior attainment at GCSE; centres with fewer than five students in a subject had the teacher's grade used unchanged, and between five and fifteen the teacher's judgement carried more weight; roughly 36% of A-level entries in England came out lower than the teacher's grade, about 3% two grades lower; private school results went up while state school results went down (Scotland's separate version cut the pass rate for the most deprived pupils about twice as much as for the wealthiest); the government did a U-turn on 17 August and swapped the algorithmic grades for the teacher-assessed ones, except where the algorithm had been more generous; and the head of the regulator resigned about a week later.

So: analyze the whole episode as a case study in the impossibility results. Which fairness criteria was the model actually built to satisfy and which did it violate — was Ofqual even aiming at something like calibration, or at a distributional constraint that doesn't appear in the taxonomy at all? Which criteria were the critics demanding, when you translate their complaints (equal false negative rates? individual fairness in Dwork's sense? something about the protected attribute entering only through a school-level proxy rather than directly)? And is the standoff between Ofqual's defence — teachers inflate, this cohort would get an advantage over the ones before and after it — and the critics' complaint — a strong student at an historically weak school was punished for her school's history, and small cohorts bought an exemption that mostly benefited fee-paying schools — an instance of the standard impossibility, or a genuinely different trade-off? Do the mapping carefully rather than reaching for the punchline: who the players are, what counts as the score, what counts as the ground truth, what the protected attribute is, where the decision threshold sits.

My hunch, which I want you to try to break, is that the impossibility results don't reach this case at all, because they all assume you already have a score and a well-defined outcome variable and then argue about how to threshold it, whereas here there was no outcome variable — the exams never happened, so the thing being predicted was a counterfactual that will never be observed, and three years of school-level history was a proxy for it. I also suspect Ofqual's stated goal, keeping the national grade profile in line with previous years so that a 2020 A means what a 2019 A meant, is a separate criterion — call it comparability across cohorts — that trades off against within-cohort statistical parity in a way no theorem I've read covers. Tell me if something in the literature already handles that (temporal fairness? fairness across time? measurement fairness?), and tell me whether the U-turn is better described as fixing an unfair algorithm or as relocating the unfairness onto the cohorts on either side of it, since the teacher-assessed grades then inflated and universities had to cope. Where the theory goes silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak, and finish with a straight verdict on whether this episode confirms the impossibility framework, is an anomaly for it, or is a case it was never designed for. A table pairing each criterion with what the model did and what the critics wanted would be great to have at the top.
