I'm six chapters into a novel and I've hit a wall on the one chapter I care most about, so I want to read it from outside my own head. Write me a short story — treat it as a standalone piece I'll cannibalise later — about 1,300 words, don't go over 1,600.

The setup: Nadia is a postdoc who runs a colony room. Every night before she leaves she checks that the incubator door latch has seated, by pressing on it twice with the heel of her hand, and then she writes the time in a logbook in pencil. That's the whole thing. That's all it is.

One night her partner Des wedges a folded index card into the doorframe so the latch physically can't seat. He isn't being cruel, he's being funny, he wants to see what she'll do. She presses twice, feels no give, opens the door, finds the card, takes it out, shuts the door, presses twice, writes the time in pencil.

Intercut with that: her study organism, a digger wasp I invented, so dont fact-check me against real entomology, just use my description. She stings a caterpillar, drags it to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out, grabs the caterpillar, takes it down. If you move the caterpillar six inches to the left while she's underground, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the threshold, goes in to check the nest again, comes out, and starts over. She will do this until she drops.

Here's the part I actually need help with, because it's where I keep failing. Don't write me a story about habits. A habit gets cheaper every time you do it, it sinks into the background, it frees up attention — this doesn't. Three years in, Nadia's double-press hasn't gotten faster or fainter or more automatic at all. It's freshly performed, in full, every single night, and it costs her exactly as much attention as the first time. Its the opposite of a habit and I want the prose to know that even though no character says so.

Give me both faces of her flexibility, and let them sit next to each other without comment. Face one: she substitutes happily. No pencil, she uses a pen and marks it in the margin. Logbook full, she starts a new one and writes the old number inside the cover. Nobody watching would call her rigid. Face two: one morning the incubator is out for service, the door is standing wide open and the latch block has been unbolted and set on the bench, and she still presses twice with the heel of her hand — on nothing, in the air — and still writes the time down. I want the reader to feel that these two faces might not be different in kind, and I want the story to refuse to settle the question.

Three more things. Don't give me a cause: no dead mother, no miscarriage, no childhood fire, no diagnosis. The second you explain where the ritual came from you've dodged what I'm asking. Don't use the words compulsion, OCD, automatic, robotic, or free will, and use the word ritual at most once. And somewhere in the middle give me two or three sentences in the flat register of an ethogram — operational descriptions of Nadia's behaviour only, no mental verbs at all, exactly as she'd write up the wasp — then switch back into her head mid-paragraph without flagging it.

Third person limited, past tense, close on Nadia. Des gets maybe four lines of dialogue and he never explains the joke. No tidy ending, no insight she arrives at. Give me two or three alternative last lines to choose from afterwards, and don't explain them.
