Our Moore's paradox reading group has been circling the same two paragraphs for two years and I've started to suspect the reason is that we're having two arguments and calling it one. Can you diagnose it for me?

Camp A says the puzzle is entirely in the speech act. Asserting p presents you as believing p, so p & ~Bp is pragmatically self-undermining and nothing deep is going on. The giveaway, they say, is that the past-tense and third-person versions are perfectly assertable — which is what you'd expect if the trouble comes from what an assertion commits you to, since its only the present first-person case that goes wrong. Camp B says the interesting fact is about belief, not assertion: you can't coherently hold that content, because belief is transparent to the subject, or aims at truth, or is partly constituted by a readiness to avow it; and the weirdness of saying it then just follows from a linking principle, that what you may assert tracks what you may believe. Then there's Camp C, Rosenthal-style, which denies there's any belief-level problem at all and puts the whole weight on assertion — which, notice, is Camp A's conclusion reached by a route that concedes Camp B's framing of the question.

Hypothesis one, and I want you to stress-test it rather than nod along: A and B aren't answering the same question. One asks what an utterance commits a speaker to, the other asks what states a subject can be in, and neither side has said which explanandum it's actually offering — the felt absurdity of the sentence, or the impossibility of the belief. That would explain why each camp hears the other as trivialising the phenomenon: A hears transparency talk as a redescription of the assertion norm, B hears the assertion norm as a way of changing the subject away from the only interesting fact.

Hypothesis two, smaller, please attack this one specifically: the omissive/commissive split (p & ~Bp versus p & B~p) may be doing hidden work. My thought is that it lines up with the occurrent/dispositional split on belief. People routinely believe p dispositionally while occurrently believing ~p — the self-deceived patient, the friend who keeps insisting their marriage is fine. If you seperate those, the omissive form stops looking like a paradox about belief at all, since it describes an ordinary divided mind, while the commissive form keeps its bite. On that reading the standing argument over whether one explanation has to cover both forms is an argument about a distinction nobody is stating out loud.

Data any diagnosis has to handle, and assume I can't supply sources for it and don't want any back: past tense fine; second and third person fine; first-person future tense disputed; the asymmetry, that p & ~Bp is absurd while ~p & Bp is not; and no other attitude generates it, since saying it's raining while hoping it isn't raises no eyebrow.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really — a verbal one, two questions under a single label, a substantive fight about the nature of belief that everyone is mis-describing, or an ill-posed question?
