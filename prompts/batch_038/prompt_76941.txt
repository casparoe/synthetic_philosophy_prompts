I run a philosophy-of-math reading group — 14 people, 75 minutes, mixed background, and about half of them have never seen a non-constructive proof in the wild. I want them to feel the strategy-stealing argument (the Hex-style move where you show the first player must have a winning strategy, because any winning strategy for the second player could just be borrowed after an arbitrary first move) without me explaining it up front. Design the whole session around a connection game they actually play on graph paper with two coloured pens.

What I need it to do:

- They should run into it themselves. First the frustration of playing several rounds and being unable to pin down a winning opening, then a mechanic — I'm picturing sealed "oracle" cards that hand one side a supposed winning strategy for the other player, plus a partner who plays it straight — so the borrowing manuever is something they do with their hands rather than something I assert.
- One round that stresses the premises: maybe a stone pre-placed for somebody before play begins, maybe a variant where an extra mark can actively hurt. You choose and say why.
- The technique, and the word "stealing", stay unmentioned until the debrief.

Give me: materials list, the run sheet, the oracle-card text written out so I can print it as-is, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the two assumptions the argument leans on, and onto the odd fact that the proof certifies a winning strategy exists while telling you nothing about what it is. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I recover, and an honest note on which feature of the argument the room simply can't reproduce.

Format: the run sheet as a table with columns Phase | Minutes | What they do | What they should hit | What I say or do. Then a JSON array named `premise_audit` — objects with fields `premise`, `role_in_argument`, `how_activity_exposes_it`, `breaks_in`.
