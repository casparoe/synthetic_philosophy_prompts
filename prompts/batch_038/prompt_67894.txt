Working on a chapter for an edited volume on the epistemology of historical knowledge, and what I want is an argument, not a literature review — skip the references entirely, I know the field, I just need somebody to build with me and push back where I'm soft.

Setup: everyone grants there's a real difference between historical revisionism and denialism. The functionalist/intentionalist fight, the demotion of Wannsee from "the meeting where it was decided" to a coordination meeting, the endless argument over Nanjing casualty figures, the collapse of the clean-Wehrmacht picture — revisionist, contested, sometimes ugly, but inside the practice. Whereas "the gas chambers weren't there," "the Armenian deportations involved no deliberate killing," "Nanjing was invented out of doctored photographs" — that's a different thing, a different *kind* of thing. Trouble is nobody has a criterion for the difference that survives contact with the sophisticated cases.

The three usual candidates look broken to me:

1. consensus — makes denialism whatever contradicts current orthodoxy, which is question-begging and can't explain why overturning an orthodoxy (Wannsee, the Wehrmacht myth) is legitimate work;
2. method — the good stuff now mimics method almost perfectly: footnotes, archival gestures, self-styled forensic reports waved around in courtrooms, house-journal formatting. You can only apply the criterion once you already know the conclusion, which makes it a rationalisation rather than a test;
3. motive — inaccessible from outside, and half the field has an agenda.

My view, and the one I want you to argue for as hard as you can: the line is dispositional. What makes a position denialist isn't its content, its method, or its holder's motives, but that it's held in a way no possible evidence would dislodge — and that shows up as a pattern rather than a single move. Two features: inverted scrutiny (hostile evidence gets forensic treatment, friendly evidence gets waved through) and auxilliary proliferation, where each refutation is met not by revision but by a fresh rescue hypothesis that is never itself independently tested and gets dropped the moment a new one is needed. On this account denialism isn't a degenerate research program, it's a closed doxastic system that happens to have historical content — an epistemic vice, not a bad methodology.

Build the case. Specifically:

- show that this does work the method criterion can't, and does it prospectively rather than post hoc;
- take the killer objection head-on: dispositions arent observable, so either the criterion is empty or it collapses back into method, since the only way to spot immunisation is to watch somebody's inferential behaviour over time. I need a real answer here, not a dodge;
- handle the hard cases. Nanjing is my favourite, because the numbers fight is live inside respectable scholarship and shares primary material with the "nothing happened" crowd. And: does the criterion wrongly condemn the committed partisan — a Marxist historian, a survivor who won't entertain a lower death toll, someone whose identity is fused with the claim? If conviction and closure look identical from outside, is there a principled line between them, or should I just bite the bullet?

Then be straight with me about where the argument is most exposed — the two or three places you think I'll lose the room — and tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or counterexample would make you abandon the dispositional criterion and retreat to method. Take the side and push; hedging into a balanced survey is the one failure mode I don't want.
