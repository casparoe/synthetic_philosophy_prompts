I am working through the closing argument of the *Meno* and have hit a wall at the point where Socrates distinguishes knowledge from true opinion, and I would be grateful for help deciding how much weight the passage can bear. I am reading W. R. M. Lamb's Loeb translation (1924). The relevant stretch runs from 97d to 98a; I have elided the intervening pages, where Socrates rehearses the case of the man who knows the road to Larissa versus the man who merely opines correctly about it, so the second excerpt below picks up mid-sentence.

> *Socrates:* It is because you have not observed with attention the images of Daedalus. But perhaps there are none in your country.
> *Meno:* What is the point of your remark?
> *Socrates:* That if they are not fastened up they play truant and run away; but, if fastened, they stay where they are.
> [...]
> [...] and effect all that is good; but they do not care to stay for long, and run away out of the human soul, and thus are of no great value until one makes them fast with causal reasoning. And this process, friend Meno, is recollection, as in our previous talk we have agreed. But when once they are fastened, in the first place they turn into knowledge, and in the second, are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more prized than right opinion: the one transcends the other by its trammels.

My difficulty is that the Daedalus image seems to invite two incompatible readings of what the "fastening" consists in, and I cannot tell which one the text licenses. On the first reading, the criterion is retention: an opinion is knowledge once it has stopped running away, and the reason it stopped is incidental. On the second, the criterion is etiology: the opinion must have been fastened *by causal reasoning*, so a tie of any other kind, however durable, leaves it an opinion. The closing line, about knowledge transcending true opinion by its trammels, sounds to me like the first reading — a difference in the security of the fastening rather than in its source — but the identification of that fastening with recollection sounds like the second, and I do not know how to hold both.

The reason I care is that I am trying to work out what this passage implies about forming moral beliefs on someone else's authority, and I have constructed a case to test it. Call her Ada. Ada's moral beliefs were installed in childhood by a grandmother she revered, and let us stipulate that every one of them is true: in sixty years she has never held a false moral opinion. She can give no reasons for any of them; asked why promise-breaking is wrong, she says only that it is, and that her grandmother said so. But nothing dislodges them either. She has kept promises at considerable cost to herself, refused bribes, told unwelcome truths to people she was afraid of, and not one of her moral opinions has ever run away. Then she is asked something with no precedent in her upbringing — whether a scarce medicine should go to one stranger rather than another — and she has nothing to say. She does not guess. She goes quiet and looks to whoever is standing nearby.

Ada seems to satisfy the retention test perfectly and the causal-reasoning test not at all, yet her failure on the novel case makes me suspect that the fastening is supposed to do a third thing that the Daedalus image does not advertise: it is supposed to make opinions *generative*, able to issue in correct verdicts about situations the believer has never encountered. If that is right, the image of the statue that stays put is doing less work than the argument needs, and I would like to know whether you think I am reading something into it.

There is a further wrinkle. Socrates identifies the fastening with recollection, and the one worked example of recollection in the dialogue is the slave boy, whose correct geometrical opinions were drawn out of him by another person's questioning — he would never have produced them unaided. Does that not leave open the possibility that a believer's opinions can be fastened by reasoning that someone else conducts, so that the passage becomes an argument for the epistemic value of moral testimony rather than against it? Or does the boy's case show the opposite, since Socrates is at pains to insist that the opinions were in him all along and merely needed to be stirred into knowledge by being questioned repeatedly?

So: which reading of the fastening does the passage support, what follows for Ada, and does the *Meno* here give any comfort at all to the thought that a moral belief acquired on another's say-so is second-rate even when it is permanently correct? Please tell me your answer to that last question in your first paragraph or two, and then work through the reasons; if you think one of my readings simply will not survive contact with the Greek idiom of "trammels" or of the reasoning about causes, say so plainly rather than trying to accommodate both. I am not after a survey of the commentary — I do not have the secondary literature to hand and would rather not be sent hunting for it — just your own close reading of what is in front of us. Please write to me in continuous prose, as one would in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables, and no numbered lists of the readings, even though I have set out two of them that way myself.
