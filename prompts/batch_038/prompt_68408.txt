I have spent a couple of months reading across the decision-theory literature on Newcomb-style problems — causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and the updateless family of views that came out of work on artificial agents — and I keep hitting the same wall. I am not looking for a verdict on which theory is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Let me first set out the debate as I understand it, then give you my hypothesis and the alternatives I have considered, then say what I want back.

The three positions, as I read them:

1. Causal. What makes an act choiceworthy is the causal upshot it produces, so the agent should evaluate acts by what they bring about, holding fixed everything the act does not influence. On Newcomb's problem this yields two-boxing, and the causal theorist accepts the resulting poverty: the contents of the boxes were settled before the choice, so taking both cannot make you worse off and can only make you better off. On the smoking-lesion case — where a genetic condition causes both a disposition to smoke and cancer, and smoking itself causes nothing — the causal theorist takes this to show that evidential reasoning recommends abstaining for a reason that is no reason at all, since the act would be evidence of your condition rather than a cause of your misfortune.

2. Evidential. What makes an act choiceworthy is how good a sign it is, so the agent should evaluate acts by conditionalizing on having performed them. This yields one-boxing, and the evidential theorist regards the appeal to causation as unmotivated: it does no work that conditionalization does not already do, and where the two come apart, the causal notion has to be supplied from outside decision theory. When pressed on the lesion case, the standard reply is a screening-off manoeuvre: an agent with introspective access to its own inclinations already knows what it is inclined to do, so conditioning on that knowledge removes the correlation the lesion was exploiting.

3. Updateless. The object of choice is not an act but a policy, and the policy is to be evaluated without conditionalizing on the information the agent happens to hold at the moment of choice — because in any problem where the state of the world was settled by what kind of reasoner the agent is, conditionalizing on your present evidence is precisely the step that goes wrong. On this view the two-boxer's remark that the money was already there is not false but misdirected: the money is already there because of the kind of reasoner you are, and the question to ask is which kind you want to have been, not which box you want to reach for. The same machinery handles the case of the driver in the desert who picks you up on your promise to pay him later, and whom, once you are safe, you are tempted to stiff: an agent that settles its policy before learning how things stand pays up, and an agent that re-decides at each node does not.

Two further things all three sides acknowledge. First, in a long run of Newcomb-like choices, an agent that pays in advance to bind itself to one-boxing and then unbinds at every node ends up strictly worse off than an agent that simply one-boxed, and the causal theorist does not dispute the arithmetic. Second, no side claims to have refuted the others; each claims the others have misdescribed the problem.

And yet nothing moves. Each stock objection has a stock reply that the objecting side treats as no reply at all. The taunt that the causal agent ends up poorer is met with the observation that this expresses a wish about precommitment, not a reason bearing on the choice now open. The lesion case is met with the screening-off manoeuvre, which the causal theorist says assumes the point at issue, since it is only a manoeuvre if you already think that evidence which does not bear on outcomes is not a reason. And the updateless theorist says both rivals share the deeper mistake of evaluating a choice from inside a moment rather than asking which policy to have — a charge the others hear as changing the subject, since on their view the agent is, ex hypothesi, already the kind of reasoner it is.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: the dispute is not about what to do in Newcomb's problem, and not fundamentally about counterfactuals either. It is about what counts as a decision problem — in particular whether the agent's own decision procedure is an exogenous parameter handed over by the problem, or part of the world-state to be modelled along with everything else, and correspondingly whether a claim about what would happen if the agent did otherwise is evaluated holding the agent's reasoning type fixed or allowing it to vary. If that is right, the three camps are answering different questions under one heading, and no case will ever settle it, because each camp builds its cases so that its own question is the one on the table.

The alternatives I have considered:

(i) The dispute is verbal — causal, evidential, expected and rational are being used as terms of art whose contents the camps do not share, and once the terms are pinned down the disagreements dissolve or turn into agreements.

(ii) The dispute is substantive but concealed behind a formal question: whether any decision rule can simultaneously be dynamically consistent across sequences of Newcomb-like choices, ratifiable — still optimal in the agent's own eyes once it has made it — and immune to exploitation by someone who can predict you; and the camps simply differ about which of these to surrender when they cannot all be had.

(iii) The dispute is bedrock: an unargued difference about whether an agent can be credited with, or held answerable for, facts that were settled before it acted, which no argument reaches on either side.

(iv) The whole family of problems is ill-posed: a predictor whose reliability is logically entangled with the agent's own reasoning describes no coherent situation, so the camps are quarrelling over an empty subject matter and the correct diagnosis is that there is nothing here to disagree about.
