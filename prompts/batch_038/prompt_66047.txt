I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy, and in three weeks I am giving the guest lecture on the problem of logical omniscience in our department's first-year sequence in formal epistemology. The cohort has completed one term of probability theory: they handle credences, conditionalization, and Bayes' theorem without difficulty. They have had no modal logic and no possible-worlds semantics of any kind, and I cannot presuppose either. I have seventy-five minutes and a whiteboard, and I will be followed later in the term by three sessions on Bayesian confirmation theory that simply assume the classical axioms.

What I want from you is not an exposition of the problem but a plan for teaching it.

1. **The dependency structure.** The problem does not exist independently of a way of representing belief states, and my students do not yet have one. Lay out the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what each stage licenses at the next. In particular, advise me whether to build the representation first and let the closure result fall out of it as a discovery, or to open with the closure principle stated informally and work backwards to the semantics that generates it. Tell me how much of the apparatus is genuinely load-bearing and how much I can gesture at, and whether any single diagram is worth drawing.

2. **The carrying example.** I had settled on a mathematician who accepts the Peano axioms and therefore, on the standard account, already believes every theorem of arithmetic — including the one proved by somebody else last week that she could not in fact prove. Assess that example rather than adopting it: state what an example here must have, and must lack, in order to do the work; say whether mine meets those conditions for this audience; and if it does not, propose a replacement and justify it against the same conditions.

3. **The misconceptions.** I anticipate three: that this is an idealization on the model of the frictionless plane and therefore unobjectionable; that the problem is really just that people are slow at arithmetic; and that moving from flat belief to degrees of belief dissolves it. For each, say where in the sequence it must be headed off and with what. I had assumed I could dispose of the third quickly by observing that the probability axioms force credence one in every logical truth; tell me whether that is the right thing to say at this level, or whether it concedes too much or too little. Then name two or three further errors I have not thought of that this audience is likely to form, and identify any of my three that is not in fact an error I should be fighting.

4. **Downstream risk.** Which of these errors, if it survives the session, will most seriously corrupt the students' work in the confirmation-theory sessions that follow, and what I can do in the last ten minutes to inoculate against that one specifically.

5. **A closing question.** One question I can pose in the final five minutes whose answers would distinguish students who have grasped why the representation forces the problem from students who can only repeat that real agents are not logically omniscient. Tell me what a good answer and a bad answer to it sound like.

6. **Omissions.** Say explicitly what I should refuse to attempt in seventy-five minutes — including whether the various repairs on offer belong in the lecture as names to be recognized or as positions to be understood — and what I should tell the students I am leaving out, and why.
