Last term I sat in on the tail end of a Thanksgiving argument between my uncle, who teaches introductory macroeconomics at a state university, and my cousin, who is in her second year of a PhD program in classics. They were arguing about whether Aristotle has anything that deserves to be called a theory of value, and I wrote down what I could remember afterward and reconstructed it as six moves. I am not taking a side; I mostly want to know where each move actually lands, because both of them sounded convincing to me at different points and I never got a clear sense of which parts of the disagreement are real and which are just the two of them talking past each other.

Move 1 (my uncle): Aristotle does have a theory of value, and it is a demand-based one. In the Nicomachean Ethics discussion of exchange he says that everything traded has to be comparable, that what actually serves as the common standard is need or demand, and that money is only a conventional stand-in for that need — which is why he connects the word for currency to the word for law or custom, and adds that money can be changed and rendered useless at will. On his reading, that is close to the modern claim that value tracks what people want rather than anything intrinsic in the object, and that a monetary unit is a social technology with no natural worth of its own.

Move 2 (my cousin): That is anachronism. The Ethics passage is about justice and about how a city of unequal people holds together — builder and shoemaker, farmer and physician — and the proportion he sets up is a diagonal one across social roles, not a price. Aristotle is asking what makes an exchange fair and what keeps an association going, not where prices come from, and there is no term in the text doing the work that "value" does for an economist. She also said the need point is not subjectivism at all: need for Aristotle is indexed to what a household and a polis actually require for a decent life, so it is a normative standard, not an aggregation of individual preferences. And the line about money being renderable useless at will is a warning about the fragility of the convention, not a claim that any convenient token would do just as well.

Move 3 (my uncle): Then Aristotle contradicts himself. In the Politics he tells an origin story about money in which barter became awkward, people agreed to give and accept some genuinely useful and portable commodity such as iron or silver, sized and weighed, and only later stamped it so that nobody had to assay it each time. That is a story about money emerging from practical convenience among traders, with no legislator involved — the same story an economist would tell, and closer to commodity money than to fiat. He cannot both say money arises naturally out of the inconvenience of barter and say it exists purely by convention and could be abolished at will.

Move 4 (my cousin): No contradiction, because the two texts are answering different questions: the Politics passage is about why people settled on a particular useful substance as a medium, and the Ethics passage is about what makes that medium authoritative as a measure. Her stronger point was that the Politics critique of trade and interest rests on teleology, not on a measure of value. Money's proper function is to serve as a measure and to facilitate getting what a household needs; charging interest perverts it by making the measure itself into a thing that generates more of itself, and retail trade is criticized because it aims at accumulation that has no internal stopping point. That is a claim about the function of a thing and about limitlessness, not a claim about what determines price.

Move 5 (my uncle): Fine — but if the justice of an exchange depends on the trader's purpose, the criterion is unusable. He offered a test case: a market maker who buys and sells the same index fund within minutes, never intending to use or consume it, purely to capture the spread. Aristotle's condemnation of trade for profit would have to reach that, since the trader's aim is money itself. But by the same standard it would have to reach a farmer who sells surplus grain to a dealer at a profit, and Aristotle surely does not want to condemn that. So either he needs a distinction my cousin cannot supply, or his criterion collapses into "whatever I happen to find distasteful about finance."

Move 6 (my cousin): The distinction is available and it is not about distaste. The limit on household management is set by what the household and the polis need, so the farmer selling a surplus stays inside an activity that has a natural ceiling, while the market maker's activity has no ceiling at all — its end is a quantity that can always be larger. He also pointed out that the Ethics seems to require that in a just exchange the products be equalized before reciprocation, and that if the bargain is unequal the association stops; but if a just exchange is one in which equal value is given and received, it is hard to see what either party gains, and an account of exchange that cannot explain why anyone trades in the first place is not an account of exchange. My uncle's reply, which is where they were still stuck when I left the room, was that "no limit" already presupposes a measure of what counts as enough, so Aristotle is committed to a substantive and objective theory of value after all — just the opposite of the one my cousin was denying he had.
