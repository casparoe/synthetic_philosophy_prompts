My working life has been spent tuning feedback loops — thermal and motion systems, PID with gain scheduling, deliberate deadbands, feedforward paths on measured disturbances, reference governors to keep a command inside its constraint envelope. Beyond a single undergraduate ethics course I have no philosophy training, which is why I am approaching this from my own side. I would like the philosophical debate over nudging translated as faithfully as possible into control vocabulary, because I suspect that debate collapses into something I already understand, and I want that suspicion tested rather than indulged.

Here is the material, with one-clause glosses of my own. Where a gloss is wrong or tendentious, correct it before translating; do not translate my error and then report it as theirs.

1. Thaler and Sunstein's nudge: an alteration in how options are presented — the default, the ordering, the framing, the effort required — that changes behaviour predictably while forbidding nothing, leaving economic incentives roughly intact, and keeping exit cheap.
2. Their welfare standard: an intervention is justified insofar as it makes people better off as those same people would judge it.
3. Sunstein's publicity requirement: a government must be willing to describe its nudge openly to those it affects, and, he argues, that openness need not destroy the effect.
4. Sludge and sludge audits: friction — forms, waiting periods, redundant steps — that raises the cost of exercising an option, deployable either to obstruct people or, occasionally, to make them pause; Sunstein wants the friction audited in both directions.
5. Gigerenzer's alternative of boosting: rather than exploit an agent's fast, automatic responses, train her statistical and risk competence; his standing complaint is that a nudge leaves the agent no more capable than it found her.
6. The autonomy objection pressed by critics such as Sugden and Rebonato: because a default also shifts the preferences by which the agent later evaluates the outcome, the position must posit some cleaner preferences she would have had she been thinking clearly, and preserving a cheap exit is not the same as preserving her direction of her own life.
7. The replication worry: once publication bias in the nudge literature is corrected for, average effect sizes shrink toward nothing, raising the question whether nudging names a mechanism at all.

My tentative mappings, offered for attack rather than endorsement: a nudge is feedforward compensation of a known disturbance applied on the input channel, leaving the plant untouched; a boost is a re-identification of the plant, or a relocation of the controller inside it; sludge is an intentional deadband or hysteresis inserted to prevent nuisance actuation during transients; the publicity requirement is documentation of the loop rather than a property of it.

Please deliver the following, in this order:

(a) An item-by-item translation of 1 through 7 into control terms, stating in each case what in my vocabulary is doing the work.
(b) A verdict for each item: translates cleanly; translates only with distortion, with a precise statement of what is distorted and why; or has no counterpart.
(c) A residue report on the items that fail to translate, with an explicit judgment in each case as to whether the residue marks a genuine gap in control vocabulary, which cannot express something real, or a sign that the philosophers' disagreement is thinner than it looks.
(d) Two cruxes, answered directly. First: in control terms, is the standard in item 2 self-validating — can a controller always improve its tracking performance by moving the reference toward the output — and if so, does that show the standard circular, or only that it requires an exogeneity condition on the reference which control theory cannot state? Second: is the objection in item 6 expressible as a structural property of the loop, namely feedback from output to reference, or does it require a normative claim about who owns the setpoint; and if the latter, is ownership of the setpoint something my framework lacks, or something it deliberately abstracts away from?
(e) The reverse report: distinctions control theory draws that the debate collapses — for instance, among steady-state error, tracking lag, and disturbance rejection, or between a gain so low it sits inside the measurement noise and no intervention at all. Say which of these refinements would actually change how the philosophers argue, and which are artifacts of my formalism.
(f) A closing section of no more than two hundred words stating whether my opening suspicion is right, wrong, or right about some items and wrong about others, and naming the single item on which I am most mistaken.

Take my glosses as the data; I am not asking for references or for a survey of who said what. Flag every point at which you must stipulate something the philosophers do not say, and do not allow control theory's own instrumentalism — the setpoint is given, the task is tracking — to settle silently a question they are actively arguing about. Between 1,400 and 1,700 words, in numbered sections corresponding to (a) through (f).
