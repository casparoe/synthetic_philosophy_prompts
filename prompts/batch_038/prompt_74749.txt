I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about information cascades, and I'd like you to turn the disagreement into a two-page proposal for a one-year postdoc fellowship.

His position: the much-cited result that cascades are fragile — that a modest amount of public information can overturn a settled consensus — does no real explanatory work, because in every case anyone offers as a genuine cascade (a bank run, the adoption of a standard, an early consensus about a technology, a run on a claim that turns out false) the public information arrived only after people had made costly and hard-to-reverse commitments. So what looks like fragile belief is really just belated action.

My counter: he is collapsing two things that the models keep separate, and the collapse is what makes the fragility result look trivial. A cascade can be belief-fragile and action-robust simultaneously — the private posterior of someone inside a cascade may shift almost immediately on new evidence, while the behaviour it licenses stays locked in for months or years. If that combination is coherent, it is precisely what makes herding dangerous, and fragility results that measure only belief change have been telling us nothing about the phenomena people cite them for.

Write the proposal around the question I've only half-formed: is the belief-fragility/action-fragility distinction a real distinction with different formal signatures, or an artifact of how the canonical models are parameterised? It should contain a precise statement of the question with both terms defined; an account of why the standard treatments leave it open, naming the assumption that does the hiding; the method month by month, with a defence of why the right first move is the one you choose rather than the obvious alternatives (a toy model with a reversibility parameter, a reconstruction of the existing proofs to see where the conflation enters, or a small case analysis); what a positive result would look like; what would count as the project having failed rather than merely stalling; and an honest note on where twelve months is probably not enough. Refer to positions and arguments as needed — I'll supply all the citations myself.
