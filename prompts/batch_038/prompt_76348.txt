Acting as a philosopher of science who's spent a lot of time around the Anthropocene-start-date fights — and think carefully about this one, it's much more an inference puzzle than a knowledge test, so please start with some real brainstorming before you commit to anything. You don't need the web for this and I don't want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; if you mention any real work, paraphrase it, don't quote it.

Situation: I'm on a nine-person panel at a science museum. Our flagship exhibition opens with a wall that announces a single start date for the Anthropocene, and we have to re-commit to it for the reopening in eight months. The panel is four scientists (two stratigraphers, a climatologist, an ecologist), three historians, one philosopher, and me (curator, not a specialist). We've ruled on seven things over the past six years, and each ruling got one minuted sentence:

1. Mid-20th century, anchored on the worldwide radionuclide layer from thermonuclear testing (~1952) plus the takeoff curves in CO2, reactive nitrogen and extinction rates. ACCEPTED as our headline date, 7–2. Minute: the only candidate where the rocks and the graphs agree, and where a visitor can see that what changed is a state, not just the arrival of a new kind of grain in the sediment.
2. The late-1760s patent for Watt's separate condenser, offered as the start of the fossil era. REJECTED, 9–0. Minute: an emblem, not a layer — you can't core it.
3. The 1610 low in ice-core CO2, tied to the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas after contact. REJECTED, 5–4. Minute: a dip is an excursion; and separately, half the panel was uneasy that the proposal's own framing makes the date turn on a particular story about who did what to whom.
4. Early-Holocene candidate: paddy methane and pre-industrial land clearance bending atmospheric composition from several thousand years back. REJECTED, 9–0. Minute: a real trend with no date — nothing in the record points and says here.
5. First cities: irrigation salinization and deforestation around Mesopotamia, ~5,000 years back. REJECTED, 8–1. Minute: spectacular, and regional.
6. 1945, the first artificial radionuclides. We refused it as the headline but let it into the timeline as a first-appearance datum. Minute: we date an epoch, not an event.
7. Procedural ruling two years ago: a submission whose main argument was that an international stratigraphic body had endorsed its boundary was refused a hearing. Minute: endorsement isn't evidence about the rocks, and if we can't explain a marker to a twelve-year-old we don't get to point at it in a gallery.

The new proposal landed last month from a visiting researcher. She wants the Anthropocene dated to a horizon of spheroidal carbonaceous particles — fly-ash microspheres — together with the first global appearance of synthetic aluminium-alloy fragments and reworked concrete dust in lake and peat cores. She gives 1886 as first appearance in a single Welsh lake, claims global synchrony by about 1905, argues this is the earliest point at which a geologist with no access to written documents could identify humanity at all, notes that her marker type was just added to a professional body's list of candidate boundaries, and says plainly that the horizon records the worldwide spread of one specific British-German industrial toolkit.

What I need from you:

(a) Brainstorm at least four candidate decision-rules, each of which is consistent with all seven items above. For each, say which verdicts it explains cleanly and which it only explains with a shrug.
(b) For each rule, the verdict it predicts on the new proposal and the one-sentence reason.
(c) Your best single guess at what the panel decides, including a predicted vote split out of nine, and which rule you think is actually driving us. Be honest about which of our minutes are doing real work and which are just noise we wrote to make a compromise palatable.
(d) The closest analog among items 1–6, and precisely where the analogy strains.
(e) If we switch, what principle we'd be adopting that we've never stated out loud, and what principle we'd be quietly giving up.
(f) Invent one further fake candidate boundary — describe it in three or four sentences, with a marker, a date, and a claim about its spatial reach — that would maximally discriminate between the two rules you think are most likely, and say which rule predicts accept and which predicts reject.

Keep the whole thing under about 900 words and don't pad. Assume the panel isn't stupid: the answer I actually want explains how a smart, well-informed group could have exactly this track record and still not know its own rule.
