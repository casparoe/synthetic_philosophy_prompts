I am in the final year of a joint philosophy and economics degree, and the capstone seminar I am taking on decision theory requires an approved proposal before any of us begins drafting. The paper is due at the end of term, should run to about 5,000 words, and I have eleven weeks. I would like you to turn my half-formed idea into that proposal.

My starting point is the sunk-cost effect. The received view is that unrecoverable past expenditure has no bearing on whether to continue, and that continuing anyway is a defect of rationality; the standard explanations of why people nonetheless do so appeal to loss aversion, to self-justification, or to the reputational value of appearing to be someone who does not quit. What strikes me is that all of these treat the agent's own earlier investment purely as a psychological cause, or as a signal to others, and never as a source of evidence about the project itself. Yet sometimes it looks like exactly that: an earlier decision to commit may have been made by someone who had information I have since lost, muddled, or argued myself out of. In such cases, holding fixed everything I now believe, does the bare fact that I once invested carry information about the value of continuing? If so, honoring sunk costs would be a form of deference to a better-informed earlier self rather than a mistake, and it would have more in common with the evidential treatment of one's own acts as evidence than with any of the familiar stories.

My hunch, which I want the proposal to put at risk, is that this can only work under narrow conditions. If my past act followed from evidence I still possess, learning that I performed it should tell me nothing new; the act is, so to speak, already screened off by my current evidence. The account would therefore have bite only where the earlier decision drew on something my present evidence fails to reproduce: forgotten data, a judgment made with fresher eyes, a colleague's assessment I have since discounted. The paper I want to write asks whether those conditions can be stated precisely, and whether the resulting position survives the paradigm cases. Here I expect a real difficulty that the proposal should build in rather than smooth over: in the war pursued because of the lives already spent, the past expenditure seems evidentially inert, whereas in the case of the non-refundable ticket one might argue that my earlier purchase did encode a judgment about my own enjoyment that deserves deference. The paper will have to say whether that second intuition is admissible or is simply the fallacy wearing a respectable coat.

Please write the proposal itself, containing: a statement of the research question in one or two sentences, precise enough that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the positions currently in play leave it open, describing the families of view I must distinguish myself from without naming authors, since I will supply all references; the method I would actually use, and why it beats the obvious alternatives, namely running further psychological experiments or simply demonstrating that the effect violates a norm of decision theory and stopping there; the argument's structure laid out week by week across the eleven; what a positive result would look like, and what would show the project had failed, where I suspect the failure condition is this — if rescuing a case always requires that the past expenditure be correlated with the project's value, then I have merely redescribed those cases as ones in which the cost was not sunk after all, and nothing has been gained; and a candid note on which part of this is too ambitious for 5,000 words and should be cut or deferred.

A small amount of notation is welcome where it earns its place, but the proposal must remain readable by an instructor whose specialty is not decision theory. Please do not include a bibliography or in-text citations.
