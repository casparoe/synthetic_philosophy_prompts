I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm reporting a fight inside the history of science that has turned personal: one camp wants narrative books built around named discoverers, arguing that a public shown what one particular person managed will treat further achievement as possible rather than as mystery; the other camp wants granular archival work — notebooks, instrument repairs, decades of routine correspondence — and holds that the discoverer-centered story is largely an artifact of reading the past backward from whoever won.

I want to frame the piece with Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and above all with his monumental and antiquarian modes. Please compare those two modes as they would apply to these camps, and identify what each mode's characteristic pathology looks like in the specific case of science writing, since Nietzsche's claim is not that one is better than the other but that each rots in its own way. Then locate the third mode: is the debunking impulse — showing, say, that a celebrated series of measurements quietly dropped the awkward data points — an instance of critical history, and does Nietzsche's objection that the critic is himself a product of what he condemns gain any traction in this setting, or is that worry tied specifically to the case of nations and peoples that he is writing about?

Please also separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this essay — including whether Nietzsche intends to recommend any of the three modes at all, and whether the fact that he uses one particular German word for history in the title rather than the more ordinary one does any real philosophical work — and do not pass off a contested reading as the consensus.
