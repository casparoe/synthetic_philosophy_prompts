Please write a dialogue between two decision theorists who have each received the same extraordinary offer and who meet the evening before the deadline to argue about what that offer requires of them.

**The offer**, in the terms both characters have read: an anonymous donor has sent identical letters to exactly twenty people, all drawn from the roster of the same summer institute in formal decision theory. Each recipient must decide independently and in secret whether to mail back the enclosed card by noon the following day. If exactly one card is returned, its sender receives ten million dollars and the other nineteen receive nothing. If two or more cards are returned, or if none are, no one receives anything. The letter forbids any communication among recipients about the offer and warns that detected collusion voids the prize for all twenty. Finally, the letter states — accurately, so far as either character has reason to doubt — that all twenty recipients have already read the standard analysis of this kind of problem, including the familiar prescription that a superrational thinker should not mail the card outright but should randomize, mailing it with probability one in twenty.

The offer is a variant of the structure known as the Platonia dilemma, but the variant is what matters here: unlike the familiar version, the letter guarantees that every recipient is exactly as well informed as every other, so the premise on which superrational reasoning depends is handed to the characters rather than assumed by them.

**The characters.** Yael is a superrationalist. She holds that because all twenty reason alike about a problem they have all already studied, her deliberation is not an isolated event but a sample of a reasoning process that will be replicated nineteen more times; she therefore chooses as though the choice were made once and applied to all twenty. She insists this involves no causal influence and no mysticism — the same argument, competently run, ends in the same conclusion, and a thinker who ignores that fact is not clearer-eyed but merely incurious about her own situation. Callum is a causal decision theorist. He holds that deliberation requires treating one's own act as an open variable and every other agent's act as settled by causes one does not control. He concedes freely that Yael's premise gives him excellent evidence about what the others will do, and he is willing to reason from that evidence. What he denies is that the evidence licenses her inference.

**Requirements.**

1. Neither character may be a strawman, and neither may be permitted to win by rhetorical flourish. Each must at least once narrow or refine their own position in response to an argument from the other.
2. The dialogue must contain, worked out explicitly and correctly, two calculations: the one that yields the one-in-twenty prescription together with the probability it assigns that anyone at all wins, and the one showing what a recipient gains by mailing the card with certainty while believing the other nineteen will follow that prescription. Callum should press the asymmetry these two results create; Yael must answer it without disputing the arithmetic.
3. Callum should also object that the letter guarantees only that the twenty reason alike, not that they value ten million dollars alike, and should draw out what that does to a prescription whose derivation assumes symmetry. Yael must give a substantive reply.
4. The two should converge on agreement about every empirical and arithmetical fact, so that what survives is a disagreement about the logic of choice rather than about the world. In the closing exchange, each should state what they take the surviving disagreement to be; the two statements must not be equivalent.
5. Neither may dismiss the other with a label — no appeal to magical thinking, wishful thinking, or sophistry — unless the label is immediately cashed out as an argument the other must answer.
6. Because disclosing an intention would violate the letter's no-collusion clause and forfeit everyone's prize, neither character will say what they mean to do. Let that constraint shape the conversation, keeping it at the level of principle rather than bargaining, and let the reader feel its cost.
7. Argue only from the terms of the letter and from decision theory. No character may cite, paraphrase, or invoke the authority of any real author, article, or historical case.

Write between 1,300 and 1,800 words, with speaker labels, in American English.
