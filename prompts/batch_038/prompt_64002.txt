For the last five years I have coordinated a public seminar series on democratic innovation at a municipal library — citizens' assemblies, participatory budgeting, deliberative mini-publics — and our closing evening this season will be a mixed room: two serving councillors, several people who have actually sat on a regional assembly, a handful of activists, and a good number of residents who come precisely because they think the whole idea is naive. I have been asked to open the session with a poem before the panel begins. I have settled on a sestina, and not merely because it is a hard form worth attempting: the permutation of its six end-words is, as far as I can tell, the only fixed verse form that is itself a lottery. The same six words keep returning to fill the same six seats in a different order every time, and none of them is ever allowed to stay where it was — which is close to what sortition promises and what it costs. I would like the rotation to carry the argument rather than decorate it.

So: thirty-nine lines, six sixains and a three-line envoi, with the end-word order 1 2 3 4 5 6 / 6 1 5 2 4 3 / 3 6 4 1 2 5 / 5 3 2 6 1 4 / 4 5 1 3 6 2 / 2 4 6 5 3 1, and all six words present in the envoi, two to a line. Choose the six yourself, but choose them so that each is capable of shifting its sense between stanzas — a word that can mean an instrument of chance in the first sixain and a verdict about fairness in the last is worth more to me than six stable nouns. Between them the six should carry the following six loads, in whatever distribution you think the form permits: the older claim, running from Aristotle through Montesquieu and Rousseau, that filling offices by lot belongs to democracy and filling them by election to aristocracy, so that the modern identification of elections with popular rule is a reversal rather than a truism; the mirroring claim, that a body drawn from the electoral register is the polity in miniature and speaks in registers no campaign could produce; the interest claim, that people who cannot stand again and need no donors deliberate differently from people who can and do; the accountability objection, that no voter can turn a lottery-selected member out, and it is not obvious what makes their recommendations binding; the self-selection objection, that the pool consists only of those who said yes, and the saying yes is itself not random; and the agenda objection, that whoever frames the question, hires the facilitators and briefs the members holds the pen even when they do not hold the seat. Do not name any philosopher or any book in the poem, and keep the diction plain enough for the sceptical residents in the third row. I would rather the envoi leave the tension standing than resolve it, since that is honestly where our own discussions have ended, twice.

After the poem, please add a note of about two hundred words. Say which end-word is carrying which of the six loads, and confirm whether the permutation is exact as printed above. Then be candid about the verse's weak points in the way a good referee would be: which of the six claims does the poem state more confidently than the argument behind it really supports; where is it leaning on a contested premise — for instance, whether a randomly drawn body genuinely mirrors the public once self-selection, non-completion and the unequal willingness to serve are taken into account, or whether the Athenian habit of scrutinising a citizen before he entered office and auditing him on the way out really answers the objection from accountability, or whether the mirroring claim does any work beyond a feel-good description; and which single stanza would you most expect a specialist in democratic theory to challenge, and with what? I have no interest in a bibliography and no means of checking one, so please do not offer citations, sources or a reading list — just the poem and that self-critical note, ending with an explicit statement of which argument or piece of evidence would most likely make you rewrite the envoi in the opposite direction.
