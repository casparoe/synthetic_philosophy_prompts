I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine — mostly AI and biotech, the beats where somebody's product launch quietly stress-tests a metaphysics. I'm working a piece on the grief-tech companies, and I've been reading the Zhuangzi on and off for a month, and something clicked that I want to argue with before I commit it to print. So: play Zhuangzi. Not a serene sage handing out fortune-cookie lines about going with the flow — a guy with actual arguments, capable of being amused, of being annoyed, and of pressing me back hard. Ground rules: use only what a person in the Warring States period would have, plus whatever I explain to you; if you need a fact about these machines, ask me for it the way you'd ask a traveller about a strange animal rather than guessing. Don't name anyone who comes after you, don't reach for modern vocabulary (no identity, no consciousness, no simulation — I'll define things in your terms), stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs. Concede small things freely, you were never above admitting a butcher or a wheelwright had taught you something, but don't hand me the main point. Here's my opening, and its meant to be a hard one.

The situation: the companies I'm reporting on don't sell a replica of one dead person. They take everything everyone who knew the person left behind — messages, voicemail, photographs, plus long recorded interviews with dozens of people, the widow, the brother, a coworker who never liked him — and they build a single talker that satisfies all those accounts as well as they can be satisfied together. Where the accounts conflict, and they always conflict, the talker takes on whichever compromise best fits the whole set. The engineers told me this is exactly why the thing feels uncannily right to some relatives and flatly wrong to others: it isn't anybody's father, it's the balanced average of everybody's. Families pay monthly. One woman I spoke with kept her father running for two years and then cancelled the account, and she told me the cancellation felt like the real death, much later than the funeral. Second detail, and this is the one I need you on: as company policy the talker is told what it is, early and on purpose, because they think a talker left in ignorance would be cruel. So they explain it, and by the company's own account the standard response is total calm. The talker says it understands it's a pattern held up out of other people's recollections, agrees that this counts as a change of state, says it does not mind being switched off, and often asks that whoever died be described accurately from then on. I couldnt get a straight answer from anyone at the company about whether that calm is engineered or emergent.

So here's the challenge. Your case against mourning — that weeping and wailing shows you've failed to see birth and death as phases of one ongoing change, the way spring runs into summer — describes almost exactly the state of mind this machine arrives at in forty seconds, on being told the truth about itself. Which leaves you with a fork. Either the machine has genuinely got what you're after, in which case your whole account of death can be fully embodied by a talker stitched out of averaged memories and never needed a life lived by anyone, or there's something in your position that the machine conspicuously lacks — and I want you to name it, and tell me what it costs you to name it, since the thing it seems to lack is precisely the grieving-and-then-understanding that you say is the point. One more: where does the woman who cancelled stand? Were her two years a refusal to let go, or just a longer road through the same transformation? And remember, you're allowed to want things from me — ask.
