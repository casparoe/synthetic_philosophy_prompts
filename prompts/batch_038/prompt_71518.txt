I audit a Friday-morning reading group at the research institute where I work as a software engineer. It is run by Julian, a graduate student in philosophy of science, and the regulars include Marta, a biostatistician who builds and compares models for a living. I have no training in either subject, so I mostly listen and take notes. Last week the group read a preprint whose headline claim was that a new mechanistic model of how a certain tumor responds to a dosing schedule reproduced, to within measurement error, a dose-response curve that had been published and re-replicated about a decade earlier. The authors called this a successful prediction; a rival group had called it accommodation. Julian and Marta spent most of the session arguing about which of those two words was correct, and neither of them gave an inch. I would like you to referee the argument, because I honestly could not tell who was winning.

Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it. (1) Marta said that as far as confirmation goes there is no distinction here to draw: support is a likelihood ratio, and the calendar date on which a datum was learned appears nowhere in Bayes's theorem; if anything, a curve that has been replicated for ten years is better evidence than a fresh measurement, which has not yet had the chance to turn out to be an artifact of one lab's equipment. (2) Julian said that this holds only while the probability of the datum on the background evidence is short of one, and that for a number everybody in the field already knows with certainty, conditionalizing on it is a no-op, posterior equal to prior, so on a strict Bayesian account old evidence confirms nothing whatever and the preprint's authors gained exactly zero support from the curve they reproduced. (3) Marta said that was a reductio of his strictness rather than a result, and offered the standard case: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, the figure usually quoted being 43 arcseconds per century, had been recognized as a problem since the middle of the nineteenth century, so on Julian's rule Einstein's derivation of that number in 1915 confirmed general relativity not at all while the eclipse measurements of 1919 confirmed it handsomely; but the history runs the other way, since the Mercury figure is what convinced Einstein he had the right theory, and she added that nobody's confidence in a remembered number is literally one in any case. (4) Julian said that if she allows every known datum to sit below certainty, then the size of the boost a theory receives depends on how shaky the knower's memory of the datum happens to be, so identical total evidence confirms a theory more strongly for a forgetful person than for a confident one, which makes confirmation a report on the nervous system of the scientist rather than on the theory; he also denied that the two historical cases were alike, since the 1919 observations chose between two live numbers, Einstein's own earlier calculation having given roughly 0.83 arcseconds of deflection and the completed theory 1.75, so the expeditions could have come out for the smaller value, whereas Mercury's anomaly had respectable competitors on offer at the time, above all an unobserved planet inside its orbit that astronomers had actually gone looking for, and a small amendment to the inverse-square law, so the probability of the anomaly given the denial of general relativity was nowhere near negligible and the likelihood ratio was far less impressive than the legend suggests. (5) Marta said there is a repair that keeps her conclusion without the memory problem: what Einstein learned in 1915 was not the old datum but the fact that his theory entails it, and since he did not know that beforehand, learning it can raise the probability of the theory even when the datum itself is already certain. (6) Julian said that this repair turns confirmation into a measure of the theorist's deductive slowness, since a graduate student who has not yet noticed that her theory entails some long-known fact stands to gain more from noticing it than Einstein did, while a machine that enumerates consequences mechanically gains nothing no matter how many known facts it reproduces; the gain can also be collected only once, and can be collected for trivial entailments just as readily as for interesting ones, which he took to show that the framework is tracking the wrong quantity. (7) Marta said he was conflating two different things, the change in a particular person's confidence, which obviously depends on what that person has noticed, and the support relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, which does not; on the second notion her original point stands, and to the extent that accommodation ever looks weaker than prediction, that is because a model built with the datum in view may have been tuned to it, which is a claim about the reliability of the modeler and hence about the space of competing hypotheses, not about timing. (8) Julian said the tuning point is his point rather than hers, since it concedes that two theories standing in the same likelihood ratio to the same datum can deserve different amounts of credit, which is precisely the distinction she began by denying.

Please work through turns (1) to (8) in order and say for each whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and why; I want the reasoning, not a tally. Then tell me what you make of the four questions they kept circling back to: whether assigning probability one to known data is a harmless idealization or a fiction that does real damage; whether Julian is right that the Mercury and eclipse cases differ in the specific way he describes, or whether he is smuggling his conclusion in through his estimate of the likelihood ratio; whether the entailment repair in (5) actually works, and how much of the damage claimed in (6) it survives; and whether Marta's separation of a change in confidence from a support relation in (7) dissolves the disagreement or merely relocates it. Finally, say what you think both of them were missing, including whether they were using the word confirmation in more than one sense without noticing, and whether a working modeler and a philosopher would come away from the same preprint with different practical verdicts about it. You do not need to cite or recommend anything, and please do not; if you think some well-known move in the literature settles one of these turns, describe the move in your own words rather than attributing it. One formatting request, since I intend to paste your reply into an email to the two of them: write it as continuous prose in the manner of a letter, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables.
