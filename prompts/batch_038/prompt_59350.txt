ok so I'm a senior and I run my school's philosophy club, and next month we're doing a session on this idea that where you come from is essential to you — the claim that you literally could not have come from any other sperm and egg than the ones you actually came from, so there's no possible world where your parents conceived a slightly different combination and got you anyway. The version I read starts with tables: a table couldn't have been made out of a totally different hunk of wood. Then it runs the same move on people. Half my club is just going to nod along at that, so I want to walk in with a case that actually puts pressure on it.

Can you build me that case to spec? Here's what I need:

- Real world, no sci-fi. A fertility clinic, not branching universes. No teleporters, no time travel, no souls, no perfect predictors.
- Use the specific setup where the clinic takes an egg from the mother, pulls out the nucleus, and drops it into a donor egg that's had its own nucleus removed, so the kid ends up with the parents' nuclear DNA but the donor's mitochondrial DNA — the "three-parent baby" thing. Write it so somebody who has never heard of mitochondria can follow.
- I want two cases side by side, one paragraph each. Case A is the mito swap. Case B is a control that's the same in every way except the clinic just uses a different egg from the same mom, so the nuclear DNA comes out different in the normal way. I want to see whether the theory gives different answers for A and B, and whether that difference in answers lines up with any difference in what people actually feel about the two cases.
- The difference in origin has to be sharp and non-vague. Don't let the whole thing slide into "well, how much of the egg can you swap before it's a different egg" — I don't want a sorites, because then the defender just says the boundaries are fuzzy and we're stuck. I want a yes/no question the theory is forced to answer.
- The case has to generate the reaction, not assume it. If you have to write "and obviously this is still the same kid" to make it land, it isn't working.
- If doing the swap after fertilization instead of before makes it bite harder (like, at that point both things involved are already zygotes?), do that instead and tell me why it's harder to dodge.

Then after the cases:

- Walk through what the origin essentialist is committed to saying about A and about B, seperately. I especially care about the retreat move: if they say "fine, forget the egg, what matters is the zygote / the first cell," does that actually save them in case A, or does the exact same question just pop up one level down as "what makes that zygote the same zygote"?
- Tell me which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and what happens to the verdict if I pull that feature out.
- Tell me how a defender would attack my case — do they bite the bullet and say the kid in A is a different person, do they say my description is misleading and the donor egg makes it more like a twin, or do they narrow the thesis so it doesn't cover this? Rank those responses by how much damage they do to me.
- And be straight with me: does this actually function as a counterexample, or does it just relocate the problem? I'd rather find out now than get picked apart in front of twenty people.

Page and a half max, and American English please — I'm in the US and all my club handouts use American spelling. Keep it at a level where a smart 17-year-old who's never taken a philosophy class can follow, and define any term you end up needing. Also don't give me a bibliography or a list of papers to read, I can't look any of that up from my phone during lunch and I don't want made-up sources sitting in my handout.
