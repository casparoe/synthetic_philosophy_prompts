In my school's mathematics seminar we read a popular account of how the continuum hypothesis was shown to be independent of the usual axioms of set theory: first shown to be consistent with them, and then twenty-five years later shown to be unprovable from them. The author explained what this amounts to by way of an analogy I keep returning to. He said the continuum hypothesis stands to set theory exactly as the parallel postulate stood to Euclid's remaining axioms, and that just as mathematicians eventually gave up asking which geometry is the true one and simply worked with whichever suited the problem, we ought to give up asking how many real numbers there are. On his view there is no fact of the matter, and set theorists who say otherwise have failed to absorb the analogy. My instructor objected that the incompleteness of arithmetic is the better comparison, since nearly everyone holds that a Gödel sentence for a given formal system is either true or false of the natural numbers even though the system cannot settle it, and she asked us to explain why the two episodes might not teach the same lesson.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on realism about sets. Please set out what is supposed to map onto what — the axioms, the intended subject matter, the construction of alternative models, the eventual attitude of practitioners in each case — and then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the author's conclusion from those that merely complicate it. I am particularly interested in whether the analogy quietly assumes something about what axioms are for and about what it takes for a question to have a determinate answer; whether the arithmetic comparison my instructor prefers is really a better model of what independence shows, or has difficulties of its own; and whether the fact that set theory still supports live programs of axiom-search aimed at settling the hypothesis one way or the other, while geometry supports nothing comparable, belongs on the list of disanalogies or is only a difference in the sociology of two fields. Please close by saying what a fairer analogy for the continuum hypothesis would be, if there is one, and whether the author's deflationary conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

I would be grateful if you answered in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, much as one would write a letter.
