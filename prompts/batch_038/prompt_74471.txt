Here is the student's answer (the paragraph numbers are mine, I added them for grading):

(1) The first thing to notice is that Nadia's directive is an exercise of precedent autonomy: it isn't the family's guess about what would be good for her, it's her own earlier decision about a situation she anticipated and described. So the daughter and the son aren't really arguing about what's in Nadia's best interests in the abstract; they're arguing about how to read a document she wrote. That matters, because if Part B has been triggered then Part A is simply gone, and the question becomes an ordinary best-interests question about a woman who currently seems content. It's also worth noticing how strange Part B is procedurally: normally only a person with decision-making capacity can revoke a directive, but Part B delegates the revocation to something closer to a reflex than to a decision. Nadia isn't deciding to revoke anything on the third Sunday; her distress is doing it for her.

(2) Dworkin is the obvious way to defend Part A. He distinguishes critical interests, which are the interests a person has in the experiences she most deeply values — the ones that make a Sunday afternoon feel worth having from the inside — from experiential interests, which are about whether a person's life adds up to something, its integrity and its shape as a whole. On his view the second of these is what gives an advance directive its force: what matters is that Nadia's life be the life she wanted it to be, not that her final years feel a particular way. The Sunday recording serves her critical interests, since it keeps her connected to the courtroom and the case she cared about, and that's exactly why Part B can sit inside the same document as Part A without contradiction.

(3) Parfit gives a different kind of support. He thinks personal identity isn't some deep further fact over and above psychological continuity and connectedness — overlapping chains of memory, intention and character. And Nadia-now is plainly still connected to Nadia-then: she asks for the recording, she recognizes that the lady on the screen is someone who matters, she has retained fragments of the values she used to hold. So on Parfit's view the same person survives, and Part A is the decision of that same person, which is why it governs. Identity is what makes an advance directive authoritative, and here identity is preserved, so the daughter's reading of Part B can't be allowed to erase Part A on the basis of one bad afternoon.

(4) Dresser objects to the whole project. Her point is basically that the demented patient is now a different person, so honoring the directive doesn't honor anybody's autonomy — it just imposes the values of a stranger on someone who can no longer understand them. If Dresser is right, Part A has no force whatever, and the family should do what's best for the woman in the chair, which this Sunday looks like turning the tablet off when she pushes it away and turning it back on when she asks.

(5) Part B is also a kind of Ulysses contract in Elster's sense. Nadia binds her later self against her own predictable future wishes, exactly as Ulysses has himself lashed to the mast. The only difference is that Nadia builds in a way for the knot to be untied by her later self's reaction, which makes her directive more modest than the standard self-binding cases.

(6) So: Part B is coherent, and on the facts it has been triggered. But notice that once the trigger is an experiential one — how Nadia feels while watching — the Dworkin/Dresser dispute sort of drops out. If we let her present distress revoke the directive, we aren't overriding anybody's critical interests and we aren't imposing a stranger's values either, so there's nothing left for the two of them to disagree about. The son's reading isn't crazy, but it's the daughter who is following the document as written. The family should treat her pneumonia if it comes, and Part B turns out to be a clever piece of drafting precisely because it makes the philosophical debate unnecessary.
