I spent nine years as a process-safety analyst and am now working part-time on an MPhil in the ethics of technology, so I sit on both sides of this. Across something like thirty incident reviews — a chemical dosing controller at a water treatment works, a conveyor interlock at a distribution centre, an automated dispensing cabinet on a hospital ward — I have noticed the same conversational move recurring, and I cannot find a name for it. When something goes wrong, agency migrates downwards into the machine: the report says that the controller decided to increase the dose, that the software chose the route, that the system failed to register that the line was occupied, and the human beings in the room appear mainly as people who were unable to stop it. When the same team reports a success, agency migrates back up again: the press release credits the engineers who designed a controller that cut overdosing by forty per cent. The direction of travel is remarkably consistent — down for blame, up for credit — but what puzzles me more is a second feature. In a seminar last term, someone argued that no one can properly be held responsible for what a sufficiently autonomous system does; the reply was that of course someone can, since the developers and deployers are responsible for putting it into the world. Ten minutes later, someone argued that developers cannot reasonably foresee how such a system will behave in the field; the reply was that in that case the behaviour is the system's own doing, and it ought to be treated and regulated as an agent in its own right. Both replies relocated agency to whichever level supported the conclusion being defended, and neither speaker appeared to notice or to be insincere about it: which level the agency sat at seemed to be settled by the question that had just been asked rather than by anyone's interests.

I am aware that the general area has a literature — there are well-known arguments that harms caused by autonomous or heavily automated systems can leave nobody properly to blame, and well-known objections to those arguments — but that debate concerns whether such a gap exists and how it might be closed. What I am describing is something else: a feature of how the participants talk, a pattern in the way agency is attributed and re-attributed across levels of description, and one that seems to be available to both sides of the debate equally. I expect someone will tell me this is simply equivocation, but I do not think it is, because the words do not change their meaning between the two uses and each attribution taken on its own may well be true; nor is it plain hypocrisy, since the direction of the move tracks the question asked rather than the speaker's side. It may be that the pattern has no name at all, in which case I would like you to supply one, but the name is the least important part of what I am after. What I want is the carving: what exactly this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. I would like to be able to spot it in a transcript, and to know when shifting between levels is legitimate — after all, a genuinely emergent property of a complex system really is not attributable to any individual designer — and when it functions to make a conclusion immune to refutation.

Please answer as a philosopher of action and technology who also reads engineering ethics, incident investigation and the AI-responsibility literature, and who is willing to say plainly that a phenomenon has no established name rather than stretch a term to fit.
