I write about brains and ideas for a magazine and I've spent the last month shadowing a philosopher of mind who works on pain — mostly the odd clinical cases. Tomorrow I'm taping an interview and I want to walk in knowing roughly what she'll say, so I can ask a follow-up that actually bites instead of just going "huh, interesting."

Her verdicts so far, from my notebook and from two of her talks. Paraphrasing, not quoting:

1. Hand on a hot pan. Ordinary full-strength pain, you hate it, you yank your arm back. Bad for you, obviously — she treats this as the baseline case.
2. A stroke patient with pain asymbolia (she mentioned the insula). He'll tell you he feels the pinprick and exactly where it is, then sort of smile about it, doesnt pull away, doesn't seem to care. Her line: he really is in pain, and its not bad for him.
3. A cancer patient on a decent dose of morphine who says the sensation is just as strong as it was before but that it doesn't bother him now. Not bad for him, according to her.
4. A marathon runner at mile 23 — legs on fire, says he hates it and wants it to stop, keeps running anyway. She says that pain isn't bad for him either. That one threw me, since he's reporting something close to case 1 from the inside.
5. Somebody with congenital insensitivity, never felt pain at all, chewed through their lip as a kid, walked on a broken foot. She says that's a worse situation for them than having pain.
6. A consenting masochist in the middle of a scene, enjoying it. Not bad for him while he's enjoying it.

The case I'm putting to her tomorrow is a woman I'll call Dot. 68, wrecked her back in a fall twelve years ago. Dot describes her pain readily and calmly — calls it "the weather in my back" — says she doesn't mind it and doesn't want it gone, and genuinely laughs about it. No resentment anywhere in the account. But: she gave up her allotment, stopped driving, barely sees anyone, and spends most days in the same chair. Asked whether she's happy, she says yes, she's content.

So: will she say Dot's pain is bad for Dot?

What I want from you isn't mainly the answer, it's the scaffolding:

- Give me the candidate rules she could be running that fit all six verdicts. I want at least three that actually fit, and for each one tell me which of the six it's leaning on hardest. If a rule looks like the obvious choice but one of the verdicts kills it — I suspect 4 kills a couple — say so explicitly, that's useful to me too.
- For each rule that survives, what it predicts about Dot, and why.
- Your best guess at her actual answer, and what tips you toward that rule rather than the others. (Notice that 2 and 5 pull in different directions, I think, and I'd like to know how a rule handles both.)
- One more case I could put to her on tape that would force the leading candidates apart — a case where they give opposite answers, so I can find out whether she's consistent or whether she's been sliding between two theories without noticing.

Don't give me citations, book titles, or the names of positions from the literature — I can't check any of it and my editor won't let me run a seminar. Plain-language rules I could describe to a reader. And if you think the six verdicts arent jointly consistent after all, tell me which one she'd most likely give up under pressure and why.
