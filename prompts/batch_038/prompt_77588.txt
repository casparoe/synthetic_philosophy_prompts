I'm auditing a seminar on interpretation across disciplines this term — literary theory, scripture, law — and I keep coming back to an inconsistency inside a single legal system that nobody in the seminar seems bothered by, so I'd like you to treat it as the object of analysis. In common-law jurisdictions the same judiciary applies three markedly different regimes for working out what a written text means. Probate: the court treats the dead testator's intention as the controlling consideration, seats itself imaginatively in the testator's position, and admits evidence from outside the four corners — letters, drafting instructions, what the testator told friends and family — to work out whom or what a description picks out; and in many American states, under the Uniform Probate Code's reformation provision and the corresponding Restatement approach, a court may rewrite a will or trust to correct a mistake of fact or law, on clear and convincing evidence, *even where the language is unambiguous*. Contract: the court asks what a reasonable person in the position of the addressee would have understood the words to mean, treats an unexpressed private intention as legally inert, and where both parties attached materially different meanings to the same phrase and neither knew or had reason to know of the other's, has been willing to hold that no contract came into existence at all — the 1864 English case of the two Bombay-to-Liverpool ships both named Peerless is the standard illustration — though where one party knew of the other's meaning and the other did not, the innocent party's meaning governs. Statute: a textualist court says the enacted words, in their public meaning, are the law; that a legislature is a body rather than a mind and so has no intention to be found; and that committee reports and floor speeches may not be used to displace what the text clearly says.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of meaning each regime presupposes, and an assessment of whether the three can be reconciled. Start by stating, for each practice, the account of what makes a text mean what it does that the practice only makes sense under — and be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably. Then ask whether one underlying semantic theory plus three different sets of background facts (one author who can no longer be consulted and whose instrument is a gift rather than a bargain; two living parties whose reliance and cooperation have to be preserved; an institution whose authority is constituted by the public act of enactment) can generate all three regimes, or whether the system is in fact committed to rival and incompatible pictures of meaning, so that a probate judge and a textualist appellate judge are not disagreeing about evidence but about what meaning is. Pay particular attention to a deflationary possibility: that the real differences are epistemic and institutional rather than semantic — that everyone involved holds the same theory of meaning but the law has different tolerances for unreliable evidence, different allocations of risk, and different conceptions of what each instrument is *for* — and tell me how I could distinguish that reading from the genuinely semantic one, or whether the practice leaves the question underdetermined.

Finally, run the counterfactuals in both directions. If the jurisdiction adopted a uniform intentionalism, what would have to change in contract litigation and in statutory adjudication, and what would break — is there anything about bargains, or about the authority of a legislature, that resists being folded into an author-psychology model? And if it adopted a uniform anti-intentionalism, the position a literary critic takes when she refuses to consult a novelist's letters, what would have to happen to probate: would courts have to leave a misdescribed bequest standing, refuse reformation, and treat the document as severed from the mind that produced it? I'd also like to know which of these two uniform regimes is more internally stable, and whether there is a domain — trusts, treaties, constitutions, recorded music, standard-form terms of service — where you'd expect the practice to sit closest to each. Please argue the reconstruction rather than surveying positions, and don't spend space telling me that the intentional fallacy debate exists; I want to know which side of it, if either, these three bodies of practice are tacitly voting for.
