I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether moral compromise can ever be a genuine trade — a case of gains from trade in the economist's sense — and I came away unable to answer her. Here is the setup, then what I want from you.

The case we were working with: a small private foundation has two trustees who must agree unanimously or the endowment goes unspent for another year, so deadlock is the non-cooperative outcome. Trustee A is a thoroughgoing welfarist who wants the entire ten million dollars to go to the most cost-effective global health interventions she can find. Trustee B holds that the money was given by local families on the understanding that it would serve local people, so sending it abroad breaks faith with the dead; for B this is a duty of fidelity, not a claim about anyone's welfare. They settle on a 60/40 split. Each says they are better off than under deadlock.

My position was that this is exactly like a trade: each trustee holds a scarce asset, the power to block, and what they exchange is forbearance, so each ends up with more of what she values than she could have obtained alone. My friend had two objections.

(1) There are no two goods here. In a trade I hand over something you want and you hand over something I want. Here there is one pot and one decision, and each trustee regards the portion the other controls not as a benefit but as a wrong. Splitting a wrong in two does not manufacture two gains; it only makes two people partly complicit.

(2) The "better off" has no currency. To say both gained, you need a scale on which A's partial win and B's partial win can be compared with what either would get alone — but whether such a scale exists is precisely what the two trustees disagree about. The surplus is real only from a third-person standpoint, which is the standpoint of a theory neither of them accepts.

Please make the strongest argument you can for my side: that the 60/40 settlement is a genuine gain from trade by each trustee's own lights, not merely from a neutral observer's. Take both objections head-on rather than steering around them; concede whatever must be conceded, but do not surrender the main claim. You may bring in any machinery that helps — bargaining theory and threat points, threshold deontology, the difference between promoting a value and realizing it, the way duties admit of degrees of satisfaction — but the argument must land on this case, not on compromise in general.
