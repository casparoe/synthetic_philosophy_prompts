US Treasury Regulation § 20.2031-1(b) fixes the value of property for federal estate tax purposes as follows: "The fair market value is the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or to sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts." The same willing-buyer/willing-seller formula, in near-identical wording, governs the valuation of property donated to charity, and something like it recurs throughout American tax and valuation practice. The regulation adds that fair market value "is not to be determined by a forced sale price," and that where an item is ordinarily obtained by the public at retail, its value is the retail price rather than what a dealer would pay for it — so a decedent's used car is valued at what a member of the general public would pay for a comparable one, not at what a wholesaler would offer.

Set this against a familiar result in the economics of markets. If agents share common priors, are known to be rational, and each already possesses reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts, then trade cannot get started: an offer to buy is itself evidence that the offerer knows something the recipient does not, so a rational recipient declines. Voluntary exchange is generated by exactly the features the definition removes — asymmetric information, divergent beliefs, and differing needs or endowments, along with whatever pressure those needs exert. The regulation therefore stipulates conditions under which the hypothetical transaction would arguably never take place, and then reports the price it would have fetched.

There is a second wrinkle. The hypothetical is in practice settled by evidence drawn from actual transactions: comparable sales, and the price of the item in the market where it is most commonly sold. Those are transactions between real parties with unequal knowledge, unequal bargaining positions, and reasons of their own for trading. The practice defines the relevant price by excluding the very transactions it then relies on to determine it.

What theory of price — and of value more generally — does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory explicitly: does it treat a price as a property an object possesses, disclosed once the distorting circumstances of particular traders are subtracted, or as a relational fact indexed to a specific pair of agents and a specific information partition? Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out (price as a condensation of dispersed and partly incommunicable knowledge, price as the residue of a bargaining process, price as a bookkeeping convention)? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with: perhaps it presupposes no theory of value at all, only that a single, contestable number be producible for the tax base. Identify where the practice is underdetermined, and whether its two halves — the idealized definition and the actual-sales evidence — are consistent with one another, or only with different theories. Finally, suppose the definition were replaced by a frankly indexical one, on which an asset is worth what it is worth to a specified buyer under a specified state of information. What would have to change in the regulation, in appraisal testimony, and in the assessment of the tax base — and would the law then be obliged to say whose valuation counts, and on what grounds that party's is the right one?
