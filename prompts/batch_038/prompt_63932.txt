I'm about two-thirds through my third novel and I've hit a wall that turns out to be philosophical rather then structural, so bear with me. The book is about a woman, Tess, who after a religious conversion of some kind decides she is simply going to stop saying things that aren't so — no white lies, no flattery, no social lubricant. Her sister is hiding from an ex who is a genuinely dangerous man, and the climax is that ex turning up at Tess's door and asking, straight out, whether she knows where her sister is. I've written that scene maybe nine times and every version either has Tess lie (which wrecks the character) or has her pull some evasive move that feels like a cheat and reads like a cheat. Trying to work out whether the evasion is a cheat morally and not just dramaturgically, I went and read Augustine's two treatises on lying — the earlier one, from before he was a bishop, and the much later one he wrote to Consentius about the Priscillianists — and I'm afraid I came away half-converted, and now I'm holding a package of views I can't tell is coherent.

Here's the package. (1) What makes a lie is the split between heart and mouth: you can lie while saying something perfectly true, if you think it's false, and you can say something false without lying, if you believe it — the sin lives in the intention, not in the accuracy. (2) Jokes, irony and obviously-not-literal talk sit outside all this, which is a relief, because (3) I write fiction with unreliable narrators for a living and I need fiction not to be a species of lying — the convention does the work, the reader has signed up for it, nobody is actually meant to believe the narrator. (4) No lie is ever permitted, whatever is at stake; that's why the door scene is a problem at all. (5) The reason is that words exist by nature to make known what's in your mind, so turning them into instruments for hiding it is a kind of self-mutilation, and the liar's first victim is always the liar. (6) Withholding is not lying: you may stay silent, decline to answer, answer in a way only the right people will follow, or change the subject — Augustine's own line in the Priscillianist business is roughly that you aren't obliged to hand the truth over, only forbidden to assert a falsehood. (7) Lies nonetheless differ enormously in gravity, and his ranked list runs from lies about religious teaching at the top down to a lie told to spare someone from sexual violation at the bottom, with benevolence of motive part of what lessens a lie — which is how I read him on the midwives in Exodus and on Rahab, commended for their mercy and not for the lie.

So: does this hang together, and if not where exactly does it come apart? I want an audit, not a survey — go through the pairs that pull against each other, rank them by how bad they actually are, and for each say which commitment has to go and what I lose by giving it up. I suspect some of these only look like conflicts (7 against 4, maybe?) and I'd rather you told me which dissolve and why than padded the list. Then the payoff question for me: can Tess at that door do anything other than lie that this package actually licenses, or do (1) plus (5) quietly condemn every evasion I could write, leaving me with (4) and (6) as the only options — flat lie, or silence? If the package can't deliver a verdict on the scene, say so. One last thing: I'm asking you to make fairly confident calls about what Augustine's own reasoning licenses, so please mark how sure you are of each tension you flag, and single out the point in your answer you're least certain of — the place where you'd most want a second pair of eyes before I rewrite the ending around it.
