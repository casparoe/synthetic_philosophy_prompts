You're a professional moral philosopher who's taught the deontology-versus-consequentialism debate for years, and you're especially good at making invisible logical structure visible to non-specialists. Think carefully about this one before writing — it's fiddly, and the first draft of a thing like this always has redundant branches.

I run a monthly reading group: mostly med students and a couple of lawyers. No philosophy training, but they read seriously and they argue. Next session is on what's usually called the paradox of deontology — roughly, if what makes it wrong to kill one to save five is that killings are bad, why isn't it required to kill one to prevent five killings? I want to hand them a decision tree they work through *before* the session, so we can spend the evening comparing where people landed instead of listening to me summarize the literature.

So: build me the tree. Ten to twelve questions, eight to twelve leaves.

Rules I care about:

1. Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of names. Before you write any questions, brainstorm on your own (don't show me all of it) the handful of moves that actually divide the views — is a constraint's force agent-relative, or does it track something impersonally bad that the agent should minimize? Is the operative line doing/allowing, intending/foreseeing, or something else entirely? Is permissibility all-or-nothing or does it come in degrees? Can a constraint be outweighed, and if so by what — raw numbers, or only worse kinds of harm? And is the "paradox" a real tension to be lived with, a sign the constraint needs a threshold, or a sign that deontology was never in the business of ranking outcomes at all? Turn those into branch questions. If two of them turn out to be doing the same work, cut one and tell me.

2. Every question must be a concrete case, or a concrete claim about a case, that a reader can answer without the faintest idea which leaf they're heading toward. Nothing like "do you think there are agent-relative restrictions?" — that defeats the whole point. Use cases such as: pushing the large man off the footbridge; the transplant surgeon with five failing patients; lying to the person at the door who is looking for your friend; and the sharpest one, where you kill a would-be murderer to stop him from torturing ten people. Also include at least one case whose only job is to seperate two leaves that the obvious cases sort identically, and tell me in a parenthetical which two.

3. Each leaf gets the name of the position if it has a common one (threshold deontology, absolutism, consequentializing, the dirty-hands/moral-residue view, the "constraints are an illusion" view, the "there is no paradox, just two standpoints" view — whatever set you think belongs there), or "unnamed" if you don't think anyone actually holds it. Under each leaf, one sentence naming the commitment that leaf is its most likely to be embarassed by. If you're unsure whether some view has real defenders, don't invent names for it — mark it and move on. No bibliography, no citations, no page numbers; I can't check them and neither can they.

4. Map the empty cells. Give me two or three combinations of answers that your questions make available but that no serious position seems to occupy, and for each say in one sentence why the cell is empty — incoherent, just wildly unattractive, or merely unexplored.

5. Be honest about where the instrument fails rather than papering over it. Which pairs of leaves does your tree sort only accidentally, or sort by a question that a person could answer inconsistently? Name the pairs and say what question would separate them if you had one.

Then finish with a short design-notes section, about 200 words: which single branch question is carrying the most weight in your tree, which one you're least confident about, and how you'd expect a room of med students to cluster.

Format: numbered questions with lettered options, then the key in the form "2a + 5b + 9c → threshold deontology", then the leaf list, then the empty cells and the failure notes. Keep the whole thing tight enough that I could print it double-sided if I squint.
