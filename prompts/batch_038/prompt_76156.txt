So this has been rattling around in my head for years and it came back last week — I'm Dutch, lived in Manchester since 2010, no vote in the 2016 EU referendum, while my wife, who's Irish and came over a year after me, did get one. I want to work the referendum franchise through the boundary problem: Dahl's point that you can't democratically settle who belongs to the demos, plus the usual fixes people propose — all-affected interests, all-subjected-to-coercion, and the stakeholder/national-membership line.

Facts as I have them: it was basically the Westminster franchise plus Gibraltar, so Irish residents and qualifying Commonwealth residents voted and so did members of the House of Lords, but the roughly three million other EU nationals living here didn't, Brits abroad more than 15 years didn't, and 16 and 17 year olds didn't either even though they'd voted in the Scottish referendum two years earlier. Map that onto the criteria — which one the actual electorate best approximates, which one it most cleanly violates, and whether the mismatch is principled or just path-dependent (an election franchise bolted onto a question about a polity the UK was in the middle of leaving).

Then the bit I can't work out myself: does affectedness even get a grip on a vote like this, where who counts as affected depends on which way the vote goes? If Leave wins, the EU nationals here and the long-expat Brits are the ones whose lives get remade; if Remain wins almost nobody's status changes. That makes the principle seem unstateable in advance rather than just hard to apply. Is that a real problem for all-affected views or is there a way to fix the relevant set beforehand without smuggling in the answer — and does Brexit end up confirming the boundary problem, being an anomaly it can absorb, or showing that nobody actually wants the demos to track affectedness when it costs them anything?
