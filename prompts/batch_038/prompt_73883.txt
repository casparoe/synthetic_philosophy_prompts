I run a fortnightly reading group in the back room of a pub — mix of second-year philosophy students and a couple of people who've drifted over from the economics department — and next week we're on coherence arguments, i.e. that whole family of arguments saying that if your beliefs or preferences aren't shaped the way expected utility theory wants, you'll end up paying for it: Dutch books, money pumps, the representation theorems. Last session we did the sure-thing stuff and it went absolutely nowhere; everyone nodded, agreed that incoherence is bad, and then we spent twenty minutes talking about the crisps. I want to hand them a case that actually puts up a fight, and I want it built to order rather than lifted out of a textbook, since they'll have seen every textbook version already.

So: design me a decision-making body with a genuine preference cycle over three options, which then gets cleanly money-pumped by an outsider who happens to own all three. I'd like it to be a small committee rather than an individual — three people on a council sub-committee or a charity board, say, choosing between three ordinary things it could plausibly lease or buy. Constraints. Each member has a perfectly transitive ranking of their own, and you show me what it is and why they hold it. The cycle has to be generated by the way the committee votes, not by anyone being muddle-headed, and not by ties or vagueness. The pumper charges for each swap, but there are no other frictions — so don't wriggle out of it with legal fees, admin costs or stamp duty. Stakes a real body could face: no sci-fi, no exotic payoffs, no predictor of any kind. Keep the scenario itself to two paragraphs, then walk me through the sequence of swaps one at a time with the vote at each step — and do get the direction of the cycle right, thats the bit people fumble.

Then the part I actually care about. Once the committee has been pumped round twice and is properly out of pocket, lay out the repairs on offer — a chair with a casting vote, a fixed order of criteria, some kind of points system — and for each one show what it makes the committee choose and whether it agrees with the others; if one of them deadlocks instead of picking anything, that counts as an answer too, so tell me. The claim I want tested is that the coherence argument gets them as far as "you must transitivise" and then goes silent on which transitivisation to adopt, and I want to know if that's right or if I'm overstating it. Finish with the honest bit: which single feature of the case is doing all the work here, what happens to the argument if I delete it and there's just one decision-maker instead of a committee, and how someone who wants coherence arguments to stay in the business of establishing rationality would attack my case — whether by denying the committee is an agent at all, or by pointing out that the other coherence arguments don't even get a look-in here because there's no single preference relation for a representation theorem to represent. The point of the exercise is to get the group to see that exploitability and irrationality can come apart, and that "coherence argument" is a family rather than one argument, so please generate the conclusion out of the case instead of announcing it up front — a case that just stipulates its verdicts is useless to me. And if you think the whole thing collapses for a reason I haven't thought of, say so straight and explain why; I'd much rather find out now than on Thursday.
