My father died in March. He was a trial lawyer, raised Jain, who stopped going to temple at twenty but never stopped arguing, and the family has asked me — his daughter, and the only one of us who still keeps the practice — to give the eulogy. The room will be roughly a third Jain relatives, who know what anekāntavāda is and what the sevenfold schema of qualified predication is for, and two-thirds of his old colleagues and clients, who have never heard either phrase and would walk out if I lectured them.

Here is the difficulty I need you to solve. His standing objection to everything I was raised with was a version of the self-refutation charge: if every assertion holds only conditionally, from some standpoint, then that claim holds only conditionally too — so stop qualifying and tell me what you actually think. He lived by flat, unqualified commitment; in court he never conceded a fact he could avoid conceding. And a eulogy is structurally the kind of speech he wanted: it has to say what he was, not catalogue the respects in which he might be described otherwise.

Write the eulogy, six to eight minutes spoken. It must genuinely commit — definite claims about him that I would stand behind with no qualifier attached — and yet must not be what the tradition calls ekānta: a standpoint that denies every other standpoint its due. I want the doctrine doing structural work rather than decoration, so that it constrains what the speech can and cannot say, and I want it to answer his self-refutation charge rather than slide past it, in a form a lawyer would have recognized as an answer. Use no more than two technical terms, gloss each in passing, and don't include the blind men and the elephant — he hated that story. Don't end on a hedge.

Make the definite choices yourself: invent the particular memories, the particular turns of phrase, and the order of the argument, rather than giving me a template with blanks. Then add a short note saying where the speech leans on a contested reading of the doctrine — above all, anywhere it lets unqualified commitment do work that a stricter account of qualified predication would forbid.
