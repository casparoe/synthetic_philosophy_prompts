Background first, briefly, because it's why I'm asking rather than just googling. I'm a retired biochemist — thirty-eight years at the bench, mostly enzyme kinetics, the last ten running a lab. Since I stopped I've been reading political philosophy badly, meaning a lot and in no order, and I've got properly stuck on one thing.

The thing is church and state. My working life was split between Germany and England, so I've actually lived inside two of the arrangements people write about, and I've yet to find a philosopher's description of them that matches what they feel like from the inside.

Here's what I keep running into. Every account I read treats disestablishment as the neutral baseline and establishment as the deviation that owes you a justification — the French and American models come out clean, the English and German ones come out compromised. But the more I read the more I suspect there's no neutral option at all, and that each regime just hides its own commitment somewhere boring and administrative where nobody thinks to look for it. In my old line you'd call that an unexamined parameter that everything else got fitted to.

So, the raw material, as I understand it:

In England the monarch has to be a Protestant in communion with the Church of England, up to twenty-six of its bishops sit in the House of Lords, the church's own measures only become law once Parliament passes them, and as far as I can work out a parish priest still has to marry and bury the people of his parish, believer or not — the church is obliged to serve you whether you belong to it or not.

In Germany the state collects the church tax for you, as a surcharge of eight or nine percent on your income tax depending on the Land. Your employer withholds it because your religious affiliation is sitting in a register at the federal tax office. The only way to stop paying is to go to a civil office and formally renounce your membership — so it's the state, not the church, that decides who counts as a member for billing purposes. And then the bishops say that if you do renounce it you've cut yourself off from the sacraments. A civil form has a sacramental consequence, and a religious body's membership roll is a state database.

In France the state recognises and funds no religion, which is a genuinely impressive idea — except in Alsace-Moselle, which happened to be German in 1905, so the old concordat still runs there and the French Republic pays Catholic priests, Lutheran pastors and rabbis out of the national budget and has religious instruction in the state schools.

What I'd like you to write is an essay that argues this out and lands somewhere. Not a survey. Specifically:

1. Is there any real sense in which one of these three regimes is neutral and the others aren't? Or is the honest description that the choice is between different kinds of non-neutrality, and if so, on what grounds do you rank them?

2. Take the German case of the person who still believes everything the church teaches but doesn't want to pay. Who is doing the establishing there — the state, which keeps the register, or the church, which says leaving it costs you communion? I genuinely can't tell whether this case is an argument against church taxes or an argument that they're more honest than the American arrangement, which gets to pretend religion is a private club you opt into.

3. This is my own half-formed hunch and I can't get it straight: each regime seems to assume a different picture of what a person's religious belonging is. The American one assumes it's a voluntary association you choose, like joining a gym. The English one assumes you're inside a territorial church whether you like it or not, by geography. The German one assumes you're a registered member of a public-law body. If that's right then the whole neutrality debate is downstream of a question about which theory of belonging is actually true, and nobody in the debate wants to answer that question. Push on this. Tell me if it's confused — it probably is partly.

4. Then commit. Which regime would you defend, and give me the strongest objection from one of the other two, answered properly rather than waved at.

Two house rules. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I want the argument and I can't check references anyway. And do correct me if I've garbled any of the mechanics above — I've written them out from memory of living in them and I'd much rather know. Something I can read in one sitting, and say plainly at the end what you concluded.
