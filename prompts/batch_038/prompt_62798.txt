I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I've been circling a piece about negative evidence — how often a study that finds nothing gets reported as support for the hypothesis it was hunting. Every dark-matter null result, every failed replication, every survey that comes back empty quietly assumes that not-finding-X tells you something about X. Which is exactly the move Hempel's ravens makes you defend, so I want to build the companion interactive around that instead of around the science, because the raven thing is a clean little machine for sorting how people actually think about evidence.

So: a decision tree / self-sorting quiz, aimed at smart readers who have never seen the paradox and won't know any of the labels. About seven branch questions, each one a concrete situation rather than an abstract thesis — the green apple, the white shoe, that kind of thing — and phrased so someone can answer without already knowing which camp they're steering toward. Please make sure these cases are in there: (a) a plain green apple on a kitchen counter, does it give you any reason at all to think all ravens are black; (b) an ordering question, because I think it's the most diagnostic one — you're handed an object in a bag and told it isn't a raven before you look and see it's green, versus being told it isn't black before you look and see it's a shoe, and whether those two feel evidentially the same to you; (c) a background case — you've already gone through the whole crate of apples and now the last one turns out green, versus finding a green apple in a world you know nothing about; (d) a magnitude question about how much a black raven is worth compared to a white shoe, and whether the right answer depends on how many ravens versus non-ravens you think are out there; (e) something that gets at whether catching a black raven could ever lower your confidence in the hypothesis, which is the Good move I've seen people fight about for years; and (f) a question about whether a non-black non-raven counts as evidence only when you went looking in a way that could have turned up a counterexample. No formal notation, keep any arithmetic to a sentence of plain English.

Then the leaves, each with the name of the position (or "unoccupied" if you think nobody seriously defends it) plus one sentence on the most awkward thing that position is stuck with. I want the tree to at least be able to land people on: Hempel's own response, which keeps the equivalence of the two formulations and bites the bullet about Nicod; the line that rejects the equivalence condition and so lets the two statements come apart evidentially (Scheffler-ish); the standard Bayesian answer, yes-but-infinitesimally; the background-sensitive Bayesian answer where the amount and even the sign can flip; a natural-kinds or projectibility answer on which "raven" and "non-black" just aren't the sort of predicates the paradox needs; and a falsificationist answer on which a white shoe doesn't corroborate anything because you never put it to the test. Two more things I'd like: mark the combinations of answers that are logically available but empty, and say for each whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it just hasn't occured to anyone; and be honest about which pairs of leaves your questions can't actually seperate — I have a suspicion the Hempel bite-the-bullet camp and the Bayesian tiny-amount camp give identical answers to every case a layperson can be asked, and if that's right the tree should say so out loud instead of pretending it can tell them apart.
