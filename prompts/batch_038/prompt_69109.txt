I've just started work as a clinical evaluator on a state unit that assesses people held under our sexually violent predator statute, and I would like help thinking clearly about what I have walked into. Please take the role of a philosopher of criminal law with deep expertise in the retributivism–consequentialism debate, and think carefully rather than reaching for the standard talking points.

Here is the practice, described as neutrally as I can manage. A person convicted of a specified sexual offense serves their sentence in full — every day the court imposed. Before release, the state asks a court to find that the person has a "mental abnormality" or personality disorder that leaves them with serious difficulty controlling sexually violent behavior and makes them likely to reoffend. If the court agrees, the person is moved to a secure facility run by the corrections department, indefinitely, with periodic reviews. The state maintains that this is civil treatment rather than punishment: it is justified by what the person might do, not by what they did, and it ends when the condition ends rather than when a term expires. In practice, the facility is largely indistinguishable from a prison, release effectively requires completing treatment, and many residents stay for decades. Meanwhile the state will not detain anyone who has never been convicted of a qualifying offense, however strong the evidence of their dangerousness.

What I want from you is the presupposition analysis. Begin with a short diagnostic pass listing the features that seem to pull against one another, then work through:

- What theory of punishment, or what hybrid, must be true for this arrangement to be coherent? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and mark where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license it.
- The trigger problem: eligibility requires a completed conviction, but the stated justification is entirely forward-looking. On what principle can a past offense matter to a purely preventive rationale without the whole thing collapsing back into punishment?
- What does the practice assume about responsibility and disorder, about dangerousness as a diagnosable condition, and about whether the civil/criminal distinction tracks the legislature's declared purpose or the detainee's experience?
- Which rival views does it rule out, and which does it betray while claiming to honor them?
- Finally, what would concretely have to change in the institution if we replaced the presupposed view with strict retributivism, with unconstrained consequentialist prevention, and with a quarantine or liability-to-defensive-harm model.

No citations or references — I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
