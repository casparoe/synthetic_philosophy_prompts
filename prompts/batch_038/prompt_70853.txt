I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and over the past eighteen months three unrelated assignments have kept running into the same slogan: reasons without a reasoner — sometimes phrased as free-floating rationale, a formulation I associate with Dennett, though half the people who deploy it have plainly never read him. The first was a public lecture by a philosopher of biology on functional explanation, where the slogan did the load-bearing work in an argument that the design argument is finished: hearts pump, eyes resolve images, and talk of what a trait is for is perfectly legitimate even though nobody ever had the purpose in mind. The second was a conference panel on large language models, where a researcher remarked that a trained system is itself a site of free-floating rationale — its behaviour is explained by reasons represented neither by its designers nor, on his own account, by the system — and then slid without pause into the claim that we should therefore take the model's reasons seriously when predicting what it will do, as though the slogan spanned that gap. The third was a book review I was editing, in which the reviewer objected to an adaptationist work of popular science on the grounds that if a rationale need not be represented by anyone, then positing one can never be a mistake: the notion explains everything and so explains nothing.

What I need is not an explanation of the slogan, and not a defence or refutation of it, but a disambiguation I can carry into interviews. Lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims that there are reasons without reasoners could be making, ordered from the ones that amount to little more than a platitude about how we talk to the ones that would be genuinely revisionary if true. For each, say what has to be the case for it to hold, whether it is plausible, and — the part I care about most — whether it is strong enough to do the work each of my three speakers wanted from it. My hunch is that the biologist needs something quite modest, that the AI researcher needs something far stronger than he is entitled to, and that the reviewer's unfalsifiability charge only bites on one particular reading; I simply cannot say which.

Please mark explicitly where the ambiguity sits: what is being claimed about the rationale (a counterfactual-supporting explanation cast in the idiom of reasons, a normative consideration counting in favour of an outcome, a content that something bears, a purpose attributable purely from the outside), and what free-floating is meant to deny (that anyone represents it, that anyone owns it, that it depends on minds at all, that anyone intended it). Note which readings collapse into one another and which are genuinely in tension — in particular, whether a reading on which the slogan is a harmless restatement of design-without-a-designer can also be the reading that makes worries about the instrumental status of intentional-stance ascriptions bite. Don't send me to the literature and don't hand me a bibliography; I want the menu, not the citations. And close with one short question I could put to each of the three speakers that would reveal, from the answer alone, which reading they are committed to.
