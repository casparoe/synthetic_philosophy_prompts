I am preparing a statement of my position in accuracy-first epistemology for a workshop at which the other participants will be asked to attack it, and I would like a rigorous audit of the package before I circulate it. Below are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can manage. I am not looking for an exposition of the framework, and I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or references to particular authors or papers; please argue entirely from the content of what I have written. I have no prior attachment to any item and would rather be told which one has to go than be reassured that everything coheres.

1. Veritism about epistemic value. Accuracy — the closeness of a credence function to the truth — is the only fundamental epistemic good. Coherence, evidential support, and reliability have no independent normative weight; each counts as a norm only insofar as accuracy-based considerations explain why it does.

2. A two-track methodology. Static norms are established by accuracy-dominance arguments: a credence function is irrational just in case some alternative available to the agent is strictly more accurate than it at every possible world. Dynamic norms are established by expected-accuracy arguments: an updating rule is rational just in case, assessed by the agent's current credences, it maximizes expected accuracy among the rules she could adopt. I regard the dominance track as free of any charge of question-begging, because it never consults the agent's own credences in order to determine what counts as accurate, and I want that feature to be a general virtue of accuracy-first argumentation rather than a local one.

3. Probabilism follows from the dominance track for any legitimate inaccuracy measure. Measure-invariance is a desideratum for me: no conclusion I defend should rest on a partisan choice of scoring rule.

4. Nevertheless, legitimacy is tightly constrained. A measure of inaccuracy is legitimate only if it is bounded, additive across propositions, continuous, and truth-directed. On those conditions the quadratic score is essentially the only candidate, and I count that uniqueness as an achievement of the framework rather than a limitation on it.

5. Strict propriety, and hence immodesty. Every rational credence function expects itself, by its own lights, to be strictly more accurate than any rival. Without this the enterprise is self-undermining: an agent who expected some alternative to be more accurate would have an accuracy-based reason to abandon her own doxastic state.

6. Permissivism. For at least some bodies of evidence, more than one credence function is rationally permitted. What moves me here is the phenomenon of agents who appear equally well informed and equally reflective yet settle on different doxastic attitudes, without either being criticizable.

7. Imprecision. For propositions about which the agent has no relevant background, or where her evidence is genuinely indeterminate, rationality requires a set-valued credence rather than any precise real number, and it is a mistake to sharpen arbitrarily. My standing reply to the objection that such sets are accuracy-dominated is that every member of the set is itself a probability function, so Probabilism is satisfied and nothing has been conceded.

8. Actual accuracy is never a ground of criticism. An agent whose credence lands near the truth by luck is not thereby rational, and one whose well-formed credence happens to miss is not thereby irrational. Norms must be statable without reference to how things turn out.

9. The side-effects objection misses my view. It is said that maximizing total accuracy permits sacrificing accuracy in one proposition in order to gain it in another, and so licenses believing against one's evidence when doing so pays off elsewhere. I do not think this touches me: because my measure is additively separable, inaccuracy decomposes proposition by proposition, and there is accordingly no such thing as pooling accuracy across propositions in order to trade one off against another.

Two private suspicions of my own, which I expect may be misplaced. First, I keep feeling that (1) and (8) cannot both be true: if accuracy is the only epistemic good, why should the good that actually obtains play no part at all in assessing an agent? Second, I worry that (5) and (6) conflict, since immodesty appears to say that each credence function is uniquely best from its own point of view while permissivism says that several may be permitted by the same evidence.

What I want from you is an audit, ordered by severity, not a survey. Specifically:

- Identify every pair or larger subset of these commitments that is genuinely in tension. For each, state the mechanism of the conflict in a few sentences, then give the cheapest revision that removes it and say what that revision costs — including which of my results (Probabilism, the expected-accuracy argument for an updating rule, measure-uniqueness, immodesty) survive it and in what weakened form.
- Say explicitly whether (2) can sustain both the anti-question-begging claim and the expected-accuracy track simultaneously, and if not, which half of the framework has to be given up or reformulated.
- For (7), say what a set-valued credence is supposed to be scored by at a world, whether strict propriety has any formulation at all in that setting, and whether my reply about the members of the set actually engages the dominance objection or simply restates the view under attack.
- For (9), say whether additive separability does the work I claim for it, given how I propose to argue for dynamic norms.
- Separately, list the apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection and explain why, including an explicit verdict on each of my two suspicions, whether or not it is what I expect.
- Finish by naming the single revision that buys the largest reduction in overall tension, and by saying whether what remains is a recognizable position or an unstable hybrid of two incompatible research programs. Flag any commitment that is doing load-bearing work in an argument I have not noticed.
