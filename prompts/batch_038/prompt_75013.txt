So our philosophy-of-biology reading group made everyone write up a one-pager on where they actually stand, and I'm the only working systematist in the room, so mine ended up being about species. Problem is, now that it's written down in one column I can't tell whether the thing hangs together, and the philosophers will smell blood. Here's the package, roughly as I'd defend it:

1. I'm with Ghiselin and Hull on the individuality thesis: a species is a spatio-temporally bounded chunk of lineage, a particular and not a class, and an organism belongs to it by historical connection rather than by satisfying a description.
2. So no essentialism about species. There's no property set that makes something a member, and I think people still hunting for species essences just havn't absorbed what Darwin did to that project.
3. But in my own delimitation practice I treat fixed diagnosability as what settles the question, and I'd defend that as principled rather than as a cheap proxy for something else. If a population has a character that's fixed in it and absent elsewhere in the group — morphology or mitochondrial, I don't much care which — I'll go ahead and describe it. I don't think I need gene-flow data or an argument about reproductive isolation to make the call.
4. I'm a pluralist about species concepts in the ordinary sense: no one definition is going to run across animals, plants and prokaryotes at once, and which concept you reach for depends on the question you're asking.
5. All the same, I think there's a determinate fact about how many species are in a given clade, and my conservation-adjacent work leans hard on that. Richness counts, phylogenetic diversity metrics, triage lists — they only make sense if the number isn't up to us.
6. Case verdict I actually care about: the Ensatina ring around the Central Valley (yes, I know people dispute the ring story). My view is that the whole ring is one species, because it's one continuous genealogical nexus, *and* that the two forms that come together in the south are two species, because where they overlap they behave like textbook biological species. I don't think I have to pick between those answers, and I don't think it's just a borderline case or vagueness in my concepts.
7. I'm a nominalist about genus and everything above it — those ranks are conveniences we impose — but I do think the species rank is objectively privileged, because it's the rank at which lineages actually split.
8. And I want bdelloid rotifers and bacterial clades to count as species in the same sense as my geckos, not as a homonym or an honorary usage.

A friend who reads this stuff for fun told me the obvious wreckage is 7 next to 4, and that I'm just special-pleading for my own rank, but I don't buy it and I'm probably not the right person to judge. So: go through the list and tell me which of these genuinely can't live together and which pairs only look like they can't. For each real tension I want to know how bad it is — rank them, don't flatten them into a list of equally worrying problems — and what specifically would have to go to fix it, including what giving it up costs me in practice, since I do have descriptions to publish and a committee to satisfy. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most coherence for the least damage. I'm not after a tour of the species problem or a survey of who said what; I want this list audited, line by line, with the ones that are fine explicitly cleared.
