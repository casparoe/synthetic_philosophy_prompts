I am drafting a paper on the inefficacy objection to claims about individual contributions to collective harms — the sort of objection raised against the wrongness of buying meat, taking a flight, or failing to vote — and I would like critical comments on a near-complete draft. I have deliberately built the argument so that its own final section threatens to undermine it, and that is where I most need pressure.

---

**The Act-Slot Picture: Why the Inefficacy Objection Is a Category Error That Refuses to Stay Dead**

*1. The debate as usually staged.* Against the claim that individual consumption or emission is wrong, the inefficacy objection says: this act of mine makes no difference to anyone's welfare, and an act that changes nothing cannot be wrong. Replies come in two families. Causal replies deny the premise: because production and distribution respond in discrete increments, there is some probability that my purchase is the one that triggers a new order, so my expected marginal harm is positive and roughly proportionate to the aggregate harm divided among the contributors. Non-causal replies deny the premise's relevance: wrongness here is a matter of integrity, complicity, virtue, or of what I would be doing together with others were we acting jointly.

*2. A shared picture.* Both families, I argue, accept what I call the act-slot picture. On this picture there is a fixed slot — the individual act token — and the entire dispute is over what belongs on the card inserted into it: expected consequences, expressive content, a maxim, a disposition. Because the two families write different things on the card, the disagreement looks like a substantive moral one. But it sits downstream of a question nobody in the debate asks, namely whether the individual act token is the right bearer of the wrongness at all.

*3. The proposal: participation first.* In paradigm contribution cases, wrongness is borne primarily by a participation relation between an act and a set of acts that jointly produces harm, and only derivatively by the act itself. The inefficacy objection is then a category error: it demands of an act that it carry a property belonging to a relation. This explains something otherwise puzzling — the stability of the debate. Neither side can answer the other, because each is supplying a card for a slot the other believes to be the wrong slot.

The view is immediately threatened by promiscuity. Participation is cheap: I participate in sets that fund practices I deplore, in sets whose members I will never meet, in sets that will outlive me. So the account needs criteria for admissible sets. I propose three. (i) *Non-gerrymandering*: the set must be specifiable without reference to the harmfulness of the act whose wrongness is at issue; otherwise participation is merely consequences under a new name. (ii) *Avoidability*: membership must be escapable at a cost that is not unreasonable, so that the relation says something about the agent's situation rather than about the world's. (iii) *Monotonicity*: the harm must be an increasing function of the number or proportion of members, which lets us say that more members means more harm without claiming that any one member causes it.

*4. The crux, which I cannot currently resolve.* Criterion (iii) is where the trouble is. If harm is a monotone function of the participation number, and that number is what it is whether or not I join, then my participation does not change the harm — and the account has rebuilt, one level up, the very structure it was meant to escape. The inefficacy objection returns as a set-level objection. My tentative response is that monotonicity secures the counterfactual sensitivity of the *relation* rather than of the outcome: fewer members, less harm, and my act is among those that make the number what it is. But this sits disquietingly close to the truism that my act is part of the aggregate, which holds of any act with respect to any aggregate containing it. So either the proposal is empty, or it is covertly the threshold story again, with thresholds relocated from distributors to sets.

*5. The conclusion I may not be able to reach.* If the crux in section 4 is fatal, the honest conclusion is not that the inefficacy objection wins but that the act-slot picture is inescapable: contribution-type wrongs must be analyzed in terms of what individual acts do, and the non-causal replies are best read as claims about the character or meaning of acts rather than as claims about a distinct bearer of wrongness. That is a deflationary result, and it costs the participation view its reason for existing.

(b) Whether the argument of section 4 succeeds — that monotonicity reproduces the inefficacy objection one level up. (e) Beyond these, identify the single weakest paragraph, and say whether the label "act-slot picture" is doing real work or is only a memorable name for a distinction that fails to carve the subject matter at a joint.
