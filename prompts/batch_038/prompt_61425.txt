I am preparing the closing session of an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of money, in which we work through the first book of the Politics and the treatment of exchange and reciprocity in the fifth book of the Nicomachean Ethics, and then turn to early modern drama. Last night I had a long and rather heated argument with a friend who teaches in the English department about how to frame the Merchant of Venice session, and I would like an independent judgment before I commit to a reading in front of students. Her position, which she defended with some force, is that the play is a straightforward Aristotelian fable: Shylock practices what Aristotle calls unnatural acquisition, his interest is money breeding money, Aristotle's term for it doubling as the word for offspring, and the plot simply enacts the punishment that Aristotle thinks the practice deserves; Antonio, who lends without interest, stands for the natural economy of the household, and Belmont's land and goods are the natural wealth against which Venice's ledgers are measured. My counter was that this reading is both textually lazy and ethically indefensible, since it launders the play's anti-Judaism as Aristotelian orthodoxy, and that the genuine pressure points between the play and Aristotle lie elsewhere: in the claim that money exists by convention rather than by nature, which is what the casket test actually examines, since Morocco and Arragon fail by reading worth off the appearance of the metal while Bassanio succeeds by choosing lead, that is, by accepting that the sign carries no intrinsic value; in the requirement of a single common measure for reciprocal exchange, which I think is what makes the pound-of-flesh bond unintelligible rather than merely cruel, since flesh admits of no unit in which an exact return could be rendered; and in the fact that Antonio's own wealth is aboard ships, a value consisting entirely in expectation and risk, which is not household management either and may be closer to the unnatural kind than Shylock's ledger. I also raised the ring plot as a limit case: a token given as inalienable turns out to be perfectly transferable, which suggests the play doubts that anything can be withdrawn from exchange at all.

What I want from you is a full critical reading of the play through this Aristotelian lens, of roughly two thousand words, written as criticism rather than as a summary of the plot or of the theory. Take both sides seriously: assume your reader knows the play and has read Aristotle, so no scene-by-scene narration and no exposition of the Politics beyond what is needed to make a point. Work through the four elements I have listed, say which of them genuinely hold up under scrutiny and which I overstated at dinner, and attend specifically to whether Portia's insistence on the letter of the bond — the argument that the deed grants flesh but not blood — is better understood as a sophistical trick or as a serious demonstration that no unit of measure can individuate what the bond demands. I would also like you to say what to do with the mercy speech, which is usually read as Christian grace opposing legal justice but which seems to me closer to Aristotle's account of equity as the correction of law's necessary generality, and to assess whether that alternative survives contact with the scene. Then do the honest critic's work in the other direction: identify where the play actively resists the Aristotelian frame, where forcing it would require distorting either the theory or the drama, and how a reading like mine can avoid simply ratifying the caricature of Shylock as the embodiment of sterile money. Point me to the scenes that carry the argument, giving act and scene where you are confident of them and describing the scene otherwise; I do not want verbatim quotation of Shakespeare or Aristotle, since I will be working with the texts in hand, and no bibliography or reference list either, as I will supply those myself.

Two further constraints. First, I know perfectly well that there is an established scholarly tradition linking Renaissance drama to scholastic and Aristotelian debates about usury, so please do not reproduce the standard charity-versus-usury line; what interests me is the narrower and stranger set of claims about convention, commensurability, and the sterility of money, and about whether the play's ending — the news of the ships, the restoration at Belmont, the rings returned — describes an economy Aristotle would recognize as natural or one his categories simply cannot see. Second, end by telling me what the play contributes to the philosophical question that argument alone does not: whether there is something about being treated as a term in a contract, or about demanding an exact return from someone who cannot be measured, that drama can show and treatise cannot state. If your conclusion is that the Aristotelian lens is worth one session but not three, say so plainly and tell me what I should teach instead.
