I am a novelist, and the third act of the book I am working on turns on a document that does not exist. Two of my characters have spent nine years attacking one another in print, and a certification authority — invented, though I need its product to be real — has compelled them to sit down and issue a joint statement on a question that has no settled answer anywhere: how much assurance a deployed AI system must carry before it may be placed in front of people who cannot withdraw from it.

I would like you to write the statement. Not an outline of one, and not a description of what such a document would contain, but the artifact itself, complete enough that I could set it as an epigraph or print it as an appendix and have a reader who works in the field tell me I got it right.

The parties. Ilse Brandt proves things. Her position is that an argument whose force depends on the environment's continued cooperation is not a claim about the future but a summary of the past; that the distance between a system's measured behavior and its behavior under conditions nobody sampled is exactly where catastrophic failure lives; and that she does not assert worst-case guarantees to be presently attainable for large learned systems, nor proofs to be cheap — she holds that a deployment made without one is running on credit that someone else will eventually collect. Amos Reyes evaluates things. His position is that every guarantee of every strength rests on assumptions whose only warrant is empirical and revisable; that a theorem about a model class is silent concerning the artifact in the rack, which belongs to no class anyone can characterize; and that conditioning deployment on worst-case assurance is not a neutral rule but a filter, favoring systems simple enough to be certified over systems that are in fact least dangerous. He takes the honest unit of assurance to be a maintained, adversarially probed record of what a system has been made to do.

Between them sits the clause that brought them to the table: the authority's residual-uncertainty provision, which allows an applicant to substitute a documented empirical case for a guarantee whenever a guarantee is not attainable by any presently known method, provided the applicant states what the missing guarantee would have been worth. Both my characters believe this clause is either the most sensible or the most dangerous sentence in the instrument, and they disagree about which.

The document should contain:

1. A brief preamble fixing the scope of the statement and specifying what it is not — in particular, what the two have agreed not to claim.

2. A numbered section of claims both signatories accept. I want at least ten, and each must be one a serious partisan could plausibly have refused to sign. Nothing of the form "safety matters" or "the question is difficult" belongs here.

3. A disagreements table, with these columns: the disagreement in neutral wording; Ilse's formulation of her own side, in language she would accept without amendment; Amos's formulation of his, likewise; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or terminological, with one sentence of justification. Where you judge a dispute verbal, say which part is verbal and what is left standing once the words are settled.

4. Three to five cruxes — sub-questions such that a clean answer would actually move at least one signatory — together with an account of what moving would consist in for each of them. For every crux, give a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, before the result is known. A crux on which both predict the same outcome is not a crux; if you find such a thing, say so, then sharpen it or drop it.

5. A treatment of the three questions the authority has formally put to them: what the residual-uncertainty provision should require of an applicant, and whether a guarantee whose assumption set has been tuned until the theorem applies differs in kind from an evaluation whose suite has been tuned until the system passes; how one is to tell evidence presented in the rhetorical register of a bound from evidence that is a bound — a practice Ilse calls assurance laundering and Amos says her camp commits more of than his; and whether the population over which an average-case claim is measured is fixed by the deployment environment or is partly chosen by the system whose behavior is being measured.

6. A closing procedural section: what the two commit to doing over the following year, which of the cruxes they will pursue jointly, and what would oblige either of them to retract a claim appearing in the agreed section.

7. A signature block, followed by one short dissenting note from each party, stating the single thing they refused to let be softened.

Constraints. No citations, no references, no named researchers, papers, standards, or products: my fact-checker treats every such name as invented, and I would rather not check. Where the argument needs a technical anchor, describe the mechanism instead of naming its literature. Prefer qualitative predictions to invented quantities; when a magnitude genuinely matters, give an order of magnitude and attribute it to the signatory who holds it. Do not allow the two to collapse into mutual appreciation — the worth of the document lies in how exactly the residue of their disagreement is stated, and it should read as though written by two people who distrust each other and have agreed to be scrupulous. American English throughout, plain declarative prose, tables where a table earns its place. Roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
