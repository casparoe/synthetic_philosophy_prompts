I've been reading late-period Nietzsche on and off for a few years, mostly through the lens I picked up on the rationalist blogs: treat the man's claims as testable hypotheses about psychology rather than as aphorisms to be admired. What keeps pulling me back is how much weight he puts on the body's inputs. In the autobiographical retrospect he devotes an unusually long stretch to nutrition, climate, and metabolism — arguing, in effect, that the question of how one feeds oneself is not a domestic triviality but a genuinely philosophical problem, and that the collapse of a culture can be read off its cooking and its drinking. Elsewhere he treats alcohol as one of the two great corrosives in European history and insists that a single glass is already one too many for a productive mind. And then there is the biographical complication: the same man who preached total abstinence spent decades dosing himself with sedatives and opium preparations for his insomnia and migraines, and treated his migrations between Alpine and Mediterranean locations as a kind of dietary-metabolic technology rather than as mere travel.

I want a serious counterfactual, not a costume drama. Suppose Nietzsche were resurrected intact — his physiological commitments, his diagnostic temperament, his hostility to anything that smells of asceticism or of moralizing about the body, his conviction that health and philosophy are continuous — and confronted with the current landscape of metabolic self-management. Run him through the following, one at a time: GLP-1 receptor agonists taken by people who are overweight but not diabetic, purely for body composition; continuous glucose monitors worn by non-diabetics as an optimization instrument; the moralized vocabulary of "clean eating" and the orthorexia-adjacent wellness culture; the biohacking stack of supplements, creatine, metformin taken for longevity, and nootropics; the sober-curious and mocktail movement; and the practice of relocating for climate and air quality on the theory that environment determines output. For each, tell me what he would actually say, and rank them from what he'd consider the healthiest expression of self-mastery to the most decadent, with your confidence stated for each ranking.

The question I most want pressed is the one his framework seems least equipped to answer. Nietzsche wants to distinguish discipline that affirms life from discipline that negates it, yet the observable behavior in the two cases can be identical: one person declines a drink, another injects a peptide that abolishes appetite, and from outside they look equally abstemious. What criterion, if any, can his own thinking supply to tell these apart — is it the affect accompanying the refusal, the resulting capacity for expenditure, the honesty about one's motives, something else? And how should we handle the sedatives: is his own pharmacological self-medication a straightforward hypocrisy that a resurrection would force him to confront, or is there a distinction available to him between medicine taken to restore the capacity to work and narcotic taken to escape it? Please flag wherever the extrapolation is doing more work than the evidence — I'd rather see the seams than a confident character study. Paraphrase his positions rather than quoting them; I don't want reconstructed sentences in quotation marks, and I don't want a bibliography.
