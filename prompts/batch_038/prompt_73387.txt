I'm the science and ideas reporter at a mid size digital magazine, and I also sit on our three-person standards and corrections panel, which is me, the managing editor, and a retired contracts lawyer who reads everything twice and underlines things. Here's the mess. In the spring we ran a feature on a de-extinction start-up that says it has produced three living calves whose genomes were built by editing Asian elephant embryos with variants recovered from permafrost specimens — mammoth variants, mostly, but also some that turn up in mastodon and in living elephants, pulled from something like a dozen different animals that died tens of thousands of years apart. Our headline, which the desk loved, was "The Mammoth Is Back." The reporter defends it, and honestly so do I on most days: the company says mammoth, the funders say mammoth, the embryos were gestated in elephants under a mammoth-branded program, and the piece itself is 4,000 words of careful hedging in which basically every expert we quote says the animal is at best a rough functional approximation. But one paleogeneticist we interviewed wrote in afterwards — not to us, to a listserv that someone forwarded me — objecting that the headline asserts something that isn't just unproven but impossible, and that we've handed readers a metaphysical claim dressed as a news item.

That's the part I can't get my head around, and it's why I want the memo thought through before Thursday. I half-remember from a college seminar, and from a podcast I put on during a red-eye, that there's a whole apparatus for this kind of thing: the idea that a term like "water" picks out H2O rigidly, so once you've found out what water is you can say the look-alike liquid on Twin Earth isn't water even though it fills the same role, and that this is supposed to be a necessity you discover empirically rather than by definition. Does any of that carry over to species names, or does it break down, because species don't seem to be substances with essences the way water is — I've seen people argue a species is more like a historical individual than a natural kind, in which case "could this calf have been a mammoth" gets weird in a totally different way. And then there's the origin business: the calves came out of elephant eggs and elephant wombs, so if lineage is what fixes identity, no amount of sequence-splicing could ever make one a mammoth, whereas if what fixes the kind is the functional genome, the verdict flips. I'd like the memo to actually sort out which of these is a claim about the world and which is a claim about our word, because my strong suspicion is that the paleogeneticist and our reporter are using "impossible" in two different senses and that's the real disagreement underneath the email fight.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate to the panel. I need a clear bottom line — correction, editor's note, headline stays with a clarification added to the top of the piece, or we stand pat — plus a time frame and the actual wording of whatever we'd post, since we will definitely get asked about it. Then the reasoning, and I want the strongest version of the reporter's side stated seriously rather than as a strawman: she's smart, she'll read this, and I'd rather we lose the argument cleanly than win it cheaply. If you think we should adopt a standing rule for how we headline engineered organisms whose identity is contested, propose it in a sentence or two that a copy editor could apply on deadline without a philosophy degree. No reading list and no citations, please — I can't check them and neither can my colleagues, and I'd rather have the reasoning than the name-dropping. American English, and keep it under about 900 words.
