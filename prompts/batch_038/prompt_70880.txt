Last night I spent the better part of two hours arguing with a friend about John Ford's *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance*, and we ended up further apart than we began. His reading is that the film is simply disenchanted: the statehood agitation, the literacy school in the back of the *Shinbone Star*, the arriving railroad, and Ranse Stoddard's whole ascent to the Senate are the trappings of a progress myth, and the closing moments — Hallie's confession about the cactus rose, the conductor's deference to the man who supposedly shot Liberty Valance — prove that Ford no longer believes a word of it. My position was that the film is doing something more precise than cynicism: that it stages the manufacture of a progressive narrative in real time and then asks whether that narrative was worth the truth it cost. I would like you to adjudicate between us by reading the film through Herbert Butterfield's critique of the Whig interpretation of history.

I want the reading to apply Butterfield's specific charges rather than the loose sense of "Whig" as a synonym for smug optimism. Consider, in particular: the charge that such history "studies the past with reference to the present," which the film's structure performs literally, since the entire flashback is narrated by a sitting senator at the funeral of the man whose deed he is credited with; the concept of the abridgement, and whether the film shows us which events are selected in and which are dropped out; the substitution of a "line of causation" for actual causal enquiry; and the dualist casting in which one party is made to have "contributed nothing to the making of the present," serving only as a foil to the virtues of the winners. Then test that casting against the film's own personnel. Liberty Valance and the cattle interests who hire him are the obvious obstructing party, and Stoddard the obvious agent of progress — but Tom Doniphon belongs to neither column, since he produces the outcome and cannot be admitted into its story, and Pompey, whose rifle does the actual killing, is excluded twice over. Is Butterfield's dualism an accurate description of what the legend does, or does the film expose a third category his taxonomy lacks: the person who makes the present and is therefore edited out of it?

Two further questions matter to me. First, is the legend even false? Stoddard did fire; Doniphon shot Valance at the same instant with Pompey's rifle. Whether the sentence "the man who shot Liberty Valance" is true of Stoddard, false of him, or true of both depends on an analysis of simultaneous and pre-emptive causation that the film declines to supply. Second, what is the status of the evidence? The only account of the ambush survives inside a senator's own reminiscence, the newspaper office has already been wrecked, and the present-day editor burns the reporter's notes on the ground that one should "print the legend" once it has hardened into fact. Treat that decision as a piece of historiography performed by a professional narrator of the present, and say whether the film endorses it, indicts it, or merely exhibits it. You may also find it useful to bring in Hayden White's talk of emplotment — is the surface story a romance of law triumphing over violence, and does the frame convert it into tragedy? — and Danto's idea of the narrative sentence, the description whose truth only becomes available later, in the light of what followed.

Please be as scrupulous about the film as about the theory. Do not reduce *Liberty Valance* to an illustration of Butterfield's pamphlet, and do not bend Butterfield to make it fit. Butterfield's target was the professional historian, and his alternative — approaching the past for its own sake, delighting in the concrete and the complex — may simply be unavailable to a two-hour popular narrative, which must abridge by definition. Say where the lens strains, where the film's own nostalgia for the frontier it depicts cuts against an anti-Whig reading, and whether Butterfield's polemic carries a presentism of its own that the film quietly exploits. Paraphrase dialogue and scenes rather than quoting them, and do not bother with references or further reading. End by telling me plainly which of the two positions in my argument the film supports, if either, and by saying what *Liberty Valance* contributes to the debate about progress-narratives that a philosophical argument about them, on its own, could not.
