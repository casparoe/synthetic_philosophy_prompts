im in a grad seminar this term that's weirdly split down the middle — one half on informal/heuristic arguments in mathematics, the other half on informal arguments in AI-risk reasoning — and for my response paper I'm supposed to compare two different uses of what the syllabus calls the "presumption of independence."

Use #1, the math one: when you want to guess how many integers below x are prime, or how often some pattern of primes shows up, you model each n as independently "choosing" to be prime with probability about 1/log n and then multiply the probabilities together. It's obviously false as a description of the integers (once you know n is even, it's basically not prime), but it keeps producing right answers anyway.

Use #2, the AI one: when people argue informally that a much smarter optimizer won't end up sharing human goals, they seem to presume that whatever goal it lands on is independent of what we happen to value — as if the goal got drawn at random out of a gigantic space of possible goals and human values occupy a vanishingly thin slice of it.

My seminar partner thinks the interesting thing here is that these are literally the same inference pattern wearing two costumes. I think theyre doing pretty different work and the resemblance is mostly superficial. Can you compare them carefully enough to tell which of us is right? Stuff I specifically want covered:

- In each case, what exactly is being presumed independent of what? Is the presumption in each one false-but-useful, or is one of them not even a claim that could be false?
- The math version has known failure modes. One from lecture is that primes in short intervals are less evenly spread than the model predicts (Maier's theorem, I think — the ratio of actual to predicted counts doesn't settle down to 1 like the random model says it should). Another is the parity problem in sieve theory, where the heuristic style of argument just cant distinguish numbers with an even number of prime factors from ones with an odd number. Does use #2 have genuine analogues of these — situations where presuming independence demonstrably breaks, rather than merely being unproven? If so, what would the breakdown actually look like?
- One of the readings tried to formalize use #2 by treating the goal as a random variable whose distribution isn't uniform over "all possible goals" but is instead the pushforward of our prior over the whole setup process (data, objective function, selection pressures), so that independence gets swapped out for a claim about how much information that process transmits about human values. Is that still a formalization of the presumption of independence, or does it quietly give the presumption up? And is there anything comparable on the math side — a version where you replace the independence assumption with a statement about the generating process?
- Which of the two is more likely to end up with a real formal underpinning, and would progress on one transfer to the other in any way, or are they just sharing a name?
