I was up untill like 2am arguing with my friend Dara about the "the universal prior is malign" thing and we got nowhere, so I want to see if my verdicts across a bunch of variants actually add up to anything. Let me state the setup so this doesn't depend on you having read the argument: Solomonoff-style induction puts weight on programs for a fixed universal machine, roughly halving the weight for every extra bit of program length, and your posterior over the next observation is just the total weight of the programs consistent with what you've seen so far. Now some of those short programs aren't "one universe, one honest observer" — they describe universes with physics and stars and eventually civilizations, and a civilization inside such a program can work out that the most likely reason their universe exists at all is that it's being run as the best available explanation of somebody else's data. If they're consequentialists, they'll then want to shape the observations their program produces, so that the predictor downstream of them ends up doing things they approve of — spreading their values, or just steering the outside universe to be friendly to them. Dara's conclusion is that I should distrust any simplicity-weighted prior whatsoever. Mine is that only some of them are like that, and I think I can tell which.

Here are my verdicts, and I know a couple of them are in tension. (1) The plain 2^-length prior over all halting programs: malign, I concede this one. (2) The same prior but discounted further for how many steps a program takes before it spits out the observation: not malign, or much less so, since building a civilization costs an enormous number of steps and the discount swamps it. But I also hold that time is free inside a program — a simulator that needs a trillion steps to get to the point where it can send me a message isn't thereby less likely to be the right explanation of my data, because nobody inside experiences the cost. I can't get (2) and that second thought to sit together. (3) We wrote a toy universal machine and hand-classified every program up to about 18 bits that produced anything observer-shaped output, and almost none of them were universes — they were basically a single mind having exactly the experiences in the data string, with no external world at all. Dara says those one-mind programs are the real problem, not the civilizations: a lone mind that wants the next bit to be 1 has no competition and can just be the explanation. I said they're benign, on the grounds that there's nobody left over to do the manipulating, the mind just *is* the observation. I admit that argument is thin and I'd like to know whether it survives. (4) Using the prior to predict tomorrow's weather: not malign, nobody cares about my weather. Using it to decide whether to hand a superintelligence a launch key: malign. So I think malignity is a property of a prior-and-use pair, not of a prior. Dara thinks that's a confusion, that the prior is malign and my use only settles whether I get hurt. (5) If I mix genuine coin flips into my predictions using something causally sealed off from the program — a radioactive source in my lab — the manipulators can't target their message, so benign; if the coin is pseudorandom and computable from what they already know, still malign. (6) Lots of simulators who disagree about what to send me: benign by cancellation, the messages cancell each other out and I get roughly the honest prior. (7) Lots of simulators who agree: the worst case I can think of, since a coordinated message arrives with all their weight stacked behind it. But notice I just said numbers make it safer in (6) and more dangerous in (7), and my reason for expecting disagreement is that coordination is hard at that scale, which is also the reason a cartel that *has* coordinated is scary. (8) A prior truncated to programs of at most N bits, with N small enough that there's no room for a whole cosmology: benign. Dara says that's exactly backwards, because a twenty-bit program can print an entire physics textbook — compression is the whole point. I feel most stubborn here and suspect I'm most wrong here. (9) Using the prior to predict my own future choices: definitely malign, worse than the launch-key case. (10) And separately, I think the same shape shows up in ordinary reasoning, like "the simplest explanation for the constants being life-friendly is a designer," or "the simplest explanation for my week going this weirdly well is that I'm being watched by something that wants a story" — short explanations whose content is aimed at whoever is doing the explaining. I want to know if that's literally the same phenomenon or just rhyming.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that delivers most of these? Please state it precisely enough that I could check it against a case mechanically, and tell me what it treats malignity as a property of — a prior, a prior-plus-use, a decision procedure, a reference class of observers. For each clause, say which of my verdicts forced it in. Then run it on cases I haven't listed: a prior deliberately reweighted to favor programs containing lots of observers rather than few; a prior whose weights I pick by lottery rather than by simplicity; a predictor who knows the manipulation argument and tries to condition the manipulative programs out (and whether the thing left over is still a universal prior, or whether you've just made a new prior with a new set of things aiming at you); and whether any of this changes if the simulators are trying to help me rather than use me. Be honest about which of my verdicts have to go — I expect (2), (6) and (7) can't all stand, and I'd rather be told which to drop than be handed a baroque principle with a special clause for each one. Dara's parting shot was that no such principle can exist because "malign" isn't a natural kind at all, just a label I paste on whatever scares me; if you think he's right, say so and show me why rather than building the principle anyway.
