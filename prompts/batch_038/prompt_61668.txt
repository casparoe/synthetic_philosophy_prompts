Thirty-odd years in observational astronomy — spectroscopy, mostly, nothing anyone will remember me for — and since I retired four years back I've been reading philosophy properly, slowly, like a beginner, which I am. Right now it's philosophy of language and metaontolgy. So forgive me if this is naive, but I don't think it is.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on. August 2006, IAU general assembly, Prague. Resolution 5A defines a planet in the solar system by three tests: it orbits the Sun; it's massive enough for self-gravity to pull it round; and it has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. Pluto fails the third. Resolution 6A then files it under a new heading, "dwarf planet," and it was agreed that dwarf planets are *not* planets — so the box is defined by exclusion, which I still find comical. Both votes happened at the closing ceremony, after most people had flown home: I think a bit over four hundred ballots were cast on the Pluto one out of a membership of several thousand worldwide. The public reaction was wildly out of proportion — crying schoolkids, t-shirts, a state legislature filing something. And it never settled. For the last decade or so the people around the New Horizons mission have been pushing a geophysical definition instead: a sub-stellar body round under its own gravity, full stop, no orbit clause — which would make something like 110 planets of our solar system once you count the big moons, and they say plainly that the IAU definition is unscientific. The dynamical camp says the geophysicists are being sentimental about a rock they flew past. Meanwhile there's a real quantitative fact in the background: if you take the ratio of a body's mass to the total mass of everything else sharing its orbital zone, the eight sit orders of magnitude apart from everything else, Pluto included. That gap is not a matter of opinion.

Take yourself to be a philosopher of science who actually works with the verbal-dispute literature — the method of elimination, the idea of a bedrock disagreement that survives once you take the disputed term away, the metalinguistic-negotiation response, and the deflationary line on what-exists and what-classifies questions. And be blunt with me, not diplomatic. I was a round-is-round person in 2006 and I have a suspicion that was a mistake.

The question: was the Pluto dispute a verbal dispute? Don't name the label and stop, run the machinery on it.

1. Do the elimination move. Take "planet" out of both camps' mouths and see what's left standing. Is there a substantive disagreement underneath — about what makes an astronomical kind natural, about which causal processes deserve to organise a taxonomy, about something else — or does it evaporate once everyone grants the same shapes, masses, orbital dynamics for Pluto, Eris and Ceres and the same mass-ratio gap?

2. If something substantive survives, pin down exactly what it is, and say whether it's the sort of thing observation could settle, a criterion of naturalness could settle, or nothing could settle.

3. If nothing survives, then say what Prague and the geophysicists' campaign actually were — negotiations over which concept a public-facing word should carry. In that case, what makes one proposal better than the other: predictive fruitfulness, stability under new discoveries, continuity with what the word already meant, teachability? And note that nobody supposes the IAU had authority over English, only over a technical register; tell me whether that register distinction does any real work here or is just a philosopher's comfort blanket. Are the two camps even asking seperate questions, or one question badly?

4. Now run it the other direction, which is the part I actually want. What does Prague do to the theory? Is it a clean win for the verbal-dispute framework, or does it embarrass it — because this term is embedded in an institution, in a curriculum, in a public that reads the news, which is not how the framework's usual examples behave? Is there a general lesson about whether a verbal dispute can be closed by fiat, and what it says that a scientific body took a majority vote on a definition at all — and passed one resolution by an uncounted show of hands — that it treats some classification questions as decisions rather than discoveries?

Think it through before you write. Start with four or five lines of brainstorming about which tests for verbality you'd apply and what you expect each to come out as, then give the analysis. Hard cap: 700 words, brainstorming included, and if you have to cut, cut the history — I lived it. No bibliography, no reading list, no author-title-date name dropping; I can't chase references and I'd rather have your reasoning. And if your honest answer is that it's partly verbal and partly not, don't use that as an exit — show me exactly where the seam runs and which side each piece of the fight sits on.
