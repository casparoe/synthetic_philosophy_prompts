Just got off the phone with my friend Ravi after two and a half hours of arguing about Dennett's four kinds of creatures and I'm still nowhere near settled, so I want to figure out what I actually think instead of what sounds tidy. Here are my gut verdicts, case by case. C. elegans: Darwinian, mostly, even though it can learn to avoid the bacteria that made it sick — I don't think one conditioning paradigm upgrades an animal. Honeybee on its own: Skinnerian. The slime mold that finds the shortest route through a maze and that habituates to a bitter chemical without having a nervous system: I said Darwinian, Ravi said I was being obtuse, and honestly habituation feels like it has to count for something, so maybe minimal-Skinnerian. The octopus that carries coconut halves around and assembles them into a shelter: Popperian for sure, it's clearly trying things before committing, but not Gregorian, because nothing accumulates between octopuses — every one starts from scatch. A bumblebee that learns to pull a string for nectar and then spreads the trick through the colony so it survives the original trainer: I said Gregorian, and Ravi said that flatly contradicts my octopus call, and I think he's right that the ingredients look identical. AlphaZero: Popperian, no hesitation, even though the space it searches dwarfs any animal's. A frontier language model: Gregorian, obviously, since it swallowed everyone else's design tools wholesale — but I also think if you froze the weights today and cut off all further data and tool access it would still be Gregorian, which suggests Gregorian-hood is about what's stored rather than what the thing can currently do. A dog that fast-maps a new toy's name by exclusion after one exposure: Popperian shading into Gregorian, and I couldnt tell you which. A beaver dam, a termite mound: neither, because the design work was done by genes — Darwinian animals with fancy extended phenotypes. A thermostat: not a creature at all, just a switch.

My tentative principle, which I half-hate: the rung you're on is fixed by where the search for good behavior happens — in the gene pool across generations, in the individual's own trial-and-error lifetime, in an internal model where bad options get eliminated before the animal has to be, or in a shared external stock of tools and representations that any individual can borrow from without reinventing. Ravi's counter is that this is one sliding scale rather than four kinds, that the only real variable is how far the search has been externalized from the genome, and that on his view the octopus and the string-pulling bee are literally the same kind of creature so my split verdicts are just squeamishness about insects. He also thinks I'm smuggling in a species-level judgment — that I'd happily call the same animal Gregorian if it were furry and deny it if it were invertebrate.

So: is there a principle that gets all of my verdicts, or the most of them, and can you state it precisely enough that I could run a new case through it and get a definite answer? I want to know what each clause is doing and which of my cases forced it in. Then tell me what it says about the ones I didn't list — a New Caledonian crow population whose hooked-tool designs differ valley to valley and seem to get more elaborate over generations; a person who grew up with no language at all; a chess program handed a library of annotated grandmaster games but no way to add annotations of its own; a self-driving system whose entire repertoire comes from human-built scaffolding it cannot modify. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts you'd overturn rather than twisting the principle to save them. Mostly I want to know whether the octopus/bee pair is a genuine inconsistency in me or whether there's a distinction separating them that I'm just failing to articulate — because if they really can't be separated, I think I have to concede Ravi's sliding-scale picture and admit the four "kinds" aren't kinds of anything.
