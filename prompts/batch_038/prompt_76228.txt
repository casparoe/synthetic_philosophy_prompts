Been down a rabbit hole on Parfit's hitch-hiker and the whole "rational irrationality" line of argument — the case where a stranded traveler needs a driver to believe he'll really pay later, so the traveler's best move is to become the kind of person who can't help but keep a promise, even though keeping it is against his interest once he's safe in town. The general shape, not the details, is what I want to build on.

Can you write me a short story, 1400–1800 words, that dramatizes that problem? Some constraints, because I want it to actually be its own thing rather than a philosophy-dialogue-with-names-on:

- Set it on the dry lake bed stretch of highway out past Kalida, where an unlicensed towing co-op runs. Crucially, the co-op has no way to enforce anything — no contracts, no bonds, no courts that would touch them. A promise is literally the only currency they take.
- Their screening isn't a lie detector on your words. It's a "consistency probe": a headset that shows you a rendered sixty-days-out version of your own life and reads whether you still endorse the intention you just formed. You only get the tow if the sixty-day you still says yes.
- Your protagonist, Marisol, has just come out of a private clinic that dampens your capacity to revise your own commitments — an "anchor," in the clinic's language. She passes the probe easily. But she also knows the dampener has a documented decay curve: about five weeks. So the story's real crux lands on day sixty-one, when the anchor is worn off and she no longer particularly wants to pay.
- Give her a foil: a second stranded traveler, completely unmodified, who is honestly and visibly planning to stiff the driver, and gets left on the salt flat for it. Then complicate it — he turns out to be fine anyway, for a reason that has nothing to do with his character. I want that to sting a little.
- Nobody is allowed to say "rational," "irrational," "precommitment," "Parfit," or "Newcomb" anywhere in the story. The argument has to be carried by what people do and say to each other.
- Stage the probe scene in real time with dialogue, don't summarize it.
- End on the payment decision, shown rather than explained, and make the final line something physical and small — a hand, a receipt, dust, whatever. Not a thematic button.

After the story, add a seperate note of about 200 words. I don't want a balanced "readers can decide for themselves" bow — take a position. Tell me which side of the rational-irrationality debate you actually sided with in the way you built the ending, and why you built it that way rather than the other way. Then flag at least two spots where the story takes liberties with the argument or leans on a reading of it that's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff, and say what you gave up by making those choices.
