I sit on the standing committee of a federation of worker co-ops — about 40 affiliates, a shared relief fund, and a congress where the affiliates vote. We don't write policy. All we do is rule on who has standing in a given congress decision: binding vote, advisory vote, right to speak, right to a written response, or nothing. Our charter language is uselessly vague — it says standing belongs to "those with a legitimate stake in the outcome" — and we've been filling that in case by case for eight years.

The precedents:

- Residents of the town next to our largest affiliate's plant, who would have smelled and drunk the output of a wastewater-treatment decision: no vote, but a mandatory public hearing and a written response to every comment filed. Recorded reasoning: affected, not governed.
- Third-party cleaning and security staff who work full-time inside our buildings and are bound by our site safety rules: binding votes in referenda on workplace safety and site rules; no vote on board elections or on distribution of surplus.
- A supplier co-op in another country whose workers would have lost roughly a third of their income had congress voted to bring the work in-house: advisory vote plus a mandatory written response; no binding vote.
- 16- and 17-year-olds living in affiliate housing: no binding vote, but a youth delegate with agenda-setting power — able to force any motion onto the floor.
- A former member who left three years ago but still holds non-refundable capital in an affiliate, capital that a congress decision would revalue: no vote of any kind, but a right to claim compensation.
- A river trust holding legal standing over the estuary two affiliates draw water from: non-voting seat, right to speak, and power to delay any decision touching the estuary by one session.

Here's the new one, and it's a mess. Congress is deciding whether to move the relief fund's shipping contract to a cheaper handler. The current handler is a non-affiliated logistics warehouse, about 200 people, none of them subject to any Federation rule — their employer sets their conditions and they sit outside every jurisdiction we operate in. If congress switches, most of them probably loose their jobs. Two wrinkles: maybe 15 of them currently live in housing owned by one of our affiliates, so they *are* subject to Federation rules, just in their capacity as tenants; and about 30 are former members of an affiliate who left in good standing. So the affected group is internally split, and the split runs by capacity rather than by person — two people on the same warehouse floor would get different standing.

What should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analog — I keep flip-flopping between the supplier co-op and the former member with the capital stake, and I can't tell whether that indecision is me being dumb or the record genuinely not deciding it. And be explicit about what rule we'd be committing to with each option: are we an all-affected body, an all-subjected body, a two-tier body that gives binding voice to the governed and advisory voice to the merely affected, or something else that happens to fit all six verdicts? I want the candidate rules the precedents can't tell apart, what each one implies for the warehouse, your best guess at what we should actually do and why you'd prefer that reading, what principle we'd be adopting if we go the other way instead, and then the next case we should take up — a hypothetical is fine — that would really discriminate between the rules, because I suspect we've spent eight years splitting the difference without noticing that we were doing it.

American English, please.
