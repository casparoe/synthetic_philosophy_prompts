I'm a handling editor at a middling behavioural science journal. My own background is social psychology — doctorate on attitude change, about six years in this job — and I have no philosophy of science training whatsoever. Maybe once a fortnight a manuscript lands on me where the main explanatory move is evolutionary: some pattern in modern human behaviour gets explained by saying it solved a problem for our ancestors. I don't want to reject these as a class, and I don't want to just pass them to referees with a bland "please assess", because half our referees know the authors and the other half write "this is a just-so story" and refuse to say which step failed.

So: write me a procedure I can run on such a manuscript in about forty minutes, before I choose between desk rejection, revise-and-resubmit, and sending it out. I want the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each one, and for each failure whether it's fatal, fixable by the authors, or merely something to point the referees at. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of biology has actually worked out about testing adaptation claims — I know there's a literature going back to the spandrels argument in the late seventies and I've never read a word of it, so tell me which of its distinctions matter to a referee and which are purely academic. It has to end in sentences I can paste into a decision letter, since an author is entitled to know which specific test they failed; "your account is post hoc" is not an answer I'm willing to send.

Then run it on three submissions. One: a cross-national study, roughly 2,400 participants across fourteen countries, of willingness to enter a tournmanet-style pay scheme instead of a piece-rate one; the authors find the sex gap is wider in countries that score higher on gender-equality indices and argue this rules out socialisation accounts, which they say predict the gap should narrow with equality; no other test is offered. Two: a paper claiming nostalgia is an adaptation for holding coalitions together, supported by three experiments — loneliness priming increases nostalgic recall, nostalgic recall increases willingness to help a member of one's own group, and both effects are stronger in people scoring high on a need-to-belong scale; the authors say this shows the mechanism is functionally specific, and the alternatives section notes that cultural transmission was considered, without describing a test of it. Three: a paper arguing that fear of snakes is a dedicated adaptation, on the grounds that snake pictures are detected faster than flowers in visual search, that six-month-olds look longer at snakes paired with a frightened voice, and that lab-raised rhesus monkeys avoid a model snake after watching wild-caught ones react to it. I like the third and I'm suspicious of the first, so I would rather find out now if the procedure says the opposite.

Tell me where a step is really a judgement call wearing a procedural costume, and where the thing will misfire — I'm specifically worried that it punishes authors who are upfront about speculating and waves through confident ones. Say plainly what it can't do at all. And answer me straight on this: can "just-so story" be operationalised into something a decision letter can actually use, or is it a slur that any rejection will be heard as, in which case I should stop saying it.

Continuous prose only please, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know a procedure usually wants to be a list, but I dont mind as long as it reads as "first ask whether…, then ask whether…" and I can build the list myself. British English throughout.
