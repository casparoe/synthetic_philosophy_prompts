I do research for a small documentary company, so basically my whole job is chasing down where a claim actually comes from, and for the last couple of years I keep running into the same wall with no word for it. Concretely: we were doing a piece on a small town's founding and I turned up three seperate 19th-century accounts of the same incident — a newspaper item from the 1870s, a county history from the 1890s, and a family memoir published in 1911. They agree on the shape of the story, and the two later ones add detail the first one doesnt have: a name for the dog, a line of dialogue, what someone was wearing. On the face of it that's three independent witnesses and the extra detail looks like better access. In fact all three descend from one pamphlet from the 1820s, and none of the extra detail is in the pamphlet — each reteller filled a gap with something plausible, and then those inventions got treated as mutually confirming.

Same shape, different era. I've been reading around how anyone knows anything about the classical past — Herodotus at least flags which of his stories he doesn't personally buy, which turns out to be the good case — and you get these later compilers who reproduce chunks of an earlier historian whose own text is lost, except they're not photocopiers, they add speeches and motives and casualty figures. People trying to reconstruct the lost original end up leaning on the added material, partly because its the only material they've got, and partly because when two later compilers both contain the episode they read that as confirmation. But the second one may have been working from the first, or from something in between, so the confirmation is being manufactured by the copying itself.

So: does this have a name? I know the crude version — the same claim going round and round until everyone's citing everyone else and it looks well sourced. That's adjacent but not it, because in my cases the thing gets *richer* each lap rather than just repeated, and the enrichment is precisely what makes people believe it. I also know the folk observation that legends pick up decoration as they're retold, but that's about the growth, not about the growth being mistaken for verification. I want the carving, not just a label: which established concepts — from history, philology, biblical scholarship, folklore studies, journalism, or the more formal stuff about testimony and evidence — come closest; exactly where each one stops short of what I'm describing; and if nothing really covers it, what would you call it, with a definition I could hand a colleague, two or three boundary cases that look like it but aren't, and a rough test for telling a genuine instance apart from actually-independent witnesses who happened to agree. No need to point me at books, I just want the concepts.

Five or six rows max, and the final row should be your verdict — either the best-fitting established term or the coinage you're proposing, with its score.
