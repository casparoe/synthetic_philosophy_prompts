The candidate's script:

(a) Schelling's point is that a deterrent threat deters only if the adversary believes it will be executed, and that belief is hard to sustain once the game is written out in sequence. Suppose B attacks. Retaliation cannot undo the attack; it can only add destruction on top of it, including destruction to A. Since the attack has already occurred, A's best response in that subgame is to absorb the loss rather than to escalate. B, reasoning forward from the end of the game, should therefore discount the threat to zero, and deterrence fails — not because B doubts A's capability, nor because B doubts A's resolve in the ordinary sense of the word, but because the threat is not sequentially rational. In the vocabulary of the extensive form, the threat is not subgame perfect: it is a promise that A would want to break in every branch in which it becomes relevant.

(b) Schelling's answer is that A can restore credibility by giving up the very choice that makes the threat incredible. In a threat that leaves something to chance, A does not commit to a deliberate act of retaliation; A instead arranges a situation in which the disastrous outcome may come about without A ever deciding on it — forces moved toward a border, alert levels raised, naval units intermingled, so that an exchange could be touched off by an accident, by a subordinate, by a misread radar, or by the friction of a prolonged crisis. What A commits to is not an action but a probability: a steadily rising risk of an outcome neither side wants. Because that risk accrues without any further decision by A, B cannot comfort himself with the thought that A will behave sensibly when the moment arrives, and the deterrent force of the threat no longer depends on A's rationality at the point of execution. Retaliation, in other words, becomes rational ex post precisely because it has ceased to be A's choice.

(c) A related point is usually made about the balance itself. When both sides possess forces that survive a first strike, neither can disarm the other by striking first, the incentive to pre-empt in a crisis disappears, and the retaliatory capability guarantees that an attack will be answered. This is what resolves the paradox: with secure second-strike forces the threat is executed automatically, so the difficulty identified in (a) never arises. The stability–instability paradox is the same problem viewed from the other end. Where strategic forces on both sides are survivable, the horror of a general exchange constrains both parties so tightly that neither can afford to let a lesser quarrel escalate; each has a strong interest in keeping any conflict limited and in demonstrating to the other that it does not want general war. The result is that crises below the nuclear threshold become safer than they would otherwise be. Nuclear stability, on this view, produces stability all the way down.

What follows from all of this, and what I take to be Schelling's most durable claim, is that credible deterrence rests on a cultivated incapacity to behave rationally at the decisive moment: the rational move is to make oneself unable to make the rational move. Bargaining strength in a nuclear crisis therefore depends less on the size of an arsenal than on the ability to look as though one has lost control of events, and leaders gain by making their own retreat publicly expensive. Whether a policy of that kind can be justified is a separate question, which I have not attempted here; as an account of why threats work, it explains the deliberate ambiguity surrounding most nuclear signalling.
