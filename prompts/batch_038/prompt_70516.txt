I spent thirty-six years in analytical chemistry and retired three years ago; since then I have been reading philosophy with the sort of patience that a working life does not permit. I am writing about a configuration I have come across repeatedly, in my own case and in the accounts of others, and for which I have been unable to find a name. I do not believe it is the familiar point that being told a work is beautiful cannot by itself give you knowledge of its beauty, though I take it to be adjacent to that.

Let me first note a contrast from my own training, because it explains why the configuration strikes me as odd. In the laboratory, another person's report was a routine epistemic instrument. I accepted a colleague's measurement because I knew the method, the calibration, and the error bars. Acceptance did not require that I see anything for myself, and when I later did look, I looked for something the report had already specified. The report and my subsequent observation were, in principle, independent checks on one another.

Now the two instances.

First. A critic whose judgment I trust tells me that one of Beethoven's late quartets is among the greatest things in the repertoire. I have not heard it. I form the belief and act on it: I make time, I buy the recording. On first hearing I detect nothing of what was promised; it strikes me as diffuse and self-indulgent. Yet I do not abandon the belief. I take the failure to lie in me — my ear, my patience, my unfamiliarity with the idiom — rather than in the report. After perhaps a dozen hearings something shifts, and I begin to hear the movement as coherent, and eventually as remarkable.

Second. The same shape with a painting. I am told that a certain portrait in a major collection turns entirely on its handling of light. Looking, I see only a brownish surface with a face in it. I keep returning, because the testimony told me what to look for and how long to look. After several visits I do begin to see the light doing what I was told it does, and I would now defend the judgment by pointing to what is on the canvas rather than to anyone's say-so.

The feature that puzzles me is this last stage. Once I perceive what was described, the belief has become first-hand: its support is no longer the report but my own experience. And yet the looking was organized from the outset by the description I was trying to confirm. I cannot now tell whether I came to perceive a property that was there all along, or whether I acquired the capacity to produce a certain response and to report it in the critic's vocabulary — nor whether those two could ever come apart in practice. The testimony appears to function not as evidence for a belief but as instructions for constructing an instrument; and once constructed, the readings of that instrument are indistinguishable from independent ones. Provenance is erased by success.

In my old field this pattern would raise eyebrows. A claim that survives a failed observation by reclassifying the observation as defective, and that then gathers support from measurements taken with a procedure the claim itself prescribed, is not the sort of thing one is taught to admire. I am not asserting that the aesthetic case is thereby discredited; I suspect the training is what makes the perception possible in the first place, and that there may be no such thing as an untrained ear. That suspicion is part of what I want to examine.

So I should like to know whether this configuration has an established name, and I should like the carving rather than the label. Please tell me which recognized concepts come nearest to it, how what I have described differs from each of them, and which, if any, is the best fit. If nothing fits, then coin something, with a definition — but above all with boundary conditions, since the definition alone will not tell me when I am looking at the phenomenon and when I am not. Two cases in particular I cannot place. One is the acquired taste that arrives with no testimony at all: a person who simply returns to a difficult work out of stubbornness and is eventually rewarded. Is that the same thing minus an ingredient, or a different thing entirely? The other is the person who, after the disappointing first encounter, concludes that the critic was wrong or pretentious and drops the belief. What does that failure of the loop tell us about the cases where it succeeds?

I would also value a word on whether the asymmetry runs both ways: if I am told that a much-praised work is in fact overrated, does the same circuit operate in reverse, with the description shaping the search for defects until defects are found? And I am not asking for a reading list or for citations, which I could not easily chase down. What I want is the distinction itself — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
