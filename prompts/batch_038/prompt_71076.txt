Co-teaching a seminar on housing politics this term and I've stuck A Raisin in the Sun on the syllabus right next to Lefebvre on the right to the city and a stack of YIMBY/NIMBY arguments, mostly because I want the students to hit the play with concepts it wasn't written for. My worry is they'll just summarize it as a case study in historic redlining and feel done. So I want you to write the reading I'm trying to talk myself into — actual criticism, not a theme summary.

Start with Karl Lindner. He shows up for the Clybourne Park Improvement Association, is polite to a fault, insists nobody in the neighborhood bears the Youngers any ill will, hints that things could get difficult, and offers to buy the house back from them at a profit. Take him seriously as a NIMBY and map him onto the homeowner today who writes to the planning board about "neighborhood character," tree canopy, and parking. How much of that mapping really holds? One thing I keep tripping over: exclusion in the play works by covenant and by the threat of violence — there's a scene most productions cut where the neighbor Mrs. Johnson cheerfully predicts the family will end up in the paper as a bombing victim — whereas exclusion in most of my students' cities now works by price and zoning, where nobody ever has to show up at your door. Does a story about being kept out still speak to a city that prices people out, or does the swap quietly change the subject from race to class and lose what's specific to Hansberry? (For what it's worth her own family was on the receiving end of a real restrictive-covenant fight in Chicago that went all the way to the Supreme Court, so the play isn't inventing its antagonist.)

Then the harder bit, and the one where I suspect the play beats me. Lefebvre's right to the city is supposed to be a collective right to remake the city, not an individual right of access to the housing market. But the Youngers are doing something much closer to the second: Mama buys in Clybourne Park partly becuase it's the cheapest house with a yard that she can find, Walter's whole horizon is owning a business, and Beneatha's two suitors stage the choice between assimilationist prosperity and something else entirely. So is the play really endorsing a right to buy in? And when Walter refuses Lindner's money in front of Travis at the end, has that become a collective claim on the city or is it still one family's dignity, privately won and bought with an insurance check? Point me at the moments that resist the Lefebvrian reading as honestly as the ones that support it, and tell me what the play understands about exclusion that the theory misses.

If you know Bruce Norris's Clybourne Park — same house, act one set in 1959 with Lindner pressuring the white sellers not to sell to the Youngers, act two in 2009 with white buyers wanting to knock it down and rebuild bigger while a Black board member named Lena, related to the Youngers, tries to stop them — say briefly at the end whether it settles the question or just relocates it.
