For the last two years I have been auditing a Thursday-evening reading group at our public library. I retired six years ago from a career in sedimentary geochemistry — my working life consisted of inferring past conditions from noisy, indirect proxies, and I am still adjusting to a field where the data are arguments rather than measurements. Philosophy is largely new to me, which is why I am writing this down rather than settling the matter myself. Our group has eight members; two of them, Owen and Nadia, spent most of two consecutive sessions on a question I could not referee. Owen taught secondary-school history for thirty-five years and assembles our reading list; Nadia spent her career handling logistics for a humanitarian agency, including roughly six years posted in Niger and Bangladesh. The question was whether the cross-cultural data on moral judgment tell against the existence of objective moral truths. I have tried to report the exchange in the order it happened, and deliberately not to signal which of them I found more persuasive — as far as I can tell I do not know.

Nadia opened. (1) She said that the standard questionnaire used to measure moral concerns — the one that scores people on care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion and sanctity/degradation — produces wildly different profiles across populations, and that among the samples psychologists actually draw on (the Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic ones) educated liberals weight the first two and treat the last three as matters of taste. She said that in the communities she lived and worked in, eating with the wrong hand, or a widow remarrying too soon, or a family's neglect of a shrine, is condemned in the identical register and with the identical insistence as striking someone. Her conclusion was not that those people hold false moral beliefs but that they do not share ours: what counts as inside the moral domain at all differs, so "moral truth" has no stable subject matter, and the question "are there objective moral truths?" cannot get a determinate answer. Owen replied with (2): variation in judgment is only evidence against there being facts if we would have expected convergence, and we would not. Astronomers disagreed for a century about whether the spiral nebulae lay inside our own galaxy; nobody concluded there was no fact of the matter. He added that a large share of the apparent variation is variation in non-moral background belief — about souls, about what ingesting a given substance does to the body, about who counts as kin and what depends on them — fed into principles both sides share; if you sincerely believe a polluting food endangers the whole household's standing with the ancestors who control the rains, refusing it is not a matter of etiquette.

Nadia then pressed the disanalogy (3): science has a feedback mechanism, and prediction and measurement force convergence over time, whereas moral disagreement has none. Better arguments and more information do not reliably resolve it; judgment tracks the judge's upbringing, and where apparent convergence has occurred — over slavery, over women's civic participation, over same-sex relations — she said it tracks market integration, urbanization, schooling, and material security rather than anyone being convinced. So it looks like the diffusion of one particular ecology's norms, not discovery. She mentioned in passing a study in which people made harsher moral judgments in a foul-smelling room, and Owen said it had not survived large-scale replication; she conceded the point and said that if the only sensitivities to irrelevant context that replicate are framing and question-order effects, the objectivist has lost his best evidence that any given intuition is tracking anything. Owen's answer (4) was that what Nadia calls diffusion is exactly what everyone else calls moral progress, and that the willingness to rank our own current practices as still falling short of the standard — he said he judges his grandparents' views on this subject as wrong, not merely dated — is only explicable if the standard is not identical to our practice. He added that if all convergence is diffusion, then Nadia's own recommendation, that we should stop imposing our norms on others, is itself nothing but the local product of one culture spreading, and she has no grounds for expecting the rest of us to accept it. Yet she plainly does expect that; she spent two sessions arguing for it.

Nadia made two replies (5). First, that the charge does not touch her: she is making a claim about the semantics and metaphysics of moral language, not a first-order moral claim, and one can consistently hold first-order views while denying that they are objective. Second, she offered what she called the Pluto analogy: until the astronomical union adopted a definition in 2006 there was no fact about whether Pluto was a planet, and the definition was adopted because it served a purpose. "Moral," she said, may be that kind of term — its extension is fixed partly by our own practices, and since the practices differ, the term is fixed differently in different places. Owen's response (6) was that this is a disanalogy in the wrong direction. A classificatory term like "planet" does no practical work beyond organizing a catalogue, whereas moral terms are the instruments by which people hold one another accountable — blame, demand, forgive, resent — and you cannot restipulate that vocabulary without changing what the concept is for. Worse for Nadia, he said, the 2006 definition was chosen precisely because it tracked a real distinction, whether a body has cleared its orbital neighbourhood, which obtained independently of anyone's decision; stipulation there was answerable to the world. He then raised a measurement point: nearly all of the comparative survey data are collected with instruments designed by researchers in the samples Nadia complains about, translated, and administered on rating scales, and populations differ systematically in whether respondents use the extremes or the middle of a scale, so some of the reported variation may be an artifact of the instrument rather than a difference in the underlying judgment. And in the communities she describes, he said, people do in fact treat striking someone differently from breaking a dress code — the second is more susceptible to excuse, to authority, to local rule — which suggests the moral/conventional distinction is present with different contents rather than absent.

Nadia conceded the scale point in part (7) and then turned it around: if our instruments cannot reliably measure the difference, then neither side has the evidence it claims, and Owen is left asserting a shared moral faculty on the basis of data that cannot settle the question. On the moral/conventional distinction she said that how a culture sorts violations is precisely what is in dispute, so pointing to the presence of the distinction already presupposes the frame it was meant to establish. She also put a question Owen did not answer: if moral facts are the sort of thing that no one can observe and nothing can be predicted from, why would we expect a faculty sensitive to them to have evolved, and what work would those facts be doing in explaining anything we do? Her closing position was that we cannot address the objectivity question until the domain is fixed, and that fixing the domain is a decision, not a discovery. Owen said that this concedes his point, since choosing the better way to fix a domain implies there is a better; Nadia said better *for us*, which is her point. The group then went in circles for forty minutes and broke for coffee. I have no idea who won.
