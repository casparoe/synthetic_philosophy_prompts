I retired three years ago after thirty-four years in Quaternary science, most of it spent reconstructing past climates from proxy archives — lake sediments, ice cores, tree-ring series — where the quantity you actually want is never observed, only inferred from a noisy correlate through a calibration you can only partly check. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and specifically the epistemology of testimony and of knowledge about the past, and I have been struck by how much of the historiographical method I keep encountering resembles what I did for a living, and how much of it does not. I now serve on the editorial board of a county historical society's journal. I am one of five readers; we receive roughly eighteen submissions a year, each assigned to two readers, each reader allowed ninety minutes and no travel beyond the county seat; our recommendation goes to a seven-member board, four of whom have no training in any historical discipline. The journal is the society's public face, and after an embarrassing genealogical claim two years ago a principal donor has said he will not underwrite a second error. None of us has palaeography, Latin, or any budget for laboratory analysis.

I would like you to act as a historian of historiography who is also a philosopher of testimony, writing for an intelligent layperson with strong training in the natural sciences and none in the historical disciplines. Think the problem through carefully before you draft anything, and be willing to tell me plainly where my instincts from instrumental science are wrong. Please give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no named attributions to particular scholars or works; where you would normally gesture at an authority, state the reasoning instead. Invent nothing and cite nothing.

The deliverable is a decision procedure my fellow readers can actually run on a submission in ninety minutes, grounded in whatever source criticism genuinely knows about establishing claims from documents. It should be ordered so that the cheap and disqualifying tests come first, and it should be explicit about the distinction I most want preserved: that reliability attaches to particular claims a source makes, not to the source or its author as a whole, so that one document can be excellent evidence for one sentence of a submission and worthless for another. I also want a test for arguments from silence that asks not merely whether a record is absent but whether such a record would have been created and whether it would have been expected to survive — and I want the procedure to say what to do when two sources each survive criticism and then flatly contradict one another.

Please structure your answer as follows.

First, before any procedure, a short diagnosis of three places where my proxy-science intuitions would mislead a documentary critic — for instance, that a sediment core has no interest in what I conclude from it whereas a witness may; that the "independence" of two replicates is a genealogical question about their production rather than a numerical one; and that an instrument's calibration is not stable across the whole range of things it can be used to record. Let this diagnosis govern the design that follows.

Second, the procedure itself as a single JSON array, one object per step, each with exactly these fields:

- step: integer, the position in the order a reader should run it.
- test: the question the reader puts to the submission, in one sentence, phrased so that a non-specialist can act on it.
- how: the concrete thing the reader does with the material in front of them to answer it, within the time and access limits described above.
- passes_if: what counts as a sufficient answer.
- fails_to: one of "reject", "accept_with_conditions", "suspend_pending_expertise", "escalate_to_board".
- requires_judgment: true or false.
- judgment_note: if true, what the reader is in fact deciding and why no rule can settle it for them; if false, "n/a".
- depends_on: an array of the step numbers that must be run first, or an empty array.

Third, a plain-language summary of the whole procedure in a single paragraph, short enough to print on the inside cover of our readers' handbook.

Fourth, a candid account of the procedure's limits: which of its steps are dressed-up judgment calls; what it cannot do at all, in particular whether it can ever license a positive claim about what happened as opposed to a claim about what the evidence permits us to assert; and which step a scientifically trained reader is most likely to skip out of overconfidence and why.

Fifth, run the procedure on the three submissions below and report the results in a Markdown table with exactly these columns: Submission | Steps that decide it | Verdict | What further evidence would change the verdict | Where judgment, not rule, did the work in this instance.

Submission one. A paper argues that an 1883 fire at a mill in this county killed three workers because the owner knowingly operated a defective boiler, and that he afterwards had the coroner's inquest record removed from the county files. The evidence offered is: two newspaper accounts published within four days of the fire, in rival papers in two different towns, which the author treats as independent corroboration; a tape recording made in 1961 by a local enthusiast, of an elderly woman recalling what her grandmother, a former mill hand, had told her about the fire; and the absence of any inquest record for that year, read as evidence of removal. Two features complicate this: the rival papers share an identical and rather unusual turn of phrase in describing the boiler, and both print the same wrong death toll in the headline while correcting it in the body; and the county's inquest series for the 1880s has known gaps in several years, the archivist having noted that a basement flood in 1937 destroyed unbound loose papers from that decade. I should tell you that we have already printed and sold four hundred copies of a walking-tour pamphlet repeating the suppression claim, and that the owner's descendants still live in the county. I expect the procedure to cost us something here, and I want it to.

Submission two. A paper uses forty years of a country doctor's near-daily diary to support three claims: that the winter of 1891–92 was the coldest in the county's recorded history; that county mortality rose sharply in the spring of 1893; and that a named neighbouring farmer was a habitual drunkard who neglected his children. The diary was published in 1938 by the doctor's granddaughter, who says in her preface that she omitted matter of no interest to a modern reader. The state climate office has long used these weather entries as a documentary series and considers them unusually careful. The farmer's descendants dispute the third claim, and for it the diary offers only the doctor's bare assertion, recorded during a period when the two men were engaged in a boundary dispute that reached the county court.

Submission three. A paper reconstructs the toll of an 1849 fever in one parish from that parish's burial register, which is complete and legible throughout. On inspection, roughly six months of entries are in a different ink and a slightly different hand, appear out of chronological order bunched at the end of the year, and contain three duplicated dates; the surrounding pages resume the original hand and sequence. Letters of the incumbent, held locally, mention that he was ill for months and that he made up the book when he was able. The author treats the entire register as contemporaneous.

Take your time with the design. I would rather have a shorter procedure that survives contact with these three submissions than a longer one that quietly assumes the reader has training nobody on our board possesses.
