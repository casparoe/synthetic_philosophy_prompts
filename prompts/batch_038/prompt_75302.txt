My reading group has spent a term on logical omniscience, and one member — a semanticist — has been settling every proposed counterexample the same way, with a flat verdict on whether it is true to say that the agent believes the consequence. Her record so far:

1. A student who believes that if it rains the pitch will be closed and that it is raining, and assents at once when asked: yes, she believes the pitch is closed.
2. A number theorist who accepts the axioms of Peano arithmetic, where the claim in question does follow from them but only by a proof nobody has yet produced: no.
3. A man who learned from an email that a colleague was in Paris on Tuesday and from a separate conversation that she was in Rome on Tuesday, and who has never compared the two: he does not believe a contradiction, and he certainly does not believe everything.
4. The same man with both facts written on one sheet in front of him, asked whether they can both be true: now it is true to say he believes something contradictory.
5. A graduate student for whom a one-step consequence could only be stated using a technical term she has never met: no, she does not believe it — though once the term is defined for her she accepts it immediately.
6. A physician working through a differential diagnosis, where the conclusion takes a dozen effortless steps from premises all in play in a single consultation: yes.
7. A mathematician who proved a theorem thirty years ago and can no longer recall the proof, though she could reconstruct it in an afternoon: yes, she still believes it.

Next week we bring her this one. A woman has known all her life that her sister is severely allergic to shellfish, a fact she uses constantly when cooking for family. This afternoon, in an entirely separate context, she learned that tonight's tasting menu includes a langoustine course. She has never mentioned the two things in the same breath; the inference between them is one trivial step; every concept involved is hers; she is at work and thinking about neither. Does she believe that tonight's menu will make her sister ill?

Predict her verdict. Then reconstruct the rule she is running: which candidate accounts her seven verdicts positively rule out (unrestricted closure, a pure count of inference steps, conceptual mastery alone, a requirement that the belief be currently active), which remain consistent with all seven, and which of the survivors best explains the pattern rather than merely fitting it. In particular, say whether her treatment of cases 3 and 4 commits her to something structural about how beliefs are organized, or only to a claim about what she would say if appropriately prompted — and finish by giving me one further case that would decisively separate those two readings.
