**The question, as set:**

> Question 2 (compulsory; approximately 1,200 words; 50 marks).
> Amartya Sen holds that an egalitarian theory must answer the question "equality of what?" before it can answer the question of how much equality justice requires.
> (a) Explain what the first question is asking, and why it is not simply another way of posing the second. (10 marks)
> (b) Compare three proposed answers to it: welfare, resources, and capabilities. In discussing resources, distinguish Rawls's primary goods from Dworkin's account. Say what the choice among these metrics actually turns on. (25 marks)
> (c) A city currently delivers its winter assistance as an in-kind heating service of identical physical quantity to every eligible household. It proposes to replace this with an identical cash payment to every eligible household, on the grounds that households know their own needs best. Follow-up surveys find that average reported satisfaction under the new arrangement equals reported satisfaction under the old one. Evaluate the proposal from the standpoint of each of the three metrics discussed in (b). Do the metrics disagree about the verdict? If they do not disagree, does that show the dispute in (b) is merely verbal? (15 marks)

**The script:**

(a) The question concerns the metric of equality — the space in which equality is to be measured. Asking how much equality justice requires presupposes that we already know what counts as an equal distribution; it asks only how far we are prepared to depart from equality for the sake of other values, such as liberty or efficiency. The metric question is prior and logically distinct, because the same pattern of holdings can be perfectly equal in one space and grossly unequal in another. Sen's point is that self-described egalitarians and their critics have rarely disagreed about equality as such: nearly every political theory is egalitarian in some space and inegalitarian in another, so the substantive dispute is over the space.

(b) Welfarism takes utility — happiness, or the satisfaction of preferences — as the metric. Sen's case against it has two parts. First, utility is a mental state, and mental states cannot be compared across persons with any reliability, so a utilitarian egalitarianism cannot even determine whether it has succeeded. Second, and more importantly, utility is distorted by adaptation: a person deprived over a long period lowers her expectations, takes pleasure in small consolations, and reports contentment, so her utility understates her deprivation. A metric must therefore be anchored somewhere other than the subject's own reports.

Rawls's answer is an index of primary goods: liberties, opportunities and powers, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect. These are all-purpose means, wanted by anyone whatever else they want, and they are publicly observable, which is what allows a public conception of justice to be built on them. Dworkin keeps the resourcist metric but changes the justification. For him the issue is responsibility: people should be compensated for bad luck that is not their fault but not for the costs of choices they made. The envy test states the criterion — a distribution is equal if no one would prefer another person's bundle of resources and obligations to their own — and the hypothetical auction together with the insurance market are devices for approximating it. Because Dworkin wants the distribution to be ambition-sensitive, he treats disability and expensive tastes as two instances of the same problem, namely that a person's bundle must be adjusted to her circumstances, and the insurance market supplies the adjustment: since no one knows in advance whether she will turn out to be disabled or to have a taste for claret and plover's eggs, people insure against both, and the payout defines what justice owes them.

Sen says welfare and resources both locate advantage in the wrong place. Resources are means; justice should look to what people are able to do and be with those means. The key notion is that of functionings — beings and doings such as being adequately nourished, being able to appear in public without shame, taking part in the life of the community. A capability is a functioning that a person actually achieves, so the capability metric is a metric of realized outcomes rather than of inputs. That is why the wheelchair case does the work it does: an equal bundle of primary goods yields unequal realized functionings for a person who cannot walk, and the resourcist sees only the equal bundle. The advantage of anchoring the metric in realized functionings is that it removes the arbitrariness of freedom-based metrics. The ascetic who fasts and the person who is starving have the same realized functioning, undernourishment, and on this account they are equally badly off, which seems right: whatever the reason for the deprivation, the deprivation itself is the same.

Two clarifications. First, the metric question is separable from the question of distributive principle: identifying a space in which to measure advantage is not the same as prescribing equality within that space, and the capability approach by itself recommends no principle. The disagreement between Sen and Nussbaum illustrates this. Nussbaum is the strict egalitarian, requiring full equality across each of her central capabilities, whereas Sen asks only that everyone be brought above a threshold, so that on his view the space is fixed but the principle is modest. Second, the familiar objection that capabilities are plural and cannot be aggregated cuts both ways: Rawls too must collapse several heterogeneous primary goods into a single index in order to identify the least advantaged representative person, so the indexing problem is not peculiar to Sen and cannot by itself settle the choice between them.

(c) The metrics give different verdicts. For the welfarist the change is neutral or an improvement, since reported satisfaction is unchanged. On the resources view the old scheme was the unequal one, because an identical physical quantity of heat is not an identical command over resources across differently situated households; identical cash is identical resources, so the proposal is a gain. On the capability view the proposal fails: households differ sharply in their ability to convert cash into warmth — insulation, age, disability, number of occupants — so equal cash yields unequal capability to be warm, and withdrawing the in-kind service removes exactly the part of the bundle that protected the worst converters from their own conversion disadvantages.

This disagreement, though real, is largely verbal. In any actual society primary goods and capabilities are strongly correlated: a household with more cash almost always has more of what it can do and be, and the cases in which the correlation breaks down are marginal. So the choice of metric rarely changes the ranking of policies, and the dispute Sen opens is best read as a quarrel about how to describe the same distribution rather than about what justice requires.
