I am preparing a talk for a philosophy-of-economics seminar on idealization, and before I write it I want to pin down my own position rather than rehearse the familiar exchange between Friedman and his critics. Here is the case I intend to run the talk on.

Consider the classical user-equilibrium model of traffic assignment. It assumes that each traveler has adequate knowledge of network conditions and picks the route minimizing their own travel time, so that at equilibrium every used route between an origin–destination pair carries equal and minimal cost. As a description of drivers this is false: people know their habitual routes, switch sluggishly, misestimate delays, and often settle for something satisfactory rather than optimal. Suppose nevertheless that on ordinary days the model reproduces aggregate link flows to within a few percent — that is the stipulation I want you to work with, not a matter for you to dispute. Now the city asks it the question such models are actually commissioned for: what happens to network-wide travel times if a central bridge is closed for a year of reconstruction? The apparatus that fitted the traffic counts is now being used to describe a state of the world no one has observed, and the closure is exactly the sort of disruption in which congestion spillback, driver re-learning, and route churn dominate — the very things the equilibrium assumptions were built to suppress.

Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics says this is not merely permissible but admirable, and the resulting position has come to be known as the F-twist: the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be. I want to know what you make of the twist itself, stated sharply:

**Claim T.** Whenever a model's predictions are sufficiently accurate over the domain for which it was built, the fact that its assumptions are descriptively false — as distinct from merely idealized in ways that do no work — carries no further, independent epistemic cost. Once predictive accuracy is established there is nothing additional to worry about; in particular, the model earns no discount when it is applied to a situation inside its intended domain but outside the range of data that confirmed it.

Give me your credence that T is true, as a single number between 0 and 1, and give me the derivation. If you think T is ambiguous between a strong and a weak reading, choose the reading you consider most defensible, say in one clause what you chose, and report your number for that reading only — I do not want a table of numbers for every construal.

For the decomposition, here are candidate terms. Treat these as raw material, not as a checklist: add terms I have missed, delete ones you think are redundant, and re-cut the question if my carving is at the wrong joints.

(a) Whether "accuracy over the intended domain" can even be specified independently of which assumptions are load-bearing, or whether identifying the domain already presupposes the approximate truth of some of them.
(b) Whether there is a principled line between harmless idealizations and false assumptions that are doing real work. One familiar move is to sort assumptions into kinds — claims that some factor is negligible, claims fixing the theory's domain of application, and devices used only to simplify the derivation — and to hold that the twist survives or fails differently for each kind. Say whether you think such a taxonomy rescues or undermines T.
(c) Whether accuracy inside the confirmed range gives any warrant for a prediction about an intervention, where the model's own assumptions are what generate the counterfactual answer rather than what the data constrain.
(d) Whether T, if true at all, is true of science generally, or only in fields where controlled experiment is unavailable and the alternative to a false-assumption model is no model.
(e) Whether T is empirically hostage at all: if every predictive failure can be blamed on the domain clause or on auxiliary conditions rather than on the assumptions, is T false or merely empty?

Then tell me how the terms combine. Say explicitly whether they enter conjunctively, as a weighted average, as a minimum, or in some other way, and show the arithmetic that gets you to your headline number, including where you have taken care not to double-count overlapping terms.

On sensitivity: identify the term your number is most elastic with respect to, move that term by 0.2 in each direction, and tell me what T's credence becomes. Also name the term you assigned with the least confidence and say why.

On disagreement: my credence in T is 0.3. A colleague in the transport-engineering department who uses these models every working day, and who regards the objection as philosophers' hand-wringing, is at 0.8. Which term do you think actually separates us, and is the gap substantive or a disagreement about what T asserts? If you had ten minutes with each of us, what single question would you put to establish where the divergence lives?

Finally, tell me what would move you: describe the argument or the result — described, not attributed, since I want reasoning and not a bibliography — that would most change your number, and in which direction.

Please answer in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. **Bottom line** — 3 to 5 sentences: the number, T restated in your own words, and the reading of T you are answering.
2. **Terms** — a numbered list of four to seven items; each with its probability, at most two sentences of justification, and a flag saying whether it enters conjunctively or as a weight. About 500 words in total.
3. **Combination** — about 150 words, with the arithmetic and an explicit note on any overlap you discounted.
4. **Sensitivity** — about 150 words, including the ±0.2 perturbation on the most elastic term.
5. **Where my colleague and I differ** — about 120 words, ending with the single diagnostic question you would put to each of us.
6. **What would move me** — about 120 words.
7. **Verdict on the bridge closure** — about 100 words: what your number implies for the city's decision, and if the model should be trusted, with what discount and on what grounds.

Aim for 1,100 to 1,300 words overall, and lead with the number rather than with a survey of the positions.
