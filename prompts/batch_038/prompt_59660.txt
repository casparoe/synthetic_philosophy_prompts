So — slightly odd question and I'm coming at it sideways. I got roped onto the charter review commission in my town this fall (about 14,000 people, we have an actual town meeting, everyone knows everyone). Last spring a developer made a pitch to buy the municipal water system, and two of the selectboard were audibly interested, and now the main proposal in front of us is a charter clause designed to make that basically impossible to revisit: three-fifths of the town meeting, then a general election has to intervene, then a second three-fifths vote by the *new* town meeting, then a town-wide referendum. I'm the one who volunteered to write up the conceptual rationale for it, and I've discovered I can't. Not the practical part — I can do "it slows things down and forces a second look" — but there's something underneath that I don't know how to say out loud, and I suspect the clause is committed to it whether or not anyone who voted for it would sign up for it.

Because I went looking, and it turns out this is a whole genre. The Netherlands won't let you amend the constitution without a first reading, then dissolving the lower house and holding an election, then a second reading in the new parliament where you need two-thirds — so the people who finally decide are not the people who started. Some constitutions go further and declare certain principles flatly unamendable, so that no vote count whatsoever can touch them. And there's the cautionary one: a national parliament that made calling an early election require a supermajority of its own members, and a later parliament repealed the whole thing with an ordinary majority. Our situation is a small version of that last one, since the state legislature can override our charter by ordinary statute and nobody seriously thinks they wouldn't if it mattered to them.

Here's what I actually want from you. Reconstruct the theory this clause is running on, and be careful to separate what it *strictly* presupposes from what merely sits comfortably next to it — I'd rather know where it's philosophically underdetermined and several incompatible views would license the exact same clause. Specifically: what account of the town-as-an-agent-across-time does it need? That the 2025 voters and the 2037 voters are one agent with two selves, one of which is reliably worse at this particular decision? Or is it not about selves at all but a standing mistrust of majorities that's in real tension with the democratic stuff in the same document? And there's an internal wobble I can't resolve: requiring an election to intervene sounds like *deferring* to future voters, but the whole motivation is that future voters will be talked into something by a man with a rendering of the reservoir. Which is it? Can the practice hold both at once, or is one of them a cover story for the other? Then the Ulysses detail everyone skips — he didn't only get himself tied to the mast, he had the crew stop up their ears so they physically could not hear him begging to be let go. Almost none of these constitutional devices have an earwax component; they just make reversal expensive. Do the friction version and the earwax version presuppose different things about the future self — like, does the friction version assume he'll eventually be *right*, and the earwax version assume he'll never be? Finally: what breaks, institutionally and not just as a matter of taste, if we drop the whole apparatus and go straightforwardly majoritarian? I need to know what I'd be arguing against at the meeting, not just what I'd be arguing for.
