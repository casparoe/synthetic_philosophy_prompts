ok so my friend Dana and I spent about four hours going around in circles on the paradox of fiction and I want to break the loop by making us both sign one document. She's the Waltonian: what I feel during a novel is a quasi-emotion, part of a game of make-believe, not really fear or really grief. I'm the other side: the feelings are perfectly genuine emotions, and the whole paradox only looks like a paradox because it assumes emotion requires believing the object exists. Neither of us has budged, and I'm fairly sure part of it is us using the same words differently, but I can't pin down which part.

Write the joint statement the two of us should have produced.

First, a numbered list of claims we can both sign. Include claims about the Anna Karenina-type case itself — does the weeping reader believe Anna exists? does she behave as if she does? does she grieve that Anna doesn't exist? — plus claims about what would count as a genuine emotion versus a quasi one, and about what emotions need cognitively. I want ones we'd actually both accept, not filler like "emotional responses to fiction are philosophically interesting."

Second, a disagreements table, with these columns: the disputed claim / the wording Dana would accept for her side / the wording I would accept for mine / whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal / the crux question underneath it / a case or argument we'd both agree bears on that crux / and each side's prediction written down in advance.

For the cruxes, start from these three that we kept circling, and add any you think are more load-bearing:

1. Whether there is any behaviour whose object is the fictional situation rather than the unpleasant experience. She says no — covering your eyes, refusing to watch the rest, walking out of the cinema, all of it aimed at the feeling, not at the monster. I say: in an interactive story where you can duck your character behind a wall, the move is aimed at the fictional danger, and that's not experience-avoidance at all.

2. The mismatch case. The same person sobs over a character and is unmoved by a famine statistic. She takes that as evidence the two responses differ in kind; I take it as evidence that emotion tracks vividness and narrative shape, not belief.

3. The guilt case we made up. A novel narrated in the second person that asks you not to tell anyone what you're reading, and that lets you know a character is hiding something you already skimmed ahead and discovered. I said I felt genuinely guilty; she said quasi-guilt about my own reading is just embarrassment with a costume on.

Third, write in a protocol we'd both commit to in advance. Here's the one I proposed and she grudgingly liked: two textually identical stories, one presented as a translated memoir with a real author and dates, one presented as a novel; measure self-reported distress, heart rate, and afterwards how much people donate to a charity named in the story. Then the relabelling twist — a group reads it believing it's a true memoir and is told afterwards it was invented, and reports whether the feeling they had changes retrospectively. Her prediction: they'll revise, and say it wasn't real grief after all. Mine: the feeling doesn't change, only how they describe it. Write both predictions into the table so neither of us can fudge later.

Last section: what would actually make each of us give up the view, and be honest if you think nothing would. Also say whether you think the thought-theory move — emotions only need an entertained thought, not a belief — dissolves our argument, and if so which of us it rescues more.
