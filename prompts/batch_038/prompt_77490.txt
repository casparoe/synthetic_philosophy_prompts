I am drafting a piece for a policy journal in defence of state apologies for historical wrongs — the sort of thing done when a parliament apologises, in the name of the Crown, to indigenous groups whose land was taken and whose language was suppressed, or when a government accepts the findings of a public inquiry into a killing by its own soldiers and declares what was done unjustifiable. The objections I have to meet are familiar by now: that no living person did any of it; that the descendants of the victims would not exist had the wrong never been committed, and so cannot claim to have been made worse off by it; that the apology consequently transfers nothing, commits nobody, and is theatre.

To meet them I assembled the argument below, largely from premises I expected the sceptic to grant. It now troubles me, because I believe it is valid, or very nearly so, and its conclusion is one I cannot accept.

1. An apology is an agent's acknowledgement of its own wrongdoing. A speaker who did not perform the act cannot apologise for it, and can at most express regret that it occurred.
2. States are agents. A state can act, and act wrongly, and what it does is not the sum of what its members do, since a single continuous course of action — a policy maintained for decades — can be carried through by a state whose membership has entirely turned over in the meantime.
3. So a state can apologise for wrongs it committed, and the objection that no living person committed them simply identifies the wrong party: the apologiser is not the living person.
4. Apology requires that the apologiser be the agent who acted; and being the agent who acted is a matter of identity, not of resemblance, continuity of practice, or succession.
5. So a state that apologises today for what it did a century ago is numerically identical with the state that did it: one persisting corporate agent, rather than a series of agents linked by succession.
6. A corporate agent has no resources of its own. Everything it can promise, repay or hand back is held by its current members, and it can act only through them.
7. So when a state discharges a liability, the liability is discharged by its current members, out of what they hold.
8. A liability that falls on a person and is discharged by that person is a liability that person bears. If it were not, then the claim that the state pays would name no actual payment, and (5) would secure a persisting corporate agent at the cost of making it inert — which would simply concede the sceptic's original point.
9. So current members bear liability for wrongs committed before any of them was born.
10. Nothing in (1)–(9) limits that liability by benefit received, by knowledge, by consent, by degree of involvement, or by the passage of time. So every member of every long-lived state stands liable in respect of every wrong that state has committed, for as long as any part of the harm is still being suffered.
11. That is intolerable. It makes moral liability descend by birth and residence, which no account of individual responsibility can absorb, and it is not different in kind from the inherited guilt the practice of apology was meant to displace.
12. So at least one of (1)–(10) is false.

I want to keep (2), (3) and (11): I want states to be capable of apologising, and I do not want liability to be inheritable. Beyond that I have no settled view about where the break lies, and I should not like to be told which premise to abandon merely because abandoning it is the customary move.

So: identify the step you would reject, and say precisely why it looked secure to me when I wrote it. Then tell me whether the argument harbours a genuine equivocation — I have a hunch that bearing liability is doing two different jobs between (8) and (9), and that the state pays may be shorthand for something less alarming, though the hunch may be wrong and the flaw may sit elsewhere — or whether it is genuinely valid, in which case the only question is which premise is cheapest to surrender. Price the alternatives I care about: what I lose by dropping (4), what I lose by dropping (6), and what I lose by dropping (8), and in particular whether surrendering (8) really does reduce the state's apology and its payments to a fiction, as the sceptic will insist. Finally, tell me whether anything could settle the underlying issue rather than redescribe it — a case, a test, a distinction — that would show whether a persisting corporate agent can hold obligations without those obligations becoming personal to its members; and if nothing could settle it, say what that reveals about the practice of state apology itself.
