I am collecting my own verdicts about relying on other people's moral judgments and trying to find out what, if anything, they add up to. Below are the cases, with my honest reactions rather than a consistent set; I expect some of them to be in tension, and I would rather you expose the tension than smooth it over.

1. **The joke.** A colleague I trust, who belongs to the group a certain joke is aimed at, tells me the joke is racist. I do not see it; it strikes me as making fun of everyone equally. I stop telling it and take myself to have good reason to stop. Verdict: deferring here is not merely permissible but the right response, and I feel no loss in it.

2. **The second child.** A friend who has thought hard about the matter tells me that, given climate and given my circumstances, having another child would be wrong. I have not thought it through myself. I defer and do not. Verdict: fine.

3. **Meat.** A mentor whose judgment I rate above my own tells me that eating meat is wrong. I have looked for an argument I can accept for years and found none. I stop anyway, and stay stopped for a decade. Verdict: the action is right and I am not defective for performing it, but I do feel something is missing — not that I acted wrongly, that I do not know why it was right.

4. **My mother.** My mother tells me it would be wrong to start seeing my ex's closest friend. I think her moral judgment is worse than mine across the board. Verdict: not fine; this is one I should think through myself.

5. **The panel.** On a hiring panel, a colleague whose judgment I trust tells me the leading candidate behaved disgracefully at a previous institution and that I should vote against him. I could not say why the conduct was disgraceful, nor defend that it outweighs everything else, to the panel or to anyone. Verdict: not fine. Something about occupying that seat requires that I be able to give reasons, and here I would only be voting on testimony.

6. **The young doctor.** A doctor in a hospital in the early 1930s accepts from his seniors that sterilizing certain patients serves the good of the race, and acts accordingly. Verdict: the act is monstrous and he is culpable — but less culpable than a colleague who reasoned his way to the same conclusion on his own, and much less culpable than he would have been had he had doubts and suppressed them.

7. **The same doctor, abstaining.** In a parallel situation he declines to participate, unable to agree with everyone senior to him, though he could not articulate an objection. Verdict: he is blameless and praiseworthy, but slightly less praiseworthy than the colleague who refuses on conviction. Note the shape of my two reactions: in case 6 I want deference to cut blame a great deal, and in case 7 I want it to cut credit only a little. I cannot see one thought generating both.

8. **The slur.** A friend asks whether a term she has been using is a slur. I am not in the group it is applied to and have no feel for it; everyone I know in that group says it is. Verdict: here deference is obligatory. Insisting on working the question out for myself would be a failing, not integrity.

9. **The non-moral comparisons.** I ask a wine merchant which bottle to order, a financial adviser which pension option to take, and a critic whose verdict I respect whether a novel I enjoyed is in fact bad. Verdict: no problem and no sense of loss, though the aesthetic case is marginally less comfortable than the other two.

10. **Harassment.** Whether a colleague's pattern of behaviour over two years amounts to harassment. Verdict: this feels like the moral cases, not like the wine merchant, even though much of it looks like a question of fact and of what a term means.

11. **My past self.** Ten years ago, after long thought, I concluded that a certain arrangement between employers and workers is exploitative. I no longer recall a single reason I had, only the conclusion, and I have relied on it since. Verdict: fine. But this looks like taking moral testimony from myself, and if it is unobjectionable here, I cannot see what is objectionable in case 3.

12. **Wholesale adoption.** A philosopher I admire tells me I should be a utilitarian. I adopt the theory on his word and thereafter work out particular cases for myself. Verdict: worse than case 3, not better, despite leaving far more of the downstream reasoning to me.

My own tentative principle is that deference is defective exactly when, in deferring, you surrender the possibility of acting for the reasons that make the act right or wrong, in circumstances where you could have been in a position to act for them. It seems to handle 4, 5, 9, and 12. It also says 1 and 8 are defective, and those are the two verdicts I hold most firmly.

What I would like:

- Say whether a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, accounts for this pattern. State each precisely enough to be applied mechanically to a new case, and for every clause identify which of the cases above forced it in.
- Diagnose whether my verdicts are even answering the same question. I suspect the defect I sometimes want to locate attaches to the action, sometimes to the belief, sometimes to the agent's character, and sometimes to the office the agent holds, and that cases 1 and 5 differ mainly in that respect. Tell me whether you think so, and build that into the candidates if you do.
- Apply the candidates to at least six cases of your own devising that I have not listed, including one in which the candidates disagree, and say which of those disagreements I should treat as the real decision point.
- Say plainly which of my verdicts a simpler principle would overturn, and what I must give up if I want to keep both 1 and 8 alongside 5. Those three look structurally identical to me — deference to someone better placed, no grasp of the reasons, action taken on that basis — and I cannot find my way between them.
- Say whether 6 and 7 can be brought under a single account of the relation among deference, blame, and credit, or whether I am holding two incompatible intuitions about one underlying thing.

Please use the following headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. **Bottom line** — under 150 words: the principle, or the choice between principles, you end up recommending.
2. **Fault lines** — about 250 words: which verdicts genuinely conflict and why, before any principle is offered.
3. **Candidates** — the bulk of the reply, roughly 900 words; one subsection per candidate, each with a formal statement, a clause-by-clause map back to the cases that motivated it, and a verdict for each of my twelve cases.
4. **New cases** — about 350 words.
5. **Costs** — about 250 words: which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and what the cheapest revision of my set of verdicts looks like.

I am not after references, a survey of who defends what, or a reading list; name positions if it helps, but the deliverable is the principle and its verdicts.
