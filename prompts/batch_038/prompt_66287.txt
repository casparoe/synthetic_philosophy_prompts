ok so context. I'm a fifth-year PhD student in economic history and next Thursday I'm workshopping a chapter at our cliometrics seminar, except this time it's a joint session with the macro group and they've invited a philosopher of science from across town as the designated discussant. Room'll be maybe fifteen people: mostly quantitative economic historians who live in parish registers and price series and think narrative history is vibes, three growth theorists who have their own unified-growth-flavored models of the demographic transition where the driver is some quantity-quality human capital mechanism, and the philosopher, who works on calibration and long-range prediction and has strong feelings about ad hoc reasoning. Nobody in that room is a Malthus fan. Two of them have said some version of "the guy was just wrong" at lunch in the past month.

My chapter argues that Malthus's core model — population pressing against the means of subsistence, held down by what he called the positive and preventive checks — was not actually refuted by what happened in England after roughly 1800, and that "he was right but early" can be made into a rigorous claim rather than a face-saving excuse. The pitch is: the negative feedback is real and you can see it in the pre-industrial record, but two of its parameters got shifted by a shock he had no way of anticipating — sustained productivity growth instead of one-off improvements, and the preventive check turning out to be strongly income-elastic in exactly the direction that matters. So the theory doesn't get rescued, it gets re-parameterized, and the re-parameterized version still bites wherever the old parameters hold.

Here's the piece I most want you to go after. I try to state a general criterion for when "right but early" is legitimate and when it's just rescue, and mine is roughly three conditions: (i) the parameter change has to be independently dated by evidence that wasn't recruited for the purpose of saving the theory, (ii) the same mechanism has to remain testable and operative in the regimes where the old parameters still hold, and (iii) you should be able to say in advance what sort of shock would move the parameter, not just identify it after the fact. Honestly (iii) is where I'm exposed, because for Malthus I'm identifying the shock post hoc and then dressing it up as a prediction.

So: build me the prep sheet. Give me like eight objections in order of how much damage each one would do, phrased the way each sub-audience would actually phrase it — the cliometricians will come at identification and counterfactuals and what my natural experiment even is, the growth theorists will say my "exogenous shock" is their endogenous mechanism wearing a hat and doing no explanatory work, and the philosopher will come at the Duhemian underdetermination angle, at Lakatos's progressive/problematic shift distinction, and at base rates for theories rescued this way. For each objection I want a two-minute reply, the thing I should concede in my very first sentence so I don't look evasive, and a blunt note on where the reply is softest. Then tell me which single reply absolutely has to land for the chapter to survive the afternoon, and name the question I'm praying nobody asks — I already suspect it's the one about Malthus himself bolting moral restraint onto the later editions as a new check, which looks exactly like the goalpost-moving I'm claiming he didn't do. Don't write me a defense of the thesis or a summary of Malthus, I want the questions and what I say back.
