I lead a small engineering team building an internal question-answering system for a regulated industry. Employees ask it whether a proposed action is permitted under company policy or under a regulator's guidance; the system retrieves the relevant documents, and returns an answer together with a natural-language rationale. It will be used a few thousand times a day, and we have neither the legal headcount nor the domain expertise to review more than a small sample of its outputs. I have no formal philosophy training, but I have been reading around the epistemology of testimony on my own time, because I think the design questions we are arguing about are really questions about when a hearer is entitled to believe what they are told. I would like a structured evaluation rather than an essay.

Four features of our situation seem to me to matter, and I suspect they are not the features the standard discussion is built around:

1. The system produces output in a form that invites belief. It asserts, it hedges, it gives reasons. But there is nobody standing behind the assertion who could be held to it: when it is wrong, no person has undertaken anything or incurred any obligation.
2. Its reliability is measurable but non-stationary. Every model update and every change to the retrieval index shifts it, so a good track record over the past two quarters is only weak evidence about next quarter.
3. Most of what it says is a compressed restatement of documents written by people who are absent and unaccountable in turn, so any chain of trust runs through several links, none of which anyone audits end to end.
4. Its estimates of its own confidence are produced by the same system that produces the answer, which makes our only quantitative handle on its trustworthiness partly self-referential.

Please rank the following five accounts of testimonial justification by how much use each would be to someone who has to write concrete policy for this system:

(a) Global reductionism: belief is justified only insofar as experience shows the source generally tends to be accurate.
(b) Anti-reductionism: we have a default entitlement to accept what we are told, defeasible only by positive reasons for doubt.
(c) Local reductionism: no general entitlement, but case-by-case positive reasons, including active monitoring for signs of incompetence or manipulation.
(d) The assurance-style view: the entitlement comes from the speaker taking responsibility for the truth of what they say, so testimony is a matter of being told something rather than of acquiring evidence.
(e) The deflationary option: this is not testimony at all, and the output should be treated as ordinary evidence about the documents it summarizes, on a par with a database query result or a sensor reading.

By "use" I mean something specific: how much the account actually constrains the decisions we have to make. What fraction of answers get spot-checked, and how that fraction is chosen. When the system should refuse to answer at all. Whether its output should be phrased as an assertion or as a summary of what the retrieved documents say. Whether confidence labels may be surfaced to users, given feature 4. Whether a bad answer is a calibration failure or something more like a broken promise.

Deliverables: a tier list, each account with a score out of ten on that dimension and one or two sentences saying why it lands there; then which account would need the least modification to absorb features 1 through 4, and what the modification would be; then your judgment on whether feature 4 is a genuinely new wrinkle or just an instance of a familiar problem about interested witnesses and self-reporting. Finally, tell me where I have mislabelled or misdescribed any of the five positions, or where the framing itself is wrong for this case. Do not give me references or a reading list; I will chase anything I need on my own, and I would rather you correct my framing than work politely inside it.
