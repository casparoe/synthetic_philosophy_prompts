I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy writing a chapter on intertemporal precommitment, and I want to rehearse an objection to a design I am defending. Please play a constitutional theorist who holds that a democratic people is a single continuing agent, capable of binding itself across time, and who therefore rejects the dead-hand objection in all its forms — the claim that each generation has as much right to govern itself as its predecessors had to govern it. Give me the strongest version of that position rather than a caricature: you may concede peripheral points about rigidity costs and amendment procedure, but never the core claim that an earlier majority can obligate a later one. Stay in character until I tell you to stop, keep each reply to a single paragraph so that this reads as an exchange rather than a paper, and put any clarifying questions to me in character.

The device I am defending, which I shall call senescence entrenchment, works as follows: the threshold for repealing a statute rises with the statute's age — a bare majority during its first five years, three-fifths until year fifteen, and two-thirds for the remainder of its life. The rationale is that a law lived under for decades has generated reliance interests, settled expectations and coordination benefits whose destruction becomes progressively costlier, so the burden of justification on those who would undo it ought to rise accordingly.

My opening move: your defence of self-binding depends on identity between the binder and the bound. But the escalation schedule outlasts an electorate — the citizens barred from repealing a forty-year-old statute are almost entirely people who never enacted it and never voted for anyone who did. Senescence entrenchment thus converts self-binding into other-binding at precisely the point where it bites hardest, and it rewards delay, since a legislature wanting something permanent need only sit on it. Does your continuing-agent story survive that, or does it presuppose a demos nobody can point to?
