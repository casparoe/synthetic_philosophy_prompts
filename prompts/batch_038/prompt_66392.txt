I am drafting my seminar essay on the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers — bubbles as structures that leave outside voices unheard, echo chambers as structures that actively undermine them — and before I build the argument on it I would like to test a principle of my own against my verdicts on a run of cases.

My tentative principle has two clauses. A structure is a bubble just in case it withholds contrary testimony, and it is cured by exposure. A structure is an echo chamber just in case it installs a standing discount rate on contrary testimony, and it is not cured by exposure.

Here are the cases and what I say about them.

1. My uncle reads a single newspaper, has never encountered the arguments on the other side, and bears no ill will towards them. When I put a contrary report in front of him he revised. Bubble; exposure cures it.

2. An online community that warns newcomers in advance that the public-health authorities are captured by industry. When members are shown those authorities' own studies, they cite the warning as confirmed. Echo chamber; exposure makes it worse.

3. A language-isolated community whose members consume only home-country media — not from distrust, but from cost and language. I want to call this a bubble, yet exposure plainly would not cure it, because the obstacle is access rather than mistrust. The two clauses of my principle have come apart.

4. The methods seminar in my own department: everyone has read the rival literature closely and can argue against it line by line, and all of them dismiss it as confused. Nobody is ignorant of the other side. I still want to call this an echo chamber, even though maximal exposure and active discrediting coincide here.

5. Someone who reads only hostile coverage of a group she already dislikes, precisely in order to keep the dislike confirmed. Not a bubble, since she does encounter that group's own voice, but only in the rendering most likely to offend her; and there is no community supplying the discredit. I am unwilling to call this an echo chamber, yet equally unwilling to call it nothing.

6. Two people who end up holding the same, mostly true, beliefs: one inside an echo chamber, one inside a mere bubble. I think the first is worse off epistemically than the second, and worse off than someone outside both who holds the same true beliefs. But if I explain this by saying the echo chamber's member cannot revise, I seem to owe an account of why the bubble's member — who likewise could not have revised, had the truth lain elsewhere — is untroubled.

Two questions. First, is there an account of these two structures that delivers all six verdicts, or must it admit a third category, and if so what distinguishes it? Second, if no such account exists, which of my verdicts should I abandon and which clause of my principle is doing work I had not noticed? Please also tell me what the surviving account predicts for a case I have not listed: a recommender system that shows me material from every outlet, including the ones hostile to my views, selected purely for engagement.
