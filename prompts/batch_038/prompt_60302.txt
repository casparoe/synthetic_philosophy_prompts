ok so, junior here, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on my own time about choices you supposedly can't make rationally. I'm not gonna name names — got it from a library book and a video essay and I've half-forgotten both — but the idea is roughly: some experiences are the kind where you can't know what they're like until you've had them, so there's nothing for you to picture when you're choosing. and worse, the experience rewrites *you*, so the person who'd be glad about it or regret it doesn't exist yet. the normal move — imagine each outcome, decide which you'd prefer, pick that one — breaks in two places at once, and not because you're being dumb, because there's literally nothing to compare. the example I remember is deciding whether to have a kid, and there's a jokey one about deciding whether to become a vampire, which sounds stupid but is actually the cleanest version of the thing.

people push back saying you can get most of it secondhand, from friends who've done it, from novels, from testimony. fine, but that doesn't touch the second half. nobody can report what *you'd* want afterwards, because afterwards you aren't you.

so: my school lit mag's theme this year is "irreversible" and I want to submit a sestina. yes, a sestina, I know. I've written two bad ones allready and I keep coming back to the form precisely because it's a machine built for exactly this problem — six end-words that keep returning in a rotated order, same six words, but they land differently every single time they come back around, and then the three-line envoi scoops all six up at once.

write it from the point of view of someone standing on the near side of one of these choices, addressing whoever they'll be on the far side of it. pick the choice yourself — I'd rather it not be the vampire, too cute for a lit mag. requirements:

- actually follow the pattern (second stanza is 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on). if the end-words don't rotate right I will notice, I have a chart taped inside my locker
- the six end-words should be completely ordinary ones the speaker uses right now with their normal meanings, and by the end they should be words the addressee could only use in a sense the speaker currently can't get. the rotation has to do that work, not decoration — I don't want the same noun coming back with a spookier adjective glued to it each time
- the speaker never once claims to be able to picture it. no "I know I'll be glad," no "I'll understand when it happens." that's the exact move that isn't available to them
- the envoi is the decision happening anyway, but not as a leap-of-faith pep talk and not as "some things you just have to feel." be honest that the choice is getting made without the thing that's supposed to make choices rational
- ~39 lines, no title needed, don't go rhyming the middles of lines just to look clever

someone who's read the philosophy should be able to tell what's going on without any of it being named out loud. someone who hasn't should just get a good poem about not being able to know your own future self.
