I am in the second year of a PPE degree and am currently taking a module on democratic theory; our assessment is a 3,000-word case study, and my tutor has explicitly banned the usual throat-clearing about Athens, the boule and Montesquieu's remark that selection by lot is democratic in nature while selection by choice is aristocratic, on the grounds that every essay he has ever marked opens with it. He has also told me that a case study earns its marks in the mapping rather than in the verdict: who the relevant actors actually were, what each of them could realistically do, and what would count as evidence for or against the theory being tested. I am writing this on a train back from a departmental trip to Belfast, where we spent two days on the consociational machinery of the Northern Ireland Assembly, which has left me thinking about what random selection does and does not buy you in a polity that is not deeply divided but is nevertheless reluctant to decide anything.

The episode I have chosen is the Irish experiment with mini-publics between 2012 and 2024, because it happens to contain both the most-cited success story in the sortition literature and, six years later, what looks like its most emphatic failure. The facts, so that you are not working blind. The Constitutional Convention sat from December 2012 to March 2014 with one hundred members: a chair, sixty-six citizens chosen at random by a polling company to match the electorate by age, region and gender, and thirty-three serving politicians from the Republic and from Northern Ireland; among its assigned topics was the constitutional clause concerning women's life within the home, on which it recommended change. Its successor, the Citizens' Assembly of 2016–18, excluded politicians altogether: ninety-nine randomly selected citizens plus a Supreme Court judge as chair. That body deliberated abortion from November 2016 to April 2017, recommended repeal of the Eighth Amendment, and proposed a legislative framework considerably more permissive than commentators had expected; a parliamentary committee took the report forward and settled on termination without restriction as to grounds up to twelve weeks; and at the referendum of 25 May 2018 the proposal carried with 66.4 per cent of the vote on a turnout of 64.1 per cent. Then, in July 2019, the Oireachtas established a Citizens' Assembly on gender equality, which reported in June 2021 and was followed by a parliamentary committee that reported in December 2022; out of that process came two constitutional amendments, one extending the definition of the family beyond marriage to durable relationships and one replacing the clause on women's duties in the home with a gender-neutral article on care. At the referendums of 8 March 2024 both were crushed — 67.7 per cent against the family proposal and 73.9 per cent against the care proposal, on a turnout of 44.4 per cent — despite polls showing a comfortable Yes lead, despite every major party in the Dáil and most of the prominent civil society organisations campaigning for Yes, and despite the No side consisting mainly of a small think tank, a single-issue pressure group and an ad hoc committee of barristers. Only one constituency voted Yes on the family amendment and none at all on the care amendment; these were the largest No votes in the history of Irish referendums. One further detail, which I only came across in the assembly's own records and which I suspect is theoretically important: the random selection was not clean. Of the original ninety-nine members of the 2016 assembly, seventeen had withdrawn before the first working session and a further eleven before the final session on abortion, and seven of the replacements admitted in January 2018 had to be withdrawn when it emerged that they had been recruited through acquaintances of an employee of the polling firm rather than selected at random.

So: analyse this arc as a test of the case for sortition. I want you to be explicit about the mapping first — who counts as the deciding agent at each stage (the legislature that sets the remit, the polling firm, the ninety-nine, the chair, the experts and stakeholders who constitute the assembly's information environment, the government, the referendum electorate, the campaigns), what each of them could actually have done with a recommendation once it existed, and what the assembly's own votes and reports can and cannot tell us. Then run the theory against the two outcomes. Sortition is supposed to deliver descriptive representation, freedom from the electoral incentive and from the career structure that goes with it, and better-formed preferences through deliberation under conditions of good information; say which of those mechanisms is doing the work in 2018, which is doing the work in 2024, and where the theory simply has nothing to say — I suspect it is remarkably quiet on ratification, on question wording, on salience and on the difference between processing a demand that a social movement is already making and manufacturing a proposal that nobody asked for. My tutor keeps pressing a distinction I would like you to take seriously: between sortition as a supplement, where an advisory mini-public feeds an elected parliament, and lottocracy proper, where a lot-selected chamber holds real power, along the lines of Manin's argument that election was adopted by the American and French revolutionaries precisely because it filtered. Both Irish bodies were supplements; the radical proposal was never on the table. Does that make the 2024 result irrelevant to the radical proposal, or is it evidence against the milder one? Relatedly, is the transfer problem — the question of whether whatever a mini-public generates in the way of informed judgement and legitimacy is portable outside the room, or whether it is trapped inside it — a genuine theoretical resource here, or an ad hoc rescue that lets the theory absorb any failure whatsoever by relocating the failure to a stage the theory does not govern? And what is one to make of the opposite transfer, in 2024, whereby a recommendation arriving with an institutional pedigree hands a No campaign a target and lets opponents present the proposal as the product of a committee rather than of the electorate? Finally, treat the selection failures as evidence: if descriptive representation is the load-bearing claim, what does it mean that the composition of the body drifted and that the randomisation broke down, yet almost nobody objected, because the assembly's standing rested on its output rather than on how it was constituted? Conclude by saying what a proponent of lottocracy ought to make of Ireland — in a form that is not special pleading — and state what would have to be true of the 2018 and 2024 results for them to count as evidence about the same theory rather than about two different things.
