I maintain a service that estimates the condition of industrial equipment from instrument readings, and two conventions in it have always struck me as more than engineering choices. I'd like to know what they commit us to.

Here is the setup. The service holds a probability distribution over a fixed list of about a dozen candidate fault states. When a reading arrives, we do not jump to the distribution that reading would license on its own; we move partway. Each instrument carries a trust score k between 0 and 1, and the new distribution is the old one plus k times the difference between the reading-implied distribution and the old one, component by component. Our code review checklist rejects any change that sets k to 1, and the stated reason is that k = 1.0 means taking the instrument's word as final. Separately, k is not a property of the reading alone: if an instrument has drifted in the past, its k is lowered permanently, so the same physical value moves our estimate less when it comes from that instrument. And a third convention: the table saying what each fault state implies for the rest of the plant, and what we should do about it, is never touched during an update. We redistribute confidence among possibilities we already had; we do not revise what follows from each of them.

Meanwhile the same team treats the test suite as all-or-nothing. A single failing assertion refutes a hypothesis outright, and nobody has ever proposed a test that lowers confidence in a claim by sixty percent.

Five questions:

1. What view of evidence does the k < 1 rule presuppose? What would have to be true about observation for "never take the instrument's word as final" to be a principle rather than a prejudice, and which rival view does the rule quietly exclude?
2. What does the untouched implication table presuppose on top of that? Is that assumption separable from the first, or could we keep the partway update while revising what follows from each possibility?
3. Does the drift adjustment relocate the weight from the evidence to the source, and does it matter whether the number expresses something about this reading or about this instrument's history?
4. Is the test-suite practice consistent with all of the above, or does the team hold two incompatible pictures of what an observation can do? If they are incompatible, say which practice is the outlier.
5. What would have to change in the design if we adopted the rival view from question 1 — the one on which a properly calibrated instrument's reading settles the question?

Please distinguish, where you can, what the practice strictly commits us to from what it merely happens to fit well with, and note any place where a different theory would license the same code. If any of this has a standard name in the literature, name it in passing — but no bibliography and no reading list.

Format: answer as exactly five numbered items matching the five questions, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
