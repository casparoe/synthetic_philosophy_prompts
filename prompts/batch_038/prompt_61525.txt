> **Question 3** (10 points, roughly 600 words). A three-judge panel must decide whether a defendant is liable. Under the relevant doctrine, liability obtains if and only if both of the following hold: **P**, that the defendant acted negligently, and **Q**, that the plaintiff suffered a recognizable harm. Judge 1 accepts P and accepts Q; Judge 2 accepts P and rejects Q; Judge 3 rejects P and accepts Q. All three judges are logically competent, and each holds a complete and consistent set of judgments. (a) Explain why voting proposition-by-proposition on P, Q, and the conclusion R (= P and Q) can deliver a different verdict from voting on R alone, and say what the more general lesson is supposed to be for the claim that a group of individually rational agents is itself rational. (b) One proposed remedy is to adopt a premise-based procedure. Explain how it works in this case, and identify one cost or worry attaching to it, justifying your answer.

Here is the script I have in front of me, reproduced as submitted:

> (a) The panel is divided in such a way that the two procedures come apart. Voting proposition by proposition, P is accepted by Judges 1 and 2 and so carries 2–1; Q is accepted by Judges 1 and 3 and so also carries 2–1; and since liability requires both, the premise-wise vote finds the defendant liable. Voting directly on R, by contrast, only Judge 1 accepts it, so R is defeated 2–1 and the defendant is not liable. The same three judges, each of whom reasons perfectly well and none of whom holds a contradictory set of judgments, produce opposite verdicts depending on how the question is put to them. And if the panel simply takes the majority view on every proposition on the agenda at once, it ends up committed to P, to Q, and to the denial of R — flatly inconsistent, given that R just is P and Q. This is the discursive dilemma; in the legal literature the same pattern is usually called the doctrinal paradox, and the difference between the two labels is that the second is tied to a court working through a body of doctrine, while the first is meant to apply to any group that reasons from premises to a conclusion. The general lesson, as I read it, is that collective rationality does not come free with individual rationality: rationality is a property of sets of judgments, and the set that ends up belonging to the group is not guaranteed to inherit the good properties of the sets belonging to its members. A group can fail to be a rational agent even where every member is one.
>
> This is essentially the same result as Arrow's theorem reached from a different direction. Arrow showed that majority rule cannot turn individual preference orderings into a coherent social ordering; the discursive dilemma shows the same thing for judgments rather than preferences. Both are impossibility results about aggregation, and both come down to the point that majority voting ceases to be coherent as soon as a group has to settle more than one question at a time. List and Pettit's general theorem states the point precisely: given a few reasonable conditions on an aggregation rule — that it be defined for every possible profile of individual judgment sets, that it treat members symmetrically, and that it always deliver a complete and consistent collective judgment set — the only rules that avoid contradiction turn out to be dictatorial ones.
>
> (b) The premise-based procedure repairs the case by ordering the agenda. The panel votes first on the premises and only then on the conclusion, which is taken to be settled by whatever the premises turn out to be. Here it accepts P, accepts Q, and so finds the defendant liable, notwithstanding that two of the three judges think the defendant should not be liable. This is what a court is doing when it treats the doctrine as a set of reasons rather than as a single bottom-line question, and it has the advantage that the resulting judgment set is guaranteed to be consistent, since the conclusion is fixed by the premises. It also escapes the impossibility theorem quoted above, because that theorem is really directed at conclusion-based voting: the premise-based rule treats all members symmetrically and never returns an inconsistent set, so it meets the conditions the theorem imposes, which suggests the theorem is less general than it first appears.
>
> The cost is responsiveness. In this profile the panel finds the defendant liable on a conclusion that a majority of its own members reject, so the decision cannot be traced back to anyone's reasoning. Judge 2, who was pivotal on P, thinks there was no harm; Judge 3, who was pivotal on Q, thinks there was no negligence; and Judge 1, who accepts both, was pivotal on neither. If we want the court's judgment to be something its members can own and answer for, the premise-based procedure buys logical coherence at the price of exactly that.
