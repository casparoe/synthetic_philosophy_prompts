I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on how norms change — eighteen students, one 75-minute session, no technology in the room beyond a whiteboard. The class has already read Williams on shame and on the modern "morality system," and Appiah on honor codes and moral revolutions. In discussion, most of them converged on the same deflationary move: that honor is either a way of talking about interests, since standing is just a resource, or a way of talking about arguments, since an audience's ridicule is only the social transmission of a reason somebody had already articulated. I want one in-class activity that puts enough pressure on that deflation that at least some of them lose it — and lose it because of what they did, not because of what I told them.

Design the activity to the following specifications.

The practice must be invented for the game; do not stage dueling, footbinding, or slavery. It should be costly to perform, must confer a public mark of esteem on the performer, and must attach that esteem and a small but real harm to a third party to the very same act, so that participants cannot keep the honorable part and disclaim the harmful part. It also needs a rationale the participants can articulate and half-believe in their own terms.

Rank must have purchase. Honor tokens or standing have to convert into something the students actually want in the room — control over a distribution, exemption from a task, the right to be heard first — otherwise the motive I am trying to exhibit is a fiction and the activity reduces to a puzzle.

Build in a private ledger: on each round, every participant records in one column what they think of the practice and in the other what they are about to do. Those cards are not collected or discussed until the debrief. The gap between the two columns is the phenomenon.

Include a staged argument round. Write the text of a genuinely well-constructed moral argument against the practice — good enough that I would be embarrassed to answer it off the cuff — and have a student deliver it publicly. By design, it should change nothing. Tell me what makes it impotent in that setting and what would have to be added for it not to be.

Then the shock. It must introduce no new information and change nobody's material payoff: only the audience's affective response, or the boundary of who counts as a member of the honor world. Justify explicitly why the intervention is honor-coded rather than informational or economic.

After the collapse, run a third phase in which each student writes down what caused the practice to end. Sort their answers live into three buckets — arguments, interests, honor — show them that the same event licenses all three descriptions, and ask which one the ledger data actually supports.

Deliver the full mechanics: roles, materials (assume index cards, poker chips or beans, and a whiteboard), a payoff scheme with concrete numbers and no real money and no grades at stake, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a list of framings I am forbidden to voice while the game is running. Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the distinctions at issue — a norm losing its justification versus losing its audience, thick ethical concepts, the internal/external distinction in blame — and a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the student who refuses on conscience in phase one, the one who reverse-engineers the design within five minutes, token hoarding, the two or three who simply never care about rank. Say how to handle each without abandoning the point.

Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply by hand. I already suspect four things: the compression of a generation into forty minutes; the fact that my students possess the concept of a socially constructed norm and can hold their own conduct at arm's length in a way the historical agents could not; that the tokens are not connected to credit, marriage, or violence; and, most damagingly, that a game about tokens may itself teach the deflation I am trying to defeat, by making honor look like prudent self-interest in standing. Address that last one directly, and also flag where the game will teach the crude gloss that shame is about what others think and guilt about what you think — a gloss Williams himself complicates — and how to correct it in the debrief without turning the correction into a lecture.

Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; everything the students need should be inside the activity itself.
