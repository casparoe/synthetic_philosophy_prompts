I'm the type steward at a mid-size US logistics company — basically I'm the only person here whose job involves our corporate typeface. The family was drawn for us in the late 90s by a foundry that's since been absorbed twice, so there's no way to ask the original designer why anything looks the way it does; all we have is the fonts themselves and a 14-page brand PDF whose entire typographic guidance is "keep the humanist spirit." We get about twenty submissions a week: campaign headlines, product logotypes, hand-lettered stuff from agencies, one-off glyphs (a slashed zero for an annual report, an ampersand for a conference, Vietnamese diacritics for a new market), and occasional requests to "extend the family." Two junior designers do first-pass review and I sign off. They get maybe 15 minutes per submission, sometimes same-day because something's going to press, and they are good designers but not typographers. The complaint I keep hearing, from them and from the agencies we reject, is that the decisions feel arbitrary — people push back with some version of "look, it's obviously an a." And honestly they're right that it feels arbitrary, because what I do now is squint at a printout and consult my gut.

Here's the thing I suspect we're getting wrong, and it's what I want the procedure to fix: I think we're collapsing two separate questions into one. The first is whether a mark is a legible member of its category at all — does it read as an 'a' to anyone, at what size, in what context, how much does it degrade. The second is whether it's *our* 'a' — whether it belongs to this family rather than to some other one. Those come apart constantly, and I want them separated explicitly, with the crossover cases handled rather than fudged. A related idea I'd like you to decide whether is actually usable or just pretty: there was an AI research project in the 90s (Hofstadter's group, the one that did Copycat) that tried to get a computer to invent new typefaces in the style of existing ones, and the way it framed style was not as a list of shapes but as a set of dials — how far the strokes lean, how heavy they are, how open or closed the bowls are, how terminals get cut, how much the round forms overshoot the baseline — so a letterform is a position in dial-space and a typeface is a neighborhood in it. If that framing helps, I'd like the procedure to use it: maybe part of a review is naming which dials the submission moved, and whether those are dials that carry our identity or dials nobody would notice. If it doesn't help, say so and tell me what to use instead. I'd also like something on the diagnostic-letters intuition — the sense that a few letters (the 'g', the 'a', the 'e', the 'R', the tail of the 'Q') seem to carry a family's identity while others could be swapped almost freely, and whether that's real or folklore.

So: write me the decision procedure my juniors can actually run in fifteen minutes, as an ordered set of questions with, for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer and what evidence they attach to the ticket (a size test at whatever point sizes matter, side-by-side against the real family at matched sizes, a control word set, whatever you think works). Mark clearly which steps are close to objective and which are taste dressed up as a step — I don't need the taste removed, I need it labeled so we can argue about the right things. Tell me where the procedure just stops at "no," where it escalates to me because the question is really about brand strategy and not about letterforms, and give me some rule for how many identity-bearing deviations is too many, so we don't end up with either "any single deviation is fatal" or "it all averages out." Cover the case where the family has no precedent at all — a glyph nobody drew in 1997 — and say what to do when the answer depends on a feature you can only judge in context. I also want a realistic number for how often two trained reviewers should agree after following this, plus a cheap way to audit it, like a blind re-review of last quarter's decisions. Then run the whole thing on three live ones. One: a product logotype where every letter is convincingly from our family except the 'g', which has been redrawn with a closed double-bowl form that appears nowhere in our fonts, and the studio says it's for legibility on the app icon. Two: a regional office's hand-lettered wall sign in a bouncy semi-script where I genuinely cannot point to a single matching feature — wrong x-height to cap ratio, wrong slant, no shared terminals — and yet every person who walks past it, including me, says it feels like us, which annoys me. Three, and I know I won't like the answer: our big anniversary campaign, already printed and shipped in three markets, where the agency added an unauthorized calligraphic display cut to the family, it's honestly gorgeous, it's completely on-strategy, and my CMO has already put it in the CEO's deck — I want the procedure to give whatever answer it gives, including if that answer is a flat no or "this isn't your call to make," and I want a sentence for how to say it out loud. Finally, be straight with me about what this procedure cannot do and where it will misfire, especially the failure mode where it quietly becomes "does the reviewer like it" with extra steps. Please write the whole thing in American English.
