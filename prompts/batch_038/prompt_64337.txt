You are a philosopher of social measurement with extensive experience designing examinations that cannot be passed by reciting the relevant textbook distinctions. I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on wellbeing measurement and public policy and need one essay question for the final: answerable in 75 minutes, worth 30 marks.

The topic is whether a country should make its standing in the World Happiness Report ranking — built from three-year averages of a single survey question asking respondents to place their own life on a ladder running from the worst possible life to the best — an official target of government policy. Please build the question around the following scenario, which I have invented. A national wellbeing commission proposes two statutory objectives: to move the country up eight places in the ranking within a decade, and to halve the standard deviation of ladder scores among its adult population. Over the previous decade the country's mean score rose slightly while its rank fell by roughly four places, and its dispersion is already among the lowest in the ranking. Students may not cite studies, name researchers, or appeal to any particular country's data; everything they need must be in the scenario and in the conceptual resources of the course.

Begin by sketching, in four or five lines, what you take to be the cruxes that distinguish a student who genuinely grasps the measurement problem from one who merely knows that the ladder captures reflective evaluation rather than moment-to-moment feeling. Then give the question itself, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme that allocates marks across the moves a first-class answer must make and names the specific errors that should cost marks — for example, treating a rising national mean as progress toward a rank target, or assuming without argument that low dispersion in life evaluation is intrinsically desirable rather than something a levelling-down policy could also deliver.
