I run the safety management system for a mid-sized cargo airline — seventeen years in flight operations, most of them spent writing procedures, chairing review boards, and arguing with crews about deviations. I have no formal philosophy training; the only thing I have read carefully in the area is Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling*, which a first officer put in my hands after an incident last winter. A captain of mine responded to smoke in the cockpit in a way that no checklist authorized and one published procedure actively discouraged, and the airplane landed with everyone alive. Our board spent three days failing to classify her. She had not made an error; nobody wanted to say she had been reckless; and the category we reserve for departures from procedure that serve the procedure's own purpose did not fit either, because nothing in the manual had authorized what she did and we could not reconstruct after the fact what purpose it served. That failure is why I read the book, and why I want to put Kierkegaard's argument through my own professional vocabulary rather than the other way around.

Here is what I take the argument to be, and I want you to correct me if I have it wrong before you translate it. The ethical, for his pseudonymous author, is the universal: it binds everyone at every moment, and the individual's task is to subsume himself under it. The tragic hero — Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus — sacrifices something he loves for an end that his community can recognize and mourn, so his act remains inside the ethical even when it is monstrous. Abraham is different: his act serves no public end, cannot be mediated into the universal, and is therefore either murder or faith, with no third option. Around this he builds several further claims: that there is a suspension of the ethical which does not abolish it but subordinates it to something higher, so that the rule comes back into force afterward and Abraham expects, in a way he cannot justify, to get his son back; that there is an absolute duty to God which can put the individual in a position where the ethical is precisely what he must not do; that faith requires a prior movement of resignation — giving up the thing entirely — before the further movement that cannot be reasoned to; and that Abraham cannot speak, because whatever he said would be heard in the register of the universal and would misdescribe him, which is why his silence toward Sarah, Eliezer, and Isaac is itself part of the problem the book raises rather than an incidental detail.

Now restate each of those claims, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually work in: standard operating procedures and the difference between their letter and their purpose; authorized versus unauthorized deviation; waivers, exemptions, and deviations granted in advance with conditions attached; the just-culture classification I was taught, which sorts outcomes into honest error, at-risk behavior, and recklessness; reportability, and the principle that an event nobody reports is an event the organization cannot learn from; delegated authority and break-glass access; the review board and its demand that any departure be articulable to people who were not there; and the slow drift we call normalization of deviance. Two rules for the translation. First, no smuggling: if a claim only goes through by quietly reinterpreting God as the regulator, the manufacturer, or the highest-order safety objective, do not present that as fidelity — say that you had to do it and count the cost. Second, give me a residue report sorted into three buckets: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion you can specify, and what has no expression in my framework at all. I expect most of the interest to be in the third bucket, and I want you to press on it rather than gesture at it.

Three questions I want answered squarely, because they are what I have been arguing about with colleagues. Does my framework have any category at all for an obligation that is genuinely required, cannot be justified in the organization's own terms before or after the fact, cannot be reported in a form that would be understood, and is nevertheless not a violation — or does the architecture of just culture simply collapse the space between beyond justification and blameworthy, so that anything unreviewable is automatically filed as recklessness? Second, is the waiver device, which looks like the natural place to put the suspension, in fact a denial of it: a waiver is an exception authored by the system that remains in force, whereas the suspension I am reading about seems to require that the exception be authored by no one inside the order of rules at all — and if that is right, does my vocabulary contain any notion of authority that is external to the procedure yet answerable to it? Third, and most importantly: when you finish, tell me whose defect the residue is. Is it a real limitation in the way safety organizations conceive of action — one worth naming, and perhaps worth building a category for — or is the residue evidence that this is not a claim about acts at all but a description of what an unshareable decision feels like from inside, dressed up as a theory of obligation? Give me your verdict and your reasons, and tell me what, if anything, you would add to my classification scheme and what it would cost to administer.
