I direct the standards and appeals function of a housing regulator in a mid-sized jurisdiction. We administer a statutory test under which an inspector must declare a dwelling "unfit for human habitation" where listed defects — damp and mould, structural movement, cold, electrical condition, crowding — are present to a degree the statute describes only as serious, or as posing an unacceptable risk to the health of the occupants. The statute supplies no numeric thresholds and expressly commits the determination to the judgment of the officer. In practice our inspectors must decide whether a crack, a humidity reading, a surface temperature, or a room area falls on one side or the other of a standard that has no visible side.

We issue roughly 240 determinations a month across eleven field offices. Both outcomes are expensive. A finding of unfitness produces an enforcement notice against an owner who may be required to spend more than the property is worth, and about one determination in six is contested on appeal. A finding of fitness leaves a household — frequently with children, frequently with someone who is ill — in a home the inspector privately believes is harming them. The officers are building surveyors and environmental health practitioners; none has legal or philosophical training. A determination gets a forty-minute site visit or a twenty-minute desk review. A finding of unfitness requires a second signature from a duty officer who did not attend. The written reasons are the only thing the appeal tribunal ever sees, and we have lost two recent appeals because the tribunal held our reasoning to be unreasoned: it found that we had been applying an unwritten numeric practice that we could neither articulate nor defend.

Auditing those cases has shown me that my inspectors are not disagreeing about the evidence. They are disagreeing about what sort of question they have been asked, and the disagreement tracks a philosophical divide almost point for point. One group behaves as though every case has a determinate answer — as though there is a precise place in the ordering of dwellings at which the predicate becomes true, which we cannot locate but which better measurement, better argument, or a more experienced colleague might bring us closer to. They refuse to publish thresholds on the ground that any line we announce is probably false; they treat the reversal of an earlier decision as the correction of an error; and they write reasons that sound like findings of fact. The other group behaves as though the statute simply leaves borderline cases undetermined, so that our task in those cases is to choose a sharpening of the standard and hold to it. They want numeric criteria published and applied prospectively; they treat the reversal of an earlier decision as a change of rule rather than a correction; and they write reasons that sound like the application of an adopted policy. Both insist they are applying the same statute. The two practices generate incompatible habits on precedent inside the organisation, incompatible reasons-language, and incompatible conduct at appeal, and neither side can say why the other is mistaken.

I do not want a comparative essay on vagueness, and I do not want a recommendation about which metaphysics is true. I want a single determination protocol that this organisation can adopt, that an officer with no philosophical training can run in the time available, and that is built on an explicit account of what actually follows from each of the two positions — epistemicism, roughly in Williamson's sense, and supervaluationism, roughly in Fine's — about how a decision-maker in our position must behave. Where the protocol takes a side, I want it to say so. The deliverables are these:

1. The ordered procedure itself: the questions the officer asks in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at which questions the procedure terminates in a finding of fitness, at which it terminates in a finding of unfitness, and at which it stops and hands the matter to the duty officer or to me because the remaining question is not a question of fact. Include a stop rule for evidence-gathering: at what point further measurement cannot improve the determination even in principle.

2. For each stage, mark its metaphysical status: truth-tracking, stipulative, or neutral. Where a stage would produce different conduct under the opposite view, say so plainly, say which view you have written into the protocol, and say whether the protocol would still function if the organisation formally adopted the other one.

3. A rule for distinguishing two things my officers currently conflate: indeterminacy in the standard and imprecision in the measurement. These come apart, and they also coincide, and the coincidence is the normal case rather than the exception. Tell me what to do in each of the three configurations.

4. A decision on thresholds. Should we publish numeric criteria? Answer separately under each view — what a published number is on each account, a claim about where the boundary lies or an announced precisification — and what obligations attach in each case regarding prospective application, review cycles, and cases decided under a superseded version. Then commit to one practice and justify it.

5. The reasons templates. Give me a model paragraph for a borderline determination written as an accuracy claim, and one written as the application of an adopted sharpening; specify when each is to be used; and give me a drafting rule for reasons that will survive review under either conception, since we do not know how the tribunal conceives its own task and we cannot ask it.

6. A treatment of comparative constraints. If dwelling A has been found unfit and dwelling B is worse in the relevant respect, must B be unfit? Address the obvious complication that worse is itself vague, so that the constraint only bites where the comparison is definite, and specify how an officer is to establish that a comparison is definite rather than borderline without appealing to the very threshold the comparison was meant to avoid.

7. A treatment of the zone around any line. If the epistemicist's own argument — that knowledge requires a safety margin, so that no one can know of a case adjacent to the boundary which side it falls on — is sound, then there should be a band in which determination is impossible for us even though there is a fact. Say whether the protocol should posit such a band, what happens inside it, what default applies when a case cannot be moved out of it, and whether that default is a discovery about the standard or a choice we make. Then show me that your protocol does not simply relocate the sorites to the question of whether a given case lies inside the band.

8. Three worked determinations, run through the protocol stage by stage with the reasons drafted in full:
   (a) A first-floor flat with persistent condensation and patchy mould in two bedrooms; the occupant is an adult with asthma; the readings sit in a band our instruments resolve cleanly and our standard does not; remediation is moderately priced.
   (b) Two adjacent houses of identical construction, orientation and measured defect, one occupied by a household including an immunosuppressed adult and one by a household in ordinary health. Whether occupant vulnerability enters the finding of unfitness, or only the specification of the remedy, is contested internally, and the answer will decide whether these two cases may lawfully be treated differently.
   (c) A dwelling whose readings fall clearly below our published criterion, in which a child's respiratory illness is nonetheless documented and the inspecting officer believes the finding of fitness is wrong. This is the case in which the protocol must give me the answer I do not want; I would rather see it do so explicitly than discover the evasion later.

For each of the three, state whether the protocol settles the matter or leaves it to a person, and identify the officer or body to whom it hands the residue. For at least one of the three, tell me whether two honest officers of the two persuasions would still diverge under the protocol, and whether that means the protocol has failed or that it has succeeded in isolating a disagreement that no procedure can dissolve.

9. The limits. State what this protocol cannot do. Name the steps that are judgment calls dressed in procedural clothing, and say which of them could be removed at the cost of what. Predict the direction of systematic error — whether the protocol will under-declare unfitness, over-declare it, or migrate borderline cases into whichever category is administratively cheaper for us — and tell me what to monitor over the first twelve months to detect that drift before an appeal tribunal detects it for me.

No references, reading list, or citations are needed and none should be supplied; where you rely on a philosopher's argument, give the argument rather than the source.
