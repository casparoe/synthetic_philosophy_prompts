I'm a novelist — fourth book out in the spring, and the whole thing is about a guy who spends twenty years writing "lost" novellas in dead authors' voices and gets away with it until the paper stock dates him. Research has left me mildly obsessed with the forgery question, and now a writers' residency I've been at twice has asked me to run the closing Friday session: 16 published novelists, no philosophers among them, 90 minutes, a room with a big table, a printer, and index cards. They want something participatory, not me talking about my book.

Design the session as an activity they play, where the puzzle lands on them before anybody says the word "forgery" out loud — and no jargon at all until the debrief, and please don't hand me a reading list, I don't want to send 16 fiction writers off to read a paper.

Here's the shape I keep circling and want you to make actually work:

- Every participant writes a short passage in their own voice, then a passage in someone else's voice at the table (assigned). Separately I've prepared a few pieces in advance: one word-for-word copy of a real published passage, and one or two pastiches I wrote in the house style.
- All of it goes up on the wall with a provenance card, and here's the part I care about most: some cards are true, some are lies, and some say only "unverifiable" — and the group has to decide what to *do* with an unverifiable one, not just whether it's good.
- They each keep a two-column ledger. Column A: which pieces are better, ranked. Column B: which pieces I'd rather have written. I want the design to force those two columns apart and then make them look at the gap.
- Halfway through, a partial reveal — some cards flipped, not all — and they re-rank. Then we compare before and after and ask the uncomfortable thing: did your experience of the sentences actually change, or did only your report of it change?
- End with real stakes: three slots in a printed chapbook the residency actually makes, so the choosing can't be purely aesthetic.

Give me the rules, the materials list and what to print beforehand, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 90, the exact wording of the provenance cards and the ledger, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the questions underneath it (why does an indiscernible copy bother us differently than an indiscernible new work — and does that distinction even hold for text, where the words just are the work?). Tell me what will predictably blow up the first time I run it — I'm guessing someone gets their style parodied badly and it stops being fun — and how to defuse that in the rules rather than in the room. And be straight with me about which feature of the real thing a 90 minute parlour game among friends simply cannot reproduce, that I'll have to supply in the talk afterwards.
