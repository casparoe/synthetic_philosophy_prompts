Last term I attended, as a visitor, a joint seminar run by our philosophy department and the number theory group on the standing of heuristic arguments in analytic number theory. Two of the regular participants — I shall call them Nadia and Colin, as neither wished to be named — argued at length about the presumption of independence that underwrites the Hardy–Littlewood and Bateman–Horn style conjectures. I took notes, I hold no view of my own on the question, and I could not afterwards say which of them had the better of it. The exchange ran as follows.

1. Nadia began with Cramér's model: treat each integer n as prime independently with probability 1/log n. From that single assumption one recovers the correct order of the prime number theorem, the Hardy–Littlewood prediction that the number of twin prime pairs below x is asymptotic to 2Cx/(log x)², with C the product over odd primes of (1 − 1/(p−1)²), and the Bateman–Horn constants for arbitrary systems of polynomials. Since these consequences are checked against computation independently of one another, she said, the assumption is confirmed in the ordinary hypothetico-deductive sense; the presumption therefore earns a high degree of belief, high enough that asserting the twin prime conjecture on its basis is not intellectual irresponsibility.

2. Colin replied that the model is not merely unconfirmed but refuted in its literal form. Maier's theorem (1985) shows that for intervals of length (log x)^A with A > 1 the normalised count of primes has no limit — the limsup exceeds 1 and the liminf falls below it — whereas the Cramér model predicts a limit of 1; and Granville's refinement, which takes account of divisibility by small primes, suggests that the limsup in Cramér's gap conjecture should be 2e^{−γ}, about 1.12, rather than 1. What has the track record, he said, is not independence but independence conditional on all the local data at every prime. Nadia was transferring credit from a false hypothesis to a far vaguer schema. He added that the very phrase presumption of independence misleads: there is no probability space on which the actual primes are random, so nothing is literally independent, and talk of confirming the model is a category confusion.

3. Nadia said that this made the schema unfalsifiable in the sense that matters. Every failure had been absorbed by enlarging the set of conditions on which one conditions, and each enlargement had been forced by results obtained without the heuristic — both Maier's theorem and Granville's constant concern the small primes. A rule that can always be repaired by inserting a factor computed after the fact from data already in hand risks nothing, and what risks nothing is not confirmed by its successes.

4. Colin answered that the local factors are not post hoc: they are fixed in advance by the congruence structure of the problem, and the framework delivers negative predictions too. When a system of polynomials has a fixed prime divisor — when Bunyakovsky's condition fails — the Bateman–Horn product vanishes and the framework predicts that prime values occur only finitely often, which is correct. A schema that can be refuted by data is the opposite of unfalsifiable.

5. Nadia then pressed the function-field case. Over the polynomial ring in one variable over a finite field, the natural analogue of Bateman–Horn is known to be wrong: for at least one polynomial all the local densities are positive, the predicted rate is matched for odd degrees, appears to run at roughly twice the predicted rate for degrees congruent to 2 modulo 4, and provably gives no irreducible values at all for degrees divisible by 4. The repair is an additional factor depending on the degree modulo 4, which is not a local density at any prime of that ring. The failures, she said, fall exactly where the local data are most favourable, and the fix always arrives from outside them — which was her point in 3.

6. Colin granted that this was her strongest example but said it proved less than she claimed: there the extra factor was established by theorem and then matched every degree class, so the schema was extended rather than rescued, and no analogous hidden symmetry is known for the integers. The deeper limit, he said, has nothing to do with incomplete conditioning. Selberg's parity problem, identified in 1949, shows that methods built from local information about small primes cannot distinguish a number with one prime factor from one with two, and that their upper bounds must be out by a factor of two or more. That is why Chen's theorem — infinitely many primes p such that p + 2 is either prime or a product of two primes — is effectively the ceiling of the sieve, even though the heuristic supporting it is no better or worse than the heuristic supporting the twin prime conjecture. The heuristic's deliverances outrun the information class that could justify them; that is a reason to be careful about what the heuristic licenses, not evidence that its outputs are false.

7. Nadia said that if the model is a mere device it should be judged by what it produces, and offered a criterion for when its products may be relied upon: the presumption may be trusted when the quantity predicted is an average over a range that is a positive power of x, and not when it concerns a single extremal event or a range shorter than any power of x. Maier's theorem is excluded by the second clause; the twin prime and Bateman–Horn counting functions are admitted by the first.

8. Colin attacked the criterion from two directions. First, the Mertens conjecture: the heuristic that μ(n) behaves like a sequence of independent random signs gives the right typical order of magnitude for the summatory function M(x) — the law of the iterated logarithm suggests √(x log log x) — and the stronger claim that |M(x)| ≤ √x held over an enormous verified range before Odlyzko and te Riele disproved it in 1985; no counterexample has ever been exhibited. Yet M(x) is an average over a positive-power range, so the criterion admits the reasoning that produced a false belief. What failed was not the scale but the logical form: a claim about typical size was converted into a universal one. Second, the function-field failure of move 5 is likewise an average over a whole degree class, so the scale clause does not exclude it either. The criterion ought instead to separate claims about asymptotic main terms from claims about existence, non-vanishing or extrema — but then it stops licensing what anyone actually wants to believe on heuristic grounds, since no asymptotic formula entails that a count is ever non-zero.

9. In closing, Nadia said Colin's position leaves us absurdly agnostic about conjectures whose numerical evidence is overwhelming. Colin said the question of how confident to be and the question of how to allocate a research programme are different questions, that her absurdity was pragmatic rather than epistemic, and that conflating the two was the real error here.

Please take the mathematical facts as I have reported them; I am not asking for references or for further examples from the literature, only for an assessment of the reasoning. Specifically:

(a) Work through moves 1 to 9 in order and rate each — clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right — with a brief justification, distinguishing the points at which a move tells against its target from those at which it merely appears to.

(b) Is Nadia's charge of unfalsifiability in move 3 answered by the negative predictions of move 4, or reinstated by the function-field case of move 5? Say which, and why the two cases should or should not be treated alike.

(c) Does any version of Nadia's criterion in move 7 survive Colin's objections in move 8? If some version does, state it precisely enough to be applied to a new conjecture; if none does, say what should take its place, and whether the replacement can still do the work heuristics are asked to do.

(d) Both argue as though the presumption of independence were a hypothesis capable of confirmation and refutation by its consequences. Assess whether that is the right conception of it, and if not, what the heuristic is doing instead — including whether Colin's category-confusion remark in move 2 undercuts his own talk of the schema being refuted by Maier's theorem.

(e) Identify what both parties are missing, and give an overall verdict: who is more right, and does the exchange settle how much confidence should be placed in a heuristic-generated conjecture such as the twin prime conjecture?
