ok so I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend about Dennett's "Where Am I?" — the one where he has his brain (Yorick) taken out and parked in a vat in Houston while his body (Hamlet) goes off to Tulsa on a dangerous errand, all hooked up over radio. My friend says the upshot is obvious: you're where your brain is, full stop, because that's where the memories and the personality and the deliberation are, and that's where the thought gets tokened even when it seems to be happening somewhere else. He thinks anyone who hesitates is just being squeamish about grey matter. I'm not buying it, and I want the strongest version of my side written down before we do round two.

Here's the case I cooked up. Keep everything from the original — brain alive and healthy in the vat, wireless link, same person's memories and values, the body walking around and holding up its end of conversations. Two changes. First, the link now has lag: about two seconds each way, sensory input reaching Yorick late and motor commands coming back late. Second, to cope with that, the surgeons have installed a local controller in the body — think of it as a replacement cerebellum plus a big chunk of motor and language circuitry — that handles balance, gait, reaching, flinching, dodging a cyclist, the rhythm and grammar of speech, all the fast online stuff. It doesn't reason or deliberate or lay down new long-term memories; it just keeps the body competent in the moment and sends a coarse summary up to the vat a couple times a minute. Yorick does the slow work: whether to take the job, how to feel about an insult, what to do next summer.

Everyone in the story still insists the person is the brain in the vat. I want to argue that in this exact setup they're wrong — that the thing actually living a life in real time, negotiating doorways, wincing, keeping a conversation moving without gaps, is out there in the body, and that Yorick has drifted into something more like a slow remote advisor who is arguably its own (diminished, laggy) seperate person now.

So: make that argument for me, as well as it can be made. Lay out the premises, and be explicit about which account of a "point of view" or a "self" each one is leaning on — I suspect a lot of the work is being done by the idea that a perspective is constituted by real-time sensorimotor coupling rather than by where the information is stored, and I want that spelled out and defended rather than assumed. Then take on the objections my friend will definitely throw: it's still the same memories and the same values so it's still him; the body has no inner life whatsoever so it can't be where a person is; and you've just described an elaborate reflex arc, and nobody ever thought reflexes were the self. If you think my thesis is too strong as stated, argue for the weaker version instead — that "where am I?" simply stops having a single true answer once the loop is slowed down and partly rerouted into the body — but pick one and actually argue it, don't just survey the options and shrug. And tell me where my case is genuinely weakest, because I'd much rather find that out now than have him find it out during the argument.
