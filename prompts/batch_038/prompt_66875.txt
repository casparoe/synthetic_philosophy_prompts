Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about Johannes Kepler's astrology, and tomorrow evening I have to give a toast at the retirement dinner of the scholar whose work made that argument possible. I would like you to write the toast, and I want to be precise about the constraints, because most of the difficulty is in them.

**The occasion.** Marianne Fenn has held a chair in the history of science for thirty-four years, almost all of it spent on Kepler's correspondence and manuscripts. The dinner is in the college hall, about sixty people, and I am the last of three speakers. I have five minutes. After me there is only the presentation of a gift, so my toast is what ends the evening and sends people out into the rain in good spirits.

**The room.** Roughly twenty historians of early modern science who know the primary literature cold and will catch every allusion; perhaps fifteen astronomers and planetary scientists whom she spent her career making welcome in an archive they tend to regard as a museum of errors; and the rest family — her husband, two daughters, a son-in-law, and four grandchildren between the ages of six and nineteen, none of whom has read a word of her scholarship and none of whom should be made to feel they missed something.

**The argument, which the toast must be built out of rather than draped over.** Her thesis — the one I have resisted for a decade and resisted again last night — is that Kepler's astrology is not a detachable embarrassment bolted onto respectable astronomy. Her strongest formulation is that the fourth book of *The Harmony of the World*, where he treats the harmonic configurations of celestial rays and their effect on sublunary nature, and the fifth, where the harmonies of the planetary motions yield what we now call the third law, are not two projects sharing a binding. One commitment generates both: that the angles at which a circle can be divided by constructible polygons are precisely the ones capable of moving a perceiving soul, terrestrial or human. On that view the aspects he retains, the additional aspects he invents, his dismissal of the zodiacal signs as causally inert, his account of a perceiving faculty in the Earth, and the harmonic ratios among the planets' angular velocities all come out of the same place, and you cannot subtract the astrology and keep the third law.

My side was that you can. Kepler spent his working life narrowing astrology: an early tract proposing to give it firmer foundations, a 1610 intervention between a physician who wanted the whole practice abolished and a working astronomer who wanted none of it touched, the signs dropped, most of the traditional apparatus dropped, only angular configurations and the moon kept. A man who prunes like that, who casts nativities for pay for Wallenstein and for himself while his mother is on trial for witchcraft, is doing something recognizable as the beginning of our own procedure. Her reply — which I have not answered, and which the toast must not pretend I answered — was that pruning toward a theory that keeps only the aspects and grounds them in the geometry of the circle is not pruning toward us at all. The cuts are made by the harmony, and the harmony is exactly what we cannot keep.

**What the toast must do.**

- Five minutes: roughly 650–750 words, written to be spoken. Short sentences. Mark two or three places where I should stop and let the room catch up.
- It must land on her side of the main question without announcing a conversion. I have not been converted, and two people in that room heard me argue the other way at length last night. If it sounds like a surrender it will embarrass me; if it sounds like I am resuming the argument at her farewell it will be unforgivable. My residual objection has to be carried inside the affection.
- One joke that works for both halves of the room, and nothing that requires Latin to land.
- Nothing in quotation marks attributed to Kepler unless you are certain of the exact wording; paraphrase instead. No bibliography, no list of her publications, no scholar's asides.
- Forbidden outright: genius, visionary, ahead of his time, mystic, mad, the stars incline but do not compel, any comparison to newspaper horoscopes or to astrology as practised today, and any suggestion that what she did was rehabilitate a superstition.
- The family must be able to follow and be moved by at least one concrete image of what she actually did for thirty-four years, containing no technical content whatsoever.
- The subject is her. Kepler is the instrument, not the hero.

After the toast, add two or three sentences telling me where you think you landed on last night's argument, and whether the speech is honest or is quietly conceding my point in order to be agreeable at a party.
