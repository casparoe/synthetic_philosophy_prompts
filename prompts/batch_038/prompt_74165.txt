Act as a philosopher of social science who's also read the economic history literature on the Great Divergence — I want you as a referee, not a summarizer. Two people at a pub thing after my department's seminar got into it about whether the classic question (my sister called it the Needham question) — why did industrialisation happen in Britain and not in China? — is even well posed. They talked over each other, so this is my reconstruction of six moves:

1. Priya: it's malformed. "Why P rather than Q" gets a different true answer depending on the foil. "Why Britain and not the Yangzi delta" is not the same question as "why Britain and not the Dutch republic" or "why Europe and not China," and nothing in the world fixes which foil is correct — so people fighting about coal vs. institutions vs. culture are often answering different questions and don't know it.

2. Tom: cheap trick. The explanandum is a hard, datable, measurable fact — by 1800 Britain was spinning cotton mechanically at scale and China wasn't. Asking what caused a real asymmetry is what a well posed question *is*. Every historical question is loosely phrased; you don't get to opt out of explaining things on those grounds.

3. Priya: but its not the phrasing that's loose, it's the units, and the units are loaded. "Britain" is a unit we chose because we know how the story ends — maybe the real unit is the North Sea economy, or England-plus-the-Randstad, or Lancashire. And "China" in 1750 is several regional economies with completely different factor endowments. Pick your units after the outcome and you've smuggled the explanandum in.

4. Tom: so sharpen the units, that's just normal research. And the foil isn't arbitrary anyway: China led on most technologies for centuries and then didn't industrialise, so Britain-vs-China is the contrast the data hands you. Also, if interest-relativity made questions illegitimate, then nothing Priya asks about her own period would be legitimate either.

5. Priya: I never said interest-relativity is a defect — I said the pretence that there's *one* determinate answer is. Related point you're skipping: half these comparisons are real-wage or per-capita output comparisons, and whether China or India "fell behind" can flip depending on whether you price the wage in silver or in grain. The measurement basis is doing the explaining.

6. Tom: that's deflationism with extra steps, and it leaves you unable to say anything. If you can't say "Britain industrialised, China didn't, here's why," you've got no history, just a pile of local anecdotes.

Think carefully. First sketch, in a few lines, what could actually make a contrastive historical question ill posed as opposed to merely imprecise — then rate each of the six moves from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a line of justification each. Say what both of them are missing, including anything from the historiography they should have reached for (Elvin's equilibrium trap, Pomeranz and the late-divergence people, Allen's high-wage/cheap-energy story). Then give me your verdict: is the question well posed, badly posed, or well posed only after some repair — and be concrete about what the repair consists in. Don't just split the difference to be nice.
