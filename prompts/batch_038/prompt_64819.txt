I'm running a 90-minute seminar on personal identity with 14 undergrads, all of whom have alredy done the Parfit-style fission and teleporter cases to death and are starting to find them a bit stale. I want an in-class activity that gets them to feel a rather different move, the one Hofstadter makes: that a self is a pattern, and patterns don't have to live inside a single skull — a substantial chunk of any person you know closely is instantiated in your head, so that the 'I' is smeared out across everyone who carries it, and death becomes a loss of degree rather than of kind. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the thesis or the name until the debrief.

Design it so they run into this on their own. Something where the group has to reconstruct or act on a person whose parts exist only, distributed, in other students' heads — but shape the mechanic however you like. Give me: the setup and roles, the rules and materials (nothing that costs money or needs tech beyond a whiteboard), a forced choice with some small real stake attached — they should have to say who survives, or who gets to keep going, or who counts as the same person, and it should be uncomfortable — plus a running order that actually fits in 90 minutes. Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the view, and handle the pushback I know is coming, which is that my being able to predict my mother's reactions doesn't put any part of her in me.

Also tell me what will predictably fall apart the first time I run it and how to recover mid-session, and be frank about which feature of the distributed-self picture the activity just cannot reproduce, so I know I have to supply it by hand.

Keep it under 700 words — I'll be reading this on the train.
