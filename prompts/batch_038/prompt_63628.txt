Three months ago I left an academic post in behavioural ecology to become the animal welfare lead at a producer of farmed marine fish and whiteleg shrimp. My first deliverable is the company's welfare policy, and it has to survive three audiences: a certification auditor who will only credit what can be measured at visit, a retailer whose procurement team cares mainly about reputational risk, and a board that will fund exactly one major capital item next year. Because the budget forces a single priority, I need to know whether the priority I have already chosen is defensible or merely convenient. I formed the view before I joined, and six months of site visits have made it feel more obvious to me, which is precisely why I want it attacked by someone with no stake in the outcome.

I will call it the terminal-harm view. It has three claims.

First, in aquaculture the morally weighty harms cluster at the end of life and around movement: the slaughter event itself, pre-slaughter fasting and crowding, grading and handling, pumping and dewatering, transport, and live sale. These are acute, universal — every animal produced goes through them — and in current practice they are frequently botched.

Second, day-to-day conditions are second-order. Stocking density, featureless environments, no foraging opportunity, restricted vertical movement, and water quality within regulatory limits all matter, but I hold that the wild counterparts of these animals lead lives that are at least as harsh: they are packed by shoal dynamics and resource competition, heavily parasitised, frequently starving, and the large majority die young and badly, mostly by predation. Captivity removes the predators and buffers starvation; I do not think it has been shown to add a burden of comparable size.

Third, therefore policy effort and capital belong where the harm is: enforceable stunning and slaughter standards, prohibitions on the worst kill methods and on live transport and live sale, and — because total harm scales with the number of individuals brought into existence — an output cap. Husbandry enrichment I would treat as marketing rather than welfare.

What would count against this? I would like the defeaters ranked, and each tagged as decisive, serious, awkward, or rhetorical, with a sentence on why it sits at that level rather than the next one up. In particular:

- Which measurements, findings, or arguments, if they turned out true, would require me to abandon the priority ordering rather than merely to phrase it more carefully? Be concrete about what the measurement would look like and who would have to produce it.
- What has arguably already arrived? I want an honest read on the current state of the evidence, not on what would be needed in an ideal literature — including anything from fish stress physiology and welfare-outcome measures, and anything from crustacean nociception, learning, or anaesthesia responsiveness that bears on the first two claims.
- Which of these would a committed defender of my view concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss? Where they dismiss, say whether the dismissal is legitimate or motivated.
- What does the terminal-harm view predict that its rivals do not — a husbandry-first view, or a view on which the only serious remedy is to produce and eat far less aquatic animal protein? I want the disagreements stated so that they could actually be joined by data rather than argued past each other.
- Is the second claim doing real work, or is it decorative? If it were simply deleted, would the policy conclusion change? And does it fare differently for the fish side of the business than for the shrimp side?
- Two worries I have about myself. One: slaughter reform is auditable and husbandry is not, so I may be prioritising what is verifiable and dressing it as a claim about harm. Two: a discrete, vivid, terminal event may grip human attention far more than months of diffuse low-grade adversity, so my intuition about where the harm is may be an artefact of how people register suffering rather than of how these animals experience it. Assess both.
- Finally, whether the view risks anything at all. I suspect that any evidence about chronic in-life suffering can be absorbed by re-weighting — conceding that it matters while insisting it matters less. If that is right, say plainly whether it is a defect. My purpose is budget allocation, so a view that cannot be dislodged by evidence has a real cost even if it is philosophically comfortable.

I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I have no journal access here and invented references would be worse than none. Describe findings and arguments in your own terms, and mark your confidence where you are reconstructing rather than recalling.

Please close with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of these labels and nothing else on that line: FATAL; SERIOUSLY EXPOSED; EXPOSED BUT SURVIVABLE; WELL INSULATED, PROBLEMATIC; WELL INSULATED, BENIGN.
