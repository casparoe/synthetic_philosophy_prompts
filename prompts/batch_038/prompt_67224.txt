ok so I'm in a seminar on pluralism and relativism and we've spent the last three weeks on the Jain stuff — anekantavada, syad, naya, the whole apparatus — and for our final session everyone has to bring something that actually does philosophical work instead of just being decorative. I'm doing a sestina. I need you to write it, and I need you to commit to a reading rather than give me a menu of options.

here's the conceit, and please follow it literally instead of just gesturing at it:

- real sestina: six stanzas of six lines plus a three-line envoi, end-words rotating in the standard pattern (so stanza two is 6-1-5-2-4-3 off stanza one, etc). the reason I picked this form is the reason the poem should exist: run the permutation through and every one of the six end-words ends up occupying every one of the six line-positions exactly once. that's the doctrine, structurally — same six elements, no ordering of them is the privileged one. someone who knows sestinas should be able to feel that happening without anyone pointing at it.
- the six end-words should be plain english nouns or adjectives, not transliterated terms, and each should be the kind of thing that can be honestly affirmed and honestly denied depending on the respect you're predicating from. I want them concrete enough that the poem has an actual subject — pick a single object or situation and keep returning to it, don't let it drift into abstraction about truth in general.
- one stanza should visibly be doing the indexing work: same end-word affirmed and then denied within a few lines because the place changed, or the time changed, or the substance/aspect you're talking about changed. not "well, it depends," but the reader seeing which parameter moved.
- the seventh mode, the indescribable one, can't be an end-word by definition — the end of a line is exactly the spot where a word gets pinned down. so it has to live in the envoi, and the envoi should make it clear why it couldn't have had a slot in the rotation.
- please no blind men and no elephant. everyone in my section will have done the elephant.

the turn I actually want: our prof keeps pressing the self-application objection. if no proposition can be asserted without conditions, then that claim itself can't be asserted without conditions — so does the doctrine eat itself, or is self-application the one thing that makes it coherent rather than a special-pleading move? I want the poem to come down on one side of that. and specifically I don't want the shrug version, which he has a phrase for: non-one-sidedness as a very sophisticated excuse for never being wrong. If your poem's answer is "both are partly right," it's failed the assignment even if every line scans.

after the poem give me a short note, committed and not hedged: which side of the self-application problem the poem takes and why you think that's the one a Jain logician could actually defend, then flag honestly where you bent the doctrine to make the form work — compressed the standpoints, leaned on a contested reading of what "in some way" is doing, made the seventh mode more mystical than it should be, whatever you did. I'd rather know the liberties than have a pretty poem I can't defend in front of twelve people.
