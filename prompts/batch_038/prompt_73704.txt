Context first, since it'll shape what I want: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever (one intro elective I dropped in week three). I fell down a rabbit hole last weekend and now I want a story out of it, because I think the idea is more vivid than any explanation I could give a coworker.

The thing at work: we have a service that scores candidate actions for an automated system by expected value. It literally enumerates outcomes, multiplies payoff by probability, sums them, ranks the actions. Nobody on the team questions it. Then I ran into what I've seen called the Pasadena game, and it seems to break the whole concept in a way that I find genuinely spooky:

- You flip a coin until it comes up heads. If the first heads lands on flip *n*, the payoff is 2^n / n dollars, with the sign alternating: +2 for n=1, −2 for n=2, +8/3 for n=3, −4 for n=4, and so on.
- The chance the first heads lands on flip *n* is 1/2^n.
- So the expected-value terms come out to 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + 1/5 − ..., which converges to ln 2, about 69 cents.
- Except that series only converges *conditionally*. Sum just the positive terms and you get +infinity; sum just the negatives and you get −infinity. Which means you can reorder the terms to make the total come out to any number you like, or make it fail to converge at all.
- So the expected value of this bet isn't a property of the bet. It's a property of the order you happened to add things up in. In my world that's the equivalent of: same input, different answer, depending on which order a hash map iterated in.
