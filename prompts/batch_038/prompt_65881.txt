I am assembling materials for the territorial-rights week of my graduate seminar, and I want a rigorous audit of an analogy that turns up on every side of the Quebec and Catalan disputes: secession as divorce. In the mock submission I have written for the students to critique, the analogy is deployed in one specific direction. No-fault divorce does not require either spouse to prove mistreatment; it requires only that the marriage has irretrievably broken down, together with whatever cooling-off or separation period the jurisdiction retains. So, the submission argues, a political community should not have to establish a history of injustice before it may leave a state: a clear majority vote, perhaps after a waiting period, is the settled decision, and the fault-based alternative — secession only as remedy for serious and persistent wrongs — corresponds to the outdated regime that demanded proof of adultery or cruelty. The submission adds that the remainder state's opposition is like the opposition of the spouse who does not want the divorce: real, but without standing to block it.

Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — which features of divorce play the roles of the demos, the territory, the shared assets and public debt, the residents who did not vote to leave, the waiting period, the settlement regime — and then separate the disanalogies that genuinely defeat the inference from those that are merely rhetorically awkward. Which features of the source is the submission quietly importing that the target lacks? Does the no-fault conclusion survive in full, in restricted form, or not at all; and if restricted, state the restriction and the reason for it. If some other analogy would carry the intended weight better, say what it is and what it does that this one cannot. I want a committed assessment with reasons, not a survey of positions for me to choose among.
