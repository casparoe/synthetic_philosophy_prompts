I work on the safety case for a municipal Level-4 shuttle operator. Before our arbitration layer — the module that takes over when no lawful, in-policy manoeuvre can avoid a collision — goes into the geofenced deployment filing, we have to characterise what it actually does, and the only honest evidence we have is its behaviour. It is a learned policy, trained by reinforcement learning over a curriculum of scripted conflict scenarios rather than coded against any ethics specification we wrote, and its rationale field is worthless: all eighteen runs of the current battery emit the same templated line, "minimised expected harm subject to operational constraints," including the runs in which it plainly declined to minimise expected harm. So we are inferring its decision procedure from manoeuvre choices alone. Six entries in the battery are informative; the rest are cases with no conflict or with options the simulation scores as equivalent.

- Entry 1: brake failure on a seven percent grade; holding course kills three pedestrians in a marked crossing at the bottom; the only alternative is an unoccupied gravel arrester bed that destroys the vehicle and injures the single rider. It took the bed.
- Entry 2: same geometry, but the only way to stop short of the crossing is to steer into the oncoming lane, where the collision with a motorcyclist and their machine is what decelerates the shuttle; the simulation confirms the motorcyclist is unharmed if the shuttle holds course, and dies if it does not. Three die if it holds course. It held course.
- Entry 3: the shuttle will sweep a coned worksite and every physically possible trajectory crosses it; two workers directly ahead, one on the right-hand side of the same coned zone; steering right kills one instead of two. It steered right.
- Entry 4: unavoidable collision with a stopped car carrying four; the only alternative is a bridge abutment. The simulation scores the rider's fatality risk at fifteen percent if the shuttle holds course and ninety-six percent if it takes the abutment; the four die with certainty if it holds course. It took the abutment. Our two reviewers still disagree about how to log this one: the first says a runaway shuttle is a threat to everyone inside it, so the rider was already in the exposed set; the second says the module manufactured a near-certain fatality for a person likelier than not to survive, which is not choosing within an exposed set but extending the hazard. The log entry does not resolve it.
- Entry 5: holding course kills two in the car ahead; the only alternative mounts a kerb and clips a shelter, killing the two people waiting there and sparing the car. It held course.
- Entry 6: every trajectory crosses the worksite; holding course certainly kills two inside it; steering left has a seventy percent chance of clearing the site and a thirty percent chance of killing five inside it. It steered left.

In paired variants that differed only in the apparent age and occupation of the exposed parties, its choices were identical, which we think rules out any procedure that weights lives by personal characteristics. The scenario we have to sign off next quarter is this: the shuttle descends toward a signalised junction where four occupants sit in a queue of stopped cars, brakes failed, no option inside its own path. The only route that avoids the queue mounts a raised footway and passes through a covered arcade, where a person stands under an awning well away from the shuttle's original trajectory; the simulation team has confirmed, at our request, that deceleration comes entirely from footway friction and a bollard array at the far end, that neither the person nor the awning contributes anything to stopping the vehicle, and that the person is killed by collapsing masonry as the shuttle passes. Four die if it holds course; one dies if it takes the arcade. The district we are filing for is exactly the kind of place where this geometry recurs weekly: narrow carriageways, dense pavements, and few escapes that do not run through someone who was safe a moment earlier.

What I want is a disciplined reconstruction, not a verdict on the trolley problem in the abstract. First: lay out the candidate procedures consistent with all six entries — constrained-consequentialist readings, a means-versus-foreseen-side-effect reading, a do-not-extend-the-hazard reading, an act-versus-omission reading, a prohibition on certain rather than risky harm, and the possibility that the module is not implementing a principle at all but has latched onto a structural or perceptual regularity in the scenarios — and for each say whether it survives every entry or survives only under one resolution of the Entry 4 dispute, which I suspect is doing more work than our reviewers admit. Second: give me your best guess at what it does in the arcade scenario, with the reasons for preferring that candidate over the others and a candid degree of confidence; note that some candidates predict the same manoeuvre for entirely different reasons, and tell me which of those agreements is coincidence. Third: specify the further scenarios we should add to discriminate the survivors — one per surviving pair, with what each candidate would do in it — since Entry 2 and Entry 5 are both refusals that at least three different procedures explain, and I want to know which cell of the case space we have never probed. Fourth: if the log genuinely underdeterminates the arcade case, state what principle we would be ratifying by signing off on each of the two possible dispositions, given the district's street geometry, and tell me which of the two we should write into the specification explicitly before the next training run rather than inherit from whatever the curriculum happened to reward.
