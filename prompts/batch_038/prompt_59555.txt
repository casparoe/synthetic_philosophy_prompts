So I've been handed one session of an intro seminar on medieval philosophy and I want to open with something that stops the causation material feeling like a museum piece. The topic is al-Ghazālī on cause and effect — the Tahāfut discussion (the seventeenth one, I'm fairly sure, out of the twenty) where he refuses to grant that fire burns cotton by any necessity belonging to the fire's own nature, and says what we're actually witnessing is that burning happens *at* the contact, on God's customary way of arranging things. My problem is that after twenty minutes half the room files this under "so he thought the world was arbitrary," which is exactly backwards: he's not denying the regularity, he's denying that the regularity is metaphysically necessary, and he'd happily concede you can still predict tomorrow. I want the exercise to make them feel that distinction in their bones rather than recite it.

Here's the thing I'd like you to write. A fake internal underwriting bulletin from a takaful/reinsurance syndicate that has completely and sincerely internalized Ash'arite occasionalism and is now trying to run a profitable commercial book on it — deadpan compliance-and-claims-handling prose, the register of a market circular where somebody has had to think very hard about wording and capital. Around 900 to 1100 words. The jokes have to come from the doctrine being handled correctly, so a specialist reads it and thinks "yes, that is exactly what the position commits you to," not "that's a strawman." Specifically I want the syndicate's rating philosophy to be *more* relentlessly empiricist than its rivals' — it has no natures to lean on, so it prices purely off the observed frequency of the Habit — and I want the bulletin to be honest that this is a strength, not a squeamish concession. Then the mess: a solvency regulator making them hold capital against discontinuation of the customary sequence; a defined term like "Non-Customary Occurrence" that is obviously doing the work of miracles (someone's had to decide whether the Abraham-and-the-fire incident is an excluded peril or a covered one, and whether a staff turning into a serpent is a single event or an aggregation issue); a rival syndicate run by the falāsifa that wrote necessary connexion into its policy wording and is now in run-off, mentioned without malice; an actuary with Mu'tazilite sympathies who keeps insisting the loss arose from the insured's own act and therefore liability attaches, and the compliance problem that creates for everyone under the doctrine of kasb; and a three-part list of grounds for declining a claim that structurally echoes the three charges of unbelief. I'd also like the bulletin to attach, as a grudging appendix note, an external auditor's objection that is essentially Ibn Rushd's — that if things have no natures there's no such thing as knowledge and hence no actuarial science — together with the syndicate's written response, which should be good enough that a reader can't quite dismiss it.

Format: give it these sections in this order, with roughly these lengths — **1. Purpose and Scope of this Bulletin** (~120 words); **2. Definitions** (~170 words, where Cause, Habit, Peril and Non-Customary Occurrence get defined in a way that is quietly doing all the metaphysics); **3. The Habit Discontinuance Capital Charge** (~180 words); **4. Claims Handling: Grounds for Declining** (~200 words); **5. Sidebar — the Peripatetic Syndicate, and why we are not smug about it** (~130 words); **6. FAQ for Newly Appointed Adjusters** (~160 words); **Appendix A: Auditor's Objection and Syndicate Response** (~140 words). Then after the whole document, one titled section called **Afterword: what's load-bearing here** (~250 words) saying which bits are doctrinally accurate and which you've stretched for comedy, and what a serious reader of the Tahāfut would most likely object to about the framing. Two house rules: no bibliography and no citations, I'll point students at translations myself; and don't put anything in quotation marks that comes from a real text — every quote in the bulletin should be invented in-world (a circular from the Sharia board, a line from the syndicate's founder, whatever), because I don't want to be fact-checking your al-Ghazālī in front of nineteen undergrads. Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking, and let the reader work out what's being ironized.
