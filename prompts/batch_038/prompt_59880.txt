I write novels for a living, but for a couple of years now I've also run a small in-conversation series at a bookshop — mostly novelists, once in a while somebody from a completely different discipline. Next month's guest is a philosopher of science and the whole evening hangs on one idea: reference classes. Her new book argues that the reference class problem was never a problem about the world. Her line, roughly: the old rule — use the narrowest class you actually have statistics for — fails because there's always a narrower one, and the fix isn't a better rule about classes, its a better account of questions. A probability claim about a single case, she says, is only ever an answer to a question somebody actually has, and the question fixes the class. Admissible classes are the ones carved out by live questions, so the gerrymandered-predicate objection (the grue-style move where you invent a property that splits any class in half) supposedly doesn't bite, because nobody has a live question whose answer turns on that predicate. She's warm, she's funny on stage, and she's extremely good at answering a slightly different question than the one you asked.

I want eight to ten questions, case-first. My audience is novel-readers, so no question should contain a term I'd have to stop and define, and every one should open with somebody in a situation rather than with a thesis. Here are the places I think the view creaks and I want them all worked in: the one-person class — her whole argument starts from the fact that you can always narrow down to the individual, so why can't you still do it after the question is fixed, and what stops every probability collapsing to zero or one; two doctors with two different live questions about the same patient getting two different and apparently both-legitimate numbers, and whether she's a relativist or thinks one question is better; the civil case where four out of five buses in town are blue and the only evidence is that the bus that hit her was blue — on her account is that a verdict or isn't it; the doomsday-style puzzle where someone is told their birth rank among humans and asked to guess the total, which looks like a completely specified question and yet she says it dissolves; the fact that "is there a live question" smells a lot like Goodman's entrenchment with the serial numbers filed off; and the one that actually keeps me up, which is the parole-board consultant who genuinely wants to know reoffending rates among defendants of some ethnicity — that's a live question by her lights, and I can't find anything in the view that rules the class out. Plus one for me: when I write "a forty-year-old divorced schoolteacher," my reader imports base rates she picked, so is my character's probability fixed by the reader's question, and if it is, what's left of the idea that I'm describing a person rather than a slot in someone's inquiry?

For each question give me the follow-up, keyed to the answer she's most likely to actually give rather than to a generic dodge — I know roughly what she'll say to at least half of these and I want to be holding the next sentence when she says it. Flag which ones you expect her to steer into a lecture about something else. Close with one question about what would count as evidence against her account and one that lets her say the thing nobody ever asks her, because if I corner her for forty straight minutes she'll never come back to my shop. Each question has to be sayable out loud in about two sentences, none of them smug, and don't burn a single one on what got her interested in probability.
