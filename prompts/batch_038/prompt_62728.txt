So here's a slightly weird ask. I write about science and technology for a general-interest magazine, and last month I ran a long piece about the so-called alignment tax — the idea that doing serious safety work costs a lab ground in the race to build better models. The piece got more pushback than anything I've written in years, from several directions at once, and now a policy think tank has invited me to come defend it in a 25-minute talk followed by a real Q&A with about thirty people in the room. I've done panel stuff before but never a hostile-ish seminar where the audience knows the material better than I do. I need a prep sheet for the question period, not encouragement.

The argument, roughly: almost everyone talks about the alignment tax as a capability cost — the safer model is dumber, or the safer training run burns compute that could have gone into scale. My claim is that the tax is mostly mispriced, because the cost that actually binds is calendar time, and calendar time is the only currency the race is really denominated in. I split the tax into three channels: (1) compute and headcount diverted into evals, red-teaming, and interpretability; (2) release gating — shipping something narrower, more supervised, more staged, instead of one big general launch; (3) internal process, i.e. giving safety people sign-off rights that slow decisions down. My contention is that (1) and (3) are basically fixed costs that amortize across a whole product line and even partially leak to rival labs, so they barely move relative position, while (2) is the one that genuinely costs you the race, because it delays the loop of real users giving feedback that feeds the next model. The punchline I land on: when labs invoke competitiveness to justify skipping safety work, they almost always invoke it against (1) — the part that doesn't actually cost them the race — which suggests those appeals aren't really about inter-firm competition at all. They're about internal bargaining. Safety work that is cheap for the company is still expensive for the team that has to absorb it, because it draws on the same engineers and the same schedule, and "we can't afford it competitively" is a much more respectable thing to say upstairs than "this will make my group miss its milestone." So: the alignment tax is largely an intra-firm distributional conflict wearing the costume of an arms race. I know the soft spots — I have no good numbers on how many weeks anything takes, I'm assuming the user-feedback loop is a big driver of capability gains which is contestable, and the (1)-leaks-to-rivals point can be turned around as a public-goods argument for why firms underinvest even in the cheap stuff, which cuts against me.

The room, as best I can tell from the invite list: three or four ex-lab people who now do frontier-model policy and think almost entirely in evals and thresholds, so they'll want to know what "weeks" means operationally and against which counterfactual; two industrial-organization economists, one of whom works on software platforms, who I think will accuse me of treating competition as a psychological state inside a firm rather than as market structure, and who will want to know whether the race is even a race if there's no winner-take-all; a couple of people adjacent to EA money who suspect the whole tax framing is a rationalization for going fast and will ask why I take the labs' own account of their incentives at face value; one historian of technology who I'm fairly sure will say this is just the recurring speed-versus-safety tradeoff from aviation and autos and drug approval with extra steps, and that my "calendar time is the only currency" line is the novel part and also the wrong part; and at least one former congressional staffer who will be irritated that a piece this long offers nothing actionable.

Give me the eight-ish objections I'll actually get from *this* audience — not the Twitter version, not the LessWrong version — ordered by how much damage each does to the argument rather than by how likely it is. Phrase each one the way that specific kind of person would phrase it, with their vocabulary and their pet examples, and for each give me a reply I can actually deliver in about two minutes standing up, plus a frank note on where that reply is softest and what I should concede before I get made to concede it. Tell me which two or three objections have real force and deserve a genuine concession in the first sixty seconds, and which is the single reply that has to land or the whole talk collapses. And then tell me the question I'm praying nobody asks — the one that goes straight at a load-bearing assumption I can't really support in public.
