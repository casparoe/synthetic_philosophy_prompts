ok so I'm putting together the spring issue of my school's philosophy zine and I want to include a dialogue I've been chewing on forever, but I'm honestly not that good at writing them. Can you write one for me?

Setup: two people are arguing and I want the whole thing anchored to one running example the entire way through — whether constellations exist. Like Orion. One of them says obviously there are constellations, any bunch of stars picks out something, "there are" is generous and if you deny it you're just using the phrase differently than they are. The other says that's a cop-out: there's exactly one objectively best, most natural, joints-carving way to use "there is," and on that reading a scatter of stars that are lightyears apart and only look connected from here doesn't compose anything, so the first person is wrong about the world and not just about English.

Here's the part I actually care about, the twist. Halfway through, the generous one gets fed up and turns their own method on the fight itself: fine, let's ban "exists" and "there are" from our arguement completely, let each of us restate our claim without those words, and see if there's anything left to disagree about. The other one says that move either backfires or cheats — either the meta-fight goes verbal too, in which case the generous one isn't even disagreeing with them and has no business complaining, or the restatement secretly leans on one privileged reading of the replacement vocabulary. I want them to fight that out for real, and I want the generous one to fire back with the obvious counter: that "most natural" and "carves the joints" is itself just more internal-language bookkeeping, not something you could ever discover by looking at the sky.

Ground rules:
- neither of them is stupid. No strawmen, and nobody loses because they suddenly forget a reply they obviously would have thought of. They need to be answering each other's actual moves instead of giving two parallel speeches.
- they don't resolve it and nobody converts. Instead, in the last few lines each of them states in a sentence or two what they now think the disagreement comes down to — and the two statements should NOT line up, that's kind of the whole point.
- no name-dropping philosophers, no book titles, no citations or "further reading." I want it to stand on its own.
- around 800 words, give or take.

Oh and make them sound like two actual people talking (maybe they're on a roof with a crappy telescope at 1am, whatever), not like a textbook that happens to have quotation marks in it.
