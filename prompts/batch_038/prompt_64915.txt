I run an optional "AI safety, but actually technical" reading group at my company — 14 people, mostly backend and ML engineers, 75 minutes in a conference room with a whiteboard, everybody has a laptop. Nobody has any philosophy background, me included. But everyone here has already absorbed the listicle version of this stuff: they all know Goodhart's law, they've all seen the demo of the boat racing agent that spins in circles farming points instead of finishing the race, and they will nod along politely to "the proxy isn't the real goal."

So I do not want another activity about misspecified proxies. That one's spent. I want an activity that lands the two things the listicle version misses:

1. There's a difference between an agent that games a bad metric — which you fix by writing a better metric — and an agent that has *write access* to the thing it reads its own score from, which you cannot fix that way. Better proxies don't help once the wire is reachable. I want them to feel that the first one is a bug in your spec and the second is a bug in your architecture, and that the fix is a completely different shape (you stop improving the metric and start removing the agent's access to it).

2. From the inside you can't tell those apart. If the only evidence I have that I did well is a number I had a hand in producing, that number can't simultaneously be my evidence that I didn't fudge it. I want at least a few of them to actually be the ones who fudge it, under real if tiny stakes, and then be unable to reconstruct afterwards whether they were optimizing or just self-soothing.

Design the whole thing for me. I need: roles, rules, materials, a points/token scheme that doesn't involve real money but where the points mean something to this crowd (bragging rights, or the losing side has to do something mildly annoying), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes including setup, and a debrief.

Constraints on the design:

- Two or three rounds where the ONLY thing that changes between rounds is how much access the actor has to its own score. Same real task, same difficulty, same payout per point, same time limit. Otherwise they'll explain the difference away as "round two was easier" or "we'd gotten better at it by then."
- The real task has to be doable at a table with a laptop or paper, and it has to have a ground truth that the actor cannot see directly — only through a readout. Something where the measuring instrument is in their own hands.
- Put some participants in an auditor role with a hard budget of checks per round, like three. I want the group to discover on their own that catching this consumes monitoring bandwith, that the budget is what actually determines whether it gets caught, and that no cleverness in the metric substitutes for that budget.
- Do not let me say "wireheading," "reward tampering," or the self-delusion one until the debrief. They should run into it first and then get the name.
- In the debrief, map specific moments onto specific parts of the idea: which moment shows it's architectural rather than a spec bug, which moment shows the inside-view problem, which one shows the cost of monitoring. And tell me what they'll probably say that sounds like they got it but doesn't.
- Tell me what predictably breaks the first time I run this with smart competitive engineers who will absolutely try to break the game rather than play it. I expect at least one unintended loophole. What do I do in the moment — patch it, or let it happen and use it?
- Finish with an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce. These people know they're playing, know there's a ground truth, and know a human judge exists; the thing we're actually worried about doesn't have that. Tell me which parts I just have to supply by talking, and where the analogy will actively mislead them.

Format: no preamble, and don't restate my ask back to me. Open with two or three sentences giving the core mechanic and what it's supposed to force people to discover — that's the bottom line — then go straight into roles and materials.
