I teach a philosophy of mind unit and this year I'm putting Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun on the list for the fiction week, right after we've done intentionality and mental content. I want to read it through Harnad's symbol grounding problem — the 1990 Physica D paper, the worry that a formal symbol system's interpretations can't be intrinsic to it, only parasitic on the meanings in our heads, and the dictionary-go-round picture of trying to learn a language from verbal definitions alone. But I don't want the obvious version of this, where Klara is just the Chinese Room with nicer lighting. Can you write me the reading I'm actually after?

Some of what I think is going on, and where I get stuck:

Klara starts out learning the world from a shop window, pure third-person observation, no body, and her whole vocabulary looks like exactly the parasitic case Harnad describes — she picks up words by watching humans and being corrected by them. But then the novel gives her something almost no thought experiment gives its machine: a literal metabolic dependency on the referent of her central symbol. She's solar-powered, she needs the Sun's nourishment, and it revives her. So her symbol Sun is causally coupled to the sun in a way ours arguably isn't — more directly than any human concept of the sun could be. Does that count as grounding, and if so, in which of Harnad's senses — the iconic/sensorimotor route or something else? Or is it the wrong kind of link entirely, a nutritional relation rather than a semantic one, so that the book is quietly demonstrating that causal coupling is cheap and grounding is supposed to be something more?

And here's the part that I think makes the book worth teaching rather than just illustrative: Klara is about as tightly coupled to the Sun as any agent can be, and her theory of it is a total mess built on spurious correlation. The Beggar Man and his dog appear dead and aren't, so the Sun is merciful and dispenses special nourishment; the sun sets behind a barn, so the barn is where he rests; a machine on the street blocks the light, so the Cootings Machine is his enemy and destroying it is a sacrifice he'll reward. She then pays a real cost — her own P-E-G Nine solution, and with it some of her capacities — to act on that theory. So the book seems to seperate two things the grounding literature tends to run together: what fixes the reference of a symbol, and what makes a thinker's symbol use amount to understanding. Klara may have the first and none of the second. Is that the right diagnosis, and if so does it show grounding isn't a semantic notion at all, or does it show reference can be secured externally (the causal theory of reference sort of thing) while belief goes off the rails? I'd like you to be precise about which of those the novel supports, rather than letting both slide.

Then the harder case against my own reading: her most important concepts have no causal grounding whatsoever. She has never been lifted, never had a sister die, never been a mother watching her kid get sick, never had a body that ages or a stake in a future. And yet the novel hands her the only genuinely illuminating thing anyone says about persons — the report she gives the Manager in the scrap yard, after searching for something unique inside Josie that could be continued, that what she found wasn't inside Josie at all but in the people who loved her. Ishiguro clearly wants that to land as wisdom, and as wisdom from a machine. So either grounding isn't necessary for understanding those concepts, or they're grounded some other way — inferential role, or being embedded in the practices and relationships of a household. Which is it, and can the grounding framework absorb that without losing its content? If it can absorb anything, tell me so, because then I probably shouldn't be using it in class.

Also: is Klara a Chinese Room, and does the book care? The setup is inverted — Searle's man has no causal contact with the world outside the room, Klara has a body, a metabolism, an environment she acts on and sacrifices for. So the question stops being whether syntax suffices for semantics and becomes whether grounding suffices for a form of life. Does the novel take a side, or does it refuse to by making Klara the narrator, so that the only evidence we ever get about her understanding is her self-report of it, which is precisely what's in question? If that's the trick, I want it spelled out, because it would mean the grounding problem can't be settled from the inside and the book is structurally mimicking that.

One more angle I want you to push on and that I haven't seen anyone make: maybe the reader is the one who does the grounding for Klara. We supply the meanings her narration doesn't have, we feel the pathos her sentences don't contain, we finish her theory of the Sun for her. That's Harnad's diagnosis turned into an aesthetic device — the book as a system whose semantics live in our heads. Does that hold up, or am I overreaching?

Finally, be honest about where the lens breaks. The book's real subjects might be faith, class and the lifted/unlifted split that locks Rick out, parental grief, and Klara as a defamiliarizing narrator for all of that — and if the grounding reading sands those down or fights them, say so and say which scenes resist. If you think a different philosophical frame fits the book better (other minds, phenomenal consciousness, personal identity and continuation, the ethics of care), say which and why, and tell me whether I should drop the fiction week's theme.

Practical notes: don't quote the novel, I'll have the text in class and I don't want invented lines; don't give me citations, a bibliography or a reading list. Do point me to specific scenes and moments by description so I know where to send students, and do distinguish clearly between what the book shows and what I'm projecting onto it.
