Last month I finished a best-selling trade book on consciousness, and its chapter on the mind–body problem contains the following summary of epiphenomenalism, which I copied into my notebook:

> Epiphenomenalism is the view that consciousness is caused by the brain but causes nothing in return — like the steam whistle on a locomotive: real, loud, and irrelevant to where the train goes. On this picture your experience of deciding does not produce the decision; the two are joint downstream products of neural events, so nothing you consciously do makes any difference to what happens. That is why the view cannot be squared with evolution: natural selection cannot select for a trait that does nothing, so if epiphenomenalism were true, consciousness would have to be a biologically inexplicable surplus. David Chalmers, the view's most prominent defender, meets this objection with what is called the paradox of phenomenal judgment: because physical processes fully account for every word we ever utter about consciousness, our talk of experience cannot have been caused by experience itself, and so the epiphenomenalist cannot even explain why she believes what she believes — which, Chalmers argues, disposes of the objection, since a line of reasoning that would undermine the epiphenomenalist's judgments would undermine all judgments, including the objector's own. The upshot is that consciousness must stand in the right causal relation to our reports of it, and the only way to secure that relation is to identify experience with the physical processes that produce the reports.

I found the passage persuasive when I read it, but a philosopher friend who glanced over my shoulder said it was a tissue of small errors and would not say more. I do not have the training to locate them myself, and I would rather not simply take her word for it. Could you work through the passage claim by claim and tell me the following?

1. Which sentences are accurate as stated, which are defensible simplifications of a more careful thesis, and which are wrong. In particular, is "caused by the brain but causes nothing in return" the actual content of epiphenomenalism, or does the standard formulation deny something narrower — and does the view really rule out experience playing any causal role at all, including with respect to other mental states?

2. Whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment is characterized and attributed correctly. Is it an objection that Chalmers deploys against his critics, or a difficulty that he himself formulates for his own view? And how does he treat it — as a refutation of the objection, as the most serious unresolved problem for property dualism, or as something he thinks can be answered by an appeal to acquaintance or to a special class of phenomenal concepts?

3. Whether the argument, stated at full strength, shows epiphenomenalism to be formally self-contradictory, or whether it does something weaker and more interesting — for instance, undercutting the epistemic standing of phenomenal judgments without contradicting the thesis. If the latter, does the book's "undermines itself" response have any force?

4. Whether the evolutionary objection is as decisive as the passage implies. What exactly does selection operate on, and what can an epiphenomenalist say about the fact that the neural machinery correlated with experience was plainly selected for even if experience itself was inert? Is there a residual problem here that survives the reply?

5. Whether the final step follows at all. If the paradox bites, does it really leave only the identification of experience with brain processes, or does the book's framing quietly exclude live alternatives — interactionist dualism, Russellian monism, or accepting the paradox as the price of the knowledge argument?

6. Finally, whether the physicalist is genuinely better placed than the epiphenomenalist once the paradox is stated with care. Physicalism can certainly explain the causal genesis of the reports; what, if anything, does it still owe by way of explaining why those reports concern phenomenal character rather than merely accompanying it?
