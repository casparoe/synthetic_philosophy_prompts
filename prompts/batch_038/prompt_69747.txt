Next month I am giving a talk at a joint workshop in social choice theory and philosophy of economics. The room is roughly two-thirds mathematical social choice people — several of whom work on opinion pooling and know the characterization results for linear and geometric pooling functions cold — and one-third philosophers of economics, plus at least one committed prioritarian and one Bayesian statistician who will care about dynamic coherence considerably more than about welfare. Nobody in the room is a utilitarian apologist; most think Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is either trivially true or already disposed of.

The thesis I am defending is that both standard readings miss what the theorem says once the common-belief assumption is dropped. The deflationary reading — that the theorem concerns the aggregation of von Neumann–Morgenstern utility indices over lotteries and therefore licenses no claim about well-being or interpersonal comparison — and the classical reading — that it derives utilitarianism from Pareto plus expected-utility rationality — both treat the assumption that all individuals share beliefs as a harmless simplification. I argue it is not harmless. Consider the result that when the social observer is required to respect unanimity in beliefs as well as in utilities, her beliefs come out as a linear pool and her utilities as a positive linear combination, with the *same* weights doing both jobs. My claim is that this is not a technical curiosity but the theorem's real content: the two pooling problems are a single problem, and the weight vector is pinned down without any premise about the interpersonal comparability of well-being. The deliverable, then, is not "the sum of well-being" but "one set of weights, two uses" — and the long argument about what the numbers measure has been aimed at a question the theorem never answers, while the question it does answer, why there is one weight vector rather than two, has been passed over as bookkeeping.

Two objections I already expect and do not want rehearsed back to me: someone saying my point is the double-duty-weights result with a new label attached, and someone pressing the fact that linear pooling does not commute with conditionalization, so my "identity of the two pooling problems" is an artifact of a static setup. I want the rest to be ones I have not thought of.

Please prepare me for the question period, in these sections and in this order:

1. **The thesis under pressure** (about 150 words): restate my claim in the form an opponent would attack it, naming explicitly which premise each part of it rests on — the unanimity requirement in both dimensions, the representation of the social observer by a single probability measure, the static framework.
2. **The room** (about 200 words): what each of the four groups I described actually cares about and what would count as a satisfying answer for them, plus any group I have overlooked that this thesis is likely to provoke.
3. **The objections, ranked** (about 2000 words): eight objections ordered by likelihood times damage. For each, give who raises it and phrase it in that person's own idiom — a social choice theorist will not put the comparability worry the way a philosopher of economics will; a one-line statement of what the objection targets; a two-minute reply; and a note on where that reply is soft, meaning what I would have to concede or quietly assume to keep it standing.
4. **Concede up front** (about 200 words): what I should grant in the opening five minutes so that it is not extracted from me later under pressure.
5. **The load-bearing reply** (about 150 words): the single answer that has to land for the talk to survive, and the tightest way to say it in under a minute.
6. **The question I hope nobody asks** (about 250 words): the objection that would be hardest to answer honestly, why it is the hardest, and what the least evasive response to it would be.
7. **Two sentences for the close** (about 60 words).

Do not give me a bibliography or a list of references; I will supply those myself. Do keep the technical vocabulary the room actually uses — I would rather have the objections phrased as they will really be phrased than softened for my comfort.
