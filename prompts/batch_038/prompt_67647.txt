I sit on the National Commemoration Commission of a state that declared independence three years ago. In eleven months we must settle, by decree, both the date and the object of our national day; the commission meets in three weeks to agree a recommendation, and I intend to circulate a written opinion to my colleagues beforehand. Please draft that opinion.

The relevant facts are internal to research we commissioned from the state archive. Since independence we have observed 14 March as Founding Day, on the understanding that on that date a First Assembly of delegates proclaimed our independence. The archive has now established three things. No assembly met on 14 March; the date surfaces for the first time in a nationalist weekly published twenty-two years later. The body that weekly describes did convene, eleven years after the date we celebrate, and what it issued was a petition for home rule addressed to the imperial governor rather than a declaration of independence; that assembly excluded the pastoralist peoples of the northern districts — roughly a seventh of our present population — and its opening resolution transferred their grazing lands to settlers. Finally, the editor's surviving papers suggest he composed the account of the First Assembly from an oral tale in order to win a circulation contest against a rival paper.

Three options are on the table. First, retain 14 March and continue to commemorate the First Assembly as described. Second, move the national day to the documented date of the actual assembly, which the accuracy-minded commissioners favour on the ground that a commemoration we know to be false cannot be defended. Third, the proposal of our youngest commissioner: retain 14 March but change what is commemorated, making it the anniversary of the weekly's appearance — a day marking not the founding event but the moment our people began telling one another that they were one people.

The senior historian on the commission resists the third option on a general ground. He holds that a commemoration performs its function only while participants bracket its constructedness, and that an avowedly self-reflexive holiday is a contradiction in terms: either it succeeds, in which case people have ceased to notice that it is self-reflexive, or it remains noticed as such and fails.

Your memo should recommend one option, or a variant you construct, and state concretely what we should do between now and the anniversary. Beyond that, I want it to do the following work. Explain whether, and how, the recentness and constructedness of our national day bears on its legitimacy and on its efficacy, treating these as separable questions. Distinguish an invented tradition in Hobsbawm's strict sense — rule-governed, invariant, inculcating values by repetition, and claiming continuity with a suitable historic past — from mere custom or convention, and say which of the three options yields which. Address whether a community imagined in Anderson's sense is thereby fabricated or false, and what turns on that distinction in this decision. Say whether the senior historian's objection is sound; if you think it fails, describe a form of commemoration that would survive it, and if you think it succeeds, say so plainly and show what it costs the third option. Take account of the fact that public money has already been spent on school materials, postage, and currency bearing 14 March, and that our neighbour observes the same date as a day of mourning. Finally, since some thirty further disputed dates and symbols are queued behind this one, give the commission a criterion it can apply to the next case without reopening this one.

Engage the strongest consideration on the side you do not recommend in terms its own proponents would recognise, and state what would have to be true for you to change your recommendation. Please do not supply citations, page references, or a reading list; the value here is in the reasoning, not the apparatus.
