You're a philosopher of physics who has actually worked through the Bell inequality derivations rather than just reading the popular glosses, and I need you to referee an argument I watched and mostly could not follow. Think carefully. Maybe start with a few lines mapping what's genuinely at stake before you start ruling, then go move by move.

Two grad students in my department got into it in the coffee room. Neither is a crank. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Omar: Bell's theorem settles it. Any theory that reproduces the quantum predictions for entangled pairs has to be nonlocal — something going on at one detector has to depend on what happens at the other one, spacelike separated. That's what the theorem says, which is why he treats the slogan that local realism is dead as a theorem rather than an opinion.

(2) Greta: That's sloppy. The derivation needs two separate ingredients: a local-causality style factorization of the joint probabilities, and the assumption that the hidden stuff λ is statistically independent of the settings the experimenters happen to choose. Drop either one and no inequality follows. So the theorem proves a disjunction, not nonlocality. Omar is picking the disjunct he personally dislikes and then announcing the rest of the disjunction as the conclusion.

(3) Omar: Fine, look at the second disjunct then. Denying independence means the settings of my detectors were already correlated with the state of the source — a conspiracy arranged in advance. That isn't an escape, it's a way of making experiment uninformative across the board, since any result can be blamed on a pre-existing correlation. And the Everett option is no better: it doesn't deliver a single outcome per run at all, so it cant even state the correlations Bell was talking about. That's changing the subject, not answering him.

(4) Greta: Two separate replies. On Everett: the theory is deterministic and its dynamics are local — the Hamiltonian is built out of local terms and nothing propagates outside the light cone — and Bell's factorization condition is a condition on probabilities for single outcomes, which is exactly what Everett doesn't have. Declining an assumption isn't changing the subject. On the conspiracy charge: independence isn't sacred. Retrocausal approaches keep locality in the strict sense, influences staying inside light cones and merely travelling in both time directions, and they pay for it with independence rather than with spacelike action at a distance. That looks like the cheaper trade.

(5) Omar: Retrocausality is nonlocality with the serial numbers filed off. If an influence from my later measurement setting reaches back and changes what the source emitted, then what the source emitted depends on a choice made later and far away — which is precisely the dependence she said she was avoiding. Worse, the past now depends on what I decide now, so I should be able to signal to myself. If she can't say why that fails, she has a slogan, not a theory.

(6) Greta: She can. The future boundary conditions aren't controllable in the right way — all I can do is set the apparatus setting, and once you condition on everything you actually know, the retrocausal picture still respects no-signalling, exactly like standard QM does. Which is her last point, and Omar never answered it: ordinary quantum mechanics already has both features at once. Spacelike-separated observables commute and the marginal statistics at one wing are provably unaffected by what's done at the other, so there's no superluminal signalling; yet the joint statistics violate a Bell inequality. If signalling and locality were the same thing, that combination would be impossible. So Omar has been using "nonlocal" to mean Bell-nonlocal and then cashing it out as if it meant usable-for-messaging, and those are two different claims.

(7) Omar: And that's the equivocation running the other way. If the only defect in the world is that joint probabilities fail to factorize while every marginal stays perfectly tame, then the word nonlocality has no content left — it's a label for a feature of a probability table. But in a collapse picture what happens is that the state of a distant system changes when I measure here, which is a real physical change at a distance, not a bookkeeping remark. She cant have both: either the failure to factorize is a physical fact about the world, in which case it's nonlocality in a sense worth the word, or it's only about our descriptions, in which case Bell's theorem isn't about the world at all.
