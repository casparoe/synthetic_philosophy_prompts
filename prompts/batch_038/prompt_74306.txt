I write literary fiction and I've been stuck eight months in a book I can feel the floor moving under. Premise: a near-future ministry whose whole job is adjudicating between population-scale futures — how many people there should be, living how well — and the point is that nobody in the building is a villain. They have an instrument, basically a very patient spreadsheet, and it keeps returning the same shape of answer: more people, slightly worse lives, more people, slightly worse lives, and nobody ever signs anything. I know that's Parfit's terrain and that I'm borrowing it, and I don't want to write a book a philosophy-literate reader can put down in one paragraph.

My old reading group has asked me to come and talk. Ninety minutes, whiteboard, wine, fourteen people who are smart and read a lot and have zero philosophy training. One of them read a summary of the repugnant conclusion years ago and has decided it's a cheap trick with big numbers. Another is certain the fix is obvious — average instead of total, what's the problem, why is anyone still writing about this. I want to spend the first twenty minutes, before I read them a word of the manuscript, making the question real to them, so that when they meet the ministry on the page they argue with it rather than laugh at it.

So don't explain the conclusion to me, I've read the explanations. Plan how I should explain it.

What I actually want: the order the pieces have to go in, and what each one depends on — I keep trying to start from the tradeoff and it dies, and I suspect I'm missing something that has to be in place before a comparison between two worlds is even a question rather than a gotcha. Then the one scenario or picture that should carry all twenty minutes, what it needs to have and what it must not have, and why that one survives a room that's already half-convinced it's being hustled. Then the misreadings, in the order they'll actually come up, each one placed at the point in the sequence where I should head it off rather than the point where I'd naturally notice it. Then a single question I can ask at the end that separates people who got it from people being polite to a friend. And what I should flatly refuse to explain at this level, plus how to say that out loud without sounding like I'm dodging. No reading list, I don't want to send anyone home with homework, that's not what this night is.

Two things specific to me, which is where I most need you.

One: the seam that decides whether the book works is the difference between a life that is barely worth living and a life that is miserable. If the room hears the second, then the instrument in my novel reads as cartoon cruelty and everything collapses. Tell me where in the sequence that seam has to close and what closes it — a stipulation won't do it, I've tried, they just nod and go on hearing miserable.

Two: in my book the bad outcome isn't chosen, it accretes, out of a thousand small non-decisions, which is not how the argument is normally set up. Should the twenty minutes teach the clean static version and let the novel do the drifting afterwards, or does getting the drift in early buy me something worth what it costs? Give me a recommendation, not both columns.

And be straight with me about how sure you are. Tell me which single step in your ordering you're least confident of and what would make it wrong. Also flag anywhere you've quietly taken a side in something philosophers still fight about, or smoothed over a reading that's contested — I'll otherwise build three hundred pages on your hedge and never know it was one.
