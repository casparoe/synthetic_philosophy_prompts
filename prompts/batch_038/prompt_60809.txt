I am a junior and I run the logic club at my school. This term we worked through Aumann's agreement theorem: two agents who share a common prior, and whose posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, cannot differ in those posteriors. Half the club found this genuinely thrilling — disagreement revealed as a kind of failure of reason rather than of goodwill. The other half insisted it only bites because of its assumptions, and that ordinary human disagreements survive untouched, either because our priors were never shared or because nobody really has common knowledge of anybody's beliefs.

I would like to settle the argument with a story instead of another proof. Please write a short story, roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, in which two people make a sincere attempt to obey the theorem and find that they cannot.

Some constraints, so that the mathematics is doing the work rather than being worn as costume:

- Give the two characters a common prior that exists inside the story's own terms — the same teacher, the same founding document, the same years of shared observation — and let the reader feel that this common ground is a real resource that they are drawing down as they go.
- The failure should not come from their priors differing after all. Have it come from the common-knowledge condition instead. What I have in mind is an exchange conducted through some channel that cannot certify its own delivery: letters carried by a courier who occasionally loses them, messages passed through a wall, a ledger that either of them might have misread. Each character learns the other's announced posterior, and learns that the other learned it, and so on — but the chain of "and knows that he knows" stops after a few links, and each of them can count exactly how many links he has and can see no way to build the next one.
- Include at least one scene where a posterior is stated aloud and the other character updates on the statement in view of the reader, so that someone with a piece of paper could check the arithmetic. Keep the numbers small and concrete.
- Do not rescue the story with a twist that turns the failure into cheating: no secret liar, no forged letter, no discovery that they were never really talking about the same event. If they end up disagreeing, they must disagree for the honest reason, with clean hands.
- Let one of the two hold the theorem as a moral rule rather than a technical result — the conviction that persistent disagreement is evidence of bad faith, and therefore a personal indictment. The human cost of that conviction is the part of the story I most want to see.

Setting and character are yours to choose. I would lean toward something low-technology, since I suspect finite mutual knowledge is easiest to see when a reply takes four days to arrive and can never be certain to have arrived at all. Please resist explaining the theorem in dialogue as a lecture; if it is working, a reader who has never met it should still be able to feel where the two of them get stuck.
