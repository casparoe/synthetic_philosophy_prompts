Our grad reading group in formal epistemology has started doing little adversarial collaborations, and I got volunteered to draft the one between two people who have been going at it since October. The camps, roughly: side A is a hardline accuracy-firster — the only fundamental epistemic value is the accuracy of your credences, every other norm (conditionalization, the probability axioms, maybe even the principle of indifference) has to be earned from scoring-rule style dominance arguments, and a rational doxastic state is a single sharp probability function. Side B defends imprecise or "mushy" credences — when your evidence is silent or genuinely ambiguous, the respectful response is a set of probability functions rather than a point, the standard complaints about mushiness (dilation, indecision, the worry that you'll turn down a small bet you'd accept under every precisification) are either misdescribed or are actually indictments of forced precision, and demanding one exact number in those cases is fake rigor. Neither of them is a skeptic or a permissivist for permissiveness's sake; both think accuracy is the currency, they just disagree about what has a determinate accuracy.

The thing I want the document to actually dig into, because it's where their argument got interesting and neither has thought it through, is a move B keeps making from forecasting practice. B says: operational forecasters routinely report and are scored on interval-valued and quantile-valued predictions, and there are proper scoring rules for quantile forecasts, so non-point-valued doxastic states can be scored for accuracy in a way that isn't rigged — meaning A can't dismiss the mushy view as simply unscoreable in principle. A's reply is that this is a category mistake: a quantile forecast is still one precise probabilistic object (a single predictive distribution) reported lossily, and having a proper rule for its quantile functional isn't the same as having an accuracy measure for a *set* of candidate distributions. I want the joint statement to face this head on, including the follow-up question they both dodge: if a set-valued credal state only gets an accuracy once you pick some way of aggregating over the members of the set (best case, average, sup over precisifications, whatever), does that aggregation rule silently re-import exactly the sharpness the view was supposed to avoid, or is it a genuinely new normative ingredient that both sides should have been arguing about all along? Feel free to have them agree that the forecasting analogy is weaker than B wants while still agreeing it's not nothing — I suspect that's actually where they'd land, but make them earn it.

So: write the joint statement both could sign. Structure it as (1) a numbered list of agreed claims — I want at least ten, and they have to be non-vacuous, no "both sides agree accuracy matters" filler; they should include things about what a scoring rule has to be to underwrite a dominance argument, about what dilation does and doesn't show, and about which specific cases the two of them now agree are or aren't legitimate mushy-credence cases; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the disagreement in neutral wording, how side A would state it in terms A accepts, how side B would state it in terms B accepts, and whether the row is substantive or merely verbal — and if you think any row is verbal, say so and explain what's actually shared underneath it; (3) three or four cruxes, where a crux means a sub-question whose answer would move at least one of them, and for each: the concrete case, example, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, each side's prediction written down in advance, and what each side commits to conceding if the prediction comes out the other way. Please dont referee the dispute — I'm not looking for a verdict on who's right, and I dont want a survey of the debate either. No bibliography, we'll fill in references ourselves, so refer to positions and arguments rather than to specific papers.
