For four years I have kept a record of the moral judgments of a single informant — a woman I shall call H — who was for thirty years the matron of a residential home in a small town, and who is still consulted, informally but persistently, by half the families there whenever someone's conduct becomes a question. She has no philosophical training and no interest in acquiring any. What makes her worth recording is her consistency: put two structurally similar cases to her a year apart and she will place them on the same shelf without hesitation, and she will defend the placement in a sentence or two. I now have ten entries I trust, one pending case, and a problem.

The ledger, in my paraphrase except where her wording seemed load-bearing:

1. A healthy man of thirty gave a kidney to a stranger, on the condition that nobody in the town be told. H placed it "at the top of the scale." She added once that the concealment was part of why; whether she meant that the concealment added to the deed or merely testified to the man, I could not get out of her. Other compatible donors existed and did nothing; she mentioned this without drawing any conclusion from it.
2. A woman who for thirty years gave a tenth of a modest salary to charity, unobtrusively. H: decent, expected, no medal — "that is what a person owes."
3. A nurse who stayed on the ward through an outbreak, at real risk to herself. H: she had taken the post; not a gift but a debt; admirable, and owed.
4. A boy of twelve who went through ice after a dog and was pulled out half-drowned. H: top of the scale, though he was the only person who saw it happen. I pressed her on that — he was the only one there — and she said, "You cannot ask that of a child."
5. A neighbour who drove an elderly woman to dialysis twice a week. For the first four years H called it "what neighbours do." After the patient's son publicly accused the neighbour of stealing from the house, and after the neighbour's own husband became bedridden, the driving continued; H then said that from that point on nobody could ask it of her, and that the earlier years and the later ones were not the same thing.
6. A prosperous man who anonymously settled a stranger's hospital bill. Four others on the same list were in greater need; he chose the one whose photograph had been in the local paper. H: "A lovely gesture. Not the top of the scale." She did not say he had done anything wrong.
7. A landlord who forgave six months of arrears he could lawfully have collected, from a tenant he disliked. H: handsome; beyond the lease.
8. A driver who let another out in heavy traffic; a man who held a lift. H: manners — "neither owed nor to his credit."
9. A soldier who threw himself on a grenade. H refused the top of the scale: "He was the nearest man and they all knew it. He had to." I recorded my objection — the nearest man is still free to run — and she was unmoved by it.
10. A man who declined to help his brother move house because a match was on the television. H: "He did nothing wrong. But I should think the less of him, and so would his brother."

The pending case, which the family will put to her next Thursday and which I shall be present to hear. A retired commercial diver in his late fifties was the only person on that stretch of shore that morning with the training and the kit. A boy was taken out by a current. From the beach the diver could see that a throwing line and a ring buoy, both within twenty paces, would very likely have brought the boy in; the sea state made entering the water himself seriously dangerous. He entered the water anyway, got the boy out, and in doing so destroyed his shoulder permanently, ending the diving work his pension depends on. Afterwards he told the local paper, and then a television crew, that he went in rather than throw the line because the boy is the son of the developer who had him struck off a contract list eleven years earlier, and that he wanted the father to be seen owing him the boy's life, on camera, in front of the town. He said this cheerfully and said it twice. The boy is unharmed. The father has not been able to look anyone in the town in the face since.

What I need from you:

(a) Reconstruct as many genuinely distinct rules as H could be running that generate all ten verdicts as recorded. For each rule, name the entries that support it and the entry or entries that sit worst with it. Do not assume she is coherent: if no single rule can produce two of the entries, tell me which pair, and what minimal revision to one of them would reconcile the two.

(b) Say what each rule yields for the diver. I expect at least three different verdicts, and I want to see the divergence laid out rather than averaged away.

(c) Give me your best single guess at what she will say, and tell me how much weight you are putting on the two features for which the ledger supplies no precedent at all: the stated spite, and the refused cheaper rescue.

(d) Identify the closest analog among the ten entries and state precisely where the analogy breaks.

(e) Propose one further case I could put to her — short enough to describe in a sentence, and one she would answer without hedging — that would discriminate between the two rules you take most seriously.

(f) If her scheme does not have the shape my question assumes — if, for her, "beyond what anyone could ask" and "to his credit" are two separate axes rather than two ends of a single scale — say so, and tell me what she would say about the diver instead. I would rather have that than a forced answer.

Work only from the ledger as I have given it. I am not asking for references, for a survey of positions, or for what any named philosopher would make of her; I want the reconstruction and the prediction.
