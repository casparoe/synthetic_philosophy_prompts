**Question 3 (25 points).**
(a) State Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against the possibility of moral responsibility, and explain why Strawson takes its force to be independent of whether determinism is true.
(b) Consider Derk Pereboom's four-case manipulation argument. For someone who has already accepted the Basic Argument, is Pereboom's argument redundant, or does it do independent work? Justify your answer.
(c) Address the following stipulated variant, which is my construction and not drawn from the literature. An adult agent, V, has a character that is partly the product of a single undirected chance event occurring after V's birth but before V made any choice capable of influencing it — a neurological accident, say. Everything in V's character after that point is shaped by V's own successive choices, each of which issues from the character V then has. Does the Basic Argument's regress reach V? Does your answer change if V has at some later point reflectively endorsed the traits the accident produced?

**Student's response.**

> The Basic Argument runs as follows. Everything an agent does in a given situation, she does because of the way she is: her character, her desires, her values, her dispositions. So to be responsible for what she does, she must be responsible for the way she is, at least in certain crucial respects. But being responsible for the way she is means having brought about her own nature, and that requires a prior choice made by an agent who already had a nature in virtue of which she made it — which starts a regress. The regress terminates only in self-creation, in an agent who is the cause of herself, and nothing can be the cause of itself. Therefore no one is truly morally responsible.
>
> The point Strawson stresses is that the argument does not need determinism. If determinism is true, every action is the outcome of a nature the agent did not create, and the regress bites. If determinism is false, that does not help either: an action that is not determined by the way the agent is is to that extent random, and randomness is no better a source of responsibility than a nature one did not choose. The argument is therefore not an incompatibilist argument in the usual sense — it does not say that determinism precludes responsibility, but that responsibility is precluded either way. It also targets a demanding notion, true moral responsibility, meaning responsibility in the basic-desert sense in which an agent can deserve praise or blame just in virtue of what she did; Strawson is willing to grant that we may go on holding people responsible in ordinary, forward-looking ways.
>
> This is what makes Kane the interesting libertarian. He accepts that responsibility requires ultimacy — that the agent must be an ultimate source of her own will — and tries to satisfy the demand rather than reject it, by locating responsibility in self-forming actions in which the agent's will is genuinely undetermined but is still the agent's own doing. Whether that succeeds is another matter, but it is at least an attempt to meet Strawson's requirement head on instead of evading it.
>
> Turning to Pereboom: the four cases are a sequence in which an agent's action is produced by progressively less invasive forms of manipulation, ending in Case 4, where the agent is an ordinary person in a deterministic universe. The purpose of the sequence is to show that whatever intuition we have that the manipulated agents are not responsible should carry over to the ordinary deterministic agent, because there is no relevant difference between the cases. The argument is thus aimed at establishing incompatibilism: determinism rules out moral responsibility. Given that, Pereboom's cases are redundant for anyone who accepts the Basic Argument, since the Basic Argument already yields a stronger conclusion without relying on intuitions about science-fiction scenarios. And because the Basic Argument applies to every agent regardless of her causal history, it cannot distinguish Case 1 from Case 4 in the first place; the cases are at best illustrations of a single point. So the two arguments are not doing different work. It also follows that the dispute between compatibilists and incompatibilists is not really a dispute about premises, since both sides accept that responsibility requires the agent to be the source of her action; what they disagree about is whether ordinary agency can meet that requirement.
>
> On the variant: the regress does reach V. V's character is not self-created, and the accident merely fixes the starting point from which her later choices flow; those choices issue from a character she did not make, so she is not responsible for them either. Endorsement does not change this. If V endorses the traits, the endorsement is itself an act produced by the character it endorses, and so cannot ground responsibility for that character — it merely pushes the regress back one step. V is therefore not truly responsible, exactly like everyone else. The variant is not a special difficulty for the argument; if anything it shows why Strawson does not need determinism, since V's character was produced by chance rather than by prior causes.
