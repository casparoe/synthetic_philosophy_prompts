My AP psych teacher brought up the digger wasp story — the one Hofstadter made up the word "sphexishness" for and Dennett kept using. The wasp stings a cricket, drags it to her burrow, goes in to check the place is clear, comes back out, drags it in. If you move the cricket a few inches while she's inside, she comes out, hauls it back to the entrance, goes in to check again, and she'll do that all day, never once noticing that something keeps moving her cricket. Half my class was like "yeah obviously that's not free, it's a machine," and the other half said we might be the same way and not be able to see it. I think what they're mixing up is flexibility and freedom, and I want a case that forces them apart.

So: build me two ordinary human cases. Case one — someone who is every bit as sphexish as the wasp (same trigger, same routine, literally can't do otherwise in the moment, and it's clearly self-defeating) but who still comes out as free and responsible for what happens. Case two — someone as un-sphexish as you can get, forever surveying their options and revising the plan, who is NOT free. Constraints: no wasps, no hypnosis, no brain chips or sci-fi programming, realistic everyday settings, one paragraph each, and the rigidity in case one should stay invisible until somebody interferes in a way that would never happen on its own, like the moved cricket. Don't stack the deck in the telling — no words like "trapped" or "choosing" inside the stories, the verdicts have to come out of the descriptions, not be baked into them.

It's for my school's philosophy club, so after the cases do the check: name exactly what ability the wasp is missing, say whether case one has it or not, tell me which feature of each case is doing the work and what breaks if you delete it. Then give me the strongest libertarian attack on case one — basically why they'd say I havn't gotten rid of the sphexishness, I've just pushed it up a level where nobody can see it.
