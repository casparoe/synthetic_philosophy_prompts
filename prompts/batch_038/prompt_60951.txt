I have spent about twelve years in drug safety — first as a medical monitor onPhase III programmes, now running post-marketing surveillance for a mid-sized manufacturer — and my working vocabulary is entirely that of pharmacovigilance: adverse events as distinguished from adverse drug reactions, the seriousness criteria, expectedness judged against the reference safety information, background incidence in an unexposed comparator arm, attributable risk, causality assessment with its temporality and dechallenge logic, signal detection, risk minimisation plans. I have no training in philosophy and only a hobbyist's acquaintance with machine learning. A colleague on our digital side sent me a stack of AI-safety writing about "low-impact" agents and "impact measures," which I have been working through on flights, and two things strike me: the literature borrows the word side effect from my field while apparently meaning something quite different by it, and its authors seem alternately to be rediscovering problems my field worked through decades ago and to be missing problems my field has never had to face. I would like you to translate four of their central claims into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage, and then tell me what did not survive the trip.

The claims, stated in their own idiom, are these. (1) An agent's changes to the world divide into those instrumental to the task it was given and those that are not; the latter are side effects and should carry a penalty of their own, separate from any penalty for failing at the task. (2) The magnitude of such an effect can be measured without knowing anything about what is ultimately valuable, by asking how far the change reduces the agent's ability to bring about a wide variety of further outcomes later on — I believe the technical versions trade on reachability and on preserving the agent's capacity to achieve auxiliary goals, but please render them in my terms rather than theirs. (3) The penalty must be computed relative to a baseline: how the world would have unfolded had the agent not intervened, or had it simply continued whatever it was already doing. (4) Where it is unclear which changes are bad, the correct posture is conservatism — prefer the action with the smaller footprint, even at some cost to the task, up to and including taking no action at all.

For each, give me the closest thing in my own framework: the concept, criterion, or standard practice it corresponds to, named the way I and my colleagues would name it. Then say whether the correspondence is faithful, whether it holds only once the claim has been bent out of shape, or whether my framework has nowhere to put it at all — and in the second and third cases, say precisely what was bent or what is absent. Please do not smuggle in assumptions from drug safety and present them as the translation; if a claim has no counterpart here, that is the result I want, not a licence to manufacture one from the safety literature.

Answer in exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no preamble, no closing remarks, no summary table. Items 1 through 4 address claims (1) through (4) in order, at two or three sentences each. Item 5 answers the question I actually care about: does the residue you found suggest that drug safety possesses something this debate lacks — a fixed beneficiary, a specified indication, an observed rather than a counterfactual control arm, an external adjudicator of what counts as serious — or does it suggest that their founding question, whether "avoid side effects" can be defined without a full specification of value, was never well posed, because the medical notion they borrowed was never value-neutral in the first place? Two or three sentences, and take a side.
