I am putting together a graduate seminar session on decision problems with imperfect recall, and I would like a purpose-built variant of the absent-minded driver problem rather than the standard highway case. Please construct one to the following specification.

What the case is for. The stock example is usually read as showing that an agent who cannot tell his decision points apart may be tempted to abandon a plan that was optimal when he adopted it. I suspect that reading conflates two distinct ingredients: (i) the ex ante best plan — go on at the first intersection, exit at the second — may simply be unimplementable for an agent who cannot distinguish the nodes; and (ii) the agent's interim belief about which node he is at may itself depend on the plan he is evaluating, so that interim reasoning has no stable verdict at all. I want a case in which both hold, but in which the usual symptom is instability rather than a persistent felt temptation to deviate. That is the point of the exercise: to see whether the trouble comes from unimplementability or from plan-dependent self-location, since in this case the two come apart.

Specifications for the scenario.
- Mundane and realistic: a workplace, a laboratory, a commercial kitchen, a shift rota, a household. No roads, no vehicles, no intersections, no mazes, no amnesia-inducing substances, no science-fictional memory manipulation. The agent's inability to tell two successive decision points apart must follow from an ordinary feature of the situation — two identical steps performed in sequence with nothing available to mark which is which.
- Exactly two indistinguishable decision points, each offering the same two acts, which you should call "stop" and "go on", plus a terminal outcome if he goes on past both.
- A payoff table in small integers or simple fractions. Stopping at the first node yields a modest payoff; stopping at the second yields the largest payoff in the table; going on past both yields something strictly worse than stopping at the first.
- The scenario proper in at most 250 words. Everything after it is analysis.

Specifications for the analysis. Do all of the following and show your arithmetic.
1. Compute the planning-time optimum, distinguishing clearly between plans the agent can actually implement given the indistinguishability of the nodes and plans he could implement if he could tell them apart. Give the value of each.
2. Parameterize implementable behaviour by a single number — the probability with which he stops at whichever node he finds himself at, applied independently at each node — and derive, as a function of that number, his rational credence at a node that he is at the first node rather than the second. State the principle you are using to obtain that credence, and flag where it is contestable.
3. Using that credence, compute the interim expected value of stopping and of going on as functions of the same parameter, and locate the threshold at which the interim recommendation flips.
4. Show that neither pure behaviour is interim-consistent — at the credence each implies, the interim reasoning recommends the other — and that the only interim-consistent behaviour is the mixed one at the threshold, where the agent is exactly indifferent. Say whether that fixed point is stable, and describe what happens to an agent who drifts off it in either direction.
5. Put three numbers side by side: the value of the ex ante best (unimplementable) plan, the value of the best implementable behaviour, and the value of the interim-consistent behaviour. I expect them to come out strictly ordered; verify this for your payoffs rather than assuming it, and say so plainly if it fails.

Then the honest part.
6. Identify the single feature of your construction doing the work — the one without which the instability vanishes — and demonstrate it by removal. Give the minimal repair to the scenario (a tally mark, a distinguishing cue, one changed payoff) and recompute enough of the numbers to show that planning-time and interim reasoning then agree.
7. Present how a defender of interim reasoning would attack the case. At least one line of attack should challenge the principle you used in step 2 to assign the credence; at least one should deny that an agent at a node is choosing a behaviour for the whole information set rather than a single act at a single node. For each, say whether it succeeds against your construction or only against the diagnosis I drew from it.
8. Tell me whether the case can be presented to an audience that has taken no side in the disputes about self-locating belief, or whether its force depends on settling one of them first.
9. List every modelling choice you made that my specifications did not force, and say which of the requested verdicts depend on those choices. If any specification above is unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only by stipulating the verdicts rather than deriving them, say so and propose the nearest construction that works.

A case that reaches the requested verdicts by fiat rather than by derivation counts as a failure: I should be able to check each verdict against the payoff table you gave me.
