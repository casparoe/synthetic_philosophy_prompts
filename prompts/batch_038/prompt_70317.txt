so — I'm handing in a short position statement for a grad workshop on blame and the people in the room are going to tear it apart, and I want a reality check first. I genuinely don't have a feel for which of these is load-bearing, which is kind of the point of asking. Here's what I actually believe about standing and hypocrisy:

1. Whether a moral claim is true has nothing to do with who's asserting it. Tu quoque is a fallacy and I never want to slide into it.
2. But standing is a seperate thing from correctness. You can be right about the wrong and still not be in a position to blame someone for it — the criticism can be true and the blaming still inappropriate.
3. Blame isn't just a hostile feeling. It's a kind of address: you're making a demand on someone, and it has to be a demand you're also prepared to hold yourself to. So what kills standing is failing to apply your own standard to yourself.
4. That failure doesn't have to be visible. A guy who preaches thrift, secretly maxes out credit cards, and is never found out has lost standing just as fully as one who gets caught on Tuesday. Nobody's beliefs about him shift, nothing in the practice is disturbed, and he still can't blame.
5. When I ask myself what's so bad about hypocritical blame — bad enough to cost you the right to make the criticism — most of my answer is social. It corrodes trust in moral address, it lets people with power weaponise morality, it makes blame look like status management rather than protest. That erosion is a large part of why hypocrisy costs you standing.
6. Standing comes in degrees, and one way to have more of it is to be on the receiving end of the wrong you're criticising. Survivors get an elevated standing outsiders don't, and I don't think that's a pragmatic concession to rhetoric — I think it's the same property, more of it.
7. I refuse the conclusion that nobody can blame anybody. Everyone is a hypocrite about something, and if that always forfeits standing then the whole enterprise is empty, which I take to be obviously the wrong result.

Cases, both real-ish. My aunt drives everywhere, has never once felt bad about it, and lectures me about flying to conferences. My friend does exactly the same amount of driving, hates it, has tried twice to sell the car and failed, and lectures me about the same thing. I think both of them have forfeited standing with me.

Do this as an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, worst first — and don't just tell me everything is a bit tense, rank them. Which apparent conflict isn't a real one once you look closely, and what in my own list dissolves it? For each genuine tension, say which number has to go or has to be weakened, and what it costs me to weaken it there rather than somewhere else. Then tell me the single revision that buys the most consistency, and whether my two case verdicts survive it. No need to point me at the literature, I've read the usual suspects — I just want to know whether this particular set hangs together.
