A friend of mine thinks he's dissolved the whole Boltzmann brain problem and I can't work out whether he's right. Here's his argument, in roughly his order:

1. On any cosmology with an eternal or infinitely long de Sitter phase, random thermal fluctuations in empty space manufacture far more momentary observers with fake memories than planets ever produce real ones.
2. If a hypothesis says that observers who seem to be like me are hugely outnumbered by some other kind of observer, my credence should mostly go to the other kind.
3. So if such a cosmology is true, I'm almost certainly a Boltzmann brain.
4. A Boltzmann brain's memories and perceptions are noise; they carry no information about the world it seems to be observing.
5. So if such a cosmology is true, the observations and the reasoning that led me to accept it are almost certainly meaningless.
6. A hypothesis that would make my evidence for it worthless can't be rationally believed.
7. Therefore no such cosmology is true.

My worry is that steps 3 through 7 never mention cosmology. Swap in "I'm a brain in a vat" or "the universe popped into being five minutes ago complete with fossils and reciepts" and the same mould turns out a proof of the external world. That seems like far too much proving. But I also can't point at the bad step, and if there isn't one then maybe he's right and the whole literature is chasing a problem that evaporates the moment you look at it.

So: where does it break, if it breaks at all? And what does whoever rejects that step owe us in its place — I'm especially curious what's left to say against the fluctuation cosmology, and whether the alternative rule for step 2 is anything more than a way of building in the answer you wanted. For the record I'd rather keep 2 and 4 than anything else, they just look like plain epistemology to me, but I'm not attached to it if the price is too high.
