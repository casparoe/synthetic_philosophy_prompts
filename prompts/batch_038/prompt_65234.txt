I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past year I have been reporting a piece on the question underneath most AI coverage: whether a system can mean something by its own internal states, or whether anything it "says" is meaningful only in virtue of what we do with it. Reporting it has led me to a philosophical dispute I had assumed was either settled or at least cleanly mapped — the exchange between John Searle, who holds that intentionality is intrinsic to certain biological systems and cannot be manufactured out of symbol manipulation, and Daniel Dennett, who thinks the line between original and derived intentionality does not survive scrutiny. My editor wants the story built around a live, unresolved disagreement. My reporting keeps pointing somewhere less tidy, and what I need from you is a diagnosis, not a summary.

Let me set out the dispute as I understand it. On Searle's side, aboutness is not something an outside interpreter confers on a physical state; it belongs intrinsically to certain biological causal processes, which is why syntax in his sense is never sufficient for semantics. His support is the Chinese Room, together with the broader claim that computational description is always relative to an assignment: nothing in the physics of a system fixes that it is running this program rather than that one, or any program at all. On Dennett's side, the counterexample is the two-bitser — a state in a computer's circuitry that one read-head will treat as a zero and another as a one, and that can be reassigned to further roles without anything intrinsic about it changing. If the same physical state can be any number of things depending on the machinery and practices around it, there is no privileged class of states whose meaning is unborrowed, and no bottom level at which derivation stops.

What I cannot resolve is this. Both sides appear to grant the same premise about observer-relativity — that a physical state considered in itself is not intrinsically a zero, a one, a belief, or a word — and then draw opposite conclusions from it. Searle deploys it against the idea that minds compute; Dennett deploys it against the idea that anything has underived content. If the premise is common ground, the real divergence sits elsewhere, and I cannot locate it.

My working hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis: they are not answering the same question. Searle is asking what must be true of a system's physical makeup for its states to be about anything, while Dennett is asking what makes an attribution of belief correct and whether there is any fact of the matter independent of the attribution. On that reading the impasse is not a hard shared question resisting argument but two questions occupying one word, with each side's decisive example decisive only inside its own question. A second possibility I want tested is that the disagreement is real but concerns criteria of ontological seriousness — intrinsic causal powers on one side, indispensability to successful prediction on the other — making it a dispute about what counts as a genuine property rather than about minds. A third is that it is largely verbal, turning on real, genuine, and intrinsic, and that the verbal part is what keeps the substantive part alive.

I would like the following, argued rather than asserted:

1. A diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is. Which of the three readings is closest, or is there a fourth? At which step do the positions first come apart — the observer-relativity premise, the treatment of biological systems, or somewhere earlier still?

2. A square handling of the regress. Searle presses the point that if all intentionality is derived from interpreters, the chain never terminates, interpreters being physical systems too; Dennett's answer, as I read it, is that the chain need not terminate in anything mental and that expecting it to is a leftover habit. Is that a reply or a restatement? Does the objection presuppose the distinction it is meant to establish?

3. A test in ordinary reporting language. When an engineer tells me the model represents the board state, would each side call that sentence literally true, and do they disagree about its truth or about whether it is the kind of sentence that can be true? If the second, say what that reveals about the structure of the dispute.

4. Whether either side has staked anything the world could have made false. Is there a conceivable experiment, clinical case, engineering demonstration, or formal result that would count as evidence, or is the dispute built so that nothing could count — and if so, what does that imply about its status?

5. Where my hypothesis overreaches. Two philosophers have told me that journalists flatten this debate by treating it as about machines when it was never primarily about machines. If they are right, tell me what it is primarily about and whether my framing survives.

6. What would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, a formal result, an empirical finding — and whether there is any reason to expect it, or reason to think the impasse is the permanent and correct condition of the question. I want a plain answer here, because it decides whether my story is about an open problem or about a confusion long mistaken for one.

That last point feeds the one practical question I have to settle: if your diagnosis holds, is the question of whether a machine understands what it says a genuine question with a determinate answer, two questions wearing one disguise, or a question whose apparent depth comes entirely from an ambiguity that can be dissolved on the page? I am not seeking permission to write whichever answer suits me; I need to know which will bear weight.

Write in your own words and reason from the arguments themselves. I am not after quotations or a bibliography, and I would have no way to check either.
