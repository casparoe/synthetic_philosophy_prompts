I spent most of last night arguing with a friend of mine — a postdoc in cosmology, and a co-author on the paper that got me invited to do this — about whether the simulation argument, taken seriously, deflates the case for caring enormously about the far future. Her position is what I've come to call the termination reading: if we accept the third disjunct of Bostrom's trilemma, then our accessible future is bounded by a runtime we neither control nor observe, and extinction risk gets replaced by something worse, because termination leaves no physical signature, does not respond to anything we do inside the simulation, and is correlated with the fortunes of whoever is running us. On that view, the arithmetic that licenses huge present sacrifices for a vast number of future lives has lost its inputs, and the honest move is to redirect effort toward nearer and more robust goods. My position is that the third disjunct does not shrink the moral stake so much as relocate it. Inside a simulation, minds are exactly as real as they ever were, and there is no reason to think the simulated future is small: simulations can be run faster than real time, nested, and multiplied, and the inhabitants of a billion simulations count for as much as the inhabitants of one. What changes is the instrumental picture. The levers available to a nested civilization are not rockets and stellar engineering but whatever makes the simulation worth continuing, so the far-future case becomes a case about being worth keeping around — a worse argument than the one we thought we had, but not a smaller one. The third thing we fought about is the strategy I'd label be interesting: whether a nested civilization should optimize for its simulators' curiosity. She thinks it follows; I think it is both a bad bet, since ancestor simulations are presumably run for fidelity and distorting ourselves to be entertaining is a fast route to deletion, and a repellent one, since it makes the welfare of simulated people hostage to their novelty value. We stopped at two in the morning without resolving it.

The reason this matters is that I am giving the opening remarks at the first retreat of the Ashcombe Centre for Long-Horizon Futures on Thursday, and the room is a genuine problem. It will hold about thirty-five graduate students and postdocs who can recite the trilemma from memory and will notice immediately if a disjunct gets mangled; three of the Centre's principal donors, one of whom opened his remarks at last year's dinner with a sixteen-digit estimate of the number of lives still to come; two deans who are there to be reassured; and a reporter from a general-interest magazine who is, I am fairly confident, looking for one sentence — something in the neighbourhood of we are all probably living in a computer — and nothing else. The remarks run eight or nine minutes, so roughly a thousand words written to be spoken. They have to send the room into the day's work rather than out of the building, and they cannot do it by telling the donors that they were duped or the students that they are playing a video game. Please write them, and please commit: take one of the two theses above, or a sharper version of either, and argue for it in the speech itself, with the reasons in plain English — no surveying the options, no it remains to be seen, no symmetrical on-the-one-hand structure. I would like the trilemma stated correctly in a single sentence a dean can follow, with some explicit acknowledgment of which disjuncts we might be living inside, and I would like the third disjunct kept sharply distinct from the vague claim that nothing is real. No quotations, no citations, no titles of papers: you may name one philosopher if the speech genuinely needs him, and not otherwise, and nothing should be put in anyone's mouth. Fold in the be interesting strategy and give it a verdict rather than a shrug. Include one passage the grad students will recognize as an actual argument rather than an allusion, and one joke that lands with the deans. Do not invoke the Matrix, do not use the phrase simulation hypothesis more than twice, and do not end on uncertainty — end on something the room could act on Monday morning.

After the speech, add a note of about a hundred and fifty words telling me which thesis you committed to and why you think it is the one that survives the argument I described, since I would rather have your reasons than your even-handedness. In the same note, tell me where you simplified in order to make it speakable and what a specialist in the room would object to, and if you think my friend is right about something in there — in particular if you think termination risk really does gut the far-future arithmetic, or if you think be interesting is not the bad bet I claim — say so plainly instead of diplomatically.
