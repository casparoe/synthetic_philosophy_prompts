Bit of an unusual ask, but you're exactly who I want to run this past. I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — mostly psychiatry, neuroscience, public health — and this spring I'm giving one of the plenaries at a meeting whose audience is almost entirely people who run clinical trials and help write treatment guidelines. Psychiatric researchers, some epidemiologists, a handful of journal edtiors. Basically nobody who's touched metaphysics since an undergrad requirement. Slot is 45 minutes with about 15 of questions at the end.

The talk argues that the labels that end up in my headlines — depression, ADHD, autism, sepsis, burnout — aren't natural kinds in any robust sense. They're more like pragmatic clusters: categories held together by loosely correlated symptoms, by the questionnaire that happens to measure them, and by the institutional machinery (billing codes, trial inclusion criteria, school accommodations) that has a stake in keeping the boundary where it is. And a lot of science journalism, mine very much included, writes as though the category were a fixed target that each new study gets slightly closer to hitting, which is how we end up discovering the depression gene or the real cause of autism every eighteen months.

Here's roughly my argument, so you've got something to aim the objections at:

1. Real doesn't have to mean sharp. A category can pick out something genuinely there in the world while being held together by many overlapping mechanisms rather than one underlying essence — more like a weed than like gold. The problem isn't that these diagnoses are fake, it's that they behave nothing like the paradigms journalists borrow their language from.
2. These particular categories are self-modifying. Once the label exists and spreads, people recognise themselves in it, clinicians start looking for it, insurers pay for it, so the measured thing drifts toward the measurement. Prevalence can double in thirty years without much changing in the world.
3. Which means a sentence like "the drug works for depression" smuggles in an assumption the trial never tested: that there's a stable target called depression for the drug to work on. The accurate version of that sentence is longer, hedgier, and its completely unusable as a headline.
4. The fix I'm pitching is not "it's all socially constructed, none of it is real" — that's the misreading I most want to shut down in the room — it's changing what we count as the news. The interesting story is usually the category moving, not the finding landing inside it.

What I need is the Q&A, not a defense of the talk. Give me seven or eight objections this specific room will actually raise, in the order they'd realistically come, phrased the way they'd phrase them — they'll talk about construct validity, biomarkers, endpoint definitions, effect sizes, replication, and patient harm, not about kinds or essences. For each one: how much damage it does if I fumble it, the answer I could deliver from a podium in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where that answer is thinnest. Then tell me which two or three I should just concede up front and gracefully, and which single objection has to be handled well or the whole talk collapses.

And separately — name the question I'm praying nobody asks. So give me the worst version of it as a trialist who reads my column would put it, and how I get through the answer without either grovelling or getting defensive.
