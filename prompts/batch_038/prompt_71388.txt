the question we set, week on responsibility-sensitive egalitarianism:

"Every resident of a city receives an identical grant at 21, out of which they may buy insurance against involuntary job loss from any of a set of competing insurers. Anyone who buys no coverage, or coverage too thin to matter, receives nothing from the city if they are later laid off; the city runs no unemployment benefit of its own. (a) Analyse this scheme with the help of the distinction between brute luck and option luck. (b) Does the harshness objection to luck egalitarianism get a grip on it? (c) Is there a case, internal to luck egalitarianism, for making the insurance compulsory?"

the student's answer:

1. Dworkin sorts misfortunes into two kinds. Option luck covers how deliberate and calculated risks turn out, where the person could have declined the gamble; brute luck covers how things fall out that aren't gambles at all. The city's scheme is Dworkin's auction with the insurance device bolted on: everyone starts from identical purchasing power, and whatever inequality ends up in people's hands is generated by the bets they did or didn't place against their own futures. At first sight this looks like a cleaner implementation of equality of resources than any real welfare state manages, since actual states redistribute without ever asking whether the claimant's need was the upshot of a choice.

2. On (a): the resident who spends her grant, buys no coverage and is then laid off has had bad option luck — she was offered protection at a known price and refused it. The resident who bought coverage and is laid off is not receiving charity but the payout on a premium she elected to pay. The point goes deeper than that, because the insurance mechanism is what converts brute luck into option luck. A plant closure is brute bad luck considered as an event, but once insurance against it is on offer, the loss that lands on the person who declined is a loss she accepted. So the scheme doesn't merely tolerate inequality, it produces inequalities that are traceable to choice all the way down, which is what a responsibility-sensitive egalitarian is supposed to want.

3. On (b): the harshness objection is the charge that luck egalitarianism is willing to let people bear the whole cost of their own imprudence and calls that justice. Anderson puts it as a complaint about the spirit of the view — the state here administers compassion to citizens who are unluckily deficient rather than standing in relations of equality with them — and Fleurbaey's motorcyclist, who rides without a helmet and is then left to his injuries on the grounds that he is responsible for them, is the standard illustration. But both formulations are aimed at people who are badly off through no fault of their own, and the cases that do the work in the literature are the congenitally disabled and the born-poor. Our claimant was offered protection and turned it down, so there is nothing harsh about the result: she has what she chose, which is the entire point. To insist that the city should pay her anyway is to insist that people be protected against risks they knowingly declined, and that is paternalism rather than equality. So no, the objection doesn't get a grip here, or at least not with the force it has elsewhere.

4. On (c): there is a case for compulsion, and it comes from inside the view. Wolff's dilemma is that a claimant under a responsibility-tracking system has to choose between disclosing demeaning facts about her own conduct and character and going without. Dworkin's scheme is not exempt from this, because in order to pay out the city must establish that the claimant was covered, that she really was laid off involuntarily, and that she declined nothing she ought to have taken up. Since that intrusive enquiry cannot be avoided, the humane move is to remove its object: make the insurance compulsory, so that nobody ever has to establish fault or imprudence in order to be paid. Equality of resources survives and the shameful revelation disappears.

5. Anderson's alternative is usually described as a change of currency: instead of resources or welfare, the metric becomes what people need in order to function as equal citizens, and the state's task is to secure that rather than to cancel out luck. On that reading democratic equality is a luck egalitarian view that has swapped its currency and dropped the compensation-for-deficiency vocabulary, and it should endorse the city's scheme provided the insurance were compulsory and the payouts sufficient for people to keep functioning as citizens. The disagreement with Dworkin would then be about the metric, not about whether responsibility ought to shape distribution.
