I'm a novelist, and each autumn I run a two-hour workshop for twelve writers on what a story feels like from inside a head that doesn't know what it knows. This year I want the session built around a decision problem — the one about the absent-minded driver — which I'll state in full, so that nothing beyond it is needed.

A driver is heading home along a road with two exits in succession. Turning off at the first pays 0; turning off at the second, his own street, pays 4; driving past both to the end of the road pays 1. He is absent-minded: when he arrives at an exit he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, nor whether he has already passed one. Before setting out he can adopt a policy — continue at any exit with probability q — and the best policy in advance is q = 2/3. Yet once he is at an exit, reasoning only from the fact that he is at an exit, the arithmetic appears to tell him to turn off, though turning off at every exit he ever came to would leave him with nothing.

Design the workshop around this: something the twelve of us physically play over many rounds, with a payoff scheme that needs no real money, in which we commit to policies, are then put in the seat with nothing more informative than "you are at an exit," and run into the collision ourselves before anyone names it. I need the materials; the exact mechanism for reproducing his ignorance at the exit and for settling what he is entitled to believe once he is there; a running order for the two hours; and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the trouble, including — for novelists — what a narrator can and cannot do with a character in that seat. Then tell me what will predictably break the first time I run it and how to recover, and be plain about which feature of the problem no tabletop version can produce and I will have to argue for in prose.

Give me one design, chosen and defended: your decisions and your reasons for them, not a menu of variants with the picking left to me.
