**Draft: base rates for the fall of empires, or, an honest outside view**

When somebody asks how long the current dominant power will last, basically every answer you get is an inside-view answer. You get a story: fiscal strain, demographic inversion, elite overproduction, imperial overstretch, currency debasement, a sclerotic bureaucracy. The problem is a story is always available, because every polity at every moment has some strain you can narrate, and the strain in the cases that survived looks identical from the inside to the strain in the cases that didn't. What's missing is the base rate.

The standard fix is reference class forecasting: pick your class of comparable cases before you look at the outcome, see what happened to them, and let that distribution anchor the estimate. Works fine for tunnel-boring projects and airport terminals. For empires it runs straight into a problem that I think most people who attempt this just step over. The class isn't specifiable in advance. "Empire," "great power," "hegemon" — you assemble the list, but the list is assembled by people who already know which ones fell and when, and the membership criteria quietly drift across eras so that the interesting cases get in and the awkward ones don't. Then you compute a lifespan distribution over that list and it looks quantitative but the class was built with the answer in view.

So my proposal is to fix membership with a functional test written down in advance and applied mechanically to a fixed grid of polity-decades. Roughly, a polity is in the class at time t if (a) it directly extracts revenue from a contiguous core territory above some threshold, (b) it faces no peer competitor inside its reachable horizon, where reachable is fixed by the transport and communication technology of the period rather than by hindsight, and (c) it could beat any two of its neighbours in combination. Then estimate a hazard: for polities that have satisfied the test for d decades, what fraction stop satisfying it, or stop existing, in the next decade?

Four things I want to say on top of that.

1. The censoring objection is weaker than it looks. People say you can't do survival analysis here because several class members are still alive, so you don't know their eventual durations. But I don't need eventual durations, I need the hazard at each tenure length, and a living member contributes exactly what a hazard estimate consumes: person-years at risk. Provided censoring is roughly non-informative, you can estimate the hazard at each duration from whoever is still in the risk set at that duration, and the living members just drop out of the calculation once they're censored.

2. Some exits should be excluded from the numerator. I want the base rate for falling of its own weight, so I'd strike cases where the polity was terminated by something that entered from outside the competitive system entirely — a shock that no member of the class could have anticipated or defended against, the paradigm case being a hemisphere-scale pathogen-and-invasion combination. Those aren't senescence events, they're interruptions, and pooling them in makes the estimate answer a different question than the one I asked.

3. The class is very small at any given moment, sometimes one member. So I'd pool across eras and regions. That only works if the underlying process is roughly the same in each, which I'd defend on the grounds that the test in (b) already normalises for technology: it asks about relative position within a reachable horizon, not about absolute scale.

4. I need an operational definition of the terminal event, or the whole thing is mush. I'd use: permanent loss of the capacity to tax the core territory. Dynasty turnover doesn't count, moving the capital doesn't count, losing a periphery doesn't count.

The numbers I'd eventually plug in are placeholders, so don't bother telling me what you think the actual median tenure is, I haven't run anything yet.
