I have no academic background in philosophy — I have never taken a course in it, and I do not work in a university — but for the last few years I have been reading on my own about probability, forecasting, and how a person is supposed to change their mind. The practical motive is that I keep a running record of probability judgments about matters in my own life, and I want to know what I am supposed to do with them when new information comes in. I have written down the positions I have actually arrived at. Before I rely on them any further, I would like a careful audit of whether they hold together. I am not after an introduction to Bayesian epistemology, which I have read; I want to know which of these commitments pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and what the cheapest repair is in each case. Please do not send me to books or articles. I want the reasoning itself.

Here is the list. I have lettered it so you can refer back to it.

(A) Degrees of belief should be numbers between zero and one, and the heart of epistemic rationality is keeping them coherent — no combination of bets offered to me that guarantees I lose, that kind of thing.

(B) Most of what I take in from experience is not something I could state with certainty. If I glance at a shirt under bad lighting, I do not come away believing that the shirt is blue. I come away with my confidence redistributed across several colour hypotheses, none of which has become certain. So evidence can itself be uncertain.

(C) When that happens, the correct update is the one Jeffrey described: keep fixed how each hypothesis bears on each member of the partition — the conditional probabilities — and change only the probabilities assigned to the members of the partition themselves.

(D) Where I end up should depend on the whole body of experience I have had, not on the sequence in which its pieces arrived. Two people who have had the same glances, the same instrument readings, and the same reports, but in a different order, should finish in the same place. If they do not, then something other than my evidence is doing the work.

(E) The input to an update should be something I can identify afterwards. If my confidence changed, I should be able to say what it was that I took in, and that content should be what fixes the new probabilities on the partition. I do not want a rule whose input is a bare shifting of numbers with nothing behind it.

(F) There are changes in confidence that involve taking in no new fact at all, and they strike me as plainly rational. I already knew, to the limit of my education, everything worth knowing about the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion. Then I read an account of how general relativity handles it, and my confidence in general relativity rose a great deal. Nothing about Mercury became more certain and I learned no new astronomical fact.

(G) Over the long haul, where you started should not matter much. Given enough evidence, people with different but reasonable priors converge toward one another, which is why I am not especially worried about my priors being arbitrary.

(H) On empirical questions I should never sit at exactly one or exactly zero. There are things I am flatly certain of — simple arithmetic, for instance — and I do not think any further experience could rationally move me off those, but that certainty does not extend to matters of fact about the world.

Please go through this list and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be, ordered by severity. For each tension, I want to know why the two commitments could sit side by side in my head for years without my noticing, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I have deliberately not said which of these I would be most willing to give up, because I suspect I do not know, and I would rather you not be steered by a hint. If several of the commitments share an unstated assumption that is really doing the work, name it.

Please give the whole audit as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `commitment_labels`: an array of eight objects, one per letter, each with `id` (the letter), `one_line` (a compressed restatement of what I am committed to), and `standard_name` (the name the view or principle goes by in the literature, or `null` if it has none).
- `tensions`: an array of objects, sorted by `severity` descending, one for each pair of commitments that has any friction at all. Do not list pairs that are simply independent. Each object has:
  - `pair`: array of two ids.
  - `kind`: either `"genuine"` or `"apparent"`.
  - `severity`: an integer from 1 to 5, where 5 means the two cannot both be retained under any reading and one must be revised, and 1 means the friction is stylistic rather than substantive.
  - `the_clash`: what exactly follows from one that the other rules out. Be concrete about the mechanism, not just the label.
  - `why_it_stayed_hidden`: why a person holding both would not have noticed the conflict.
  - `cheapest_fix`: the minimal revision that removes the clash, naming which commitment gets revised and how.
  - `cost_of_fix`: what the revision gives up.
  - `dissolution` (only for pairs marked `"apparent"`): the distinction that makes the friction disappear.
- `ranking_note`: a short explanation of the ordering, and in particular why the top item outranks the second rather than merely tying with it.
- `single_best_revision`: an object with `revise` (the id of the commitment to change), `to` (what it becomes), `resolves` (array of the pairs from `tensions` that this one change clears), and `leaves_open` (array of the pairs it does not).
- `hidden_assumption`: any premise shared by several of these commitments that I never stated, that is doing real work, and that I should decide about explicitly.
