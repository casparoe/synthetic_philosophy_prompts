I listened to a chapter of one of those pop cultural-evolution audiobooks on a long drive and the memetics section went roughly like this:

"Dawkins's real insight was that the gene isn't special — it's just the replicator that happens to be made of DNA. A meme is anything in culture that gets copied from mind to mind: a jingle, a proverb, a technique for tying a particular knot, a cut of jacket. And because the copying is high-fidelity, memes stay recognisably the same across generations, which is what allows them to accumulate. Once you have something that varies, gets passed on, and differs in how many copies it leaves behind, natural selection follows automatically — the algorithm doesn't care what its made of. So the Darwinian theory of culture is just the selfish-gene argument moved upstairs: memes are selected for how well they replicate, not for how good they are for you, and a meme's interests can come apart from its host's exactly the way a gene's interests come apart from the organism's. That's the principled basis for treating religion as a kind of parasite of human minds. And the standard objection — that culture is far too messy, blended, and reinterpreted on every transmission for anything like copying to be going on — just misses the point. Blending is a special case, and selection works fine on it."

He was clearly competent and it all sounded very tight, but I came away uneasy and I cant locate why. Two things I already half-believe, so tell me if I'm wrong: I suspect that "the Darwinian theory of culture" and "memetics" aren't the same claim at all, and that there are people doing cultural evolution who would reject nearly every sentence above; and I suspect the gene/meme interest-conflict analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, since a gene gets copied by way of the organism reproducing and a meme doesn't obviously have any equivalent bottleneck forcing it to hitchhike on the host's success.

Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a serious defender of memetics would sign off on, what's a fair simplification, and what's an actual misrepresentation — including where the passage is unfaithful to Dawkins himself versus to what the field later built on top of him. I especially want to know whether that last move is legitimate: is it true that if natural selection doesn't require discrete replicators then the messiness objection collapses? If not, what does the objection have to be instead, and what's it actually committed to — no replicators, or no Darwinian process, or something about being able to count? Same question for the substrate-neutrality line: is that a real argument or a slogan, and what would it take to turn it into one? And where the passage is wrong, do its two conclusions (religion-as-parasite, meme-vs-host conflict of interest) still survive, since they might follow from a weaker version of the view that he didn't need to overclaim for. No need to point me at sources, I can't look anything up right now — I just want to know how much of it to trust and where.
