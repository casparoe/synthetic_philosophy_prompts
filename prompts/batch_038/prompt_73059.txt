I'm a staff engineer on a small evaluation team inside an applied ML org, and I've ended up holding a philosophical position I can't properly defend, so I'd like you to attack it as hard as you can. Background: over the last year my team has become the gatekeeper for internal capability claims — statements of the form "this model can do X," where X ranges from multi-step tool use to some arithmetic skill that appears only above a certain scale. After a few painful episodes (including one where a widely repeated claim about a sharp, scale-triggered capability largely dissolved once someone swapped a discontinuous pass/fail score for a continuous one, which made the "emergence" look like an artifact of the metric rather than a fact about the model), I taught myself some philosophy of science from the demarcation tradition and drafted a criterion that we now informally use to decide which claims are worth running experiments on. I have no formal philosophy training — I picked this up from Popper and from summaries of the Duhem–Quine and Lakatos material — so please treat my formulation as amateur and correct it where I've garbled something.

Here is the position, which I'll call P. (i) A claim belongs to the space of empirically decidable disputes only if its advocates have specified, in advance of the test, some class of possible observations they are committed to treating as refuting it; a claim with no such class is not thereby meaningless — I reject the verificationist reading of Popper that I keep encountering — but it is outside that space, and can at best serve as a heuristic or a source of conjectures. (ii) Demarcation is a matter of degree, and the degree is set by three properties of the pre-committed refuters: how specific they are, how cheaply they can be obtained, and whether the community in question would in fact accept them if obtained. (iii) A retreat from a refuted claim is legitimate only if the amendment independently yields content that the original claim did not already carry; otherwise it is an ad hoc rescue and the whole bundle loses standing rather than gaining a reprieve. Two colleagues have already pushed on this. One, a data scientist, says that no statistical claim is ever strictly refuted by finite data, so clause (i) either excludes essentially everything we do or is so easily satisfied by picking a p-value threshold that it decides nothing. The other, a former journalist, says clause (ii) is a piece of amateur sociology dressed as logic: whether people would accept an observation is a fact about institutions and incentives, not about the claim, so P either collapses into a description of how my team happens to behave or becomes unstateable. And a third objection, which I made against myself in the shower and have not been able to shake, is that P is reflexively self-undermining: nobody could specify observations that would refute P itself, so P fails its own test.

Your task: assume the role of a philosopher of science who works on demarcation and on the methodology of statistical inference, and map P's exposure. Think carefully and begin with an explicit brainstorming pass — a raw list of candidate defeaters, before you have organized or ranked them — and only then produce the structured assessment. In that assessment I want you to separate objections that would show P false or incoherent from objections that would show it merely uninformative, and both of those from objections that are awkward but survivable; rank them from fatal to cosmetic. Specifically, work through: whether holism about testing (the fact that an experiment always probes a bundle of the target claim plus auxiliary assumptions about the harness, the scoring function, the sampling, and the data provenance) defeats clause (i) or only relocates the problem, and what a falsificationist is entitled to say about which conjunct has failed; whether the theory-ladenness and revisability of the observation reports themselves — our eval logs, our labels, our human-judge ratings — is a genuine threat or a red herring; whether statistical hypotheses can be refuted at all in the strict sense, and if not, what P should say instead without becoming a Bayesian or an error-statistical account in disguise (and if it does collapse into one of those, say which one and where the collapse happens); whether clause (iii) has a non-circular criterion for "content the original did not already carry," since my own clause looks suspiciously like the very move it is trying to police; whether P misclassifies whole domains — it seems to exclude mathematics and logic from the decidable space, to be oddly lenient toward some falsifiable-but-junk claim styles such as highly risky conspiracy theorizing, and to have nothing to say about fields like the historical sciences where the refuters are expensive and unrepeatable; and whether clause (ii)'s third property, about what a community would actually accept, is gameable in a way that lets a motivated team write refuters it has no intention of honoring. I'd also like you to say what P risks: what does it predict or forbid that a purely Bayesian or a purely social-epistemic rival permits, and is there any case in which P and those rivals give visibly different verdicts on a real methodological dispute? Finally, give me an honest answer to the reflexivity objection — is it a fair defeater or a cheap trick, and does the answer depend on whether P is meant as a normative rule of practice rather than a descriptive claim about the world? For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of P (that's me) should concede it as counting against the view or is entitled to dismiss it, and where you propose a repair, say whether the repair is ad hoc by P's own lights.

Format and constraints.
