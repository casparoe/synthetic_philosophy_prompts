Sunday reading group, three of us slogging through I Am a Strange Loop with bad coffee, and I'm the one with the CS background so I keep tripping over the level talk. Last week my friend Priya built an argument she thinks rescues the symbol level, and I'm pretty sure its a trap, but I can't locate where it goes wrong. First the setup as I understand it — tell me if I've mangled it. The brain is a careenium full of simmballs; each simmball is the neural stand-in for one symbol; simmballs careen around and collide; the collisions are arranged so that a bump from the grandmother simmball onto some other simmball mirrors an inferential connection between the symbols involved; and what gives a simmball its content is its hookup, the causal tethers running out to patterns in the world. That's the picture on which symbols push each other around, and the pushing, seen from the symbol level, is what thinking is.

Priya's argument:

(1) What any given simmball does — which simmballs it bumps, which ones bump it, when — is fixed entirely by the careenium: the wiring, the thresholds, the current physical state.

(2) What the simmball stands for makes no difference to any of that. Re-tag the very same careenium so that the simmball which realized grandmother now realizes uncle, and not one collision changes.

(3) If content made a causal difference, re-tagging would have to change which collisions occur, and then the careenium would not be physically closed.

(4) So symbols never push anything. All the pushing is balls pushing balls, and the symbol level is a way of narrating that pushing, not a level at which anything occured.

(5) The same move works at every level above the bottom: temperature doesn't bend the bimetal strip, pressure doesn't burst the tyre, the meaning of a stop sign doesn't make you brake. In each case the parts did all the work and the high-level story is narration.

(6) But (5) is false. Thermostats work, and saying the mean kinetic energy of the molecules did it isn't a rival account of why the strip bent — it's the same account at a finer grain.

(7) Therefore (1) through (3) are false: symbols really do push the careenium around, and downward causation is unproblematic.

She wants (7), and so do I — I'd much rather keep the symbol level real than buy (4), and I'd also rather not give up physical closure, which is why (3) scares me. My unease is that (7) seems to purchase far more than she needs, and that the trip from (6) to (7) is doing something sneaky; but I honestly can't tell whether the real damage is late, at that last step, or early, in the (2)-(3) pair, and I have a vague feeling she's using closure to mean two different things in two different places, though maybe I'm wrong about that. So: name the step you'd reject and explain why it looked fine on first reading — (2) and (3) feel airtight to me and I want to know what makes them seductive. Then price the alternatives for me. If I reject (2) instead, am I signing up for the view that content isn't a free tag but is fixed by causal history and hookup, and what does that cost me — swampman-style cases, translation indeterminacy, the whole worry that nothing pins content down uniquely? If I reject (3), am I just saying closure is false, and can that be softened into something a physicalist can hold? Also, she says (4) self-destructs, since if no symbol ever pushed anything then the meanings of her own premises never caused her to write them; I suspect that's a cheap shot and a red herring, but tell me if it actually has force.

Two extras. First, does your diagnosis generalize to my home turf: a compiler where a variable called balance seems to push bits around while the voltages obviously do all the work — or does the fact that simmballs are tethered to a world break that analogy? Second, and this matters to me, please separate what's settled here from what's genuinely live among people who work on this. I know non-reductive physicalists have answers to exclusion arguments and I also know those answers are disputed, and from the outside I can't tell whether that's a real stalemate or whether one side has quietly lost. Mark explicitly which of your claims about the state of the debate are consensus and which are you picking a side, and flag the step in Priya's argument where you're least confident in your own reading of it — I'd rather get one clear verdict with a stated soft spot than six options weighed evenly against each other.
