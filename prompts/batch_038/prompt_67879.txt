I write novels, and I've been living inside Dennett's multiple drafts model for a year now for my new book, so what I want isn't a summary, it's someone willing to have the argument with me. Essay, British English please. The question: does the multiple drafts model actually abolish the Cartesian theatre, or does it just relocate it? My worry is that once you've got a swarm of parallel editorial processes all revising content at once, you still owe an account of why at any given moment there's a fact about which version the character would report if asked — and fixing content like that looks suspiciously like a theatre sneaking back in under another name. The obvious comeback is that reportability does all the work and no moment is privileged; take that seriously rather than knocking it over, and deal with the Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque stuff about how after-the-fact revisions are handled, plus whether the temporal-ordering illusions (colour phi and its cousins) genuinely favour one side or are compatible with both. Then commit. I'd much rather you were wrong in an interesting direction than balanced.

And here's the bit I actually need settled. One scene in the book gets printed twice, in two mutually incompatible drafts, and I never tell the reader which one the narrator goes on to report afterwards. Argue whether that's a faithful rendering of the theory or a con, on the grounds that the reader becomes the single place where the content gets fixed — the theatre I claimed to have demolished, rebuilt one level up.
