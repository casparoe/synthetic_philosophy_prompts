so I'm in a phil of mind seminar this term and we've spent three weeks on pain, and every discussion collapses into the same place: someone says pain is bad because it hurts, someone else says that's circular, and then we burn fourty minutes relitigating it. I want to break the loop with a sorting instrument, and honestly mostly for my own benefit, so I can work out which camp I'm actually in rather than which one I like the sound of.

Can you write me a decision-tree quiz, say 9 to 11 questions, that takes someone from "I accept that pain asymbolia is a real syndrome — people who report feeling a pain but don't wince, don't withdraw, decline an offered painkiller, sometimes grin or laugh when the site is prodded" all the way to a named position on what makes a pain bad for the person having it?

Rules I care about:

- every question is a concrete case with two or three answer options. no question is allowed to be a doctrine in disguise — "do you think unpleasantness is built into the feel of the sensation?" is banned. the sorting has to happen by what people say about cases.
- question stems should be followable by an undergrad who has never read anything on this. all the technical vocabulary goes in the key at the end, not in the questions.
- the tree has to keep two questions apart, because our seminar runs them together constantly: what makes an experience unpleasant, and what makes a pain bad for the person who has it. some positions answer both, some answer only one. the key should say which for every leaf.
- I want at least these families somewhere in the space, though split or merge them as you think is right: the view that unpleasantness is an intrinsic feature of the feel itself; the view that it's the experience's evaluation of some bodily state as bad; the view that it's a command-like content telling you to stop; the view that it just is the subject's concurrent desire for the sensation to cease; the view that what's missing in asymbolia isn't aversion as such but any sense of the body as being in jeopardy; the view that pain isn't bad in virtue of how it feels at all but in virtue of what it does to your attention and your projects; the two-tier view that separates pain from suffering and makes only the second bad; and the deflationary view that there's no fact of the matter here to discover. if some combination of answers maps onto a position nobody actually holds, keep the leaf in and say why it's empty — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
- flag the pairs of leaves your questions genuinely can't separate. I already suspect a couple (the evaluative-content view and the command-content view both seem to predict exactly the asymbolia pattern), but tell me which separations you think are hopeless and why, rather than papering over them.

Also, one case that's mine and not from the readings, and I want the tree built to handle it, because it's the one that split our group in half. Call her Patient S. After surgery she says the pain is just as awful as it was before, rates it 9 out of 10 when asked how unpleasant it is, and describes it in vivid detail — but she declines analgesia every time it's offered, doesn't guard the limb, laughs when the wound is prodded, and shows no surprise at her own reactions. Asked how she expects to feel about the same pain next week, she calls it unbearable. So either her introspective reports are accurate and only the aversion machinery downstream is broken, or the reports are a verbal habit she's kept and there's nothing unpleasant in there at all. I want at least two questions that force a choice between those readings, and I want the key to show that people who disagree about S land in different leaves even when they agree about every ordinary asymbolia case.

If you're not sure a view has a standard name, just call it unnamed rather than guessing — same for who defends it. I don't need attributions or a bibliography, I've already got the reading list.
