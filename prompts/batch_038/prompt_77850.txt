So the classic dust-specks case: torture one person horribly for fifty years, or give each member of some astronomically large but finite crowd a barely-noticeable speck of dust in the eye. A lot of people's gut says the speck side wins, or at least that no finite number of specks ever adds up to the torture.

Here's a variant I want an essay on. Instead of spreading the specks across the crowd, put them all on one person: she gets a speck in the eye once a minute until the running total exactly matches the crowd version (yes, she lives a very long time). Same number of specks, one bearer. Does the right verdict change once the aggregation is intrapersonal instead of spread out over that many seperate lives — and what does the answer tell you about where the anti-aggregation intuition actually comes from? Was it ever really about the tininess of each individual harm, or about the separateness of persons?

Take both sides seriously: lexical thresholds, the sorites-style worry that refusing to aggregate makes "better than" intransitive, the fact that at any given moment she only has one speck in her eye so no snapshot of her life ever looks that bad, and whether the badness of a life is just the sum of the badness of its moments. But actually land somewhere — no fence-sitting, though a conditional verdict counts as a verdict.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question back at me.
