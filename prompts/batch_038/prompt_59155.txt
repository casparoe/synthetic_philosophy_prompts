I'm in the second year of an M.A. program, drafting a thesis proposal on the free will debate, and over the last several months I have become preoccupied with a dialectical pattern that turns up constantly in that literature and that nobody seems to have a word for. Here it is, described as concretely as I can manage. An incompatibilist argues that determinism rules out free will, typically by tracing an action back through the laws of nature and the remote past to factors the agent never had any say about. A critic then objects that the principle doing the work is not specific to determinism at all: run the same reasoning against the libertarian alternative and it destroys that too, since on indeterministic causation the crucial step is a matter of chance, and an agent's reasons and character are no more genuinely up to her in that setting than they were in the deterministic one. What I expect at this point, from every other argument I have ever worked through, is revision: the incompatibilist narrows the principle, or explains why the extension to rival accounts fails, or concedes the objection and retreats to the weaker conclusion. What I keep finding instead is the reply that this is precisely the point — since neither determinism nor its denial delivers the control we want, we should conclude that free will is impossible outright, and turn our attention to whether blame and punishment can survive that. The objection that the argument proves too much has been absorbed and converted into an independent reason to accept something strictly stronger than the claim originally defended: a conditional thesis (no free will if determinism is true) has become a global one (no free will, whatever the metaphysics turns out to be), and the attempted refutation now counts in its favor.

The same shape recurs in the manipulation cases. A compatibilist is presented with an agent who has been covertly designed or manipulated so as to act as she does, and is asked why that agent is free when the deterministic agent is not; the expected resolution is a concession that manipulation, so described, does not undermine freedom after all. But a very common resolution runs the other way: the absence of any principled difference is taken to establish that the ordinary deterministic case is exactly as bad as the manipulation case, so it is the compatibilist position that collapses. And I think I have seen the move outside this debate entirely, in cases where a skeptical argument is charged with overgeneralizing so far that it would also defeat the standard its objector favors, and the skeptic responds by accepting the overgeneralization and concluding that no standard is available at all.

Three questions, and I care about the carving far more than about the label. First, does this move have an established name anywhere — in the free will literature itself, or in informal logic and argumentation theory, where I would expect such things to be catalogued? I have a shortlist of candidates that all feel adjacent and none of which I think is it: biting the bullet; grasping or embracing the horns of a dilemma, though as I understand it that phrase is supposed to mean denying one of the conditionals, which is close to the opposite of what these authors do; the tu quoque reply, which normally uses a shared defect to neutralize an objection rather than to deepen a conclusion; reading an opponent's modus tollens as a modus ponens; and the name for the resulting position, hard incompatibilism, which labels a view rather than the argumentative route to it. I would like to know, for each of these, exactly why it does or does not cover the case, and what the nearest thing in the argumentation literature is. I keep circling back to Popper's discussion of immunizing stratagems and self-sealing reasoning, but that concerns protecting a theory against refutation, whereas what I am describing looks like a theory emerging stronger from the refutation attempt, with a broader scope than it started with. Second, is there anything systematically interesting about the move: does it have a recognizable logic, and under what conditions is it legitimate rather than a way of winning by making one's position unassailable? It strikes me that the move depends on the space of alternatives being genuinely exhaustive — either determinism or indeterminism, either manipulated or not — and I would like to know whether that requirement is doing real work or is decoration, given that nobody has established which horn of the metaphysical disjunction is actual. Third, if there is no established term, coin one for me: give me a definition tight enough that I could apply it to cases I have not seen, tell me explicitly what falls outside it (mere bullet-biting; a plain argument from the failure of all the alternatives; an honest reductio that the original arguer simply accepts), and give me one or two boundary cases where you would be genuinely unsure whether to apply the label. I am not looking for a name to drop into a footnote; I want to know whether the thing I have noticed is a real category or an artifact of my reading too much of one corner of the debate.
