You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of philosophy of mind, animal ethics, and decision theory, and who has supervised a good many seminar papers. I am in my final year and enrolled in an advanced seminar on moral uncertainty; the assessed deliverable is a two-page research proposal, to be followed by a paper of roughly six thousand words. Please think carefully, and before you draft anything, spend a short passage brainstorming three or four candidate framings of the question and saying which one you would choose and why — I want to see the selection, not only the result. Then write the proposal proper.

Here is my half-formed idea. Much of the debate about insect sentience runs through what I will call the scale argument: even a low credence that insects are sentient, multiplied by the enormous number of them, yields an expected amount of suffering large enough to dominate our other moral priorities. I am not interested in whether insects are sentient — I could not settle that, and neither could the paper. I am interested in whether the argument's scale factor and its probability term are each well defined, and whether the practical conclusion survives once they are properly specified. Two things worry me.

First, the scale factor is a headcount, and I suspect it should be something else. Call the alternative subject-time: the total duration across which a candidate subject is present, weighted by the intensity of what it is like for it, in the way public health replaces a body count with a measure that folds in years lost and severity. On that measure the ranking of taxa changes. An adult mayfly is around for a matter of hours, whereas the reproductive at the centre of a termite colony persists for years (figures I would obviously have to check). And if a colony rather than an individual is the candidate subject, the unit of counting changes altogether. Relatedly, the class "insects" is doing quiet work: larvae and adults, solitary and eusocial species, arthropods more generally.

Second, the probability term is usually handled as an idiosyncratic uncertainty, a per-individual lottery. But the likelier source of our ignorance is systematic: we do not know which theory of consciousness is right, and whichever turns out to be right will settle the matter for a whole clade at once, so the individuals are not independent. I recognise that expectation is linear and that this may therefore make no difference whatsoever to the expected total. If so, the paper has to locate where the difference really bites — variance, the value of information, whether a single number is even well defined before a theory of consciousness has been chosen — rather than pretending it lives in the arithmetic.

Turn this into a proposal with the following parts: (a) a precise statement of the question in a form that has a determinate answer, plus one clearly subordinate question; (b) why the positions currently on offer leave it open, describing those positions and their arguments rather than naming sources — I will add the references myself, so please include no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations; (c) the approach I would take, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as surveying the empirical literature on insect cognition or defending one theory of consciousness; (d) how the paper would handle the empirical inputs it cannot settle, since I want to treat them as parameters and be explicit about which conclusions are sensitive to which parameter values; (e) what a result would look like, including what would count as the project failing, and whether a negative result — the scale argument turning out to be robust under both changes — is still a worthwhile outcome; and (f) an honest assessment of whether this fits six thousand words and one semester, and what I would have to cut if it does not.

One request about how you present all of this: wherever your framing rests on a claim about the state of the debate, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the topic, and do not dress up a contested view as a consensus. A proposal that flags its own contestable premises is more useful to me than one that conceals them. Define any technical term you introduce, keep the register of a planning document rather than an essay that already defends a conclusion, and hold the proposal itself to two pages — the brainstorming passage may sit above it.
