Context first, because the case only makes sense with it. I freelance on science and ideas — mostly neuroscience, some AI policy — but a few years back I wrote a piece for a small print quarterly about how contested numbers get locked into public memory, and they put me on their editorial advisory board out of it. Five of us, we meet by video, we commission maybe a dozen long reported essays a year. Our written policy on contested history is one line and it's useless: we don't run pieces whose central claim contradicts the settled record. Nobody on the board can tell me what "settled" is doing in that sentence, and it was drafted after a dust-up over a climate story, which is a different animal entirely.

The case. A freelance historian — a real one, does archival work on Ottoman-era population registers, no institute behind her, publishes in ordinary peer-reviewed places — has pitched us 5,000 words on the death toll of the Armenian genocide. To be clear about what she does and doesn't argue: she accepts that the deportations and mass killings happened, that they were state-directed, and that the term genocide applies, and she says outright that anyone disputing that is arguing with reality. What she disputes is the number our own style sheet has used without comment for years, roughly 1.5 million. Her claim is that the defensible figure for 1915–18 sits somewhere in the 800,000–1.2 million band; that 1.5 million is doing double duty by folding in the longer 1914–23 window plus a great deal of famine and epidemic death that wasn't killing as such; and that the number has hardened into a shibboleth because a community that has spent a century fighting for recognition has entirely understandable reasons not to reopen it. She wants to write about that hardening, not just about arithmetic. Two things have made the board unhappy. One of us went back through the pitch and noticed that she demands of the incumbent figure a standard of archival precision that no estimate in that evidentiary class could meet — consular reports, parish registers, survivor counts, all fragmentary — while asking us to take her own reconstruction, built from the same sort of fragments, as firmer than it can be. And her pitch cites approvingly a chapter in a volume put out by a foundation with a long record of funding work that does deny the genocide; she says she cites it for one narrow point about restrictions on access to Ottoman state archives and would happily drop it, and she may well be sincere. Meanwhile two board members have been emailing each other with me copied, one of them used the word "denier" about her, her agent has apparently heard about that, so theres a live grievance on both sides. Spring issue commissioning slots close in nine days and we meet Tuesday.

So: write the memo I'll circulate Monday night. I want an actual decision — commission as pitched, commission with conditions, decline, or some fourth option you think beats those three, in which case say what it is and why the others lose — and then the general test the board could apply next time, phrased so a non-specialist editor could actually use it. That test should grapple with whether denialism is a property of a conclusion or of a way of conducting yourself, whether a claim can be true and still have been arrived at denialistically, and whether we're permitted to treat a downward revision differently from an upward one when both are equally licensed by the evidence — because if we aren't, I can't explain to the board why we ran the climate piece that challenged a consensus view. Deal with uptake too: in a general-interest magazine with no scholarly apparatus, the sentence a reader takes away is "the numbers are disputed," and I want to know whether conditions like an editor's note, a published response from a historian on the other side, or an explicit framing of what is and isn't in question can genuinely fix that or just launder it. Be fair to the strongest case against whatever you recommend, and don't dodge the donor question — one of our funders is Armenian-American, and I dont want the memo pretending that's irrelevant, but I also don't want it to be the deciding factor. Close the memo with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of: commission, commission-with-conditions, decline, reframe-and-commission.
