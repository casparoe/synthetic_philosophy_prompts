The question:

"Many philosophers hold that in at least some choices, one option is neither better nor worse than the other, and yet the two are not equally good either. Explain how this third possibility is supposed to work, giving the central argument for it. Then say what, if anything, can still make a choice between such options rational rather than arbitrary."

The student's answer:

"The phenomenon in question is usually called incommensurability, but the important thing is what it is not. It is not ignorance. If Nadia simply doesn't know enough about what medicine or performance is actually like, then more information would settle the choice, and there would be nothing especially hard about it. Nor is it vagueness in our concepts, the way it's vague whether a man with thinning hair is bald. The claim is about the values themselves: in some cases it is not true that A is better than B, not true that A is worse than B, and not true that they are equally good, and no further information could change any of that.

The standard way to argue for this goes through small improvements. Take Nadia, who has to decide between going to medical school and staying at the conservatory to study cello. Neither option seems better. Now improve one of them slightly: suppose the conservatory can offer her a marginally better practice room, or the medical school bumps her stipend by a hundred dollars a year. That improvement obviously doesn't settle the choice — the bumped-stipend option is not now the one she ought to take. But if the two options had been exactly equally good, then any improvement at all to one of them would have tipped the balance, since equally good options are precisely balanced. So they weren't equally good. And they weren't better or worse either, since neither one dominated. All three of the standard relations fail, and what's left is a fourth one, sometimes called parity: the options are on a par.

The hard part is what this means for choosing. If the options are on a par, then nothing about their values settles which one Nadia picks. The reasons run out. Every consideration in favor of the cello is matched by a consideration in favor of medicine, and more information won't help either, since we can't tell whether small improvements make a difference in cases like this. So the choice can't be justified by reasons at all. What's left is the will: Nadia commits, and in commiting she makes it true that she has a reason to keep playing. In that sense the choice is arbitrary, though not irrational — rationality only asks that you respond to whatever reasons there are, and here there are none on either side. Both options end up equally justified. That's also why hard choices feel so consequential to us, I think. They're the cases where the values can't decide for you, so you find out who you are by what you commit to."
