**Can a value move you without moving you more? Two readings of the Protestant ethic thesis**

*1.* The objections to Weber are usually sorted into two families. The empirical one says the Calvinists weren't measurably more industrious or more frugal than their neighbours, that capital accumulated happily in Catholic merchant cities, and that if anything the arrow runs the other way — commercial change producing the religious change, not the reverse. One economist's summary of the literature is roughly that the thesis's most notable feature is the absence of evidence for it. The conceptual family is more interesting: a belief cannot push, only people can; "culture caused X" is either a way of saying "many people with a certain property each did their thing," in which case it's a claim about individuals and needs no special metaphysics, or it's a claim about a collective agent, in which case it's mythology. Both families, I want to argue, aim at the same target, and the target is not the most defensible version of what Weber was doing.

*2.* Call the standard target Reading A, the differential uptake reading. Doctrine gets in, changes behaviour at the margin — an extra hour of work, a consumption forgone, a sum reinvested rather than spent — and the aggregate of many such margins produces a new economic order. On A the thesis is a quantitative claim about the size of an effect, and the empirical critics are deploying exactly the right kind of evidence. They're also, I think, largely winning: on A the thesis looks bad.

*3.* But there's a second reading, and it's suggested by Weber's own choice of exhibits. He keeps quoting prudential maxims — Franklin's little homilies about time and credit and money begetting money — instead of quoting account books or wage schedules. What he seems to be displaying is a *grammar of justification*, not a ledger of behaviour. Call this Reading B, the permission reading. On B the doctrine didn't make anyone work harder; it changed what the work *meant* to the person doing it. Specifically it changed three things: whether gain had to be justified at all, whether success could be read as evidence rather than as temptation, and whether stopping was available as a live option without loss of standing.

*4.* Here's the illustration I want to build the section around. Two merchants, same city, same appetite for gain, same opportunities, same thirty years of activity, and — this is the point — indistinguishable balance sheets at the end. One of them could have stopped in year twelve, sold up, bought an estate, and been admired for it. The other could not: for him, cessation would have been a sign, and a sign of the worst kind. Nothing observable differs between them. But their lives have different shapes, because one of them is living a pursuit he can exit and the other is living a pursuit that has closed the exits behind him. B says this is what the doctrine added — not force, but a change in the modal profile of a motive. Its strength was untouched; its optionality was destroyed.

*5.* What does B buy, and what does it cost? It buys immunity from the empirical family, since B predicts no difference in savings rates, occupational distributions, or anything else the quantitative critics measure. It also makes sense of why Weber was so interested in the *feeling* of the believer — the inner loneliness of someone who must live as though his fate were already fixed. But the costs are severe, and I'm not sure I've faced them. First: the explanandum was accumulation, and accumulation is a quantity, so B doesn't explain the thing that needed explaining. Second: B looks unfalsifiable, and "the critics' evidence is simply the wrong kind" is a cheap way to win an argument. I try to block that charge with a falsification condition — B is refuted if you can find agents in the relevant settings who pursued gain systematically, experienced it as wholly optional, and had a socially sanctioned exit available — but I suspect I've cheated, because the people who satisfy that description (rentiers, tax farmers, the acquisitive gentry) are exactly the ones Weber refuses to call capitalist, so my condition may just be a definition wearing a lab coat.

*6.* Conclusion sketch. The question "can culture be a cause?" is really the question "can a value change the modal profile of a motive without changing its strength?" If yes, then value-explanations aren't rivals to mechanism-explanations; they're answers to a different question, and a century of debate has been two sides answering questions nobody bothered to seperate. If no, then B collapses into A and Weber is simply wrong.
