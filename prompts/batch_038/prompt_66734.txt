**Flexible Relative to What? A Problem with Sphexishness as an Argument About Free Will**

The story is worth telling carefully, because the version that circulates in philosophy is thinner than the original observations. A female digger wasp of the genus *Sphex* stings a cricket or katydid into paralysis, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, and then — this is the part that matters — leaves it outside while she goes in to inspect the nest. If an experimenter moves the prey a few inches during that inspection, the wasp emerges, locates it again without apparent difficulty, drags it back to the entrance, and then goes inside to inspect once more, dropping the prey at the threshold again. The loop can be repeated many times. There is a companion observation in the same literature: the wasp drags her prey by the antennae, and if the antennae are removed she will not think to take hold of a leg instead, even though she is plainly capable of gripping and hauling.

Philosophers turned this into a term of abuse. Sphexishness names mechanical rigidity: behavior that is impressively competent and yet unable to abandon its own script when the script stops making sense. The implicit argument is short. Freedom requires flexibility; the wasp is inflexible; therefore the wasp is not free. And, running in the other direction, if flexibility is the criterion, then anything sufficiently flexible — including a mechanism — escapes the charge, which is why flexibility-based compatibilism looks like it has an unusually cheap answer to the worry that we are only complicated wasps.

My objection begins with the observation that flexibility is not an intrinsic property. A system is flexible only relative to a set of perturbations, and there is no such thing as being flexible simpliciter. Now compare how the two sides are scored. The wasp is tested against a set of perturbations chosen, deliberately, to defeat her: an experimenter's hand moving prey three inches, an experimenter's scissors removing antennae. We are scored against the perturbations our own cognition was shaped to handle — novel tools, unfamiliar rooms, changed plans — and when we fail against contrived perturbations of our own, the failures are entered in a different column. The Müller-Lyer illusion persists after you have measured the lines and know exactly what is happening to you. People continue to honor sunk costs after the point has been explained to them. I have checked a locked door four times while telling myself I was checking it. The asymmetry is not that we are flexible and the wasp is not; it is that we get to choose which perturbations count, and we choose flattering ones. Call this the perturbation-set asymmetry. If the criterion can be satisfied or defeated by the selection of the test, it cannot be doing the philosophical work it is asked to do.

So I propose that we replace the single criterion with a ladder of four grades. At the bottom is parameter adaptation: habituation, sensitization, tuning a response to the local statistics. Above it is routine re-entry — the capacity to run a conditional branch and to recover an interrupted sub-goal. The wasp has this, and the standard retelling hides it: she does not freeze, and she does not seize a pebble or a leaf in place of the cricket. She searches, finds the correct object, and resumes. The third grade is the one she lacks, and it deserves its own name: constraint demotion, the ability to notice that a sub-rule has stopped serving the purpose it was installed for and to demote it from mandatory to optional. The wasp's failure is not that her repertoire is small. It is that the order of operations — inspect before entering — remains compulsory after its point has been served a dozen times. Sphexishness, properly described, is not inflexibility but the inability to relax a means that has become an end. The fourth grade would be self-directed: taking one's own dispositions as objects of assessment and revision.

If that is what sphexishness is, the term turns out to describe us far too well to mark a line between us and the wasps. Means that harden into ends are the normal condition of human institutional and personal life: procedure preserved after the outcome it was meant to serve has been abandoned, ritual politeness maintained in emergencies, superstitious routines in athletes, and — a case available to anyone writing an essay at midnight — the revision loop that no longer improves the draft. The dilemma, then, is this. Either sphexishness is common enough to be unremarkable, in which case it cannot support an argument that separates free agents from mechanisms, or the argument must be restricted to failures at the fourth grade, in which case it has stopped being an argument about flexibility and become an argument about self-representation — a different thesis, a much harder one, and one that no longer follows from the wasp.

There is a final wrinkle, and I am least sure how to handle it. The empirical basis may be weaker than the philosophy assumes. Repeated inspection of a disturbed nest might be an adaptation against parasites and rivals rather than a cognitive defect; I have read, though I could not relocate the source, that the wasp does adapt after a sufficient number of repetitions; and the anecdote appears to have reached the philosophers through an engineer's retelling rather than through the original field observations, which means it has been transmitted by people with an interest in its moral. My claim is that this does not simply embarrass the example but exposes how it functioned. A term of philosophical abuse survives when nobody re-runs the experiment. Sphexishness worked because it was never tested, and a concept that is insulated from testing in that way is doing rhetorical work rather than analytic work.
