I'm a retired experimental physicist — thirty-four years doing neutron scattering on magnetic materials, and then one day I handed in my badge and discovered I had no idea what to do with my Tuesdays. So I've fallen down the foundations-of-QM rabbit hole, and I'm in a reading group that meets in the back room of the public library: two ex-engineers, a retired schoolteacher, and a guy named Ray who did formal theory for a living but never anything to do with measurement. Ray is the one who brought this argument last week, and he's very pleased with it. I couldn't sleep over it. Maybe you can help. Act as a philosopher of physics who actually works on the Everett, Bohmian and spontaneous-collapse camps and who has thought hard about what probability could mean if everything happens — I want the real expert version, not a pop summary.

Here's Ray's argument, cleaned up a bit from how he said it out loud, with the steps numbered because he insists it's valid:

(1) A theory of the quantum domain counts as physics only if, given a specified preparation, it tells you how probable each possible registered result is — and "probable" has to mean something that could have come out otherwise, otherwise you're not predicting anything, you're just describing a record after the fact.
(2) So a theory on which no registered result is excluded — on which anything that can show up does show up — can't assign probabilities at all. Every record it permits has probability one.
(3) Everett says the universal state evolves unitarily, no collapse, and after a measurement the different outcomes are all there, sitting in different decohered branches that can no longer interfere.
(4) So on Everett nothing gets excluded: every value in the spectrum turns up in some branch, and my branch is no more special than yours.
(5) Therefore Everett can't meet the requirement in (1). Its defenders wave at branch weights — squared amplitudes — and call those probabilities, but the weights aren't forced by the unitary dynamics; they're the identical numbers Copenhagen already wrote down. So the branching talk buys no predictions.
(6) Methodological principle, and this is the load-bearing one: if a theory T' adds structure on top of T while agreeing with T on every probabilistic prediction, then the extra structure is empirically idle, and empirically idle structure isn't physics, it's decoration. My old department would have called this Occam's razor and gone to lunch.
(7) So Everett isn't a rival to Copenhagen, it's a notational variant that takes longer to write down. And the same principle kills Bohm: Bohmian mechanics reproduces ordinary QM exactly whenever the particles start out distributed according to the usual equilibrium recipe, so the trajectories are decoration too.
(8) Conclusion: there's no such thing as choosing an interpretation. The only substantive question is whether you bolt on a genuine stochastic collapse dynamics, which does shift the numbers slightly and so is a real physical claim. Everything else is typography.

Ray says this settles a debate he's found tedious for forty years. I want to say he's wrong, and I want to say it precisely, but I keep sliding between two different complaints and can't tell which one is real. One is that something switches early, in how (3) and (4) describe what Everett is even committed to — like the words "result" and "excluded" don't mean the same thing in (2) as they do in (4). The other is that (6) looks like a principle I've used my whole career without thinking, and if it's allowed to run this freely I have a nasty feeling it would strip a lot of perfectly respectable physics down to bare bookkeeping, including some things I'd die defending. I'd rather keep (1) no matter what, and I'd like to keep something in the neighborhood of (6), because otherwise I don't know how to argue with numerologists.
