Not an academic, just someone who's spent the last twelve years running a small community mediation service up in the north east — neighbour disputes, family rows that have festered for years, that sort of thing. I've read a bit about guilt and shame but most of what I think comes from sitting in rooms with people.

The line I keep hearing is that guilt is the better moral emotion because it's about what you did, so it points towards putting something right, whereas shame is about what you are, so it just makes people hide or hit back. My hunch is that this only looks true when the harm is repairable. Where it isn't — no way to give it back, no apology anyone will accept — guilt seems to run out of road and curdle into self-punishment that does nobody any good, and the people I've watched actually get through that kind of thing look more like they've taken shame on: let themselves be marked down, stopped claiming standing they used to claim.

Could you argue that case for me, properly? So: that shame rather than guilt is the better moral emotion specifically in the irreversible-harm cases; and that which of the two a culture leans on isn't something the culture can choose, it's downstream of how repairable the harms it mostly deals in are. I dont want a balanced on-the-one-hand-on-the-other-hand piece, I want the strongest version of the argument, with the guilt/shame distinction you're working to spelled out at the top so we're not talking past each other. Then tell me where you think it's weakest.
