I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently drafting a long feature about herding in research: how a single high-profile finding can generate a decade of confirmatory work, how failed replications end up in drawers rather than in print, how grant panels and referees converge before the evidence has really converged, and why design reforms such as preregistration and registered reports are best understood as attempts to re-engineer the flow of information rather than to improve anyone's motives. My working title gesture is to frame the piece with Sidney Lumet's 1957 film of Reginald Rose's Twelve Angry Men, which nearly every reader will know and which is almost universally described in the popular literature as a story about conformity and groupthink. That gloss is exactly what worries me. I would like a serious reading of the film through the economics and philosophy of information cascades and rational herding, and I would like to know whether the analogy I want to draw actually holds or whether an honest critic should tell me to drop it.

The question I keep returning to is whether the film depicts herding in the technical sense at all. The cascade models say that a rational agent abandons a genuine private signal once the public history of others' actions becomes decisive enough; the conformity-psychology tradition says something quite different, that people abandon private judgments under social pressure they do not even notice; and there is at least one further possibility, from the formal literature on jury voting, that under a unanimity requirement sincere voting is not an equilibrium at all, because each juror expects someone else to be the one to hold out. Which of these is the film actually dramatizing, and does it commit to one? I have been working with a distinction of my own, between the strong-signal dissenter, who genuinely knows more than the crowd, and the weak-signal dissenter, who has nothing but a doubt and a rule that says doubt is sufficient. Juror 8 is clearly the second, which seems to break the cascade model, since dissent there is supposed to require a signal strong enough to outweigh the public history. Is the beyond-reasonable-doubt standard therefore best read as an anti-cascade device, one that deliberately lowers the evidential threshold at which it becomes rational to speak against a room? And is the same true of the switch from the open preliminary vote to the secret ballot in which Juror 8 abstains, which strikes me as the film's most explicitly institutional moment, a piece of voting design dropped into the middle of a drama?

Please take the specific scenes seriously rather than treating the film as an allegory. I am thinking of the identical switchblade produced from a pawnshop, the timed reconstruction of the downstairs witness's walk to his door using the apartment diagram, Juror 3 lunging at Juror 8 and thereby supplying the defense with its best evidence against literal-minded testimony, Juror 5 sitting on his expertise about how such knives are actually held until the moment it becomes usable, the inference drawn when Juror 4 removes his glasses and rubs the impressions on his nose, Juror 12 sliding back to guilty under the force of Juror 4's argument, the jurors turning away from Juror 10 one by one, Juror 7 wanting to get to the ballgame and Juror 11 asking him whether he believes what he is voting, Juror 3 tearing up the photograph of himself and his son, and the two men exchanging names on their way out. Several of these do not look like belief revision at all, and I want to know which ones the cascade framework can genuinely accommodate and which it cannot. I am especially interested in the point that seems to me most damaging to the standard model: nearly everything that changes minds in that room is not new data but a change in how the same data is interpreted, and the two or three pieces of genuinely new information enter from outside the trial record, which is to say through conduct a court would probably not tolerate. Does the film know that its hero wins by going off-record, and what does that imply for a reader who wants to map the story onto a scientist with a failed replication? Finally, tell me what the film's form contributes: the near-total confinement to one hot room, the fact that we never see the trial except through the jurors' reporting of it, the audience's being placed in the same informational position as a juror watching a public history accumulate. I am not looking for a reading list or any quotations from the film, and I would rather you tell me plainly where the film resists this lens, or where it is really about something else, such as the dignity of an individual against a group, than have you force the fit.
