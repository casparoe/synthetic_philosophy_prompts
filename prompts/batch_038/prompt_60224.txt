A municipal commission in my city has been studying whether long-term resident non-citizens should vote in local elections, and at its public hearing two witnesses — a political theorist and a city lawyer — ended up arguing past each other for about twenty minutes. I sat in on it and took notes; neither of them would concede anything.

(1) The theorist opened that democracy's own claim to legitimacy is that those governed by law have a say in making it; a permanent resident who is taxed, policed, and bound by every regulation but excluded from the franchise is therefore governed without authorization, and the line between citizen and resident is arbitrary from the standpoint of the principle that justifies democracy in the first place.

(2) The lawyer replied that the criterion proves too much: a visiting tourist and a foreign contractor are equally subject to the same criminal law, the same traffic code, and the same penalties, so either they vote too, or being subject to law is not what makes someone a member after all.

(3) The theorist said subjection comes in degrees and durations, and that what matters is being caught up in the polity's coercive order as the standing background of one's life — a fortnight's visit is nothing like twenty years of schooling, employment, housing, and policing.

(4) The lawyer answered that this abandons a criterion for a dial, that any stopping point on a dial is itself arbitrary, and that having objected to the citizenship line as arbitrary the theorist is not entitled to help himself to a vaguer line just because it sits where his intuitions do.

(5) He added a deeper difficulty: the demos cannot vote on who belongs to the demos, because any such vote already presupposes a constituency entitled to cast it, so the boundary problem cannot be solved by the procedure whose scope it fixes.

(6) The theorist agreed no procedure can bootstrap its own boundaries, but said this is an argument against the status quo rather than for it: if the boundary cannot be democratically legitimated, then it is fixed by history, war, and inherited statute alone, which is precisely what makes the exclusion of long-term residents objectionable. He closed with a local asymmetry — a former citizen who emigrated at six and has not returned in forty years may vote in national elections, while someone who has lived, worked, and raised children in the district for twenty years may not — and said no principle distinguishes them.

(7) The lawyer said there is one: membership is a relationship rather than a snapshot, since the emigrant holds a right of return and remains oriented toward the country in a way the resident, who keeps the citizenship and protections of another state, does not.

(8) The theorist replied that a right of return is itself a legal status created by the same polity, so appealing to it to ground the franchise is circular.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than in summary: for each of the eight, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only against a weaker target than its author intended, and why. Then say who was more right overall, and identify at least one consideration that neither of them raised which would have changed the course of the argument.
