I work in organizational development for a mid-sized logistics firm and, somewhat embarrassingly, I have been reading Being and Nothingness on the train for the better part of a year. Last month our leadership offsite settled on a "culture of extreme ownership" programme whose internal language — no excuses, own your outcomes, bring your whole self to work, the org chart is not a defence — struck me as an accidentally exact implementation of Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith, down to the part where the consultants assure everyone that opting out is still a choice. Some friends put together a small print zine about work and philosophy, and I would like to contribute a piece of satire built on that observation rather than on the observation's explanation.

Please write it as a change-management memo of roughly 750 to 850 words, issued by an external consultancy to the People and Freedom function of a company that has decided to abolish excuses altogether. Give it the apparatus these documents actually carry: a header with a distribution list and a version number, numbered clauses under headings along the lines of Purpose, Definitions, Scope of Responsibility, and Implementation, a boxed glossary, and a brief FAQ at the end aimed at line managers. Invent the consultancy and the client; use no real names. The voice must be entirely deadpan and bureaucratic. The memo never winks, never names Sartre or existentialism, and never indicates that it suspects it is absurd; the target is the genre of corporate authenticity-and-accountability discourse, not any actual firm or person. The humour has to come from the philosophy being right rather than from the philosophy being mocked, so the memo should quietly commit itself to Sartre's actual positions: freedom is not a capacity employees hold but a condition they cannot decline, and declining is itself a decision they own; no role, job description, or instruction can transfer responsibility, which retires the phrase "I was executing the approved playbook" as a defence and turns every clause of the playbook into an occasion for anguish rather than fear; the past is inert with respect to the present, so a behavioural commitment recorded at last year's review exercises no binding force on the employee standing in the meeting now — this deserves its own clause and possibly a short vignette; the demand for total sincerity is self-defeating, because a person required to declare what he really is cannot coincide with it, which means the authenticity audit manufactures precisely the bad faith it was commissioned to detect; permanent visibility to colleagues is proposed as a productivity instrument on the grounds that being seen is what supplies an employee with a stable self; and organizational values must not be treated as facts lying in the world, although the memo, in the spirit of seriousness, cannot help treating them that way. Fold in two transposed case studies as well — one concerning an employee in a customer-facing role whose performance of that role is diagnosed as a shade too precise, and one concerning a one-to-one meeting in which a colleague's hand resting on the table is noticed and the conversation is then steered, at some length, toward the quarterly roadmap.

Two constraints on execution. First, no verbatim quotation from Sartre or anyone else: render every idea in the consultancy's own register, and include no footnotes, bibliography, or reference list. Second, internal consistency matters more than any single punchline. The memo should close on a clause making the entire programme a compliance requirement with attestations and escalation paths, and the reader should feel the recoil — that a policy mandating authenticity has become a textbook instance of the thing it prohibits — without the memo noticing and without you explaining it anywhere. A reader who has never encountered Sartre should enjoy it purely as corporate absurdism; a reader who has should be able to point at each clause and name what it is doing.
