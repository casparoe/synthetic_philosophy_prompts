I write literary fiction, and my working vocabulary is entirely one of craft: scene, point of view, free indirect discourse, motive as it can be shown in action, the causal chain a plot imposes, and the way a novel distributes one life across decades. I am deep into a book about three generations of a family whose grandfather prospered under a criminal regime, and I keep reaching for Karl Jaspers's division of guilt into criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical kinds, each with its own forum and its own judge, as a way of thinking about what a granddaughter could genuinely inherit. Translate that framework into my craft vocabulary, category by category: for each, say which of its claims maps cleanly onto something a novelist can build, which I can only render by distorting it, and which has no counterpart at all in the machinery of fiction. Then give me the residue report, and give it to me honestly. Above all: if metaphysical guilt — a solidarity with every human being as a fellow mortal, answerable to no human court — cannot be expressed in a form that requires one determinate person seen from one determinate angle, is that a limitation of the novel, or a sign that the category is doing less work than it appears to? Do not smuggle Jaspers's own assumptions into my vocabulary and hand it back to me as a translation; what I want to know is precisely what my form cannot hold.
