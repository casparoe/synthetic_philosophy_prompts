I've been reading about the discursive dilemma — cases where a panel votes separately on the reasons for a decision and on the decision itself, and the two votes point in opposite directions. Every version I've come across runs the same way: aggregating the reasons produces the harsher result, and aggregating the bottom-line question lets the subject off. I'd like to know whether that is a deep fact about the situation or just an artifact of how the examples are usually chosen, so please build me one running the other way.

Specifications: a panel of an odd number of people at an ordinary institution — a licensing board, a journal's editorial panel, an internal workplace review, anything but a courtroom. The panel must decide some outcome by applying a public rule that ties that outcome to two further factual questions. Each member votes sincerely on all three questions, applies the rule correctly, and holds a set of positions that is internally consistent. No ties anywhere, no strategic voting, no second rounds. Then majority-vote the two reasons and derive the outcome from them, separately majority-vote the outcome directly, and have the two disagree — with the direct vote imposing the sanction and the reasons-first route failing to support it.

Give me the scenario in one paragraph, a table of the votes, and both calculations. Then tell me which feature of the case makes the reversal possible, what happens to the result if I remove that feature, and how someone who holds that only the reasons-first procedure is legitimate would attack the case. Finish by saying where you think your construction is most likely to fail: which of my specifications you suspect cannot really be met together, or which step you would want checked formally.
