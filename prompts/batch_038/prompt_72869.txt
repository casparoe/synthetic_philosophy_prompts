I have read the rationalist blogosphere and its comment sections for the better part of two decades, which means I have now watched several generations of the same argument run to ground: could a sufficiently large computer plan an economy? Every time, the exchange seems to slide down a fixed staircase of positions, and I have never managed to find a name for the bottom step, which is the one that actually decides anything.

The first step is computational. The objector says the planner cannot possibly solve the millions of simultaneous equations that a market solves implicitly every day; the defender answers that this is a claim about arithmetic, and arithmetic keeps getting cheaper. The second step is informational. The objector retreats to the claim that the relevant facts are scattered across millions of heads and much of it is known only inarticulately by the people who have it — what this particular machine tool will actually do in this particular shop, who needs what next Thursday, which supplier is quietly unreliable — so there is nothing to feed the machine. The defender answers that this is a claim about bandwidth and incentives, and both are engineerable: ask better questions, structure the reporting so honesty pays, and the facts can be elicited.

The third step is not a stronger version of the second, and this is where I always lose the thread. Here the objector says that some of the facts the plan requires are not sitting out there unelicited, however clever the questionnaire and however honest the respondents. They come into existence only as the residue of people actually competing: two firms bidding against each other for the same input, somebody trying a production method nobody has tried and failing at it, a price that has never existed being arrived at by a rivalry that has never occurred. Before the rivalry happens there is no fact of the matter about what the number would have been, so there is nothing to survey, nothing to report, and nothing to transmit. A planner that reads the numbers off a still-running market and then abolishes it has not copied the numbers into a new setting; it has destroyed the only thing generating them and is thereafter operating from a stale printout of a process that has stopped. The same shape appears in miniature when a large firm suppresses internal rivalry, finds its transfer prices have become works of fiction, and is eventually driven to stage artificial internal markets in order to regenerate them; and again in any auction where the participants say afterwards that they had no idea what the thing was worth until the bidding told them.

So: does this have a name? I am not after references — I am after the conceptual geography.

1. Is there an established term for the claim that the inputs a plan needs are generated by the process it would replace, rather than merely hard to locate in advance of it? I know enough of the neighborhood to be confident that dispersed knowledge, tacit knowledge, and the incentive to misreport are all second-step points and none of them is this.

2. How exactly does it differ from the Lucas critique, from Goodhart's law, from reflexivity or performativity in the social sciences, and from a fixed-point or bootstrapping problem in which the input is the output of the computation? Each feels adjacent; I cannot say which, if any, is the same thing in different clothes.

3. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and mark its edges. I especially want the line drawn on two hard cases: a datum that is merely ruinously expensive to collect rather than nonexistent, and a datum produced by an institution that is not competitive in the ordinary sense — an auction the planner itself runs, with real bidders and real money on the line.

4. One worry I cannot shake: on this third step, any figure a planner produces can be dismissed as not having emerged from genuine rivalry, which makes the position impossible to lose. Does whatever concept you land on have that immunity built into it, or is there a principled point at which the objection stops applying?
