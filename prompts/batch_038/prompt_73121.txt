I teach a third-year module called Utility and Its Critics, and I'm putting together the final exam paper — two hours, closed book, and the one compulsory question I need is worth 25 marks with about 45 minutes of writing time allowed for it. By that point the students will have worked through chapters XII and XIII of the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation in some detail, plus a long edited extract from Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual offences (the ~1785 essay he never dared print, arguing that acts then hanged for ought not to be punished at all). What I want to avoid is the answer I know I'll get from half the room: Bentham was a utilitarian, he rejected the principle of antipathy as a ground of punishment, so of course he was a sexual liberal, QED. That's recitation, and it's worth about a C.

So here's the crux I want the question built on. Bentham's own analysis of the mischief of an act splits it into primary and secondary, and the secondary mischief includes alarm — the pain of apprehension that spreads out over a multitude of unassignable people — alongside danger. Alarm is real pain, and Bentham lets real pain into the calculus. Which means a prosecutor can turn his machinery against his conclusion and say something like this: four hundred householders in the parish are genuinely miserable at the thought of what happens behind that locked door, their misery is measurable pain, and hanging the offender stops it, so utility licenses the gallows. Call this the alarm loophole — it's my coinage, so feel free to have the question itself state and define it before asking anything, since the students won't have met the phrase. The question should make them decide whether Bentham can block the loophole and with what, and the honest answer is that he has more than one block available: that a trial manufactures far more publicity, and hence far more alarm, than the quiet act it punishes; that pain whose whole content is one's own cultivated disgust sits differently in the calculus from alarm about one's own security; that the act is not mischievous upon the whole, so the punishment is groundless; that it may be inefficacious, unprofitable, or needless where the mischief could be prevented at a cheaper rate. A strong answer should notice that these blocks are not all equally good and that at least one of them looks like it concedes something.

Please write the question so that it runs off a fact pattern rather than a quotation. Use this one, invented: two adult men meet once a fortnight in a back room of a house they rent and pay for between them; nobody else is present, nobody is coerced, nobody is told; a washerwoman overhears through a wall, mentions it to her employer, and inside a week 412 householders have signed a petition demanding prosecution on the sole stated ground that knowing it goes on makes them wretched and ashamed of the parish. Then ask whether Bentham, on his own principles and his own account of mischief, could sign that petition. Deliverables: the exam question as the students will see it, a model answer of about 650 words, and then a marking scheme given as exactly five numbered must-hit points — each one or two sentences, saying what the point is, what it's worth out of 25, and the standard way students blow it, and flagging which two of the five are the gate that an answer can't clear without (miss both and it can't go above a C regardless of polish). No introduction, no conclusion, no wrapping remarks, and no bibliography or further reading — they can't look anything up in the exam hall and I don't want padding. One more thing: paraphrase Bentham throughout and don't put any words in quotation marks, because a fabricated line in a model answer gets memorised and fed back to me at resits.
