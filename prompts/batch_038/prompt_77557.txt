I'm putting together the final for a seminar where the last third of term was Williamson on luminosity — the anti-luminosity argument, margin for error, and then the argument against KK. Twelve students, mixed MA and strong final-years, three hour paper, and I want this to be one of two questions, worth 25 marks, answerable in about 75 minutes.

The problem is that everyone in the class can recite the cold series back at me. So I've cooked up a case that's meant to be hostile to the argument in a different way from the usual objections, and I want the question built around it. Here's the case — polish the wording if you like but don't quietly change the mechanics, and if you think it's broken tell me where rather than fixing it behind my back:

Dana sits in a soundproof booth for an hour. A tone plays the whole time and its intensity is ramped down in 1001 steps, each so small that Dana cant tell any two adjacent stages apart by introspection; at the first stage the tone is unambiguously audible, at the last it's off. Dana knows the schedule — she knows it ramps down and goes off somewhere in the run — but not where. She's also wearing a wristband that buzzes sharply whenever her attention drifts off the tone, so the only route she has into a position to know whether she is attending to the tone is to attend to it, and attending to it is precisely what makes it true that she is.

Let C be: Dana is currently attending to the tone. I'm stipulating that C shades off gradually with the tone, in that at the faint end it becomes a borderline matter whether her attention is on the tone or on the silence where the tone used to be.

You'll spot the tension there — the wristband keeps her attending throughout, so it isn't obvious C fades rather than flips at some point. I want that tension left in and made into part of the question, because sorting it out is exactly what tells a student whether this is a counterexample to the argument or just a badly chosen predicate.

The question should state the anti-luminosity argument as a numbered list of premises — luminosity, the margin for error principle, factivity, and whatever the series assumption is that lets you run the induction — so nobody can be vague about which premise they're attacking. Then three parts:

(a) Which premise does the wristband case threaten, and why that one rather than another?
(b) Is the threat real? I want them to notice that being in a position to know isn't the same as actually knowing, and that if the only way into position is a way that changes which case you're in, the induction step stops talking about one fixed series. But I also want them to say whether that actually saves C's luminosity or just makes C luminous in a trivial way nobody ever wanted to deny.
(c) Does the same checking-constitutes-the-state move rescue KK — say via the transparency idea that you settle whether you know p by settling whether p? My own view is no, since attending doesn't constitute knowing the way it constitutes attending, but I want the scheme to reward a student who reaches that by their own route and equally to reward someone who defends the opposite verdict properly.

Deliverables: the question as it'll appear on the paper, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme totalling 25 with marks broken down by part. The scheme has to say what an A contains that a B leaves out — I'm guessing something about whether they can seperate the trivial-luminosity response from the genuine objection, but you decide and be specific. Then list four or five mistakes that should cost marks, e.g. sliding between knowing and being in a position to know, or treating the case as an observation-disturbs-the-system point from physics.

No citations or reading lists please — they won't have books in the room and I don't want the model answer padded with references I'd have to check.
