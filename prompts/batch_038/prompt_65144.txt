I am preparing the opening comment for a workshop session on price controls, and the organizers have asked me to say something about why the minimum-wage debate has not closed rather than to add another estimate to it. Two of the three panelists have been on opposite sides of this question for the better part of three decades, and I want my diagnosis to survive contact with both of them. I would like you to test a hypothesis I have been developing, and to attack it rather than flatter it.

Here is the state of play as I understand it. One camp reads the post-1990s literature — beginning with the paired fast-food establishments on either side of a state border that raised its wage floor, and continuing through recent border-county designs and event studies keyed to the bunching of the wage distribution at the floor — as showing that moderate minimum wages cost essentially no jobs, and increasingly as showing modest positive employment effects consistent with employers holding wage-setting power in thin, frictional local labor markets. The other camp reads substantially the same literature, plus its own analyses of employer payroll records and its cross-state panel work, as showing employment losses concentrated among teenagers, the least experienced, and new entrants, growing with the size of the increase relative to the prevailing wage, and appearing at margins other than headcount when headcount looks stable. Both camps publish in leading journals. Both commission meta-analyses; one recent exercise adjusts the pooled US estimates for publication bias and selective reporting, while the other camp objects that pooling estimates that fail an identification test is meaningless regardless of how one corrects for selection. Each side accuses the other of specification search, and neither has moved an inch in twenty-five years.

My hypothesis is that the dispute is not primarily about the magnitude of a single employment elasticity, and that treating it as such is what has made it interminable. I think the work is being done by at least four distinct layers, and I want to know which of them actually holds the impasse:

1. Model class. Whether the counterfactual labor market is competitive and segmented or a search market with employer wage-setting power determines whether a wage floor can raise employment at all, and a regression on historical data does not identify which class one is in.
2. Estimand definition. Headcount employment, hours, total payroll, quits, non-wage amenities, prices, and capital substitution are different dependent variables answering to the same phrase "the employment effect." Two honest researchers can disagree forever because they measured different things and called them the same thing.
3. Evidential standards. Whether to weight studies by identification quality or to treat the published pool symmetrically and correct it statistically for selection.
4. Distributional weighting. If the gains fall on incumbent low-wage workers and the costs, if any, on marginal workers who are harder to observe and harder to organize, then no regression decides whether that trade is acceptable.

My guess is that layers 2 and 4 do most of the explaining, and that layer 1 contains the only genuinely resolvable question hiding in the whole mess. I may well be wrong, and I would rather find that out from you than from a panelist.

So: assess the hypothesis, and commit. Tell me which layer the impasse really sits at, rank the layers by how much of the stalemate each explains, and say for each what would count as progress — a specific kind of data, a derivation, a redefinition of the estimand, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Add any layer I have missed, and tell me which of mine you think is doing no real work. Then answer one comparative question: does the same diagnosis transfer to rent control, where the instrument varies widely (hard caps on the rent of existing units, limits on annual increases, just-cause eviction rules, means-tested allowances), and is the rough consensus among economists there a sign that the minimum-wage case is peculiar, or a sign that that debate gets resolved by narrowing what counts as the policy rather than by accumulating evidence?

I want a definite position with reasons, not a balanced menu of interpretations that leaves the ranking to me. Where you are least confident in your own diagnosis, say so explicitly and say what would overturn it. You need not cite or summarize the literature; I know it. I want the structure of the disagreement.

After the prose diagnosis, give me a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- verdict: one or two sentences stating your overall diagnosis of where the impasse sits.
- primary_layer: the name of the layer you judge to explain most of it.
- layers: an array, one object per layer you recognize (mine, revised or rejected as you see fit, plus any you add), each with: name; content (one or two sentences on what the disagreement at this layer consists in); type (one of factual, model-selection, measurement-definitional, evidential-standard, normative, verbal); resolvable_by (one of new data, a formal derivation, redefinition of the estimand, nothing—it is normative, nothing—it is underdetermined by any possible evidence); what_would_move_it (the specific result, move, or concession that would constitute progress); weight_pct (an integer giving this layer's share of the impasse, with the weights across the array summing to 100).
- rejected_layers: any of my four layers you judge to be doing no real explanatory work, each with a one-clause reason.
- rent_control_transfer: yes or no, plus the single disanalogy you think matters most.
- lowest_confidence: the claim in your own diagnosis you are least sure of, and what would overturn it.
