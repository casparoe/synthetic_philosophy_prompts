I'm a third-year PhD student in a largely analytic department, and I came to philosophy of mind from cellular neuroscience, which I suspect is why I find this literature simultaneously irresistible and exasperating. For the past year I've been part of a weekly reading group on consciousness, and one of its regulars — a visitor from a neighbouring department — defends a constitutive panpsychism: phenomenal properties are fundamental features of the world, and the microphysical constituents of a brain are themselves tiny subjects whose experiences compose ours by way of what he calls phenomenal bonding relations. His stock answer to the combination problem is an analogy. A subject composed of subjects, he says, is like a committee composed of members: the committee has its own point of view, it weighs evidence and settles questions no single member had settled and acts on the result, and we ascribe beliefs and intentions to it without the least sense of mystery. If individuals can compose a higher-level agent, he asks, what could possibly prevent them from composing a higher-level subject? The analogy is load-bearing in his argument, and I have been asked to deliver a twenty-minute response to it next week.

So I'd like a proper audit of the comparison rather than a general assessment of his position. Start with an explicit correspondence table: what in the brain plays the role of the members, and what, if anything, corresponds to the agenda, the chair, the voting procedure, the minutes, and above all the committee's own viewpoint. Then give me the disanalogies, sorted into those that genuinely threaten the use he makes of the analogy and those that are merely decorative — the ones that would only weaken my comment if I pressed them. I'm especially interested in three questions. First, does the analogy covertly depend on the members already being conscious, and if so, is that dependence fatal to it, or does it only relocate the difficulty? Second, is the committee's viewpoint anything over and above a functional arrangement of the members' own states plus conventions we ourselves laid down and can revise at will — and if it isn't, what exactly does the panpsychist gain by pointing at it? Third, might the analogy, followed strictly, cut against him: in the committee case each member's experience remains their own, and no single unified field ever shows up, so the comparison may illustrate precisely the failure of composition rather than its possibility.

I already know the standard inventory — the subject-summing problem, the palette argument, the structural version of the difficulty — so please don't survey the combination-problem literature; keep the analysis on the comparison itself and on the work it is being made to do. Close by telling me plainly whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: does accepting that individuals can compose a group agent commit anyone to group phenomenal unity, or is the inference a slide from an intentional and conventional kind of composition to a phenomenal and natural one? And if the analogy cannot carry that weight, say whether there is a better one he should be reaching for, or whether the fact that he needs an analogy at all is itself evidence about how much his view can explain. No reading recommendations, please — I have more than enough.
