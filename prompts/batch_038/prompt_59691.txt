I sit on the eligibility panel of a regional hardship fund. Our charter directs us to compensate claimants for disadvantages that arose from circumstances they could not control, and to leave uncompensated those traceable to choices made under risks the claimant understood and accepted. Our published record now runs to eleven rulings. The seven that bear on the case before us are these.

1. A professional skier whose career ended in an injury sustained on an off-piste descent she had elected to attempt in conditions officially rated severe: declined. The panel wrote that she had taken a deliberate and informed risk of precisely this kind of harm.

2. A theatre nurse with a chronic respiratory condition acquired across nineteen years of shifts, every applicable protocol followed: approved. The panel held that exposure built into the structure of work one must hold is not a gamble.

3. A man who at eighteen declined a stable apprenticeship to train as an instrument maker, aware the trade was contracting, and who now earns a fraction of his former classmates: approved. The panel wrote that the manner of the trade's collapse — a flood of imported instruments, followed by a two-year suspension of live performance — was not among the risks he could reasonably have priced.

4. A long-term smoker with emphysema: the fund covered only that portion of care our medical assessor attributed to causes other than smoking, the panel holding that an award should track the share of the harm the claimant's own choices produced.

5. A claimant who had deliberately cultivated, over two decades, a taste for a rare vintage such that ordinary wine no longer satisfies him, and who sought the cost differential: declined. The panel wrote that the fund meets needs and not acquired preferences.

6. A claimant who chose firefighting knowing the documented risks of smoke inhalation and burns, and who later developed a rare autoimmune disorder with no established connection to either: approved, on the ground that the risks he accepted were specific and bounded.

7. A man who lost his savings at fixed-odds betting and subsequently required dialysis: approved as to treatment, declined as to lost earnings. The panel noted that the fund will not make a claimant's body the instrument of a lesson in prudence.

The new application is this. At thirty-six, the claimant elected an operative procedure to relieve a chronic pain condition she did not cause and had managed unsuccessfully for nine years. The disclosure she signed named, among other risks, a 1-in-400 chance of nerve damage causing partial loss of bladder control. That complication did not occur. What occurred was an adjacent condition — a fistula — which appeared in the disclosure only within a general category of other urological complications occurring in fewer than 1 in 1,000 cases, and not by name. She now requires a bespoke appliance and periodic revision surgery, and cannot resume the physical labour that was her trade. She claims both the lifetime cost of the appliance and revisions and her lost earnings capacity.

Please provide the following.

First, identify the closest analog among our precedents, and say on which dimension the similarity does the work — the electiveness of the procedure, the specificity of the disclosed risk, the fact that she was treating a harm she did not cause, or the split between her two heads of claim.

Second, set out the candidate rules our record is jointly consistent with. I expect at least three, and I expect some will differ from one another not in kind but in where they draw the line; name that too. For each rule, state what it yields on the new application, and whether it yields the same verdict on both heads of claim.

Third, recommend the ruling that best coheres with our own record, including whether the award should be split, and state plainly what principle we would be adopting by ruling that way and what we would be foreclosing for future claimants. If some of our precedents cannot bear the weight this recommendation places on them, say which and why.

Fourth, describe one further hypothetical application — concrete enough to be filed — that would discriminate among the rules our current record leaves open, so that we can commit to the distinction in advance rather than rationalize it afterwards.

I am not asking for the literature on any of this, and please do not supply references or attributions; work entirely from the record as stated above.
