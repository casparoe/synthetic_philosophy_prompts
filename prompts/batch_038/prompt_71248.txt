I run comms at a mid-size transparency nonprofit, about 40 staff. Every week we get 10–15 proposals to publicly call somebody out — a company, a mayor, a foundation — for conduct we've campaigned against for years. The problem is the glass house. Our record is public: two years ago we took a grant from a firm that does the exact thing we'd be naming them for, we have a board conflict nobody's resolved, and one of our own program teams was doing the thing until last year. My team is split — half say the standing talk is just a way to let bad actors off the hook, half say publishing while we're dirty costs us more credibility than it costs them anything.

Can you write us a decision procedure? Constraints: whoever has the proposal runs it in about twenty minutes, they have no philosophy training, and nothing goes out without the ED signing off, so the procedure has to end in something she can approve or reject in one line. Assume the only outputs available to us are these four — a full named callout that blames the actor, an un-named policy piece that describes the harm and the fix without blaming anybody, a hold (we fix our own side first and publish after, saying so), or handing the story to a coalition partner with cleaner hands.

It should be grounded in whatever the moral philosophy of standing and hypocrisy actually has to say that's usable, and I care a lot about which distinctions survive the simplification. Please keep these separate rather than lumping them: whether our claim is true versus whether we're well placed to make it; hypocrisy proper versus having changed our mind over time versus a failure we've already owned up to publicly; standing that's lost versus standing that's just weakened or deferred; standing that's been repaired, and how you'd actually know; whether an institution can have standing at all or only the person whose name is on the release; and whether standing can be borrowed by routing it through a partner. Flag every step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume — I already suspect any test for "is our own failure relevantly similar" is basically a shrug with a flowchart around it. And say clearly where the procedure stops and hands the call to a human because what's left is a trade-off about credibility and harm, not a question of fact.

Then run it on three cases: the mayor whose procurement scandal we've been sitting on for a month, given the grant; an ongoing harm where fixing ourselves first would take eighteen months, so the hold means the damage keeps happening; and a third you pick that you think we'd have standing to publish on despite our own unresolved mess, or that we'd have to kill even though it's true — I want the answer even if I hate it. Finish with a one-page version an intern could actually follow, and a short list of the ways this procedure will misfire in practice.
