I'm the philosophy person on a small press's advisory panel — we rule on whether to take on books that dramatise real people — and I want to think through next month's manuscript before the meeting rather than during it. Our track record so far:

- Approved: a novel inventing the inner life and private conversations of a much-documented 19th-century abolitionist lecturer, with an afterword listing what was made up. Minute: "flagged, and the invention stays inside what the record leaves open."
- Approved with conditions: a graphic novel about a 17th-century midwife convicted of witchcraft, where only the trial transcript survives. Distant relatives objected to the trial being depicted at all; we required the transcript be reprinted in an appendix instead. Minute: "a family's distress is not a veto, but readers have to be able to check."
- Refused: a docudrama we'd have published in book form, sold as "a true story," no disclaimer, showing a mid-century union leader as an informant when the released files say the opposite, with his widow still living. Minute: "it isn't so, and it lands on people who are still here."

The new one. A novel about a disaster-relief doctor who died in 1978; the official inquiry records that he stayed at his post through the collapse. The book has him walk away for four hours and return to an empty ward, and its whole argument is that the inquiry was a whitewash. The afterword names every invented scene and says outright that there's no documentary evidence he left — the author calls it a counterfactual biography. His granddaughter, a historian, has written a blurb backing it, saying the family wanted someone to ask the question for decades.

Our chair keeps a distinction between filling a silence and overridding the record, and thinks only the second can wrong anybody. I suspect that's not doing the work he thinks. My own worry is a kind of free-riding: the book borrows the credibility the real man earned and spends it on a scene that never happened, and I don't see how a flag refunds that.

So — which principles are genuinely compatible with those three verdicts, what does each imply for this manuscript, and what's your best guess at how we vote? Which precedent is the closest analog? If I argue one side, what principle am I committing us to, including for future manuscripts I won't like? And what's the cheapest hypothetical case I could put in front of the panel that would force apart the transparency principle and the fidelity-to-the-record principle?
