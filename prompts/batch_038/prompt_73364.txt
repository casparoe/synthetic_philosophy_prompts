In six weeks I am giving a forty-minute paper at a workshop jointly run by a cultural-evolution modelling group and a social anthropology department, and I would like to walk into the question period having already heard it. The thesis is a deliberately limited revival of the replicator framework within the Darwinian theory of culture. Borrowing the graded picture from Godfrey-Smith's work on Darwinian populations — on which properties such as fidelity of transmission come in degrees, so that a population sits somewhere in a space rather than falling inside or outside a binary — I argue that fidelity is not one global feature of culture but a domain-relative and measurable one: formulaic and liturgical speech, counting-out rhymes, legal boilerplate, notated music, and tightly scripted craft recipes sit at the high end, while everyday beliefs about illness, gossip, and fashion sit at the low end. My argument has three steps. First, fidelity in a given domain can be estimated independently of any selection story, from agreement across independent performances and from the retention of arbitrary detail across generations. Second, in the high-fidelity domains, arbitrary and even semantically dead features persist for long stretches — obsolete words in rhymes whose meaning no living performer knows, causally inert steps in ritual sequences, formulae recited in a language nobody present understands — and persistence of that kind is precisely what content-bias and attractor-based accounts must explain away, since on those accounts there is no content left for a mind to be drawn toward. Third, therefore, in those domains the explanans for differential retention is not reconstruction toward cognitively privileged forms but the copying of a lineage-item, and the replicator vocabulary is doing work there rather than decorating it.

The audience will not be philosophers. There will be three or four mathematical modellers in the dual-inheritance tradition descended from Boyd and Richerson, who already carry parameters for copying error and for content-, frequency-, and prestige-biased transmission, and who will suspect that my fidelity measure is their copying-error rate under a grander name — a noun being added where only a rate was needed. There will be two social anthropologists with long field records, one working on ritual performance and liturgical language and one on plural medical systems, for whom the identity of an item across performances is settled by native categories and by the analyst's coding scheme rather than discovered in the world, and who know perfectly well that their discipline spent the early twentieth century abandoning the search for migrating item-lineages. There will be a developmental psychologist who will point out that children copy causally irrelevant steps with striking fidelity and that this fidelity is produced by social motives — normativity, affiliation, deference — which, if right, means my high-fidelity domains are the ones psychology explains best rather than the ones it cannot touch. And there will be one sympathetic philosopher of biology who will ask the structural question I do not yet have a good answer to.

Please prepare me for that period. Give me the eight or nine objections this particular room will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the thesis — not the all-purpose complaints about memetics that any audience would make, but the ones these people will make, in their own idiom: the modeller talking about parameters and identifiability, the ethnographer about performance and native taxonomy, the developmentalist about the mechanisms generating the fidelity. For each, give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes in my own voice, followed by a candid note on where that reply is soft and what it costs me to make it. Tell me separately which two or three concessions I should volunteer in the opening five minutes to take the cheap shots off the table, which commitments I must not surrender under any pressure, and the single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive this room at all. Then identify the objection I should be hoping nobody asks: if you think the most dangerous one is the structural question from my sympathetic colleague — that if fidelity is a matter of degree, replicator is not a kind, and I should keep the parameter and drop the noun — say so, and give me the least dishonest answer available to me if it comes anyway. Finally, if any of the objections is one you think I have simply got wrong and should cut from the paper rather than defend, say that plainly instead of helping me defend it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which single objection is most likely to sink the talk — and then give the prep sheet, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
