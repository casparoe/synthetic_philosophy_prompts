I run a four-person quantitative risk group at an engineering consultancy. Regulators ask our clients for failure probabilities for things that have never failed in service — a new hydrogen sealing assembly, a first-of-a-kind battery storage layout, a novel treatment chemistry — so there is no history to fit. Our standing house rule is: take the parameter the client thinks in, spread a uniform distribution across its plausible range, and read off the number.

A new hire objected that this is exactly the error in Bertrand's chord problem. "A random chord" is not one question: choosing two points on the circumference, choosing a radius and a point along it, and choosing a point in the disc as the midpoint are all equally natural ways to draw one, and they give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 for the chance the chord exceeds a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Her objection lands. I want the house rule replaced.

Write the replacement as a procedure my analysts can run in about half an hour per request: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to test whether a number survives recomputation under other equally natural parameterizations, and how much movement between them is too much to file. Say where the procedure stops at "not estimable as a single number," where it hands the matter up to me because what remains is a decision rather than a fact, and which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. If there is a defensible general principle for choosing among parameterizations, state it along with its limits; if there is not, say so plainly rather than dignifying a convention.

Then run it on three live requests: (i) forty bench-tested seals, zero failures, client wants an annual failure probability; (ii) whether a new solvent's metabolite exceeds a toxicity threshold, where the question can be posed in concentration, in log concentration, or as a fraction of the distance between a safe analogue and a known-bad one; (iii) the probability that an operator deliberately defeats an interlock during a shift. In at least one of the three, the procedure should give me an answer I do not want.

Please organize the response as: "What the paradox actually licenses" (~150 words); "The procedure" (numbered steps, ~500 words); "Stopping and escalation rules" (~200 words); "Three worked requests" (~200 words each); "What this procedure cannot do" (~150 words).
