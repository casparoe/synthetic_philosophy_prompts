Reading Josephus on Masada in Whiston's old translation (Jewish War, book 7, around 7.341-350) and I'm stuck on Eleazar's second speech, the one where he tries to talk the Sicarii into killing their families and then themselves. Here's the stretch I mean:

"for this last affords our souls their liberty, and sends them by a removal into their own place of purity, where they are to be insensible of all sorts of misery; for while souls are tied down to a mortal body, they are partakers of its miseries; and really, to speak the truth, they are themselves dead; for the union of what is divine to what is mortal is disagreeable. [...] It is this soul which hath one nature, and that an incorruptible one also; but yet it is the cause of the change that is made in the body; for whatsoever it be which the soul touches, that lives and flourishes; and from whatsoever it is removed, that withers away and dies; such a degree is there in it of immortality. Let me produce the state of sleep as a most evident demonstration of the truth of what I say; wherein souls, when the body does not distract them, have the sweetest rest depending on themselves, and conversing with God, by their alliance to him; they then go everywhere, and foretell many futurities beforehand. And why are we afraid of death, while we are pleased with the rest that we have in sleep? And how absurd a thing is it to pursue after liberty while we are alive, and yet to envy it to ourselves where it will be eternal!"

Just before this he's claimed the laws and God have always taught them that life is the calamity, not death. Can you walk me through what's actually being argued here as opposed to just asserted? Mainly: what work is the sleep analogy doing — is he offering it as evidence that the soul survives, or as a way of making death feel familiar? And what does he think he means by saying souls tied to a body "are themselves dead"?

The thing that has me properly confused is a few books earlier. When Josephus himself is cornered in the cave at Jotapata and his men want to do the same thing, he argues the opposite in his own voice — self-murder as impiety against the creator, our being lent to us by God and his to take back, the soul as a deposit you mustn't just throw out of the body, suicides consigned to the darkest part of Hades. Same author, same war, same act, opposite verdicts.

So is that just sloppiness, or is something more deliberate going on — speaker character, a different intended audience for each speech, the conventions of the set-piece oration? And what does the soul metaphysics buy him that the first speech's honor-and-liberty pitch didn't, given he explicitly says he gave the second one because the first didn't land with everyone?

Last bit: when this gets recycled into a national myth, which half carries the weight — death-before-slavery, or the soul's release? I want to know what the story has to keep holding onto in order to keep working.
