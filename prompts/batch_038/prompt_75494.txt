I spent thirty-four years as a physical oceanographer, most of it on ships working the North Atlantic and a few seasons up on the ice, and since I retired I've drifted into a Tuesday reading group in the back room of the library that does philosophy. We did Keynes's *A Treatise on Probability* (1921) last month, which I know sounds like an odd pick for a bunch of retirees, and it very nearly started a fight. Two things from it have been rattling around in my head. First, he treats probability as a logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion rather than as a number you compute, and he thinks it doesn't have to be a number at all: his example is whether to take an umbrella when the barometer is high but the clouds are black, and he says that on some occasions it's simply not the case that rain is more likely than not, or less likely than not, or as likely as not, and that at that point the rational move is to let caprice settle it instead of going on debating. Second — and this is the one that got under my skin — he separates the probability of an argument from its "weight," which is about how much relevant evidence you have rather than which way it points. He writes that the comparison "turns upon a balance, not between the favourable and the unfavourable evidence, but between the absolute amounts of relevant knowledge and of relevant ignorance respectively," and that "New evidence will sometimes decrease the probability of an argument, but it will always increase its 'weight.'" My whole career consisted of handing other people numbers with error bars attached, so the suggestion that some of my probabilities were never numbers in the first place, and that two of them might not be comparable even in principle and not merely through my ignorance, is genuinely uncomfortable. I tried to write something about this myself and it came out as a lecture with scenery, so I'd rather hand it to you.

What I want is a short story, call it 1,500 to 2,500 words, that gets these ideas into the plot instead of into the dialogue. Here's a seed, change what you like: a small overwintering station sitting out on the fast ice at the end of the season, with about forty tons of fuel and food to get in before things close up. Three ways to do it — run a tracked convoy straight across a stretch of first-year ice the old hands call the Shelf; wait for a supply ship to work around the lead system from the south, which is slower and may not make the freeze-up; or send two people ahead on snowmobiles to auger cores and get real thickness data along the route, which costs a day and a half nobody is sure they have. Put in a young modeler seconded from the mainland who has a spreadsheet of Monte Carlo runs and a thickness climatology and who has never been on the ice before, and an ice master who has crossed that route for thirty years and cant be budged. Now here's the beat I care most about: a fresh under-ice sonar transect comes in off a passing autonomous vehicle, and it makes the Shelf look worse — the estimated chance that it holds goes down — and yet everyone in the room, the ice master included, agrees they now know a good deal more than they did that morning, and that change is what shifts what the station commander is willing to do, in a way the spreadsheet has no column for. A second beat: at the decisive moment the two people who know the ice best flatly refuse to rank two of the three options, and when the modeler keeps asking which of them is more likely to work, the answer she gets is not "we haven't measured it yet" but "there isn't an answer to that," and the story should let the reader feel how unsatisfying and how possibly correct that is. Third beat: somebody has to choose anyway, and I want the cost of choosing on the page rather than an argument about who was right. Please don't end on a moral or a reveal, and don't have any character say the phrase "weight of evidence" or name Keynes anywhere — the theory should be load-bearing and invisible.

One small thing at the end, since I'm collecting these for the group: after the story, add a single final line that starts with `Theme:` and gives one sentence saying which of the two ideas the plot actually turns on — the non-numerical, incomparable probabilities or the probability/weight split, or both — so I can check you didn't just decorate a competent disaster story with my hobby.
