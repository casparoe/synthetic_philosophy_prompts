I work on machine-learning systems rather than philosophy — my day job involves evaluating probabilistic forecasters with proper scoring rules, monitoring calibration drift, and choosing which update rule a model should apply when new inputs arrive. Reading into the philosophical side of that machinery, I landed on the accuracy-first program in epistemology (Joyce, Pettigrew, and the work downstream of them), which I understand to run roughly as follows: the norms governing credences are grounded in a single value, accuracy, measured by an inaccuracy score defined over whole credence functions; incoherent credences are ruled out because any incoherent credence function is accuracy-dominated — beaten in every possible world by some coherent alternative; and conditionalization is recommended because, among candidate updating rules, it maximizes expected accuracy, where the expectation is taken with respect to the agent's prior credences.

The position I would like you to argue for is that this is not one argument but two arguments held together by an unacknowledged shift in decision-theoretic criteria, and that no single criterion can discharge both jobs. The dominance argument for coherence evaluates credence functions world-by-world and deliberately ignores the agent's own credences; the expected-accuracy argument for updating evaluates policies precisely by taking expectations with respect to those same credences. I want the case pressed as sharply as possible in two directions. First: what follows if a theorist insists on dominance everywhere, applying the same sure-loss standard to updating policies that they apply to states — does it prove too much, too little, or does it quietly reintroduce the agent's credences through the back door? Second: what follows if they insist on expectation-by-the-agent's-own-lights everywhere, including for the coherence norms? Here I hit a snag while tinkering, and I would like it addressed head-on rather than waved away: an incoherent credence function does not generally define a probability measure, so weighting worlds by it does not yield an expectation at all, and renormalizing seems to repair a failure to sum to one without repairing, for instance, assigning more credence to a strictly weaker claim than to a stronger one. If the self-expected-accuracy criterion is not even well-defined for the agent it is supposed to indict, is the demand for a uniform criterion simply confused — or is that incoherence exactly the point? A colleague who took a formal epistemology course tells me this is a solved problem and that I am confusing a state with a policy; I would like the argument built so that I can see whether she is right.

Please make the case concrete rather than schematic. Give me a small finite model — a handful of worlds, one partition, the Brier score — and show me the actual numbers where the two criteria come apart, including a case in which an accuracy-dominated agent nonetheless looks best by its own lights, or, if no such case is possible, a demonstration of why not. Then present the strongest defenses available to a partisan of the program, in particular the reply that coherence is a property of a state whereas updating is the selection of a policy, so different criteria are appropriate to different questions and no illicit shift has occurred, and the reply that the framework is only ever meant to be applied to agents who are already coherent, so the circularity is by design and harmless. Say whether the argument survives those replies intact or must be weakened, and if it must be weakened, state exactly the weaker thesis that does survive. Finally, since I have no reliable way of checking any of this against the literature: be explicit about which parts of the picture are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it, flag anywhere you are advancing a reading of the program that its defenders would reject as a caricature, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
