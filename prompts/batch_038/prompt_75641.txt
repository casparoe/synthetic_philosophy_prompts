Mill's claim, as I read it, is that when several causes act together there are two fundamentally different sorts of case. In the first, the joint effect is the sum of what each cause would have produced on its own, so the outcome can be computed from the separate laws; he takes this to hold throughout mechanics. In the second, the joint effect is no sum at all: hydrogen and oxygen have their properties, but the properties of water resemble neither, and nothing in one's knowledge of the separate laws would have enabled one to predict them. The laws of such compounds he calls heteropathic — they answer to causes in a manner not derivable from the laws of the ingredients, and the compound has laws of its own. Yet Mill insists that this does not place chemistry outside the domain of law: the heteropathic sequences are still uniform, they are still produced by the ingredients acting after their natures, and a deeper acquaintance with those ingredients might one day render them deducible.
