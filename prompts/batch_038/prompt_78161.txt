Our civic-tech meetup had a speaker on citizens' assemblies last week and afterwards two regulars, Rosa and Dan, got into it in the parking lot. I've been mulling it ever since and can't work out who I'd back. Trying to give it move by move because the details seem to matter.

(1) Rosa opened by saying elections were never the democratic method — the Athenians filled most offices by lot, and the old view was that choosing by vote is the aristocratic trick while choosing by lot is the democratic one, so "democracy means elections" is a modern rebranding that quietly hands power to whoever can fund a campaign. She said a randomly selected house of 400, serving one long term, no reappointment, would be strictly more democratic than anything we currently have.

(2) Dan said she was confusing fair selection with legitimate rule. A lottery makes everyone equally *likely* to hold office, but it isn't an act of authorization by anybody — no one's consent gets expressed anywhere in the process, so the resulting body rules on its own account. He said that's why a full lottocracy would feel like being governed by a standing committee that happens to get shuffled every so often.

(3) Rosa said that objection proves too much: elected representatives aren't delegates either, they vote their own judgment and can't be instructed, so if authorization is doing any real work it's doing it once every few years at a level of generality where the voter can't possibly know what they're authorizing. And she added that we already run lottocratic institutions nobody proposes to elect — juries — and we trust those with people's liberty.

(4) Dan said juries are the counterexample, not the analogy. A jury decides a question of fact under a standard somebody else gave it; its term is weeks; its deliberation is structured by an adversarial procedure and a judge who controls what it's allowed to see; and its verdict is reviewable. Strip all that out and give the same twelve people the power to write the standard itself, permanently and unreviewably, and the thing that made the jury trustworthy is exactly what's gone. He said legislation is the one activity that has no external standard to be measured against, so the analogy inverts on her.

(5) Rosa said fine, then build those features in — a lottocratic house handed narrow, well-defined questions, professional staff, transparent procedure, judicial review of whether it followed its own rules — and pointed out that's roughly what the real citizens' assemblies that have actually been run look like, and their output has been better informed and less polarized than what legislatures produce on the same topics. She also said the accountability complaint is weak, since voters are rationally ignorant anyway, so the electoral sanction against any particular bad decision is close to zero in practice.

(6) Dan said that's a bait and switch: once you add the staff who frame the questions, the experts who get called, the procedure, and the review, the random 400 are mostly ratifying whatever the permanent apparatus produced, and the lottery now contributes nothing except a guarantee that members arrive uninformed. At that point, he said, you don't have rule by the people, you have rule by a civil service with a randomly selected audience. And he added that even the assemblies she likes recruit from whoever recieves the invitation and says yes, acceptance rates are low and skewed, so the mirror-of-the-public claim fails at the very first step.

(7) Rosa's last move was that deliberation changing people's minds is the whole point rather than a bug: if a well-run process turns a descriptively representative sample into a body with informed judgment, that's the best available model of the public's judgment, and certainly better than an election, which just totals up uninformed preferences. Dan said then she's given up on democracy and is defending epistocracy with a lottery bolted on as a recruitment tool.
