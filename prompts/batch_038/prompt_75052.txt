I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of art, and the last two meetings of the term are on forgery and the value of originals. By that point the students will have worked through Goodman's distinction between autographic and allographic arts, including his argument that a forged Rembrandt and the original cannot be aesthetically on a par even when they are visually indistinguishable, because knowing that a difference is there to be found changes what we look for and trains us to make discriminations we could not previously make; and they will have read the familiar replies — roughly, that the value of the original lies in what the artist actually did, that forgery is a failure of achievement rather than of appearance, and that works are individuated by the path an artist took to them rather than by their perceptible surface. I want to screen Welles's *F for Fake* (1973; French title *Vérités et mensonges*, eighty-eight minutes) the week beforehand, but I refuse to screen it as decoration. In my experience the film gets used either as a colourful warm-up to the forgery problem or as evidence that authenticity is a bourgeois fetish, and neither use requires anyone to have read anything. What I want is criticism that treats the film as though it were doing philosophy, and that is prepared to say when it is doing it badly.

Here is the material I want you to work through, in the film's own order: the opening magic tricks for children and the invocation of Robert-Houdin's idea that a magician is an actor playing a magician; Welles's promise that everything in the next hour will be true; Oja Kodar walking through the square as a deliberate experiment in being looked at; Elmyr de Hory's dinner party on Ibiza, with Clifford Irving among the guests, Irving having written the book *Fake* about him and having meanwhile been exposed as the author of a fabricated authorized biography of Howard Hughes; Reichenbach's account of being handed pictures of doubtful provenance that the dealers chose not to interrogate; Welles's own three confessions about his career in fakery (talking his way onto a stage in Ireland by impersonating a celebrated New York actor, the *War of the Worlds* broadcast, and the plan to build *Citizen Kane* out of Hughes's life); Elmyr's insistence that he never signed a forgery; the excursion to Chartres, where the question of the signature is set against the anonymous masons whose names are lost while the building stands; and the coda, in which Kodar's grandfather paints the Picassos, is confronted, has already burned them, and asks only that the fraud go unreported so that he may have a legacy — after which Welles announces that the promised hour has expired and that the coda was invented.

With that in view, please answer the following, in prose of roughly 1,500 to 1,800 words, under headings of your own choosing, arguing rather than surveying. First: does the film have a position on the indiscernibility argument, or does it merely enjoy the tension? The experts certified the fakes, which is normally read as scepticism about aesthetic perception; but Goodman's claim is not that we can spot the difference unaided — it is that the knowledge that a difference exists is what makes looking worth the trouble. Does the film have the resources to hold those two thoughts apart, or does it slide between them? Second: what philosophical work is the signature doing, and does the Chartres sequence function as a counterexample to attribution-based accounts of value at all? Nothing at Chartres is a forgery; the masons never claimed to be anyone else. Tell me whether Welles's juxtaposition is an argument or an equivocation between anonymity and false attribution, and what is lost or gained by the slide. Third: the coda's retraction. If the Picasso story loses something the moment we learn it was invented, though nothing perceptible about the film changes, has the film performed Goodman's argument on itself — a work whose aesthetic character shifts with provenance? Or has it equivocated, on the ground that a documentary makes assertions with truth values while a painted surface does not, so the analogy between epistemic and aesthetic properties is not one the philosophy licenses? I want a verdict. Fourth: forgery as improvement. The grandfather's fakes are, within the fiction, better than what the master was then producing, and his only demand is a legacy. What does that case cost the achievement-based and historical accounts, and does the film endorse his plea or merely stage it? Fifth: where the film resists any of this. A good reading should end by conceding what it has imposed, and should say what the film gives the debate that argument alone does not — the market's complicity, the dignity of the forger, the possibility that the real issue was institutional and ethical rather than aesthetic from the start, and whether that reframing is an advance or a dodge.
