I write about research ethics and the life sciences for a general-interest magazine, and before I describe American laboratory-animal oversight in print I want to pin down the philosophy sitting underneath it. The practice, as I understand it: every protocol using a covered animal must go before an institutional oversight committee, justify the number of animals, show that non-animal alternatives were considered, and provide anesthesia and post-procedure pain relief — though the committee may waive the pain relief when the pain is judged scientifically necessary. Euthanasia at the end of a study appears as a routine line in the protocol rather than as a harm requiring its own justification. And the federal statute's definition of "animal" excludes rats, mice, and birds bred for research, along with farm animals used in food and fiber research, so that a laboratory dog is owed committee review, veterinary care, inspections, and annual public reporting while a laboratory rat owes nothing to that statute — the same rat being covered, in practice, by the animal-care policy that comes attached to federal grant money, so its protection turns on the funding channel rather than on the animal.

Critics routinely call this a Cartesian regime, animals treated as machines. That reads backwards to me: Descartes held that the machine has no experience to protect, and this apparatus is organized almost entirely around protecting experience. So what does it presuppose instead? Reconstruct the implicit account of animal minds and of moral standing — which property the system treats as the mark of considerability, and what moral work is being done by pain as against death. Tell me which rival accounts the practice rules out (mechanism; a rights-based view on which death is the central harm; any view on which status admits of no gradation by species), and where it is simply underdetermined, since the exclusion of rodents may reflect budget and workload rather than a theory of mind at all. I am especially interested in whether the system's reliance on publicly legible signs of distress quietly adopts a criterion for attributing minds closer to Descartes than the critics' rhetoric allows — a test of what we can recognize rather than of what is felt. Finally: what would have to change, in the paperwork and in the science, if the presupposed account were replaced by one on which an animal's continued life counts, and not merely its experience?
